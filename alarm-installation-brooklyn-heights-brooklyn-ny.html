The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Heights Properties Need Alarm Installation

Brooklyn Heights is a small place with a long record. Wikipedia gives it about a third of a square mile, bounded by Old Fulton Street near the Brooklyn Bridge on the north, Cadman Plaza West on the east, Atlantic Avenue on the south and the Brooklyn–Queens Expressway and the East River on the west, with Dumbo, Downtown Brooklyn, Cobble Hill and Boerum Hill around it. About 25,000 people lived there at the 2020 census. Almost all of them live in buildings that were standing long before anyone thought about electronic security, and a great many of them live in houses whose fronts are protected by law.

The neighborhood was a suburb before the word was common. Hezekiah Beers Pierrepont, a merchant who owned much of the bluff, had his land laid out as streets in 1802, according to the Cultural Landscape Foundation, and backed Robert Fulton’s steam ferry, which began regular crossings to Manhattan in 1814. Wikipedia records that after 1823 the farms were divided into 25-by-100-foot lots, that the number of houses rose from seven in 1807 to more than 600 by 1860, and that the area is often called the first commuter suburb. By 1890 it was almost fully built. Brownstone and brick rowhouses, a few late Federal houses in the northern blocks, Greek Revival, Gothic Revival and Italianate fronts, and a handful of mansions along Pierrepont Street and Pierrepont Place make up most of what stands today.

Then came the protection. The Historic Districts Council records that the Brooklyn Heights Historic District was designated on November 23, 1965, as New York City’s first historic district; the Cultural Landscape Foundation describes it as a district of about 140 acres. Inside it, changes to the outside of a building go through the Landmarks Preservation Commission. For most owners that sounds like a matter for architects and window replacements. For an alarm, it is the first design question, because the conventional alarm is a very visible thing: a siren box high on the front wall, a strobe, a wire stapled down the facade to a telephone line, a sign. The Commission’s Permit Guidebook says equipment of this general kind “cannot be on or in front of a primary facade unless no other option exists,” that it must not be attached on decorative surfaces, and that conduit should be concealed through plain masonry. We take that as a brief rather than an obstacle. A modern alarm does not need to show itself to work.

What the record also shows is how houses here are actually entered. In March 2011 Brownstoner relayed an email from the Brooklyn Heights Association reporting five daytime break-ins over four days, every one of them through a door that had been left unlocked; a resident since 1984 told the site that unlocked doors were simply part of the comfort of living in the Heights. Over the 2016 holidays the pattern shifted to windows. Brooklyn Paper reported that most of a run of burglaries on Willow, Joralemon and Montague Streets came through open windows, and Patch reported on January 4, 2017 that in some cases a burglar appeared to have climbed in through an unlocked window, possibly from a fire escape, mostly on weekdays during working hours. DNAinfo counted seven Heights burglaries in the first week of January 2017 alone, including one where the intruder used a key left in the resident’s mailbox.

Put those two things together and the shape of a Heights alarm is clear. The openings that matter are the ones the street does not see: the parlor windows above a low stoop, the rear windows over the yard, the windows onto a fire escape or an extension roof, the garden-floor door under the stoop and the back door to the yard. The time that matters is the working day, when the house is empty and the block is quiet. And the habit that matters is the one the Brooklyn Heights Association kept urging, locking up when you leave, which an alarm can turn into something you cannot forget, because the keypad tells you which window is still open before it will arm.

There is a second reason the alarm moves inside. Many Heights houses are no longer one household. Wikipedia notes that after the Brooklyn Bridge opened in 1883 and the subway arrived in 1908, wealthy families left and many mansions were divided into apartments and rooming houses. Today a typical brownstone may be an owner’s triplex over a rented garden apartment, a stack of floor-through rentals, or a co-op of five or six units. Each of those arrangements needs its own answer to who arms what, who gets the alarm call, and whose code opens which door. A single siren on the front wall answers none of those questions. A system with separate partitions, inside sirens and named user codes answers all of them.

And there are buildings that were never houses at all. Grace Court Alley, a one-block dead end off Hicks Street, began as the Remsen family’s stable lane; CityLore describes its mid-19th-century carriage houses, built to serve the mansions of Joralemon and Remsen Streets, as two and three stories tall with almost all of their original hay cranes, converted first into garages and then into homes. The Hotel St. George on Clark Street, once the city’s largest hotel with 2,632 rooms, now holds co-op apartments and student housing above the Clark Street subway entrance. Each of these needs a different alarm, and none of them is helped by a siren box on the front.

None of this is an argument from fear, and we do not sell it as one. The burglary reports above are from 2011 and the winter of 2016–17; we have no current neighborhood crime figures and do not forecast crime. The 84th Precinct covers a much larger area than the Heights. What the record does show is the method: an unlocked door at midday, an open window over a fire escape, a key in a mailbox. Those are exactly the things a well-planned alarm closes off, and they can be closed off without adding a single thing to a landmarked front.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Brooklyn Heights’ Architecture

Heights buildings were put up across most of the 19th century by many builders, so unlike a neighborhood of one developer’s rows, no two blocks behave quite alike. The types below cover nearly everything we are asked to protect between Old Fulton Street and Atlantic Avenue. Read each card as a first guess that the walk-through confirms or corrects; a century and a half of owners means sash replaced, stoops rebuilt, cellars finished and extensions added.

Brownstone and brownstone-fronted rowhouses

Three and four stories over a garden level, a high stoop to the parlor floor, wood sash front and back, and usually a deep rear yard. Most of the district’s houses are this type, many now split into an owner’s triplex and a garden rental.

Brick Greek Revival and Federal houses

Lower, plainer brick fronts in the older northern blocks around Middagh, Cranberry and Willow Streets, some with dormers and very old sash. Smaller rooms, thinner wood, and often a low rear extension with a flat roof below the bedroom windows.

Mansions and wide houses

The larger houses along Pierrepont Street and Pierrepont Place, and the wide houses on Columbia Heights near the Promenade: more windows per floor, sometimes a side or rear garden on two sides, and service stairs that were built as a second way in.

Carriage houses and mews

The converted stables of Grace Court Alley and other small lanes: wide former carriage doors, often a garage door still, a single living floor over it, and windows on the lane rather than a street.

Prewar co-ops and converted hotels

Apartment buildings and conversions such as the Hotel St. George complex on Clark Street, where co-op apartments have operated since 1984: one apartment door, a few windows, a board and a managing agent, and heavy masonry walls between rooms.

Newer buildings and conversions

Postwar towers near Cadman Plaza and the large conversions on the waterfront edge, including the former Watchtower buildings at 25–30 Columbia Heights, where New York YIMBY reported plans in March 2026 for 661 rental apartments.

Old wood windows: contacts that leave the sash alone

The single most common question on a Heights walk-through is about the windows. Many are original or early wood sash, some still with old glass, and owners are rightly reluctant to let anyone drill them. There are three ways to protect a window, and we choose per window, not per house. A recessed contact goes into the frame, not the moving sash, with its small magnet set into the bottom rail where the window closes; when it is painted over, nobody can find it. Where the sash is too thin or too precious for even that, a slim surface contact goes on the inside face of the frame, where it can be removed later with nothing but two screw holes to fill. And where a window is never opened at all, or is fixed stained glass, we leave the window untouched and cover the room with an acoustic glass-break detector that listens for the particular sound of breaking glass.

Parlor floors and plaster: where the panel and keypads go

A brownstone’s parlor is the room owners most want left as it is. We keep it that way by putting the panel and its battery in the cellar or a closet, and keypads in places that already hold switches or modern fixtures: the garden-floor entry, a back hall, the inside of a closet door on the parlor floor. Keypads are thin, and many owners now arm from a phone and a key fob, so a parlor can have no keypad at all. Cable from the cellar rises inside the walls that were already opened at some point for plumbing or electrical work, which in most Heights houses is the rear or the stair wall, and never across decorative plaster.

Party walls, cellars and the route from front to back

Every rowhouse here shares its side walls with its neighbors, so nothing we install goes into or through a party wall. Wired devices are fed from the cellar along the joists, front to back, and up through the floors at the rear and the stair. Above the parlor floor, where wiring would mean opening plaster, we switch to wireless devices that report to the panel by radio. A house that is being renovated gets the opposite treatment: while walls and ceilings are open, we pull cable to every opening, so the finished house has wired devices throughout and no batteries to change.

Commercial Alarm Installation

Business Alarm Installation in Brooklyn Heights

The Heights is mostly houses, but it has a working commercial spine, and its businesses face a particular version of the same problem: a front that is often in the historic district and a back that nobody watches after closing. Montague Street is the main street. Speaking at the Montague Street Business Improvement District’s 2024 annual meeting, as reported by the Brooklyn Eagle, the historian Martha Foley called it the former City of Brooklyn’s main commercial corridor and said it “remains the commercial heart of Brooklyn Heights to this day.” The Eagle reported in December 2020 that the BID, one of the smallest in the city, served more than 100 businesses, and that about 18 storefronts, roughly a fifth of the street, were then empty.

Montague’s alarm story is mostly about the overnight hours. When DNAinfo tallied the January 2017 burglaries, the one commercial case was a bagel shop on Montague Street where someone broke through the front door at 1:40 in the morning and took $1,000. That is the classic storefront break-in, and the answer is the classic one: the glass and the front door on their own zone, an interior motion detector covering the register and the stock room, a back door and roof hatch that report separately, and a monitoring station that calls the owner and dispatches police on a confirmed alarm.

Montague Street

Restaurants, cafés, shops and services from the Promenade end toward Court Street, including the Brooklyn Women’s Exchange, founded in 1854, many in older buildings with offices or apartments above. Storefront glass, alleys and roof hatches.

Henry Street and Hicks Street corners

Corner shops and restaurants scattered along the residential avenues, often with an apartment upstairs that wants its own alarm.

Atlantic Avenue, north side

The neighborhood’s southern edge, with shops and restaurants whose rear walls back onto Heights yards.

Clark Street and Cadman Plaza West

Ground-floor businesses near the Clark Street station and along the neighborhood’s eastern edge, where office and residential buildings share entrances.

Professional offices

Law offices, therapists and doctors in converted brownstone parlor and garden floors, where an after-hours alarm protects files and equipment in a residential building.

Institutions and houses of worship

Churches, schools and cultural buildings with irregular hours, many doors and volunteers who need their own codes and schedules.

Rules that shape a Heights business alarm

  • Who may install it. New York’s Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers: anyone who offers to “install, service or maintain a security or fire alarm system to detect intrusion, break-in, movement, sound or fire” needs a license, renewed every two years. Ask any installer for the number; ours is #12000287431.
  • The storefront itself. Inside the district, signs, lights and equipment on a storefront go through the Landmarks Preservation Commission. Its guidebook allows security cameras within spacing limits and asks that light fixtures sit within or beside a door opening. We keep alarm hardware inside the glass, so the alarm itself adds nothing that needs a permit.
  • Fire alarms are a separate system. Where a building needs a code-required fire alarm, that system follows the Building and Fire Codes and is filed and inspected on its own track. We can monitor smoke and heat on a burglar panel for an empty shop at night, but we do not present that as a code fire alarm. For that, see fire alarm installation in Brooklyn Heights.
  • Exits stay exits. An alarm reports a door opening; it never locks a required exit. Rear doors that are also a way out keep their panic hardware, and the alarm simply tells you, and the monitoring station, when they open after hours.
Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Brooklyn Heights: Questions Answered

These are the questions Heights owners, tenants, boards and shopkeepers actually put to us, grouped by what they concern. Each is answered here in full; the FAQ further down covers different ground, mostly cost, timing and practicalities.

The landmarked front of the house

Our brownstone is inside the Brooklyn Heights Historic District. Does an alarm need a Landmarks Preservation Commission permit before it goes in?

An alarm that is entirely inside the house usually does not touch anything the Commission regulates on a private home, because its jurisdiction there is the exterior. That is why we design Heights systems with nothing on the outside at all: contacts, detectors, keypads, sirens and the panel are all inside, and the communicator reports by cellular radio rather than by a new wire to the street. If you want something on the outside, such as a camera at the garden door or a light by the stoop, that is a different matter. The Commission’s Permit Guidebook lets its staff approve security cameras and light fixtures that meet set criteria, and we will tell you before quoting whether what you want falls inside them. Where a larger renovation also involves interior work that needs a Buildings Department filing, the Commission offers an expedited Certificate of No Effect for specific kinds of interior work, which its website says can be issued within three days; your architect or expediter normally handles that, and our alarm drawings can go in the same package. This is our working understanding, not legal advice, and if your house has an unusual history, such as an individually designated interior, we check with you first.

Can an outdoor siren or flashing strobe go on the front of a Heights brownstone, or does the sounder have to stay inside?

We recommend keeping it inside, and we design every Heights system to work that way. The guidebook’s general criteria say equipment cannot be on or in front of a primary facade unless no other option exists, and an alarm siren always has another option: the inside of the house. An interior siren in the stair hall is loud enough to drive out an intruder and to wake the house, and a second sounder on the top floor carries the alarm to bedrooms. The thing an outside bell used to do, which was summon help, is now done by the monitoring station and your phone, within seconds and with a record. If an insurer or a rear-yard situation genuinely calls for an outdoor sounder, the place to look is the rear of the house, not the front, and we would raise it with you as a separate decision before anything is ordered.

The parlor windows are original wood sash with wavy old glass. How do you alarm them without drilling or replacing anything that shows?

We put the hardware in the frame and leave the sash alone wherever we can. A recessed contact is a small cylinder that sits in a hole in the window frame’s sill or jamb, with its magnet in the bottom rail of the sash; when the window is shut, the two face each other and nothing is visible. On sash that is too thin or too fragile, we use a slim contact screwed to the inside face of the frame, removable later with two screw holes to fill. Where the glass itself is the thing worth protecting, such as fixed sash or a stained-glass transom, a glass-break detector on the ceiling covers the whole room by listening for the sound of glass breaking, and the window is never touched at all. We mark every choice on a window-by-window plan before work starts, so you approve each one.

Older Heights alarms ran a phone wire down the facade. If we cancel the landline, does a new cable have to come up the front of the house?

No. Most current panels report to the monitoring station by a cellular communicator built into the panel or added to it, so there is no line to the street at all. Where the house has a reliable internet connection, we add that as a second path, which uses the router you already have inside the house. If your old system is still dialing out over a copper line, the existing wire can stay where it is or be removed by whoever installed it; we do not need it, and the Landmarks guidebook’s preference for concealed conduit is easy to meet when there is no conduit at all.

How Heights break-ins have actually happened

The winter 2016 break-ins came through windows and fire escapes on weekday afternoons. Which openings should a Heights rowhouse cover first if the budget only stretches so far?

Start with the openings that are out of sight and within reach. In most Heights houses that means the garden-level door and windows at the back, the rear parlor windows above the yard, any window that opens onto a fire escape, and any window over the flat roof of a rear extension. Those are the ones a burglar can work at without being seen from the street. Next comes the garden-floor door under the stoop at the front, which is shaded and below eye level. Front parlor windows facing a busy street come later. Finally, a motion detector in the stair hall catches anyone who gets in by an opening you have not yet covered, because every route through a rowhouse passes through that hall. That order gives most houses strong protection with a starter system and leaves room to add the rest.

People on our block still leave the front door unlocked during the day, as the Brooklyn Heights Association warned back in 2011. Can an alarm deal with that habit without making the house feel like a fortress?

Yes, and gently. The simplest step is a chime: a soft tone, or a spoken “front door,” every time a protected door opens while the system is disarmed, so you know when a child, a contractor or a delivery has come in. The second step is an exit report: when you arm the system on the way out, the keypad or app tells you which door or window is still open and will not arm until you close it or choose to leave it out. The third, for houses where unlocked doors are a real habit, is an automatic reminder: if the system has stayed disarmed with nobody’s phone at home for an hour on a weekday, it sends you a message. None of this locks anyone out, and none of it shows from the street.

In one Heights burglary the intruder used a key left in the mailbox. What takes the place of a spare key once the alarm is in?

Codes, and a lock that accepts them if you want one. Every person who needs to get in, a dog walker, a cleaner, a relative, the contractor, gets a personal alarm code that works only on the days and hours you set, and the app shows who disarmed and when. If you also want a keyless front or garden door, a keypad deadbolt can be paired with the alarm so that the same code unlocks the door and disarms the system together; that is done through our smart lock installation in Brooklyn Heights. Either way the spare key can come out of the mailbox, the planter and the lintel for good, and a code given to someone you no longer need can be deleted in a few seconds.

If someone gets in while we’re asleep upstairs, how does the alarm know the difference between a burglar and us going down to the kitchen?

By arming in a night mode that matches the way a Heights house is lived in. At night the perimeter stays fully armed, every door and every window on every floor, while the motion detector in the parlor-floor hall and the kitchen is left off, so you can walk downstairs. A motion detector on the garden level can stay on if nobody sleeps there. If a perimeter opening is breached, the interior sirens sound at once on every floor. Night mode is set from a bedside keypad, a fob or your phone, and most households set it to come on automatically at a fixed hour.

Garden floors, yards and carriage houses

We rent out the garden apartment and live in the triplex above it. Can each of us arm and disarm our own floors without knowing the other household’s code?

Yes. The panel is divided into two partitions, which behave like two separate alarms sharing one box. The garden apartment gets its own keypad by its door, its own user codes, its own app login and its own notifications; the owner’s floors get theirs. Shared openings, such as a common front hall or a cellar door both households use, are assigned to a common area that arms only when both partitions are armed. The tenant cannot see the owner’s activity and the owner cannot see the tenant’s, which is both a courtesy and, in our view, the right default. If the tenant moves out, their codes and login are deleted and new ones issued, and nothing else changes.

Our converted carriage house in one of the Heights’ mews sits empty for weeks. Can it be watched as part of the same system as our main house a block away?

It can share an app and a monitoring account, even though it has its own panel. A carriage house usually needs a small system: a contact rated for its overhead or carriage door, contacts on the lane windows, a motion detector on the main floor, a roof-hatch contact if there is one, and smoke and heat detection, which matters more in a building that is empty for long stretches. That panel reports by cellular communicator and appears in your app beside the main house, so one screen shows both, and the monitoring station has both addresses on one account. Because the lane itself is shared with neighbors, we place nothing outside on the lane.

Our yard backs onto other Heights gardens full of cats and squirrels. Will outdoor motion sensors keep setting the alarm off?

They would, which is why we almost never put a burglar-alarm motion detector outdoors in a Heights yard. The alarm’s job is to report when the house itself is opened, and the reliable way to do that is with contacts and glass-break on the openings. If you want to know when someone is in the yard, that is a camera’s job, with motion zones drawn around the steps and the door and set to ignore small animals; see our security camera installation in Brooklyn Heights. Keeping the two jobs apart is what keeps false alarms, and the fines and fatigue that come with them, away from your account.

There is a basement cellar door under the stoop that we hardly ever use. Should it be on the alarm?

Yes, and it is often the cheapest valuable addition in the whole job. The under-stoop door at the garden level is shaded, below eye level from the sidewalk and easy to forget, and the cellar or areaway door behind it is often an old door in an old frame. A contact on each, wired from the cellar a few feet away, costs little and closes off the one entrance most owners never check. If a cellar door is genuinely never used, we can program it as a 24-hour zone, which reports an opening whether or not the system is armed.

Co-op boards, condos and apartments

Our Heights co-op board wants an alteration agreement before any work in the apartment. What do you give them for an alarm?

Usually less paperwork than they expect, because an apartment alarm is small and wireless. We send the managing agent a one-page scope listing every device and where it goes, the fact that nothing is installed in common areas or on the building’s exterior, that no building wiring is altered, the equipment data sheets, and our insurance certificate naming the corporation and the agent as the building requires. We also confirm the working hours the building permits. Most boards treat this as minor work; if yours wants a formal alteration agreement, the scope sheet attaches to it.

Can an alarm be put into one apartment in a prewar Heights building without touching the lobby, the hallway or the building’s intercom?

Yes. The system starts at your apartment door and stays inside your walls. A contact on the entry door, contacts on any window onto a fire escape or terrace, a motion detector covering the main room, a keypad by the door and a small panel in a closet, reporting by cellular radio, make up a complete apartment alarm. The building intercom, the lobby door and the common hall are left exactly as they are. Masonry partitions in older buildings can weaken radio signals between rooms, so we test signal strength from every device position before fixing anything to a wall.

Who should hold the master code in a small Heights co-op where four households share the front door and the cellar?

Whoever the board designates, and preferably two people rather than one. We set up the building’s common-area system, front door, cellar and roof hatch, as its own partition, administered by the board’s designated officers, while each apartment that wants one has its own separate partition or panel. The master code is used only for programming; day-to-day, every resident gets a personal code for the common doors, and the log shows which code was used. When a household sells, its codes are deleted at closing, and the new owners are added after the board approves them.

Living with the system

We spend summers away from the Heights and the house sits empty. What tells us everything is fine without a neighbor checking every day?

Three things the alarm reports on its own. First, any opening, at any hour, goes to the monitoring station and your phone. Second, the panel reports its own health: low batteries, a lost connection, a power cut, a tampered device. Third, environmental detectors add what an empty house most needs: smoke and carbon monoxide, a water sensor under the boiler or a washing machine, and a temperature sensor that warns if the heat fails in winter. A simple routine helps too: a daily “all quiet” check in the app, and a neighbor with a code who can let in a plumber if one of those sensors reports something.

When the alarm goes off while we are traveling, who gets the call, and do the police actually come?

If the system is professionally monitored, the central station gets the signal within seconds, calls the numbers on your list in order, and, if nobody answers or someone confirms a problem, asks the police or fire department to respond. You choose who is on the list: you, a neighbor, a relative in the city. If you choose self-monitoring, the alerts come only to your phone and it is up to you to call 911. We set up either, and we explain the difference before you choose, because a monitored account with a good call list is what makes an alarm useful when you are on a plane.

Will we be able to see the alarm in the parlor, with its ceiling medallion and old moldings?

Only if you want to. Detectors in a parlor can be placed where they do not compete with the plasterwork: a glass-break detector the size of a coin purse on a plain wall or above a doorway, a motion detector in a corner of the hall rather than the room, window contacts buried in the frames. The keypad can live in the hall or be dropped in favor of a phone and a key fob. Devices come in white and in a few neutral finishes, and where you have a decorator or a painter, the frame-mounted pieces can be painted with the woodwork.

Montague Street and other Heights businesses

Our shop on Montague Street closes at nine and the staff rotate. Who gets an alarm code, and how do we know the store was actually armed at night?

Every person who opens or closes gets a personal code, and nobody shares one. The panel sends an opening and closing report each day showing which code armed and disarmed and when, and it can alert the owner if the shop has not been armed by a set time, say ten o’clock. Codes for a departing employee are deleted the same day from the app. Delivery or cleaning staff who come early can have codes that work only in their window. The result is that the owner knows every night whether the store is armed without driving past it.

Can a Montague Street storefront be alarmed without adding anything to the facade above the window?

Yes. Everything a storefront alarm needs fits inside the glass line: a contact on the door, glass-break detectors covering the display windows, a motion detector covering the floor and the register, contacts on the back door and the roof hatch, and an interior siren. The panel reports by cellular radio. If you also want an exterior sign or a camera on the front, that is a separate question for the Landmarks rules and, often, your landlord; the alarm does not depend on it.

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Brooklyn Heights

Google’s AI Overview could not be loaded for Brooklyn Heights alarm searches in our build environment, so nothing below is a quotation from it. These are generalizations that recur in quick online answers, consumer how-to articles and national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, each set against what a house in this neighborhood actually needs.

“Put a siren box and a yard sign out front: visible deterrence works.”

Generic advice treats the outside of the house as free space for alarm hardware, and for most of the country it is. In the first historic district in New York City it is not.

The Landmarks guidebook says equipment cannot be on or in front of a primary facade unless no other option exists, and an alarm siren always has another option. A box bolted through brownstone is also a box someone will eventually have to remove and patch.

The deterrent that works in the Heights is the one a burglar meets inside: a siren that sounds on every floor the moment a rear window opens, and a monitoring call that follows within seconds.

“Wireless alarms are the obvious choice for old houses.”

Wireless devices do spare plaster, and we use them on every upper floor. Consumer guides push them as the whole answer for old homes.

But a brownstone has a cellar running its full depth, and that cellar makes wiring the garden level and parlor floor easy. Wired devices there never need batteries, and they are the ones guarding the openings that matter most.

Thick masonry walls can also weaken radio signals between rooms, so a fully wireless system in a Heights house needs signal testing at every device, which we do before anything is fixed in place.

“Protect the front door first.”

Many checklists start with the front door, because in a typical suburban break-in that is where it starts.

The local reporting points elsewhere. The holiday burglaries of 2016 came mostly through open or unlocked windows, some apparently reached from fire escapes, and the 2011 cases came through doors that had simply been left unlocked.

So we start with the rear and garden-level openings and with the habit of locking up, then work toward the street. The front door is covered too; it just is not where a Heights plan begins.

“A home alarm costs a few hundred dollars.”

National guides often quote the price of a starter kit and call it an alarm. That covers a hub and a few sensors, not a four-story house with a garden apartment.

On our Brooklyn list, a basic professionally installed system starts at $600, a complete home system at $2,500, and a typical three-story brownstone runs $2,500 to $5,000 installed.

The Heights pays the Brooklyn base list, with no surcharge for a landmarked house, a long walk-up or the time it takes to do concealed work properly.

“You will need a long monitoring contract.”

Quick answers often assume an alarm comes with a three- or five-year monitoring agreement, because many national packages are sold that way.

It does not have to. We install the system and you own it. Monitoring is optional, available from $19.99 a month with no long-term contract, and you can choose self-monitoring through the app instead.

Owning the panel also means a future owner of your house can keep the system rather than tearing it out, which matters in a neighborhood where houses are sold rarely and kept long.

“Every room needs a motion sensor.”

Generic layouts scatter motion detectors through every room, which in a rowhouse with pets, radiators and sunlit parlors invites false alarms.

A rowhouse is narrow, and every route from floor to floor runs through the stair hall. One or two well-placed detectors there catch nearly any movement through the house, while contacts and glass-break protect the openings themselves.

Fewer, better-placed devices mean fewer false alarms, fewer batteries and fewer visible pieces of plastic in rooms you want left alone.

“Neighborhood crime statistics tell you what to install.”

Some answers lead with crime figures. The 84th Precinct covers far more than the Heights, and the most specific local counts we found are from the winter of 2016–17.

Statistics cannot tell you which of your windows was left open on a Tuesday afternoon. The local reports describe methods, open windows, fire escapes, an unlocked door, a key in a mailbox, and those are what the plan addresses.

We plan from the openings and the people who use them, and we leave forecasting to the precinct. The NYPD will also do a free security survey of your home on request, which Patch noted in 2017; it is a useful second opinion.

Where the Brooklyn Heights Facts Come From

All the history, dates, figures, rules and news on this page were checked against the sources below in October 2026. What is not sourced is ours: the prices, which come from the AESS Brooklyn list, and every description of how we survey a house, place devices or program a system, which is our working practice and not a claim about any particular property.

  • NYC Landmarks Preservation Commission, Permit Guidebook, chapter 11: Health, Safety and Utility Equipment — equipment not on or in front of a primary facade unless no other option exists; no attachment through cast iron or decorative surfaces; conduit concealed through plain masonry, about ten inches visible at most with staff approval; staff-level permits for cameras, light fixtures and intercoms; camera spacing per 25 feet of frontage
  • NYC Landmarks Preservation Commission, Expedited Reviews — Expedited Certificate of No Effect for specific kinds of interior work in historic-district buildings, issuable within three days
  • Historic Districts Council, Brooklyn Heights Historic District — designated November 23, 1965; New York City’s first historic district; brownstone and brick residences, churches and carriage houses
  • The Cultural Landscape Foundation, Brooklyn Heights Historic District — about 140 acres; Pierrepont’s streets laid out 1802; Promenade a one-third-mile cantilevered walkway by Clarke & Rapuano
  • Wikipedia, “Brooklyn Heights” — boundaries, 0.320 square miles, population 2010 and 2020; Fulton’s ferry 1814; lots after 1823; houses 1807–1860; Brooklyn Bridge 1883 and subway 1908; mansions divided; styles; 84th Precinct, FDNY companies, Community District 2, ZIP 11201; stations; Montague Street Tunnel 1920
  • CityLore, Grace Court Alley — Remsen family stable lane; named for Grace Church (1847); mid-19th-century carriage houses with hay cranes; converted to garages and then apartments
  • Wikipedia, “58 Joralemon Street” · “Plymouth Church” · “Hotel St. George” · “Center for Brooklyn History” — landmark dates and descriptions
  • Brownstoner, “Open-Door Policy in the Heights Inviting Thieves?” (March 11, 2011) — five daytime break-ins over four days through unlocked doors, per a Brooklyn Heights Association email
  • Patch, Brooklyn Heights burglaries (January 4, 2017) — forced entries and unlocked windows, possibly via fire escapes; weekday working hours; free NYPD security surveys; keep valuables out of sight of windows
  • DNAinfo, Brooklyn Heights burglaries (January 12, 2017) — seven burglaries January 2–8, 2017 per CompStat; key left in a mailbox; Montague Street bagel shop front door broken at 1:40 a.m.
  • Brooklyn Paper, “Rash of home burglaries in Brooklyn Heights” — most entries through open windows; Willow, Joralemon and Montague Streets
  • Brooklyn Eagle, Montague Street BID annual meeting (March 5, 2024) and “One-fifth of Montague Street storefronts empty” (December 11, 2020) — commercial heart of the Heights; Brooklyn Women’s Exchange 1854; BID serving more than 100 businesses; about 18 empty storefronts
  • New York YIMBY, 25–30 Columbia Heights conversion (March 14, 2026) — 661 rental apartments planned in the former Squibb and Watchtower buildings
  • New York Department of State, Security or Fire Alarm Installer — who needs a license; two-year term

Where sources disagree, we say so. Wikipedia gives January 12, 1965 for the National Historic Landmark designation and a different date in its infobox for the city designation, while the Historic Districts Council gives November 23, 1965 for the city’s historic district, which is the date this page uses. The Cultural Landscape Foundation says the Promenade was constructed in 1946, while the Brooklyn Eagle’s 2024 report says it opened in 1950–1951; the timeline on this page shows the Foundation’s 1946 date and labels it as such.

Not everything could be checked. Our build environment had no access to Answer the Public, to Google’s AI Overview, People Also Ask or related searches, to Bing or DuckDuckGo suggestions, or to Yelp reviews. We found no Reddit thread about alarms in Heights houses. We could not retrieve the text of the city’s noise rules for building alarms, so this page makes no claim about how long an alarm may sound. Nothing on this page depends on the sources we could not reach.
