2GIG vs Qolsys:
Which Alarm Panel Is Better?

2GIG Edge and GC Touch Against Qolsys IQ Panel 5 and IQ Panel 4 · Also Searched as Qolsys vs 2GIG · Updated November 2026

2GIG vs Qolsys is close, but for most new alarm installs in New York we would pick Qolsys, now sold by Johnson Controls under the IQ name, because the IQ Panel 5 pairs a sharper 1280 × 800 screen and a 13-megapixel panel camera with encrypted, long-range PowerG+ sensors built into the panel; 2GIG is the better choice when your walls already carry 345 MHz 2GIG or Honeywell wireless sensors you want to keep, when you like the idea of disarming by face, or when budget points you to its GC Touch panel.

Quick answer: Starting fresh, in a house, brownstone or store? Pick a Qolsys IQ Panel 5 with PowerG+ sensors. Keeping working 345 MHz sensors from an older 2GIG or Honeywell system? A 2GIG Edge reads them natively, and 2GIG's GC Touch is its value-priced panel. Both run on Alarm.com, both report over LTE and Wi-Fi, and both keep running about 24 hours on battery. Both are touchscreen panels sold through dealers rather than to the public, both talk to Alarm.com for the app and video, both carry a cellular radio so the alarm still reports when the internet is out, and both control Z-Wave locks, lights and thermostats. The real differences are in the sensors each panel speaks to, the camera and screen on the front, how each one disarms, and what happens when you want to reuse an older system. The short version. A 2GIG panel and a Qolsys panel do the same basic job. Each one sits on a wall by the door you use most, listens for the sensors on your doors, windows and rooms, sounds the siren, and sends the alarm to a monitoring station and to your phone. Each one is a seven-inch touchscreen, each one carries an LTE cellular radio and Wi-Fi, each one has a backup battery its maker rates at about 24 hours, and each one is built to run on Alarm.com, the service most professional installers use for the phone app, cameras and automations. If you put either one in properly and connect it to a central station, you have a real alarm system, and either is a big step up from a kit that relies on your home Wi-Fi alone.

The difference lies in the details that decide how the system behaves over the next ten years. The first is the wireless language the sensors speak. Current Qolsys panels, sold today as IQ panels by Johnson Controls, are built around PowerG, an encrypted, two-way, long-range radio that Johnson Controls says has been in real-world use for more than a decade. 2GIG panels, made by Nice North America, are built around the 345 MHz band that 2GIG and Honeywell sensors have used for years, with encrypted eSeries sensors and, on the Edge, a newer 900 MHz extended-range line called E+. The second difference is the hardware on the front of the panel: the IQ Panel 5 has a higher-resolution screen and a 13-megapixel camera, while the 2GIG Edge offers something the IQ panels do not list, which is disarming by recognising your face. The third is what each panel can reuse when it replaces an older system, which matters a great deal in New York, where many brownstones, walk-ups and shops already have sensors and wiring in the walls.

Our Call

Who Should Pick 2GIG, and Who Should Pick Qolsys

Most people asking this question are in one of a handful of situations, and the answer follows the situation more than the logo. Here is how we decide when we are standing in the hallway with the owner.

Pick Qolsys (IQ Panel 5) if…

  • You are starting from nothing. With no sensors to reuse, the panel's own radio is the one you will live with, and PowerG+ is encrypted, two-way and long-range on every device, with no add-on card needed.
  • The building is big, tall or full of masonry. A three-story brownstone with a cellar, a two-family house with a detached garage, or a store with a basement stockroom is where long-range two-way sensors earn their keep, because the sensor and the panel talk in both directions instead of the sensor sending a signal and hoping it arrives.
  • You care about the screen and the camera. The IQ Panel 5's 1280 × 800 display and 13-megapixel camera are the sharper hardware of the two flagships, and it has dual-band Wi-Fi where the EDGE lists 2.4 GHz only.
  • The site mixes old sensors from different makers. Johnson Controls lets an installer add legacy cards for 319.5, 345 or 433 MHz sensors, which helps when a takeover inherits more than one kind.
  • It is a business that may grow. The IQ family runs from the Panel 5 up to the IQ Pro, a commercial hybrid panel with eight hardwired zones on board, up to 1,000 user codes and eight partitions, all on the same sensor radio.

Pick 2GIG (EDGE or GC Touch) if…

  • Your walls already hold working 345 MHz sensors. An older 2GIG system, or a Honeywell wireless system, may have a dozen door contacts and motion detectors that still work. The EDGE reads those natively, according to its data sheet, which can save the cost and mess of replacing every sensor.
  • You want hands-free disarming by face. The EDGE recognises enrolled faces on the panel itself, which 2GIG calls an industry first. Bluetooth disarming from your phone is offered on both brands, but face recognition is listed only on the EDGE.
  • The budget is tight and the home is simple. The GC Touch is 2GIG's value-priced touchscreen with 128 wireless zones, Bluetooth disarm, 2-way voice and Z-Wave 800, and it is a sensible panel for an apartment or a small house.
  • You want the maker's warranty term in writing. 2GIG's data sheets for both the EDGE and the GC Touch read "Warranty: Limited 3 years"; Johnson Controls' current IQ panel pages did not state one.

If you rent, or you are covering one small apartment for a year or two, the honest answer may be neither: a professional panel is an investment in a property you plan to stay in, and our ADT vs SimpliSafe vs local installer comparison covers the no-drill options. For owners, landlords and businesses, either panel is a sound choice when it is installed and supervised properly; the sections below show where the differences actually bite.

The Companies

Who Makes Them in 2026, and Why Qolsys Is Now Called IQ

Part of the confusion around this comparison is the names. 2GIG is a brand of Nice North America; the copyright line on 2gig.com reads "Nice North America LLC," and the site's news section includes a press release in which Nice North America and Alarm.com launched a "2GIG New Home Program Powered by Alarm.com." Older forum threads and installation guides call the same company Nortek or Nortek Control, which is why you will still see those names on older 2GIG paperwork. The current 2GIG panel line on 2gig.com is the EDGE, the GC Touch, the EDGE Remote Keypad, the older GC2e, and two keypads, the TS2 and the PAD1. If you have a GC2 or GC3 on your wall, it is an earlier generation of the same family.

Qolsys was the company behind the IQ Panel, and it is now part of Johnson Controls, the same group that owns DSC and Visonic. When we typed qolsys.com on November 14, 2026, it took us straight to iqsecurityproducts.com, a Johnson Controls site with an "IQ" logo, and the panels are now listed simply as IQ Panel 5, IQ5 Hub, IQ5 NS, IQ Panel 4, IQ4 Hub, IQ4 NS, IQ Pro and IQ Hub. The company's Instagram link on that site still points to a "qolsys" account, and installers, dealers and homeowners still say "Qolsys" out of habit. On this page we use both names, because that is how people search; when you see "Qolsys IQ Panel 5" and "IQ Panel 5" in a quote, they are the same panel.

Two things follow from this for a buyer. First, both brands are backed by large, long-established security groups, so neither is a small start-up that might vanish and leave your panel without updates. Second, both makers sell almost entirely through dealers. 2gig.com's "Where to Buy" button leads to a page for finding security dealers and installers, and the IQ site points visitors to "Find a dealer" and invites companies to become IQ dealers. In practice, the company that installs your panel, and the Alarm.com account it sets up, will shape your experience at least as much as the brand on the front.

Which panels this page compares

The flagship of each line is the fair comparison: 2GIG's EDGE against the IQ Panel 5. The IQ Panel 4 is still listed by Johnson Controls and is still on thousands of walls, so we note it wherever it differs, and many of the comparison articles that rank for this search were written about the IQ Panel 4 before the Panel 5 arrived. On the 2GIG side, the GC Touch is the newer, lower-priced touchscreen, built, as 2GIG puts it, on the legacy of the GC2e, and 2GIG says it won two 2025 awards, one from Commercial Integrator and CE Pro, the other from Builder Innovator. We note the GC Touch where it changes the answer. The IQ5 Hub (a cheaper Panel 5 without the camera) and the IQ5 NS (no screen at all, for a hidden install with keypads) also exist, and Johnson Controls' own chart shows them sharing the Panel 5's radios and Alarm.com integration.

Sensors

Sensor Radios and Encryption: eSeries and E+ Against PowerG

This is the section that matters most, and the one the glossy brochures skip. An alarm panel is only as good as the sensors that report to it, and wireless sensors are tied to the panel's radio. Pick the panel and you have picked the sensor family you will be buying for years.

2GIG: the 345 MHz family, plus E+ on the EDGE

2GIG panels speak 345 MHz, the band that 2GIG's own sensors have always used and the same band as Honeywell's older wireless sensors. The EDGE data sheet lists "eSeries encrypted sensors" and says the panel is "backwards compatible with older 2GIG and Honeywell sensors." 2GIG's catalog splits its sensors into several groups. The eSeries line covers encrypted door and window contacts, motion detectors, glass-break detectors, smoke, heat and freeze sensors, carbon monoxide detectors, water sensors, key fobs and a takeover module; 2GIG says encrypted sensors "cannot be disabled or taken over." The newer 345S line, designed for both the GC Touch and the EDGE, carries an on-board switch that turns encryption on or off, which 2GIG says lets dealers stock fewer parts. And on the EDGE alone, after its 1.3 software update, there is the E+ Extended Range line on 900 MHz: indoor and outdoor contacts, a motion detector, a glass-break detector and a smoke, heat and freeze detector, with two-way encrypted communication and a door sensor battery that 2GIG projects at up to eight years.

Qolsys / IQ: PowerG+ built in, legacy radios by card

The IQ Panel 5 has PowerG+ built into the panel. Johnson Controls' specification sheet describes it as "915MHz, long range, secure encrypted two-way wireless," with up to 128 security devices and up to 100 PowerG automation devices. The PowerG pages add frequency hopping and secure enrollment, battery life of up to eight years on many devices, and a range the company describes as "measured in miles, not feet." For older sensors, the Panel 5 takes add-on radio cards: the spec page says to "add legacy 319, 345, 433 or Ethernet cards à la carte," and the spec sheet lists S-Line encrypted 319.5 MHz, 345 MHz or 433 MHz sensors, depending on the model, up to 128 devices. The IQ Panel 4 also has PowerG, along with one legacy frequency set by the radio card fitted at the factory, according to its installation manual (a manufacturer document we read through a dealer's copy, because Johnson Controls' own link did not load).

What that means on a real job

If you are buying all new sensors, both makers now offer encrypted, two-way, long-range options, and the question becomes which ecosystem you prefer. PowerG has the longer track record, more than a decade by Johnson Controls' account, and a wide range of devices behind it, from water tiles and smoke detectors to repeaters and input-output modules. E+ is newer and works only on the EDGE, not on the GC Touch. If you are keeping sensors, the answer depends on what they are: 345 MHz 2GIG and Honeywell sensors are native to 2GIG, and the IQ panels need the right legacy card to read them. One honest caveat applies to every older sensor, whatever the panel: a non-encrypted, one-way sensor from fifteen years ago is still a non-encrypted, one-way sensor after the new panel goes in. We test each one and replace the ones that guard the doors that matter most.

Range

Wireless Range in New York Buildings: Brick, Plaster and Floors

Both makers publish big range numbers. 2GIG says its E+ door and window sensor reaches "up to 1.2 miles with 900MHz spectrum," and adds in small print that range "can vary depending on building materials." Johnson Controls lists its PowerG+ door and window sensor, the PGPx945E, at up to 2,500 feet, and says PowerG range is measured "in miles, not feet." Figures like these describe ideal conditions, and 2GIG's own footnote admits that building materials change them. Nobody in New York lives in ideal conditions.

What actually limits wireless sensors in this city is the building. A rowhouse party wall of solid brick, a prewar apartment with plaster on metal lath, a basement under a concrete slab, a steel-framed storefront, or a mechanical room full of ductwork will all swallow far more signal than any spec sheet allows for. Distance matters less than what is between the sensor and the panel. In practice, the long-range radios on both brands are not about reaching a mile; they are about having enough headroom that a sensor in the cellar of a three-story brownstone still reports reliably to a panel by the parlor-floor door after the batteries have aged.

How we handle range on both brands

  • Put the panel where the signals meet, not just where the door is. On a tall house we sometimes set the main panel mid-building and put a remote keypad by the entrance. 2GIG offers the EDGE Remote Keypad, a second seven-inch screen with its own camera; Johnson Controls offers the IQ Remote-PG and IQ Keypad PG.
  • Read the signal, do not guess it. 2GIG's EDGE 1.3 update shows sensor signal strength on the panel, which 2GIG says helps dealers troubleshoot without extra visits. PowerG devices also report back to the panel. We record the reading for every sensor on the day we finish.
  • Add a repeater where the building fights back. Both families include repeaters, such as 2GIG's 345S wireless repeater and the PowerG+ PGPx920. One well-placed repeater beats a dozen sensors that report most of the time.
  • Use wire where wire already is. Where a wired alarm left cable in the walls, a takeover module can bring those zones onto either panel, which we cover further down.

On range alone, a building that would struggle with one brand's long-range sensors will usually struggle with the other's too. Where we see a real difference is with the older, standard 345 MHz sensors, which have far less headroom than either E+ or PowerG. If a 2GIG system is going into a large or awkward building, we would budget for E+ sensors on the EDGE rather than standard 345 MHz devices; if a Qolsys system is going in, PowerG+ is the default and needs no special choice.

On the Wall

The Screen and What You Touch Every Day

You will look at the panel more than any other part of the system, so its screen is not a trivial point. All three panels we compare here use a seven-inch touchscreen. The IQ Panel 5 lists a capacitive glass multi-touch screen at 1280 × 800 pixels, with a brightness of 300 candela per square meter; the IQ Panel 4 lists the same size and resolution. The 2GIG EDGE data sheet lists a seven-inch LCD with 16.7 million colors and says the screen is "double in size" and brighter than earlier 2GIG panels, but it does not give a pixel count. The GC Touch has a seven-inch capacitive touchscreen and adds a dark mode.

In daily use, both flagships feel like a tablet on the wall: tap to arm, tap to see which door is open, swipe to the lights or the thermostat. The EDGE's interface puts smart-home controls one button away and shows each of its four Smart Areas on screen; the IQ Panel 5 runs Android 14, and Johnson Controls says its updated software makes features easier to find and customize. One online dealer's forum answer in the search results calls the Qolsys panel "easier to use," but that is opinion, and the best test is to try a demonstration unit. 2GIG even publishes a free "Security Dealer Toolkit" app that simulates the EDGE's interface for sales demonstrations.

Size, mounting and the hallway test

The EDGE measures 7.5 × 5.625 × 1 inch and weighs 1.39 pounds; 2GIG markets it as about half as thick as other panels. The IQ Panel 5 measures 6.1 × 7.5 × 1 inch, mounts on Johnson Controls' SmartMount wall plate, and can stand on an optional table stand; the EDGE has a desktop stand as an accessory too. In a narrow New York hallway, both fit beside a door frame, but the panel needs power from a nearby outlet or a low-voltage run, and on a plaster wall the cable route can take longer than mounting the panel itself. We plan that route before the day of the install, whichever brand goes up. One installer comment in the search results said the IQ Panel 4 "can be a pain to mount"; Johnson Controls says the Panel 5's refined backplate, tamper and power supply design was made to make installation smoother.

Disarming

Disarming: Face Recognition, Bluetooth and Codes

The moment you walk in carrying groceries is where these two panels differ most visibly. The 2GIG EDGE has a camera on the front that can recognise the faces of people enrolled on the panel and disarm the system for them. 2GIG calls this an "industry-first face recognition disarm," and stresses that the analysis happens on the panel itself rather than in the cloud, "so private data stays private." The EDGE also offers Bluetooth disarming, so the panel can disarm when an enrolled phone comes within range, and the usual keypad codes, which 2GIG lists as six-digit.

The IQ Panel 5 takes a different approach. It offers Bluetooth touchless disarming over Bluetooth 5.0 LE, and its 13-megapixel PortraitView camera takes disarm photos, so you can see who came in. Johnson Controls does not list face recognition among the Panel 5's features. The IQ Panel 4 also offers Bluetooth disarming and disarm photos with its 8-megapixel camera. Both IQ panels allow up to 242 user codes, which matters for a business with many staff or a building with many tenants; the EDGE allows 100 users and the GC Touch 64.

Which one is safer?

Convenience features should never be the only way into a monitored system. On both brands we set them up as an extra, with the code always available, and we explain to the household what the entry delay is and how to cancel a false alarm. Face recognition is convenient for a family with one entrance and good lighting at the door; it is less useful at a side door in a dim vestibule, or for a household where a lot of different people come and go. Bluetooth disarming depends on everyone's phone settings and battery. A disarm photo, which both IQ panels take, is a quieter feature, but many owners end up valuing it most: it answers the question of who came in at 2 a.m. without anyone needing to be recognised. If hands-free disarming is what you want, the EDGE has the feature the IQ panels lack. If you want a record of every disarm, both offer camera photos, and the IQ Panel 5's camera is the higher-resolution one on paper.

Communication

Cellular and Wi-Fi: How Each Panel Reports an Alarm

An alarm is only useful if the signal leaves the building. Both brands treat cellular as the main path and Wi-Fi as a second one. The EDGE data sheet lists "dual communication paths" that use "both cellular LTE and Wi-Fi. If one service goes down, the other is always available," and says the Wi-Fi connection also allows faster updates. The EDGE is sold in two versions, one on Verizon and one on AT&T, both labelled as Alarm.com panels. Its Wi-Fi is 2.4 GHz, 802.11 b/g/n, with WPA2 security, and it can also act as a Wi-Fi access point. The GC Touch carries the same LTE and 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi.

The IQ Panel 5 lists LTE CAT4 and comes in a Verizon version and an AT&T version for the United States, plus a Telus version for Canada. Its Wi-Fi is dual-band, 802.11 a/b/g/n/ac on 2.4 and 5 GHz, with a built-in access point, and Johnson Controls' chart describes the whole IQ5 family as having "LTE & WiFi Dual-Path Communication Built In." The spec sheet lists the LTE bands, and notes band 14 as "non-First Net." The IQ Panel 4 also offers dual LTE and Wi-Fi paths.

What decides signal in New York

In our experience the brand of panel matters less to cellular reliability than the carrier chosen and where the panel hangs. A basement-level store, a back room behind a steel gate or a ground-floor apartment in a courtyard can sit in a weak spot for one carrier and be fine on the other, which is why both makers sell a Verizon and an AT&T version. We check signal on site before ordering. 2GIG also sells an LTE attic antenna, which its catalog lists for the GC3 and EDGE, for panels that need an external antenna. The 5 GHz Wi-Fi on the IQ Panel 5 is a small advantage in crowded apartment buildings, where the 2.4 GHz band is packed with neighbours' routers, but it is a backup path, not the one the central station relies on.

One practical point for landlords and business owners: because both panels report over cellular, neither needs a landline, and neither stops reporting when the internet provider has an outage. That is one of the main reasons a professional panel beats a system that depends on your router.

Smart Home

Z-Wave and Smart-Home Control

Both panels double as a smart-home hub through Z-Wave, the wireless standard used by many door locks, light switches, plug modules, thermostats and garage door controllers. This is where the newer hardware shows. The IQ Panel 5 has an 800-series Z-Wave module with Z-Wave Long Range and supports up to 167 Z-Wave devices, according to its spec sheet. The 2GIG EDGE has Z-Wave Plus V2 on the 700 series, with S2 security, and lets you create scenes and schedules. The GC Touch, though it is the cheaper 2GIG panel, has the newer 800-series Z-Wave. The IQ Panel 4 has the 700 series.

For most homes the difference between 700 and 800 series is invisible: both control a front-door lock, a few lights and a thermostat well. It starts to matter on bigger properties, which Z-Wave Long Range is designed for, and on systems with a lot of devices. Johnson Controls also offers PowerG automation devices, such as smart sockets, on the same encrypted radio as the alarm sensors, which gives an installer another way to control a device where Z-Wave struggles.

Voice assistants and automations

The EDGE and the GC Touch data sheets list Google Assistant, Amazon Echo and Siri Shortcuts for voice control. Most of the day-to-day automation on both brands, such as "lock the door and turn off the lights when the alarm is armed away," is set up in Alarm.com, which runs the app on both. 2GIG's EDGE 1.3 update added Z-Wave backup and restore through Alarm.com, which 2GIG says saves installers time when a panel is serviced or replaced; in plain terms, the list of your Z-Wave devices can be put back without pairing every lock and switch again. We have not found a matching statement on Johnson Controls' pages, so we leave that comparison open.

A warning we give every owner: a smart lock on the alarm is a convenience, not a substitute for a door sensor. If the lock is opened by force, the door contact and the motion detector are what raise the alarm. We wire or pair the lock to the panel so you can see its status, but we never remove a door sensor because there is a smart lock on the door.

Video

Cameras and Doorbells on the Panel

Both panels can show live video from cameras and video doorbells on their screens, so you can see who is at the door without reaching for your phone. On the EDGE, 2GIG says you can view "up to 8 different camera feeds" directly from the panel, four at a time, and lists Alarm.com cameras on the panel, a doorbell on the panel with two-way audio and door unlock, ONVIF camera support, and the SkyBell Wi-Fi video doorbell. The EDGE Remote Keypad also has its own camera, which can stream to the main panel and to other remote keypads.

On the IQ side, Johnson Controls lists Live View and Live Answer on the IQ Panel 5 and the IQ5 Hub (not the screenless IQ5 NS), and LiveView and LiveAnswer on the IQ Panel 4, meaning you can watch a camera and answer a doorbell from the panel. The IQ pages we read did not state how many feeds the panel can show at once, so we do not claim a number. The panels' own front cameras, the EDGE's onboard camera and the IQ Panel 5's 13-megapixel camera, are for disarm photos and, on the EDGE, face recognition; they are not a replacement for a camera covering the room.

How we pair cameras with either panel

For most homes, the simplest arrangement is Alarm.com cameras and an Alarm.com doorbell, because they appear in the same app as the alarm on both brands and show on the panel. For buildings that need more cameras, longer recording or a dedicated recorder, we often install a separate camera system alongside the alarm and link them where it makes sense. Our Alarm.com camera installation in NYC page covers the first approach, and our best security camera brands comparison covers the second. The EDGE's ONVIF support is worth noting for owners who already have standard IP cameras, but how well a given third-party camera works on the panel depends on the camera, and we test before promising it.

The Platform

Alarm.com: The Service Behind Both Panels

If there is one thing that makes 2GIG and Qolsys more alike than different, it is Alarm.com. Both panels are built to work with it. The EDGE is sold in versions that 2GIG labels "Verizon Alarm.com Panel" and "AT&T Alarm.com Panel," its product page says "Alarm.com compatible," and the GC Touch data sheet says it is "optimized for Alarm.com Scenes." Johnson Controls' chart lists "Full Alarm.com Integration" on the IQ Panel 5, IQ5 Hub and IQ5 NS, and its IQ Pro commercial panel "integrates seamlessly with Alarm.com."

For you, that means the phone app, the notifications, the camera clips, the automation rules and the remote arming and disarming will look much the same whichever panel is on the wall. The differences you will notice in the app come mostly from the sensors and devices each panel supports, not from the app itself. It also means the Alarm.com account matters as much as the panel. When comparing quotes for either brand, ask each company who sets up the Alarm.com account, which app features its plan includes, such as video recording or automations, and what happens to the account if you later change monitoring companies.

Monitoring is a separate choice

Neither 2GIG nor Johnson Controls monitors your alarm. The panel sends its signals to a central station chosen by whoever sells you monitoring, and that station calls you and dispatches police or fire. That is why the monitoring company and its terms matter more than the panel when something actually happens. Our own monitoring runs through a UL-listed central station, and we cover the price in our own section below. If you are being offered either panel with a long monitoring contract, read the contract before you compare the panels.

Power

Battery Backup and Power Cuts

When the power goes out, the panel switches to its own battery and keeps reporting over cellular. The EDGE and GC Touch data sheets both list a lithium-polymer backup battery rated at 24 hours. Johnson Controls' IQ Panel 5 specification sheet lists an "18650 rechargeable lithium-ion 3,000mAh" battery "rated for over 24 hours," while its family comparison chart describes a "24-Hour Lithium Polymer Backup Battery Preinstalled"; the two documents disagree on the battery chemistry, but agree on the 24-hour rating, so that is the figure we use. For the IQ Panel 4, Johnson Controls' product page did not state a battery rating; dealer documents describe a 3,000 mAh lithium-ion battery with up to 24 hours of standby.

On paper, then, the two brands are level: about a day on battery. In practice the hours depend on how much the panel is doing. A bright screen, a cellular radio in a weak spot, Z-Wave traffic and camera streaming all draw power, so a panel that is quiet in a closet will outlast one that is busy in a hallway. Wireless sensors have their own batteries and keep working through a power cut; hardwired sensors draw power from the panel or from a takeover module, which is one more reason to check battery health on an older system.

The UL fire listing footnote

Both makers list their panels under UL 985, the standard for household fire warning equipment. For older 2GIG panels such as the GC2e, 2GIG's own installation guide noted that the standard battery did not support UL 985 installations and that a heavier console battery pack was required to meet the secondary power requirement. We have not seen the same note for the EDGE or the IQ panels, but it is a reminder that a panel used for smoke and heat detection should be installed to the listing, with the right battery and the right sensors, and not just switched on. Where a building needs a code-required fire alarm system, that is a different system altogether; our fire alarm installation in NYC page covers it.

Takeovers

Taking Over an Old Wired Alarm: Brownstones, Walk-Ups and Stores

A large share of the alarm work in New York is not a brand-new system in an empty house. It is a brownstone with a Honeywell or DSC keypad from the 1990s, contacts wired into every parlor-floor window and a motion detector in the cellar; a walk-up with an old panel in the basement that no longer reports to anyone; or a store with wired door contacts and a rolling-gate switch that the last owner stopped paying to monitor. The cable in those walls is often the most valuable part of the system, because pulling new cable through plaster and brick is slow, messy work. Both 2GIG and Johnson Controls make it possible to keep that wiring and put a modern touchscreen panel on top.

The 2GIG way: a takeover module

2GIG's 345S Wireless Hardwire Takeover Module (2GIG-TAKE100-345) sits beside the old wiring and, in 2GIG's words, "converts 8 zones per module (additional modules may be added)." It can be powered by the existing alarm panel and use that panel's backup battery, supports up to 500 mA for wired motion and glass-break detectors, and supports encryption through an on-board switch. 2GIG's product description adds two limits worth knowing before anyone promises you a takeover: the module "cannot be used to monitor Carbon Monoxide (CO) or Fire detection zones" and is "intended for indoor use only." The EDGE itself also has two wired zones and a wired bell output on the panel.

The Qolsys / IQ way: IQ Hardwire PG or IQ Pro

Johnson Controls offers the IQ Hardwire PG, which it describes as a wired-to-wireless upgrade and expansion module that can "add eight hardwired zones to IQ Pro and IQ Panels" over encrypted PowerG. It supports normally open, normally closed, single and double end-of-line wiring, has a supervised 12-volt, 700 mA siren output, and, unlike the 2GIG module, "supports CO and four-wire smoke detectors." For bigger jobs, the IQ Pro is a commercial hybrid panel with eight hardwired zones on board, addressable two-wire loops, up to 16 hardwired keypads, and room for 128 zones, available in a metal or plastic cabinet. The IQ5 family chart also lists "Hardwired Support" across the Panel 5, Hub and NS through IQ hardware on PowerG.

How we choose for an old wired system

If the old wired loops include smoke or carbon monoxide detectors that the owner wants to keep, the IQ route has the edge on paper, because 2GIG's takeover module excludes those zones and the IQ Hardwire PG lists them. For a store or office with many wired zones, keypads at several doors or a need for many user codes, the IQ Pro is the natural fit. For a house or brownstone with burglary-only wired loops and a set of 345 MHz wireless sensors added over the years, a 2GIG EDGE with one or two takeover modules can reuse almost everything. On any takeover, we first test every wired zone for faults, check the old panel's power supply and battery, and confirm what the old devices actually are. A takeover is only as good as the oldest sensor in it, and some old smoke detectors and contacts are due for replacement whatever panel goes on the wall.

Our alarm installation in NYC page notes that if an existing panel still works, we can often take over monitoring by reprogramming it rather than replacing it at all. That is the cheapest path, and on some systems it is the right one; a new 2GIG or Qolsys touchscreen makes sense when the owner wants the app, cameras, smart-home control and a cellular path that the old panel cannot give.

Buying

Installer-Only or Do It Yourself? How You Can Buy Each Panel

Neither 2GIG nor Johnson Controls sells its panels directly to homeowners on its own site. 2gig.com sends buyers to a dealer and installer finder and markets the EDGE as "revolutionizing the pro-install security market." The IQ site offers "Find a dealer," invites security companies to become IQ dealers, and lists its panels' features in terms aimed at installers, such as the "IQ Installer Interface" and SmartMount. Both lines are designed to be installed and supported by a security company.

That said, some online dealers sell these panels to do-it-yourselfers along with monitoring. One dealer we found in the search results, Surety, calls the IQ Panel 4 and IQ Panel 5 "the most capable DIY Alarm.com security panels." If you go that route, you will be programming zones, enrolling sensors and setting up the Alarm.com account through that dealer's tools, and you will be the one on the ladder. For an apartment with a handful of wireless sensors, a capable owner can do it. Warranty, Updates and Who Fixes It Later

Existing Systems

Already Have a 2GIG or Qolsys Panel? Keep, Upgrade or Replace

Many people searching this comparison already own one of these panels, often a 2GIG GC2 or GC3 or a Qolsys IQ Panel 2 or IQ Panel 4, and want to know whether to stay, upgrade or switch. Here is what we tell them.

If you have an older 2GIG panel

Your sensors are almost certainly 345 MHz. 2GIG's EDGE and GC Touch both read 345 MHz 2GIG sensors, and the EDGE data sheet says it reads older 2GIG and Honeywell sensors too, so upgrading the panel can keep most of the sensors on the walls. That is usually the cheapest path to a new touchscreen, current cellular and newer Z-Wave. Switching to an IQ Panel 5 is possible with a 345 MHz legacy card, but you would be paying for PowerG+ and using it only for new devices. We recommend switching brands mainly if you plan to replace most sensors anyway, or if your building needs the long-range two-way sensors that standard 345 MHz devices cannot match.

If you have a Qolsys IQ Panel 2 or IQ Panel 4

If your panel already uses PowerG sensors, staying in the IQ family keeps them; the IQ Panel 5 has PowerG+ built in. If your IQ panel runs on a legacy frequency card, the Panel 5's add-on cards can keep those sensors too, according to Johnson Controls. An IQ Panel 4 in good working order is still a capable panel, and still listed by Johnson Controls; there is no urgency to replace it simply because a newer one exists. The reasons to move to a Panel 5 are the newer Z-Wave 800 radio, Bluetooth 5.0, dual-band Wi-Fi, a 13-megapixel camera and Android 14.

If your panel is locked or tied to another company

Panels installed by large national companies are sometimes locked to that company's programming or account. Before we promise to take over any panel, 2GIG or Qolsys, we confirm whether we can get into its installer programming and whether its Alarm.com account can be moved. If not, a new panel that reuses the sensors is often still possible, but we tell you that before you cancel your old service, not after.

Reality Check

What Reviews and Forums Get Wrong About 2GIG vs Qolsys

When we searched "2GIG vs Qolsys" on November 14, 2026, the results were dealer blogs from Missouri, Arkansas and Los Angeles, online dealers such as Alarm Grid and Surety, Reddit threads, a CocoonTech forum thread, an Aqara forum post, installer groups on Facebook and several YouTube comparisons, some going back to the IQ Panel 2 and the 2GIG GC3. Plenty of it is useful. A few points come up again and again that we would correct or add to.

  • "IQ Panel 4 vs EDGE" is no longer the flagship comparison. Many of the top results compare the IQ Panel 4 with the EDGE. Johnson Controls now lists the IQ Panel 5 as its flagship, with a higher-resolution camera, Z-Wave 800 Long Range, Bluetooth 5.0, dual-band Wi-Fi and Android 14. The IQ Panel 4 is still sold, but a comparison that stops there is out of date.
  • "Qolsys" is now "IQ." The ranking pages we looked at do not mention that qolsys.com now redirects to Johnson Controls' IQ site, or that the panels are listed without the Qolsys name. It matters when you search for manuals, firmware notes or a dealer, and it is the same product.
  • Range figures are best-case numbers. "Miles" of range for PowerG or E+ is the makers' headline, and 2GIG itself prints that range "can vary depending on building materials." In a New York rowhouse, plan for the building, not the brochure.
  • "2GIG is cheaper" is about the panel, not the job. 2GIG describes the GC Touch as value-priced and two of the dealer blogs in the results call 2GIG the more affordable choice up front. On a real install, sensors, labour and the condition of the building usually cost more than the difference between panels, and reusing existing sensors can swing the total either way.
  • "Qolsys is easier to use" is opinion. An online dealer's forum answer says the Qolsys panel is easier to use, with more frequent updates, and an installer in a Facebook group said the IQ Panel 4 "isn't as robust for hardwired zones and can be a pain to mount." Both are fair opinions from people who install them; neither is a specification. Johnson Controls' answer on hardwired zones is the IQ Hardwire PG and the IQ Pro.
  • Nobody mentions old wiring. National articles assume an empty house. In New York, the old wired alarm already in the walls is often the deciding factor, and the two makers handle it differently, as covered above.

We also read the reversed search, "Qolsys vs 2GIG," and found the same pages in a different order, so this one page answers both. If you have read a claim elsewhere that is not covered here, send it to us and we will tell you whether it matches the makers' current pages.

FAQ

2GIG vs Qolsys FAQ

Which is better, 2GIG or Qolsys?
+

For most new installs we prefer Qolsys, now sold as the IQ Panel 5 by Johnson Controls, because it has encrypted long-range PowerG+ sensors built in, a 1280 × 800 screen, a 13 MP camera and dual-band Wi-Fi. 2GIG is the better pick when you are keeping working 345 MHz sensors, want face-recognition disarm on the EDGE, or want the value-priced GC Touch.

Is Qolsys vs 2GIG the same comparison as 2GIG vs Qolsys?
+

Yes. People search the two names in both orders and mean the same thing, so we cover both on this one page, including the older IQ Panel 4 and the 2GIG GC Touch.

What is the difference between the 2GIG EDGE and the Qolsys IQ Panel 5?
+

Both are 7-inch touchscreen panels with LTE, Wi-Fi, Z-Wave, Bluetooth disarm, 2-way voice and Alarm.com. The EDGE adds face-recognition disarm and reads 345 MHz 2GIG and Honeywell sensors natively. The IQ Panel 5 adds built-in PowerG+, a 13 MP camera, 5 GHz Wi-Fi, Z-Wave 800 Long Range and up to 242 user codes.

Is Qolsys still a brand, or is it called IQ now?
+

Qolsys is part of Johnson Controls. On November 14, 2026, qolsys.com redirected to iqsecurityproducts.com, where the panels are listed as IQ Panel 5, IQ Panel 4 and so on. A Qolsys IQ Panel 5 and an IQ Panel 5 are the same product.

Can a Qolsys panel use my old 2GIG or Honeywell sensors?
+

Often, yes. The IQ Panel 5 takes add-on radio cards for legacy 319.5, 345 or 433 MHz sensors, and 2GIG and Honeywell wireless sensors are 345 MHz. Each old sensor still needs testing, and older sensors keep their old, unencrypted one-way radio.

Can a 2GIG EDGE use PowerG sensors?
+

2GIG does not list PowerG among the EDGE's sensor families. Its data sheet and catalog list eSeries encrypted sensors, 345S sensors, E+ 900 MHz extended-range sensors (firmware 1.3 or later) and older 2GIG and Honeywell sensors.

Do 2GIG and Qolsys panels work with Alarm.com?
+

Yes. The EDGE is sold as a Verizon or AT&T Alarm.com panel and the GC Touch is optimized for Alarm.com Scenes. Johnson Controls lists full Alarm.com integration on the IQ Panel 5, IQ5 Hub and IQ5 NS.

Which panel has the better wireless range?
+

Both makers publish long ranges for their newest sensors: up to 1.2 miles for 2GIG's E+ door sensor and up to 2,500 ft for a PowerG+ door sensor. Those are best-case figures; 2GIG notes range varies with building materials. In brick and plaster buildings, panel placement and repeaters matter more than the headline number.

How long does the backup battery last on each panel?
+

About a day on both. 2GIG lists a 24-hour lithium-polymer battery on the EDGE and GC Touch. Johnson Controls rates the IQ Panel 5's 3,000 mAh lithium battery for over 24 hours. Actual hours depend on screen use, cellular signal and connected devices.

Can I install a 2GIG or Qolsys panel myself?
+

Both makers sell through dealers and installers, and some online dealers sell IQ panels to do-it-yourselfers. It is workable for a small apartment. For takeovers of wired systems, multi-floor houses, or smoke and carbon monoxide detection, we recommend a licensed installer.

What warranty do 2GIG and Qolsys panels have?
+

2GIG's EDGE and GC Touch data sheets state a limited 3-year warranty. Johnson Controls' current IQ Panel 5 and IQ Panel 4 pages did not state a term; the last Qolsys warranty we found, from 2017, was one year from purchase or two years from the manufacturing date code, whichever came first.

Can either panel take over a wired alarm in a brownstone?
+

Yes. 2GIG's takeover module converts 8 wired zones per module but cannot be used for carbon monoxide or fire zones. Johnson Controls' IQ Hardwire PG adds 8 hardwired zones to IQ panels and supports CO and four-wire smoke detectors; the IQ Pro is a hybrid panel for larger jobs.
