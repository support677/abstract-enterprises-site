The Brand

What Capture Advance Is, and Why It Shows Up on Brooklyn Quotes

Most people who call us about Capture cameras have seen the name on a quote from another installer, on a box in a basement left by a previous owner, or on the recorder in a building they just bought. Very few have seen it in a store, and that is the first thing worth explaining. Capture Advance is not a consumer brand like the doorbell and Wi-Fi camera names sold in electronics stores. ADI Global Distribution, a large wholesaler that supplies security installers, describes Capture Advance as "an ADI Exclusive Brand." In plain terms, ADI puts its own name on a full line of cameras, recorders, kits and mounting parts and sells it through its branches to professional installers. That is why it tends to arrive in a Brooklyn building through a technician rather than through a shopping cart.

ADI says the line is geared toward small and mid-sized residential and commercial properties. That description fits Brooklyn unusually well. The borough is mostly made of buildings that are too big for a single doorbell camera and a phone app, yet too small to justify the enterprise systems a hospital or a high-rise office uses. A three-story brownstone with a garden apartment, a four-story walk-up with eight units, a corner deli with two floors of apartments above it, a small warehouse in East Williamsburg: these are the properties the brand is built for. ADI lists residential security, retail, office buildings, banking, restaurants and hospitality, parking lots and garages, healthcare and manufacturing among the uses, which is a fair picture of what a single Brooklyn avenue holds on one block.

The company behind ADI has changed shape in the last few years, and it is worth knowing because it explains the app. ADI is part of Resideo. In June 2024 Resideo completed its purchase of Snap One, a company that sells smart-home and security products to professional integrators, and folded Snap One into the ADI distribution business. Resideo said the deal gives integrators "an increased selection of both third-party products and proprietary offerings." The Capture ADV app that runs Capture Advance recorders is published on Apple's App Store by Wirepath Home Systems, LLC. We mention this only so the name on the app listing does not surprise you when we set it up on your phone. The brand, the cameras and the support line all lead back to ADI.

The product range is wide enough that we can usually match a building without leaving the brand. ADI groups it into IP cameras, which come as fixed and motorized dome, turret and bullet cameras at 5MP and 8MP, and HD cameras, which come as fixed and motorized dome, eyeball and bullet cameras at 2MP and 5MP. There are DVRs for the HD cameras, NVRs for the IP cameras, ready-made kits that pair a recorder with six or twelve cameras, monitors, and the unglamorous parts that make an install last: junction boxes, pendant caps, wall mounts, pole mounts and rain shades. Those mounting parts matter more in Brooklyn than people expect, because a camera on a brownstone cornice, a fire escape landing or a shop's sign band rarely sits on a flat, clean surface.

ADI also promises what it calls "an industry competitive warranty with readily available technical support," and backs the line with a support site at capture-adv.com that holds spec sheets and user guides. The brand page does not state a single warranty length for the whole line. The one data sheet we checked closely, for the R2-5MPFXTUR 5MP turret, lists the warranty as 36 months. We tell customers that figure when that model is on their plan and check the data sheet for any other model before we quote a number. Our own service terms are separate from the manufacturer's warranty and are set out in our service agreement, as explained in the pricing section.

Capture Advance and NDAA compliance

A growing number of Brooklyn customers ask about NDAA before they ask about resolution. Some run businesses that sell to government agencies, some sit on co-op boards that were told to avoid certain camera makers, and some simply read about federal restrictions and want to be safe. ADI's brand page opens by describing "Capture Advance NDAA compliant cameras, recorders and kits," and the individual listings we reviewed carry "NDAA Compliant" in the product name: the R2-5MPFXTUR and R2-5MPTRMTRZ IP turrets, the R2-HD2MPEYE, R2-HD5MPEYE and R2-HD5MPMOEY HD turrets, the R2-HD5MPDME HD dome, the R2-HDDVR4C2T four-channel DVR and the R2-NVR16C2TB sixteen-channel NVR. When a board or a client needs it in writing, we print the product listing or data sheet for each model on the plan and hand it over with the quote, rather than asking anyone to take a sales line on trust.

We are careful not to overstate this. "NDAA compliant" is a claim the distributor makes about its products. It is not a certificate we issue, and it does not say anything about where your footage goes or who can see it. Those things depend on how the recorder is set up, whether it is exposed to the internet, and who has the passwords, and we deal with them on every install regardless of the brand.

Two details in the line that suit older Brooklyn buildings

Two features stand out when we plan for the kind of buildings Brooklyn has. The first is on the HD side. ADI says the Capture Advance HD line is "multi-format compatible with CVI, TVI, AHD and CVBS." Those are the four common ways an analog camera sends video down a coax cable. In practice, it means a Capture DVR can often accept the signal from a camera another company installed years ago, and a Capture HD camera can often be read by a recorder that is already in the building. In a walk-up where a previous owner ran coax to every landing in the 2000s, that flexibility can save a lot of drilling through plaster and brick.

The second is on the IP side. ADI says every Capture Advance IP camera includes a microSD card slot with what it calls Automatic Network Replenishment. If the cable or switch between the camera and the recorder goes down, the camera keeps recording to its own card and, once the connection is back, sends the missing video to the recorder. In a brownstone where the recorder sits in the cellar and the cable to the top-floor rear camera runs through three floors of old house, that is a useful safety net. It does not replace good cabling, but it means a loose patch cord or a tripped switch does not leave a gap at the exact moment you need the footage.

A Capture Recorder Beeping, a Blank Channel or an App That Stopped Connecting

Not every call is a new system. A good share of the Capture work we do in Brooklyn is on recorders someone else installed: a DVR in a cellar that beeps every few minutes, a channel that shows "no video" after a storm, a camera that went purple at night, or a Capture ADV app that stopped connecting after the building changed internet providers. Most of these come down to a short list of causes. The hard drive is failing or full, a power supply under the stairs has died, water has found its way into a connector on the outside of the building, or the recorder lost its address on the new router.

If a recorder is beeping, take a photo of its front panel and the screen if one is connected, and text it to (347) 934-8335. Do not unplug it repeatedly, because a drive that is starting to fail can lose more footage each time it is cut off. If you need to keep footage of an incident that already happened, tell us that first. We can usually pull the clip before doing anything else to the system, so a repair does not cost you the evidence you called about.

Choosing the Family

HD-over-Coax or IP: Which Half of the Capture Line Fits Your Brooklyn Building

Every Capture Advance system we install in Brooklyn starts with one decision, and it is not about megapixels. It is about the cable. The line is split into two families that do not mix on the same recorder. The HD family sends video over coaxial cable to a DVR. The IP family sends video and power over Cat6 network cable to an NVR. Pick the right family for the building and the rest of the plan falls into place. Pick the wrong one and you either pay to rip out cable that was perfectly usable, or you end up with a system that cannot grow the way you need.

When the HD family is the sensible choice

HD-over-coax, which the trade calls HDoC, exists to make the most of coax that is already in a building. Security Info Watch's overview of the technology puts the appeal simply: it "uses existing analog coaxial cabling to deliver high-definition video," with "no need to rip and replace existing analog infrastructure." The same article notes that most HDoC recorders are backwards-compatible, so newer HD cameras and older standard analog cameras can run on one recorder, and it warns that not every HDoC product works with every other. That last point is why the Capture HD line's support for CVI, TVI, AHD and CVBS matters. It widens the range of old cameras and recorders it can talk to.

Brooklyn has a lot of old coax. Many of the walk-ups we survey came with an older analog system and a coax run to each landing, the vestibule and the rear yard, and many shops have coax stapled along the ceiling from a camera package put in years ago. When that cable is in decent shape, the HD family lets us swap the cameras and the recorder, test each run, and leave the walls alone. On our Brooklyn price list, a four-camera HD system with 2MP turrets, a four-channel DVR and a 2TB drive is listed at $1,334 installed, and the 5MP HD packages run from $1,663 to $1,723.

HD has limits. Each camera needs its own coax run back to the recorder, and on most installs a separate power feed or a combined cable. The cameras are simpler than the IP ones and do not carry the same on-board analytics. And the largest Capture DVR on our list has sixteen channels, so a building that expects to grow beyond that is better served by IP from the start.

When the IP family earns its higher price

The IP family runs on Cat6, with power delivered down the same cable through PoE. One cable per camera does everything. The cameras are smarter. The R2-5MPFXTUR data sheet lists face, human and vehicle detection, single and double virtual fences, people counting, regions of interest and privacy masking, along with true WDR for scenes with bright backgrounds and dark foregrounds. That last feature matters in Brooklyn vestibules, where the camera is often looking from a dim hallway out through a glass door onto a sunlit stoop. The IP cameras also carry the microSD backup described above, and they can connect to Capture's subscription cloud service if a business wants off-site storage.

On our Brooklyn list, a four-camera IP system with 5MP bullets and a four-port PoE NVR starts at $2,002, and the 5MP and 8MP turret and dome packages run from $2,138 to $2,298. The 4MP full-color turret package, which uses a white light to keep color at night, is $2,097. The step up from the HD packages is real, $1,334 against $2,002 at the entry level for four cameras, but it buys you a system that can take more cameras later on an off-the-shelf PoE switch, report what kind of motion it saw, and survive a network hiccup without a gap.

A quick rule we use on Brooklyn surveys

  • Coax already reaches every spot you want covered, and it tests clean. HD family. Keep the cable, replace the cameras and DVR.
  • No usable cable, or the old runs are cut, crushed or water-damaged. IP family. If we are pulling new cable anyway, Cat6 costs about the same to run and gives you more for the money.
  • You want analytics, people counting or a clean way to add cameras later. IP family.
  • A mix: good coax to the front, nothing to the back. We usually still pick one family for the whole building rather than running two recorders. Most often that means IP, with the old coax left in place as a spare pathway or used as a pull string for the new cable.
  • A board or a client needs NDAA paperwork. Either family works, because the listings we checked on both sides carry the claim. We print the listings for the models on your plan.

Building Specialists

Capture Installation Across Brooklyn's Building Types

Brooklyn's buildings decide more about a camera job than the brand does. The same four Capture turrets go up very differently on an Italianate row house, a six-story co-op, a frame house with vinyl siding and a bodega with a roll-down gate. Here is how we approach each of the buildings we see most often.

The brownstone row house

A brick row house with a sandstone front, a high stoop, often a garden apartment below and a cellar under that. Cable routes run through the cellar and the rear wall, not across the front.

The landmarked row house

The same house inside a historic district, where the Landmarks Preservation Commission limits how many cameras can go on the front and how they are fixed.

The walk-up apartment building

Three to six stories of stairs, a vestibule with mailboxes, a buzzer panel and often a rear yard with a fire escape. Old coax is common.

The co-op or condo with a lobby

An elevator building with a board, a managing agent, a super and rules about contractors, insurance and when work can happen.

The frame two- or three-family

Wood-frame houses with siding, porches and side driveways, where cable can often run in the walls and attic far more easily than in masonry.

The storefront with apartments above

A shop on the ground floor of a mixed-use building, with its own cameras for the register and gate and, often, the landlord's cameras for the residential door next to it.

The brownstone row house: start in the cellar, finish on the stoop

Brownstoner's guide to the type describes a brownstone as a brick row house whose front is "dressed in reddish-brown sandstone," built mostly between 1840 and 1920, with high stoops and Italianate carving that it calls "the quintessential brownstone." It names Brooklyn Heights, Carroll Gardens, Park Slope, Fort Greene and Bedford-Stuyvesant among the places they cluster. For a camera installer, the part that matters is that the front of the house is the hardest surface on the property to work on and the one most people want covered.

We almost never run cable across a brownstone front. The route starts at the recorder, which in most of these houses lives on a cellar shelf or in a closet on the garden level, close to the building's internet connection. From there a cable can reach the front through the cellar wall under the stoop, come up behind the areaway wall or the newel, and arrive at a camera tucked under the stoop landing, the lintel over the garden door, or the underside of the cornice on the parlor-floor entry. At the back, the rear wall is usually plain brick, and a cable can climb inside a painted raceway or come straight out through a mortar joint at the camera.

The Capture camera that suits most brownstone fronts is a turret or a small dome, not a bullet. A turret sits flush under a stoop or a lintel and is easy to paint to match. The 2.8mm lens on the fixed models is wide enough to show the whole stoop and the sidewalk at its foot from a spot under the landing, which is what most owners want after a package goes missing. For a long garden behind the house, a motorized turret with a 2.7 to 13.5mm lens lets us zoom in on the back fence without moving the camera.

The landmarked row house: fewer cameras, better placed

Large parts of brownstone Brooklyn sit inside historic districts, and the rules there are specific enough that we plan around them from the first call. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's permit guidebook covers security cameras in its chapter on health and safety equipment. It says cameras "must be well spaced to avoid a cluttered, haphazard appearance," and that equipment generally should not go on or in front of a primary facade unless there is no feasible alternative. When a front camera is needed, the guidebook points to discreet spots: beneath a band course or the stoop, or behind an areaway wall, fence or plantings.

The guidebook also sets a count. On small residential or commercial buildings, staff can approve no more than two cameras per 25 feet of street frontage, and on large buildings no more than three. A typical row house frontage is narrower than that, which is why our plans for landmarked fronts almost always show one camera, sometimes two, rather than one per window. Cameras should attach to mortar joints, plain masonry, wood, metalwork or concrete at the areaway, and never through cast iron or decorative surfaces. Conduit should be hidden by running it through plain masonry behind the camera. Where that is not possible, staff may approve about ten inches of exposed conduit, painted to match and fixed into the mortar.

Those rules shape the Capture models we pick. A small turret or dome painted to match the brownstone or the stoop ironwork is far easier to approve than a long bullet with a sunshield. The application itself asks for manufacturer cut sheets, color photos and drawings of the existing and proposed facade, and we can supply the Capture data sheet and photos of the proposed spot for you or your expediter to file. Work that meets the guidebook can be approved at staff level. Work that does not may go to a public hearing, which is a long road for one camera.

The walk-up: the vestibule, the stairs and the yard

In a walk-up, most of the trouble happens in three places: the vestibule where packages pile up and the buzzer lets people in, the stairwell, and the rear yard where the fire escapes come down. Owners usually want the vestibule first. A Capture turret with true WDR, mounted inside the inner door and looking out through the glass toward the stoop, can hold faces against bright daylight far better than a basic camera, and a second camera over the mailboxes covers the packages.

Walk-ups are where the HD family earns its place. If a previous system left coax to each landing and the runs test clean, replacing the cameras and the DVR is a one-day job with almost no plaster work. If the coax has been painted over, cut by a renovation or chewed in the cellar, we pull Cat6 instead, usually up the public hall in a painted raceway that the super can live with, or up an old dumbwaiter or pipe chase if the building has one.

The common hallways of a rental building raise privacy questions that do not come up in a one-family house. An attorney answering a New York co-op question on Justia wrote that common-area cameras in hallways and stairwells are lawful as long as voice is not recorded and the camera is not aimed into an apartment when its door is open. We set Capture privacy masks over apartment doors where a camera would otherwise see into a unit, and we leave audio off on hallway cameras unless the owner has taken advice that says otherwise.

The co-op or condo: the board, the agent and the super

A Brooklyn co-op job begins long before the ladder comes out. The managing agent will want a certificate of insurance naming the corporation, the board may want to approve camera locations, and the super will want to know which hours we are working and where we are parking. We plan Capture systems in these buildings with a written location list and a simple drawing, so the board can approve exactly what will go up.

Many co-ops in the borough have been moving from keys to fobs and phone-based entry, and that brings in the city's Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021. HPD's summary says that owners of multiple dwellings with smart access systems must get express consent before collecting reference data, keep only what the system needs, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, and give residents a written data retention and privacy policy. They also may not sell the data or use it to harass or evict. A camera system is not a smart access system by itself, but when a board ties cameras to a fob reader, the two sit close enough that we bring the law up so the board can raise it with its counsel.

The frame house: easier walls, longer driveways

Out toward the south and east of the borough, many houses are frame two- and three-family buildings with siding, porches, side driveways and garages at the back. These are the easiest Brooklyn buildings to wire. Cable can often travel inside an exterior wall or through the attic, and a camera can sit under a soffit with the cable coming straight down behind it. The challenge is distance. A side driveway that runs past a detached garage can be long, and our installed price includes a cable run up to 100 feet per camera. On a long lot we measure every run before quoting so nothing changes on the day.

For driveways, the Capture bullet cameras make sense, because their shape points naturally down a long, narrow space. A 5MP bullet under the front soffit can cover the driveway and the side door, and a motorized bullet at the garage can be zoomed to frame the gate.

The storefront with apartments above: two owners, two systems

A shop on a Brooklyn avenue usually sits under two or three floors of apartments, and the residential door is often right beside the shop's gate. The shopkeeper wants the register, the door and the stockroom. The landlord wants the residential entry and the hallway. Very often each has a separate system, and we are asked whether to combine them. Our usual answer is to keep them separate. The shop's recorder belongs to the shop and the building's recorder belongs to the building, which keeps it simple when a lease ends or the police ask for footage.

Inside the shop, the Capture IP cameras with people counting and region-of-interest settings are useful beyond security. A shop owner can see how many people came through the door during a sale or keep a closer watch on the register area. Outside, a turret or dome under the sign band covers the gate and the sidewalk without cluttering the storefront.

Owners and Renters

Capture Systems in Houses with a Rented Unit, and Cameras Inside a Rented Apartment

A great many Brooklyn houses have an owner on one or two floors and a tenant on another, often in the garden apartment of a brownstone or the second floor of a frame two-family. That arrangement changes how we lay out a Capture system. The owner pays for it and keeps the recorder, but the tenant walks past the cameras every day and may want to see the stoop when a package goes missing. We plan for both from the start. The cameras cover the shared entrances, the stoop, the yard and the sidewalk, and none of them look into the tenant's windows or door. Where the tenant wants access to the stoop camera, the recorder can give them a separate login limited to that one camera, so the owner's other cameras stay private.

The recorder itself goes in the owner's space, not in a shared cellar where anyone can reach it, and it never sits inside the rented unit. If the only internet connection in the house belongs to the tenant, we talk that through before installing, because a recorder on someone else's network is a problem waiting to happen when the lease ends.

Renters ask us a different question: whether they can put cameras inside their own apartment. Cameras inside your own unit, aimed at your own rooms, are generally your business, and a small Capture system is more than most apartments need. What changes the answer is anything outside your door. A camera in the hallway, on the facade, on a fire escape or in the yard touches the landlord's property and the neighbors' privacy, so we ask for the landlord's written agreement before mounting anything there. A short email from the landlord or managing agent naming the spot is enough for us to go ahead.

If you plan to move in a year or two, tell us. We can mount the cameras and run the cable so the system comes out cleanly with only a few small holes to patch, and the recorder and cameras go with you to the next place.

Stoops and Packages

Package Theft from Brooklyn Stoops and Vestibules: Where a Capture Camera Actually Helps

Package theft is the reason a large share of our Brooklyn customers call, and the local news shows why. News12 Brooklyn has run clip after clip of it: a man taking packages in broad daylight in Kensington, a man who appeared to use a child in a stroller to get into a Crown Heights building and take a package, and a porch thief in Flatlands who took a two-year-old's birthday presents. In December 2024 the same station reported that a shipping store in Mill Basin, Outside the Box Shipping, was taking in about 100 packages a day from people who had given up on home delivery. One customer said she had lost more than $500 in packages. Another, who had cameras, said the thieves "look at the camera, and put their head down. They're getting smarter." The station also cited a mayor's office estimate that 90,000 packages are stolen or lost in transit every day across the city.

That last quote is the honest starting point. A camera does not stop a determined thief from taking a box, and a thief who knows where the camera is can keep his face down. What a well-placed camera does is record a usable image of the person, the time and the direction they went, and in a building with several apartments, it settles arguments about whether the package was ever delivered. The difference between a clip that helps and one that does not usually comes down to placement, not price.

Low and close beats high and wide

The most common mistake we see on Brooklyn stoops is a camera mounted high on the facade, looking down at the tops of heads. A person in a hood or a cap is unrecognizable from above. On a brownstone, the better spot is usually lower and closer: under the stoop landing looking across the steps, on the newel post area, or inside the vestibule looking out. A Capture 5MP turret at roughly head height inside the outer door, behind glass, sees faces straight on as people come up the steps, and it is out of reach.

Glass brings its own problem: at night the camera's infrared lights bounce back off the glass and blind it. When a camera has to look out through a vestibule door, we turn off its infrared and rely on the porch light, or we use the Capture full-color turret, which keeps a color image in low light with its own white light. Outside, under the stoop, the IR models work well, and the R2-5MPFXTUR's data sheet rates its infrared to 30 meters, which is far more than any stoop needs.

The vestibule and the mail area

In a walk-up or a small elevator building, packages usually end up on the vestibule floor or on top of the mailboxes. The question residents ask afterward is who came in behind the delivery driver. A camera over the inner door that sees both the mailboxes and anyone who holds the door open answers that question. In buildings with a buzzer or intercom panel, we often aim a second camera so it catches the face of anyone standing at the panel, which helps when someone keeps buzzing apartments until somebody lets them in.

What residents can and cannot get from the footage

Brick Underground answered a question from a Williamsburg renter whose packages kept disappearing. The building had cameras at the entry, but the landlord would only review the footage if the tenants called the police, and the police wanted the footage. One attorney added that there may be an argument that the landlord has a duty to act when theft is an ongoing problem, depending on the circumstances, and both suggested tenants organize, ask for a package locker, or use drop-off points like an Amazon Hub or UPS Access Point.

We pass that along because it affects how we set up systems for building owners. A Capture recorder makes it easy to export a short clip for one incident without giving anyone access to everything else. Recording and Power

Recording Time, Hard Drives and Keeping a Capture System Running When Brooklyn Loses Power

A camera that records nothing is a decoration, and most of the failed systems we are called to in Brooklyn failed at the recorder, not at the camera. Three things decide whether a Capture system has the footage when you need it: how much drive space it has, how it is set to record, and what happens to it when the power or the internet goes out.

How much recording you get

Every Capture system on our Brooklyn list includes enough hard drive space for at least two weeks of continuous recording, and we size the drive to your cameras rather than to the box. That is why some of the four-camera packages list a 6TB drive while the recorder itself ships with 2TB. Higher-resolution cameras write more data, and a 5MP or 8MP camera recording around the clock fills a drive faster than a 2MP camera does. If you want longer than two weeks, the answer is a bigger drive or more of them, and we price that on the plan.

We also set recording rules that make the footage easier to use. Continuous recording in the background guarantees nothing is missed. Motion or person events on top of that mark the timeline, so when you open the Capture ADV app to find out who took a package, you jump straight to the moments when someone was on the stoop rather than scrubbing through hours of empty steps.

When the internet goes down

Buildings change internet providers, and routers get replaced, unplugged and reset. On a properly set-up Capture system, losing the internet does not stop recording. The cameras talk to the recorder over the building's own cables, and the recorder keeps writing to its drive. What you lose is remote viewing on your phone until the connection comes back. When the router changes, the recorder may need to be added back to the network, which is one of the most common short service calls we get.

The IP cameras add another layer. Because each Capture Advance IP camera has a microSD slot with Automatic Network Replenishment, a camera that loses its link to the NVR keeps recording to its card and sends the missing video back when the link returns. We fit a card in every IP camera where the customer wants that protection, and it is especially worth it on cameras at the far end of long runs in the rear yard or on the top floor.

When the power goes out

A short power cut is the most common reason a Brooklyn recorder ends up with a gap or a damaged drive. A small battery backup under the recorder, sized to carry the recorder, the PoE switch or NVR ports, and the router, rides through most brief outages and lets the system shut down cleanly in a long one. It is not part of the packages on our price list, so if you want one we add it to the plan as a separate line. For IP systems, one backup covers the cameras too, because they draw their power from the NVR or switch. For HD systems, the camera power supply needs to be on the backup as well.

Cold, heat and weather

Brooklyn cameras face summer sun on south-facing fronts, winter wind off the harbor and the water, and the drip from cornices and fire escapes. The R2-5MPFXTUR data sheet gives an operating range of minus 40 to 131 degrees Fahrenheit and an IP66 rating, which covers dust and strong water jets. The real weak point is usually the connection, not the camera. We put the cable joint inside a Capture junction box or back box, seal the entry, and leave a drip loop so water runs off the cable before it reaches the camera. That one detail prevents most of the purple-picture and no-signal calls we see on systems other people installed.

Field Notes

Field Notes: How a Technician Surveys a Brooklyn Building for Capture

These notes describe how our technicians work through a Brooklyn survey for a Capture system. They are not a checklist we read from. They are the order of things that experience has taught us, and they explain why a survey visit takes as long as it does.

Stand across the street first. Before touching anything, the technician looks at the building from the opposite sidewalk. That view shows what a passer-by sees, where a camera would stand out, whether the facade is brownstone, brick, limestone, siding or a shop front, and whether the street trees will block a camera's view in summer. In a historic district, it also shows the band courses, cornice and stoop details that the Landmarks guidebook points to as discreet mounting spots.

Find the recorder's home before the cameras'. The recorder needs power, a connection to the building's internet, some air around it, and a place where a burglar will not see it. In a row house, that is usually a cellar shelf away from the boiler, or a closet. In a shop, it is a locked cabinet in the back office, not a shelf under the register. Once the recorder's spot is fixed, every cable route is planned back to it.

Test whatever cable is already there. If there is old coax, the technician traces each run with a tone generator and probe to find out which end goes where, then checks it for damage. Coax that has been stapled flat, kinked around a pipe, cut during a renovation or left soaking in a damp cellar gives a poor picture no matter how good the camera is. If more than one or two runs fail, the plan usually moves to IP with new Cat6.

Look at the light at each spot. A stoop that faces south gets hard sun in the afternoon and deep shadow under the landing. A vestibule looks from dark into bright. A rear yard may have no light at all after dusk. The technician notes each one, because it decides between a standard IR camera, a WDR camera and a full-color camera, and whether a small light needs to go up as well.

Mark privacy before it becomes a problem. Brooklyn lots are narrow, and a rear camera can easily see into a neighbor's garden or kitchen window. The technician points out where a camera's view crosses the property line, so the owner can decide on angle and privacy masks before the camera goes up, not after a neighbor knocks on the door.

Write the plan down. The survey ends with a plan that lists every camera by model number, where it goes, what it sees and how the cable gets there, along with the recorder, the drive size and the price from our Brooklyn list. For a co-op board or a landmarks filing, the same plan becomes the location list and drawing they ask for.

Common Advice, Checked

Things People Hear About Capture Cameras in Brooklyn, and What Holds Up

"A store-bought Wi-Fi camera does the same job"

For one door of a one-family house, a battery camera can be fine. For a brownstone with a garden apartment, a walk-up with eight units or a shop, the limits show up quickly. Wi-Fi struggles through brick party walls and up several floors. Batteries need charging and fail in cold weather. Most consumer cameras keep footage in a subscription cloud. A wired Capture system records locally, around the clock, to a drive you own, and the cameras get power down the cable. The trade-off is that it needs proper installation, which is what we do.

"Old coax is useless now"

Not necessarily. HD-over-coax was built for exactly the situation where good coax is already in the walls, and the Capture HD line reads CVI, TVI, AHD and CVBS signals. If the cable tests clean, keeping it is often the most sensible way to spend the budget. If it does not, we tell you, and we do not pretend a new camera will fix a bad cable.

"More megapixels always means a better picture"

Resolution matters less than placement, lens and light. A 5MP turret mounted low under a stoop gives a far more useful face than an 8MP camera on the third floor looking down. The 8MP models earn their price where the scene is wide and you want to zoom into a recording later, such as a shop floor or a wide garden, not on a narrow stoop.

"Cameras on a landmarked house need a hearing"

Most do not. The Landmarks guidebook lets staff approve cameras that meet its criteria: well spaced, discreet, as small as possible, attached to mortar joints or plain surfaces, with conduit hidden. The hearing route is for proposals that do not meet the rules. Planning a small, painted camera in a discreet spot from the start is what keeps a filing at staff level.

"The app is the system"

The Capture ADV app is how you watch, but the system is the recorder and the cable. The App Store listing describes it as a free app that decodes H.264 and H.265, shows up to 16 live channels and 4 in playback, and handles recordings, snapshots and alarms.
