The Neighborhood

Why Dyker Heights Properties Need Door Buzzer Repair

Dyker Heights was laid out for single houses on big lots, and most of its door buzzers were never designed as systems at all. They grew one household at a time. Walter L. Johnson began developing the hill in October 1895, and the deed restrictions reported in 1899 called for plots of at least 60 by 100 feet with houses set back from the street. Wikipedia's article on the neighborhood describes today's housing as mostly detached and semi-detached one- and two-family homes with driveways and yards, and very few apartment buildings. When the city rezoned 159 blocks of Dyker Heights and Fort Hamilton in 2007, CityLand reported, most of the area went into districts that allow only detached houses, or detached and semi-detached ones. For a buzzer technician, those facts describe the job before we arrive: a front door set well back from the sidewalk, often a gate at the lot line, a side door off the driveway, and a cellar where the bell transformer was screwed to a joist decades ago.

The two-family house is where most of our Dyker Heights calls come from. The NYU Furman Center counts 168 two-to-four-family buildings among the 2025 sales in the Bay Ridge and Dyker Heights community district, against 135 one-family houses and only 25 buildings of five or more units, and it puts the district's homeownership rate at 41.0 percent against 32.7 percent citywide. In practice that usually means an owner living on one floor and a tenant or a grown child on the other, sharing a front walk, sometimes a front door, and nearly always one transformer. When the upstairs household can hear the bell but can't open the door, or the downstairs chime rings for both buttons, the cause is in that shared wiring, and it is fixed best when both households are home during the visit.

The apartment buildings are fewer, but they are where the city's records point. We pulled every 311 bell, buzzer and intercom complaint filed from ZIP 11228 since 2020 and every intercom violation HPD has written in the ZIP. The complaints come mostly from 72nd Street, 13th Avenue and 70th Street, not from the house blocks in the 80s that visitors know from December. That is the pattern a technician expects: a 1920s or 1930s walk-up or small elevator building near the avenue, an apartment station in the foyer or the kitchen, and a riser that has been spliced at every renovation since.

Two things cut across every building type here. The first is the power. Many Dyker Heights blocks are still fed by overhead lines, and the Brooklyn Eagle reported in December 2019 that an outage left about 3,600 customers between 11th and 13th Avenues and 67th and 85th Streets without power for around three hours, with the civic association calling outages "a regular occurrence." A bell transformer, a relay or a door strike can come back from that kind of interruption wrong. The second is the door. A buzzer only works if the door closes onto the strike, and HPD's records for the ZIP show far more self-closing-door violations than intercom violations. Owners who also want the driveway or the entrance watched while the buzzer is planned often pair the repair with a camera, and we keep the two systems on separate wiring in every report.

Two Households, One Transformer

The Owner Downstairs, the Tenant Upstairs: Repairing the Shared Bell in a Dyker Heights Two-Family

Most two-family houses in Dyker Heights started with one doorbell. A button at the front door, a chime in the hall and a small transformer in the cellar were enough for a single household. When the second floor became its own apartment, often decades later, someone added a second button, ran a pair up to a chime or a handset on the upper floor, and, if the tenant needed to open the street door, fitted an electric strike and spliced its release into the same transformer. Each addition made sense on the day it went in. Together they produce the calls we hear most from the neighborhood: "the upstairs bell rings when someone presses ours," "our tenant can talk to the door but can't open it," and "since the owner put in a video doorbell, the upstairs chime only works sometimes."

The physics behind those symptoms is ordinary. A bell transformer has a fixed capacity, printed on its label in volt-amperes. A mechanical chime draws little. A video doorbell draws more, and a strike draws far more for the second or two it is energized. Put all three on a transformer sized for one chime, and it reads normal on a meter at rest but collapses when the strike pulls, so the release buzzes weakly and the door doesn't let go, or the video doorbell reboots every time someone upstairs presses release. A tired transformer can also hum, run hot and fail completely after an outage. In our experience, the transformer is the single most common failed part in Dyker Heights two-families, and replacing it with one sized for the real load, or giving the strike its own supply, solves more calls than any other repair.

Crossed bells are the second most common. Over the years, button pairs get swapped at the plate during a repaint, a chime gets moved during a kitchen renovation and reconnected to the nearest pair, or a new owner relabels the buttons without checking which one rings where. The fix is a map: one person presses each button while someone listens on each floor, then we follow each pair from the button to the cellar to the chime or station it should reach, remake any splice we touch in a sealed connector, and label both ends. It takes both households being home, which is why we ask about it when we book.

Semi-detached pairs and the wall between them

Dyker Heights has many semi-detached pairs, two houses sharing one wall, each with its own front walk and its own cellar. Usually each house has its own bell system, but not always. We have seen pairs where one transformer in one cellar feeds both houses' bells because the houses were built together, and pairs where a previous owner ran a bell pair through the party wall to reach a side door. When one half of a pair is renovated, the other half's bell can stop. If your bell died while the house next door was being worked on, tell us; the first test is whether your bell is powered from your side of the wall at all.

What a two-family repair does not need

It does not need new wire through the walls in most houses. The bell pairs inside walls and floors are protected from weather and handling and usually outlast everything attached to them. Pete Carmello of Lock and Tech told Brick Underground, "A lot of times it's user error," and in two-families a surprising number of calls come down to a release button held too briefly, a chime switched off at its own setting, or a door that needs a push while it buzzes because it was never adjusted. We check those first, because they are free to fix. The repair, when there is one, is usually a transformer, a button, a strike, a station or a splice, priced from the Brooklyn list before we start.

What the City's Records Show

Fifty-Nine Complaints and Fifty-Five Violations: Where Intercoms Actually Fail in ZIP 11228

Because so much of Dyker Heights is owner-occupied houses, most buzzer failures here never reach the city. Nobody files a 311 complaint against themselves. The complaints and violations come from rented apartments, and in ZIP 11228 they cluster on a short list of streets. We queried the city's open data on November 11, 2026. Of the 59 bell, buzzer and intercom complaints filed since 2020, 12 came from 72nd Street, 10 from 13th Avenue, 7 from 70th Street, 5 from 88th Street and 4 each from 71st and 82nd Streets. Bay Ridge Parkway had 3, and a scattering of other streets had one or two. ZIP 11228 also reaches a few blocks beyond the neighborhood's usual lines, so not every record is inside Dyker Heights itself, but the shape is clear: the apartment buildings near 13th Avenue and on the numbered streets of the low 70s generate most of the calls.

The HPD violations follow the same streets. Of the 55 intercom violations in HPD's file for the ZIP, 32 were still open on the day we checked, and all 8 written in 2026 were open. The 2026 list includes violations on 13th Avenue, 72nd Street and 73rd Street, and a pattern that matters for anyone paying for a repair: the same apartment written more than once. One 13th Avenue apartment was cited in August 2026 and again in October 2026, when the inspector wrote two violations on the same day, one for the station in the foyer and one for the station in the kitchen. A 73rd Street apartment was cited in February and again in April. When the same apartment comes back, the first repair usually fixed the symptom without finding the cause.

Reading the inspector's wording

HPD writes these under section 27-2005 of the Housing Maintenance Code, and the wording tells a technician where to start. A typical Dyker Heights violation reads "properly repair or replace the broken or defective intercom system from vestibule to north wall panel, in the foyer," followed by the floor and the apartment's position from the stairs. "From vestibule to panel" means the inspector tested the whole path, from the button or panel at the street door to the station inside the apartment, and it failed somewhere along that path. It doesn't say where. A September 2026 violation on 72nd Street names the "intercom door bell and return buzzer," which means two functions: the bell that should ring in the apartment, and the release the tenant presses to buzz the visitor in. Either can fail without the other.

The location in the violation is also useful. A station "in the kitchen" is often a handset that has hung on the same wall since the building's first intercom, sometimes beside a stove or a sink, and grease, steam and repainting take their toll on its contacts. A station "in the foyer" is more often a newer replacement. Where an apartment has both, and both are written up, we check whether they are on the same pair; if they are, one shorted station can silence the other.

Why so many complaints close without a violation

Of the 59 complaints, 17 closed with HPD reporting that it inspected and issued no violation, and 20 closed because HPD could not get in or could not finish the inspection. Only 17 ended with violations issued. For a tenant, that means an intermittent fault can easily be working on the day the inspector comes. For an owner, it means a closed complaint is not proof that the system works. A repair visit with a technician who tests every apartment and the door is the only way to know, and the dated record we leave is what an owner can show later.

Gates, Paths and Side Doors

Thirty Feet of Yard Between the Button and the Cellar: Repairing Gate and Side-Door Bells in Dyker Heights

The setbacks that make Dyker Heights look the way it does also make its bell wiring longer than in most of Brooklyn. A house that sits back behind a front garden, with a gate at the sidewalk and a driveway running down one side, can have three places a visitor might press a button: the gate, the front door at the top of the steps, and the side door off the driveway that the family uses every day. Each of those buttons needs a pair back to the transformer, and the gate's pair has to cross the yard. Some were run in pipe under the path when the house was built or rebuilt; others were laid directly in the soil, tucked under paving stones or stapled along the fence.

Yard runs fail differently from indoor wiring. Water gets into a pipe that has cracked or lost its seal and sits at the low point, slowly corroding the copper until the bell rings faintly, then not at all. A landscaper's edger, a new fence post or a paving job cuts a pair laid in soil. Roots lift and crack the pipe. The giveaway is a pattern: the gate button fails but the front door button works, or the gate button works on dry days and goes weak after rain, or everything stopped the week the front walk was relaid. Each of those points at the run, not the transformer or the chime.

We test a yard run from both ends before anyone digs. A meter tells us whether the pair is open, shorted or leaking to ground, and a tone generator lets us follow it across the yard from above so we can find the break or the wet section without lifting the whole path. Where the old pipe is sound, a new pair can often be pulled through it. Where it isn't, the options are a new pipe along the shortest route that avoids digging the driveway, a run along the fence or the house wall, or a wireless link between the gate and the house, and each is priced separately so you can choose. Digging under a paved surface, or any masonry, is named in the quote as its own line.

The release at the gate

Some Dyker Heights gates have a lock that the household can release from inside, not just a button. A gate release has to survive the weather, a gate that is slammed, and a post that shifts a little every winter. When the bell at the gate works but the release doesn't, the fault is usually in the release itself, in its alignment with the latch, or in a supply that can't drive it over a long run. We check the voltage at the gate while the release is energized, test the latch with the gate pulled and pushed, and tell you which it is. If the gate itself has sagged or the post has moved, the fix is partly a fence or ironwork job, and we say so rather than replacing a release that was never broken.

The side door off the driveway

In many Dyker Heights houses the side door is the real front door. Families come and go by the driveway, couriers learn to go to the side, and the front door at the top of the steps is used at holidays. But the bell system often still treats the front door as the main entrance. The side-door button may have been added later on a long, spliced pair, or wired to a separate transformer that nobody remembers, or never connected to the upstairs household at all. When the side-door button is the one that matters, we test it first, follow its pair to wherever it actually goes, and, where the owner wants it, make it ring the same chimes or stations as the front.

The Door Itself

Five Hundred Twenty-Eight Door Violations: When the Buzzer Is Fine and the Door Is the Problem

The number that surprised us most in HPD's file for ZIP 11228 wasn't an intercom number. It was the 528 self-closing-door violations, nearly ten for every intercom violation, and 431 of them were class C. HPD's own guidance calls a defective self-closing door "a class C immediately hazardous violation" and gives the owner 14 days from receiving the notice to correct it. In an apartment building, a door that doesn't swing shut and latch on its own is a violation by itself, and it also defeats the buzzer: if the door never latches, the strike has nothing to hold, and if the door slams past the strike, the latch bounces back out.

This matters for repair calls because a door problem often looks like a buzzer problem from upstairs. A tenant presses release and hears the strike buzz, but the visitor can't get in, because the door has sagged on its hinges and is pressing on the keeper so hard the strike can't let go. dormakaba's troubleshooting guide for its strikes names this directly: a warped or sagging door causes "excessive back pressure." Or the opposite: the closer was adjusted to slam the door shut, the latch bounces, and the door sits open an inch all afternoon, so the buzzer "doesn't matter" because the door is never locked.

We test the door with the buzzer, every time. With the strike energized, we pull and push the door, check how it closes from fully open and from halfway, and watch the latch meet the keeper. A closer adjustment, a keeper shim, a latch realigned or a hinge tightened is often the whole repair and costs little. Where the frame has split, the door has to be rehung, or the lock body itself has failed, that is carpentry or locksmith work, and we name it in the quote as theirs. HPD's rules on self-closing doors cover apartment buildings, not owner-occupied one-family houses, but the same physics applies to a two-family's front door: if it doesn't close onto the strike, the buzzer can't do its job.

Cold-weather doors

The 311 records for the ZIP show bell, buzzer and intercom complaints climbing from summer into October and November, with 16 of the 59 filed in those two months. We don't claim a single cause for that, but it matches what technicians see as the weather turns. ButterflyMX's cold-weather guide notes that metal parts contract in the cold, changing the fit between the latch and the strike, so "the signal may reach the door, but the release hardware may not have enough mechanical freedom to move." Moisture in an outdoor button or panel can condense or freeze. A door that worked in September can bind in November, and a symptom that clears on a warm afternoon isn't necessarily fixed. If your release fails mostly on cold mornings, tell us; we test the door and the strike for that.

How the building type changes the repair

The detached two-family is the quickest repair and the longest wiring. Faults are usually the transformer, a button, a splice on the cellar ceiling or the strike, and the yard run if there is a gate. We need both households home and the cellar open, and we test every button from every door, because the one that failed is often the one the family uses least.

The semi-detached pair raises one question first: whose system is it? Before we replace anything, we confirm that the transformer and the pairs for your bells are on your side of the wall. If they aren't, the repair may need the neighbor's cellar and the neighbor's agreement, and we say so before we price anything.

The older large house has layers. We find old bell wire, a room-to-room intercom from a later decade and a newer doorbell, sometimes all still connected to something. We identify which pairs are live, which are dead and which are tied together, disconnect the abandoned ones where they are causing trouble, and label what remains.

The rebuilt house is more often a settings or network fault than a wire fault. A video station that lost its link after a router change, a release relay set to the wrong timing, or an app that stopped receiving calls after an update. We repair and troubleshoot those systems as an independent company, and we check the wired door release separately so the house isn't depending on the app alone.

The walk-up near the avenue is where the city's records point. Faults are a station in one apartment, a riser splice that serves a stacked line, a weak supply or the front door itself. We work from the vestibule panel to the supply to the failing apartments, and we read any HPD violation wording on site, because it tells us which path the inspector tested.

The newer condo building has newer faults: a panel that lost its programming after an outage, a keypad or card reader that stopped talking to its controller, a heavy steel door slammed out of line, or a directory that wasn't updated when units sold. The board or managing agent approves the work, and we write each finding so it can be read by someone who wasn't there.

Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Repair in Dyker Heights: Questions Answered

Two-family houses

The transformer in our Dyker Heights cellar is labeled 10 VA, and it now runs two chimes, a wired video doorbell and the strike our tenant uses. Is 10 VA anywhere near enough?

Usually not. The VA rating is the transformer's capacity, and 10 VA is a common size for a single mechanical chime. A wired video doorbell and an electric strike each add a real load, and the strike's is the largest for the second or two it is energized. A transformer that small will often read normal on a meter at rest, then sag when the strike pulls, which is why the door may buzz but not open, or the video doorbell may restart. We measure it under load, and the usual repair is a larger transformer or a separate supply for the strike, from the Brooklyn list's transformer price.

Our grown children live on the second floor and want to open the side door off the driveway from upstairs, the same way they open the front door. Is that part of a repair?

It depends on what is already there. If the side door already has a strike that was wired to a release upstairs and has stopped working, bringing it back is a repair. If the side door has never had a strike or a release, adding one is new work, priced and approved separately from any repair on the visit. We test the side door first so you know which it is before you decide, and if a strike is added, we size the transformer for both doors.

We just bought a Dyker Heights house and the sellers left no notes. There are three buttons outside, two chimes inside and an old handset in the attic. How do you work out what still works?

We map it. With someone listening on each floor, we press each button and note what sounds, test any release, then follow each button's pair to the cellar and to whatever it reaches. Old handsets in attics are often left from a room-to-room intercom that was abandoned but never disconnected, and sometimes they are still tied into the bell pairs and cause faults. At the end you get a written list of what is live, what is dead, what is tied together and what we would keep or remove, priced line by line.

Outages, poles and yards

The utility replaced the pole and the overhead lines on our block, and the next day our gate bell was dead while the side door still worked. Could that work have reached our bell?

Not usually by touching it, because bell wiring runs from the house's own transformer, not from the pole. But crews often switch power off and on during that kind of work, and a weak transformer can fail on a restart. Because your side door still works, the transformer is probably fine, and the gate's own run or button is the more likely fault. If anything was dug or staked near the gate during the work, that points at the run. We test the gate pair from both ends before guessing.

Since an outage last winter our side door stays unlocked unless we unplug the bell transformer. What did the outage do?

The most likely cause is a release relay or a button whose contacts stuck closed, so the strike is energized all the time and the door never locks. A surge or a restart can weld a relay's contacts, and a mechanical button can stick. Leaving a strike energized for months can also overheat it. We find which part is holding the circuit closed, replace it, check the strike and the transformer for heat damage, and test that the door locks again. Until then, unplugging the transformer is a sensible temporary measure, since your strike then holds the door locked.

Apartment buildings near 13th Avenue

HPD wrote our apartment twice on the same day, once for the station in the foyer and once for the station in the kitchen. Does the landlord need two separate repairs?

Not necessarily. Two stations in one apartment are often on the same pair from the riser, and one shorted or water-damaged station can silence the other. The inspector writes each location separately because each was tested separately. We test both, check whether they share a pair, and repair the fault that actually broke the path. The repair might be one station, two, or a splice between them, and the record lists both locations so the owner can show what was done for each violation.

Our building has an outer street door and an inner door, and the violation says the intercom fails "from vestibule." Which door is the inspector talking about?

"From vestibule" generally means the inspector started the test at the panel or buttons in the vestibule, the space between the two doors, and tried to reach the apartment from there. It describes where the test began, not which door's lock failed. The fault can be anywhere between that panel and the apartment's station, including the release that should unlock the inner door. We test the same path the inspector did, from the vestibule panel to the apartment and back, and then each door's release separately.

Most buzzer complaints in our ZIP come from apartment buildings near 13th Avenue, and we own one. What does an HPD inspector actually check on a buzzer complaint?

The violation wording HPD uses in ZIP 11228 shows the path being tested: from the building entrance or vestibule to the station inside the apartment, and sometimes the "door bell and return buzzer" separately, meaning the bell that rings in the apartment and the release that buzzes the visitor in. A system that rings but can't release, or works for some apartments and not others, can still be written up. We test both functions from the entrance to every complaining apartment, which is the same ground an inspector covers.

Condos, rebuilt houses and businesses

Our condo board on 13th Avenue was told the entire entry system has to be replaced because one unit's indoor monitor is dead. Is that true?

Rarely. A single dead monitor is usually that monitor, its connector or its short run from the riser. Our dental practice is in a storefront on the boulevard. The front desk can buzz the patients' door open, but the release in the back treatment room stopped working. Is that the same circuit?

Usually yes, with the two release buttons wired in parallel to the same strike. If the front desk button still works, the strike, the supply and the door are fine, and the fault is in the back room's button or its pair. A renovation, a moved cabinet or a dental chair installation is often where that pair was cut or crushed. We trace it from the back room toward the strike and fix the break, and test both releases with the door pushed and pulled.

The apartments over our 13th Avenue bakery have their bell transformer on the store's circuit, and when we shut that breaker at night the tenants lose their bell. Can the bell get its own circuit?

Yes, and it should. The transformer's low-voltage side is our work; moving its line-voltage connection to a circuit that stays on, or giving it its own, is an electrician's work under Department of Buildings rules, and we name that part separately in the quote. Once it is on a circuit that stays live, we label the breaker and the transformer so the next person closing up the store doesn't cut the tenants off again.

Often, yes. Many strikes can take a second release input, and a standalone keypad can be wired to fire the same strike as the desk button. Our rebuilt house's gate station calls our phones, but the wired chime inside stopped at the same time. Are they one system?

On many video stations they are: the station sends the call to the app and also triggers a chime or indoor monitor through a relay or a wired output. If both stopped together, the station itself, its power or the link between it and the house is the first suspect, more than the chime. We check the station's power at the gate, the run back to the house and the station's settings, and test the chime and the app calls separately once it is working.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Door Buzzer Repair in Dyker Heights

AI summaries, national cost sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, and vendor blogs are where many Dyker Heights owners start, and most of them picture either a single doorbell on a suburban house or a lobby panel in a big apartment building. Dyker Heights is mostly neither: it is two-household houses with long runs and several doors. Here are seven claims we see repeated and how they hold up here.

"A dead doorbell is almost always the button."

DIY guides start with the button, and for a single chime with a short run, that is a reasonable first check. A worn button is cheap and easy to replace.

In a Dyker Heights two-family, the button is one of five or six suspects. The transformer may be overloaded by a second household's chime, a video doorbell and a strike. A splice on the cellar ceiling may have failed. A gate button's pair may have taken in water under the path. Swapping a button on a system with one of those faults changes nothing.

We start with a meter on the transformer under load, then test every button from every door. That tells us in minutes whether the fault is local to one button or shared by the system.

"Most intercom repairs cost $150 to $400."

Swiftlane's 2026 cost guide gives that range for minor repairs, and summaries repeat it as if it applies everywhere. For one loose wire or one cheap part, it is not far off.

Our Brooklyn list starts with a $250 service call that includes diagnosis, and a simple repair can end there or not far above it. A transformer replacement is $325 to $650, and a strike replaced and aligned is $450 to $950. A yard run that has to be traced and replaced, or a riser in a walk-up near 13th Avenue, costs more because of the time to find the break, not the part.

The useful question isn't the average. It is which part failed on your property, and we answer that in writing before any parts work begins.

"Old wiring means the whole house needs rewiring."

This is the claim that costs owners the most when it is believed. A house from the 1920s or 1930s seems like it must have worn-out bell wire.

The wire inside walls and floors, protected from weather and handling, usually outlasts everything attached to it. ButterflyMX's 2026 guide for small buildings says to repair "when the fault appears localized, compatible parts are available, and the rest of the system still does what the building needs." The parts that fail are joints, buttons, transformers, strikes and the runs that are outdoors.

We recommend new wire only for a run that tests bad end to end, and in Dyker Heights that is most often the run across the yard, not the wiring inside the house.

"If the buzzer sounds but the door won't open, call a locksmith."

Summaries split the job neatly: intercom company for ringing and talking, locksmith for the door. The release is where the two meet, and the fault can be on either side.

A strike that hums but doesn't let go may be starved by a transformer that sags under load, which no locksmith will fix. Or the door may be sagging onto the keeper, which a new transformer won't fix. dormakaba's own troubleshooting guide starts with a meter at the supply and at the strike, then moves to door alignment and back pressure.

We test both halves on the same visit, and where a lock body, a split frame or a door that needs rehanging is the real problem, we name it as a locksmith's or carpenter's job in the quote.

"A wireless doorbell is an easy fix for a dead buzzer."

In a one-family house with one door, a battery doorbell can be a quick answer. In a Dyker Heights two-family it solves half the problem at most.

A wireless doorbell tells you someone is outside. It doesn't let the upstairs household open the door, and it doesn't tell them which door the visitor is at if there are buttons at the gate, the front and the side. ButterflyMX's cold-weather guide also notes that batteries usually perform worse in low temperatures, which matters at a gate on a January morning.

Where a household wants a video doorbell, the sensible version is often a wired one on the existing pair, with the transformer checked first so the tenant's chime and the strike keep working.

"Only buildings with eight or more apartments have to keep an intercom working."

The Attorney General's Tenants' Rights Guide does say that multiple dwellings built after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments must have a two-way voice intercom with a buzz-open release, and that older buildings must have one if a majority of the apartments ask for it.

But Habitat Magazine's 2024 article on the law adds the part summaries leave out. Nancy Kourland, a partner at Lasser Law Group, said of a co-op whose intercom failed, "As a result, the co-op has a legal obligation to repair or replace the intercom," and the article notes that courts have treated intercoms as an essential service tied to the warranty of habitability. And HPD writes broken intercoms in existing buildings under the Housing Maintenance Code, which is how the walk-ups in ZIP 11228 picked up 55 violations.

We don't give legal advice, and an owner-occupied two-family is a different situation from a rental building. But a landlord with a working-but-failing system shouldn't assume the unit count settles it.

"Replacing an intercom costs about $2,500, so repairing an old one isn't worth it."

National averages for installing an intercom are often quoted around that figure, and summaries use them to argue that any repair over a few hundred dollars is wasted.

For a Dyker Heights two-family, the comparison is usually lopsided the other way. Most faults are a transformer, a button, a strike or a splice, each a fraction of a new system. For a walk-up near the avenue, a repaired riser and a few matched stations usually cost far less than a new panel and a station in every apartment.

Our approach to door buzzer repair vs replacement is to write both out side by side when both are reasonable, so you can see what the repair costs, what it leaves behind and which parts of the old system are still sound.
