The Island

Why Long Island Schools Need School Security Camera Installation Planned as a Public Project

Long Island educates its children through an unusual number of separate governments. Wikipedia’s list of the Island’s public school districts counts 125 of them, holding 656 public schools between them; Nassau County police put the Nassau share at 56 districts when they trained every superintendent in the county in 2018. Each district has its own elected board, its own budget vote in May, its own business office and its own purchasing policy. A camera upgrade in Levittown and one in Southold are not two jobs for the same client with different addresses; they are two procurements by two public bodies, each with its own rules, calendar and voters. That is the first and largest difference between this Island and New York City, where one Department of Education runs more than a thousand buildings.

The second difference is who checks the work. Outside New York City, the State Education Department’s Office of Facilities Planning is the code-enforcement authority for public school districts and BOCES; all plans for repair or remodelling of public school buildings must be reviewed and approved by the Commissioner, and the office does that by issuing a building permit. Smart Schools guidance carves out a shortcut for cameras: security work that needs no construction and capitalizes at no more than $100,000 skips the formal capital-project review, though the district must still show the work is code compliant. Above that line, or wherever a camera job turns into ceiling, wall or fire-alarm work, the State Education Department is in the loop, and in 2018 the head of the state’s school business officials told Newsday that capital projects could take nine months to clear that review.

The third difference is money. Long Island districts pay for cameras out of four pockets: the annual operating budget, voter-approved capital bonds, capital reserves, and the state’s $2 billion Smart Schools Bond Act. Smart Schools money is a reimbursement, not a grant cheque; the district spends first and the state pays back once the plan has been posted for public comment, approved by board resolution, reviewed by the State Education Department and cleared by the Smart Schools Review Board. Islip’s schools presented exactly such a plan to their board on October 13, 2026, listing 134 cameras across five buildings with installation, and warning residents that installer quotes stay valid for only 30 to 90 days while the state reviews. Any contractor working Long Island schools has to write scopes that survive that wait.

The fourth difference is labour law. A camera installation paid for by a public district is public work, and New York’s Labor Law requires the prevailing rate of wage and supplements for it. The 2026 schedule for Nassau and Suffolk carries a “Telephone and Integrated Tele-Data System Electrician” classification that, in the Department of Labor’s words, applies to all voice, data and video work, at $42.93 an hour from July 1, 2026 and $44.43 from August 1, plus supplements. That rate, not a private-sector price list, is what sits underneath a public district’s camera bid, and it is why the same camera can cost a district more to install than it costs a parish school a mile away.

Last comes the ground itself. A typical city school is a tall brick building on a single block; a typical Long Island high school is a low, spread-out building in the middle of its own fields, parking lots, bus loop and tennis courts. When East Meadow voters approved a $71.5 million bond on March 5, 2026, by 1,132 votes to 462, the money covered new entrance vestibules, classroom door locks and fire alarms alongside new turf fields, field lighting and bleachers. Cameras on a campus like that are as much about cable distance and outdoor exposure as about lenses: runs that outgrow copper, poles at the edge of a field, a press box hundreds of feet from the nearest network closet. Planning for that spread, inside the paperwork above, is what school security camera installation on Long Island really means.

Long Island numbers that shape a school camera project

  • Many buyers, not one. 125 public school districts and 656 public schools across Nassau and Suffolk, by Wikipedia’s count; Nassau alone has 56 districts.
  • Three BOCES. Nassau BOCES in Garden City, Western Suffolk BOCES in Dix Hills and Eastern Suffolk BOCES in Patchogue each run cooperative bidding for member districts; Eastern Suffolk’s program dates to August 1981.
  • Bid thresholds. General Municipal Law §103 requires formal sealed bids for purchase contracts over $20,000 and public-works contracts over $35,000 in a fiscal year.
  • Smart Schools security money. By July 2018, 23 Long Island districts had state approval to spend $10,812,920 of Smart Schools money on security, led by Brentwood at $4,537,383.
  • Scale of a single district. Middle Country reported upgrading 564 cameras in a $2 million project that also covered card access and entrance booths, finished by late 2022.
  • Private schools are numerous too. The Diocese of Rockville Centre reports 35 Catholic elementary schools and nine Catholic high schools on the Island, outside every public district’s procurement rules.

School Cameras Down on Long Island — Same-Day Help

The urgent school calls we take from Nassau and Suffolk tend to arrive in three moments: the first morning after a long break, when a recorder that ran unattended all summer turns out to have stopped; the day after a storm and a PSEG outage, when half the camera tiles come back black; and the hour after an incident, when the principal needs a clip and nobody remembers the recorder password. Phone (800) 486-0943 and we book the next two-hour window in your county. Most systems are recording again within one to two hours of arrival; a failed drive or recorder is replaced on the same visit or the next. Non-school sites go to our Long Island camera repair crew.

Before anyone drives out, check these

  1. Protect the evidence first. If an incident has just happened, export the clip to a USB stick before anyone restarts anything. A restart rarely erases footage, but a panicked factory reset can.
  2. Find the recorder’s closet and look at its lights. A dark recorder points to power; a lit recorder with dark cameras points to the PoE switch or the cable.
  3. Check the switch in the network closet. School cameras often draw power from a district switch the IT department manages. If the IT team restarted or replaced it over the break, the camera ports may not have come back with power enabled.
  4. Ask the district IT office before calling us. In many districts the cameras sit on a district network and the recorder is reachable only from inside it. A firewall change can make cameras vanish from the app while they keep recording perfectly well.
Two Counties

Nassau and Suffolk Are Different School Camera Jobs

The same paperwork applies in both counties, but the Island changes as you drive east, and so does the shape of a school camera project.

Nassau: dense districts, older buildings

Fifty-six districts packed into the smaller western county, many of them covering a single village or hamlet. Schools sit close to houses and busy roads, sightlines are short, and the work is usually about replacing analog cameras, filling gaps at doors and in corridors, and fitting cameras into new entrance vestibules. Nassau BOCES runs the cooperative bids. Our Nassau County school camera page covers the county in detail.

Suffolk: big campuses, long distances

The rest of the Island’s districts spread across a much larger county, from Brentwood, whose 2018 Smart Schools security approval was the largest on the Island, to East End districts that run a single school building. Campuses are larger, fields and bus loops are farther from the building, and the East End adds salt air and seasonal roads. Two BOCES, Western Suffolk and Eastern Suffolk, serve the county. Our Suffolk County school camera page goes town by town.

Both counties are priced from the same Brooklyn base: Nassau work carries a 1.15 factor and Suffolk work a 1.20 factor on our labour lines, which is why a camera that costs $518 to install in Hempstead costs $540 in Huntington. Public districts in both counties sit on the same District 4 prevailing wage schedule, so on a public bid the county factor gives way to the state rate.

What We Install

School Security Camera Installation Services on Long Island

Every service below is something a Long Island district, BOCES or private school has asked us to price. Most projects combine three or four of them.

Bid-ready scopes and drawings

A camera-by-camera schedule with mounting heights, lens choices, cable routes and closet locations, written so a business official can drop it into a sealed-bid package, a BOCES cooperative purchase or a Smart Schools Investment Plan without retyping.

Entrance and vestibule coverage

Cameras that see a visitor’s face at the outer door, the inside of the new vestibule and the reception window, matched to the video entry station so the person releasing the inner door sees the same image the recorder keeps.

Corridors, stairs and cafeterias

Ceiling and wall cameras along the long, single-storey corridors common in Island schools, aimed so every exterior door is seen from inside, as the State Education Department’s design guide recommends, with cafeterias, gyms and libraries covered as assembly spaces.

Fields, lots and the bus loop

Pole and building-mounted cameras over parking fields, the student drop-off line and the bus loop, with higher-resolution cameras where a licence plate or a face at a gate needs to be readable, and fibre where copper runs get too long.

Analog-to-IP replacement

Swapping aging coax cameras and DVRs for network cameras and a modern recorder, reusing conduit and pathways where they are sound, and documenting which old cables are abandoned so they can come out during the next ceiling project.

Recorders, storage and servers

Network recorders or video management servers sized to the camera count and the retention the district policy sets, placed in a conditioned closet on backup power, with the drives, licences and archivers listed as separate quote lines.

Network integration with district IT

Working inside the district’s own network rules: camera VLANs, switch ports, PoE budgets, firewall rules for remote viewing and the accounts IT wants to control, coordinated with the technology director rather than around them.

Private, parish and charter schools

Single-building systems for Catholic elementary schools, yeshivas, independent schools, charters and preschools, scoped to fit nonpublic safety grants and reimbursement paperwork, and paired with Long Island access control where the front door needs it.

Building Specialists

School Security Cameras for Long Island’s School Buildings

Walk enough Nassau and Suffolk campuses and the same six building shapes keep turning up. The shape, more than the enrolment, settles where the recorder goes, which way cable has to travel and which cameras earn their place first.

Single-storey elementary schools

Long, low buildings with wings off a central office and cafeteria, flat roofs and drop ceilings. Cable runs are horizontal and long rather than vertical, so the number and placement of network closets decides whether every camera stays within copper distance.

High school campuses

A main building with a field house, auxiliary gym, athletic fields, tennis courts, a bus loop and several parking lots. Here the camera list splits in two: an indoor system that looks like a big office, and an outdoor system that looks like a parking-lot or utility job.

Middle schools with additions

Original buildings with one or more later wings, so ceiling types, closet locations and even cable pathways change at every addition line. We survey each wing as its own building before writing one scope.

District offices and BOCES centres

Administration buildings, transportation depots and BOCES program sites, where the recorder room may also be the district’s data centre and the camera system has to fit the IT department’s standards exactly.

Small East End and island schools

Districts that run one K–12 or K–6 building, sometimes on a barrier island or a fork, where a single recorder serves everything, the nearest service visit is a long drive, and salt air reaches the exterior cameras.

Parish, yeshiva and independent schools

Buildings shared with a church, a synagogue or a community hall, often on the same lot, where the camera plan has to respect who owns which door and which hours the school actually controls the building.

How the method changes from one building type to the next

  • Closet math first. Network cable for PoE cameras is limited to 100 metres from switch to camera, so on a long single-storey school we count closets before we count cameras. A wing without a closet may need a new switch location, or fibre to a field enclosure.
  • Ceilings decide labour. Lay-in tile ceilings make corridor cabling fast; hard plaster, exposed deck or gymnasium trusses slow it and may need a lift. The scope says which is which, wing by wing.
  • Additions change everything. Fire-rated walls between an original building and its addition need properly sealed penetrations; we mark each crossing on the drawing so the district’s architect or the inspector can see it.
  • Outdoor cameras are a separate trade decision. Field poles, trenching and new outdoor power usually involve an electrician and sometimes the district’s architect; we price our share and list theirs as allowances.
  • Shared buildings need written door ownership. In a parish hall or a synagogue school, the scope lists which entrances, corridors and recorders belong to the school and which to the congregation, so nobody’s privacy or budget is assumed.
District & BOCES Projects

Public District, BOCES and Private-School Camera Projects on Long Island

On this Island the people who buy school cameras fall into four groups, and each group follows a different set of rules before a single camera is ordered.

Public school districts

Elected boards, May budget votes, capital bonds that need voter approval, General Municipal Law bidding rules, prevailing wage on installation, and State Education Department oversight of anything that becomes construction. Most of the Island’s school cameras sit inside these 125 districts.

Nassau BOCES

Headquartered at the George Farber Administrative Center, 71 Clinton Road, Garden City. Its Cooperative Bidding and Purchasing service gives participating districts, other BOCES and municipalities an electronic bidding system for shared contracts. In 2013 it also built the fibre network and command centre that let Nassau police view school camera feeds during emergencies.

Western and Eastern Suffolk BOCES

Western Suffolk BOCES runs its central administration from 507 Deer Park Road, Dix Hills; Eastern Suffolk BOCES from 201 Sunrise Highway, Patchogue, where cooperative bidding began in August 1981. Both also run their own buildings, which need cameras like any district’s.

Private and religious schools

Catholic schools of the Diocese of Rockville Centre, yeshivas and day schools in the Five Towns and Great Neck, independent schools on both shores. They are not bound by public bidding law, but grants such as the Nonpublic School Safety Equipment program come with their own documentation rules.

Rules every Long Island school buyer should know

  • State Technology Law §106-b. Schools in New York may not purchase or use facial recognition technology. Every camera we install in a Long Island school has any face-matching feature left switched off and unlicensed.
  • Manual of Planning Standards, section S818. The State Education Department’s 2026 manual says cameras go only where there is no reasonable expectation of privacy — corridors, entrances, parking lots, cafeterias, libraries, gyms — and never facing any interior part of a toilet room, locker room or changing room.
  • Education Law 2801-a. Every district and BOCES adopts a district-wide safety plan after 30 days of public comment and a public hearing, and keeps building-level emergency response plans that are confidential and filed with local police and State Police.
  • General Municipal Law §103. Purchases over $20,000 and public works over $35,000 in a fiscal year go to formal sealed bids, advertised at least five days before opening; cooperative and state contracts are the main exceptions.
  • Labor Law §220. Installation under a public-work contract pays the prevailing wage and supplements for the locality — on Long Island, the District 4 Nassau–Suffolk schedule.
  • Education Law §2-d. A vendor that receives or stores student data for a district, including data storage services, must sign a data protection agreement, and the district posts supplemental information and a Parents’ Bill of Rights.

How Island police already work with school buildings

Camera planning on Long Island happens alongside a long-standing relationship between districts and the two county police departments. In 2018 Nassau’s police commissioner told Newsday that every one of the department’s 177 on-duty patrol cars was required to visit a school in the county once a day, and that the department had trained more than 400 principals and the superintendents of all 56 Nassau districts, as well as many private and parochial schools and yeshivas, in active-shooter response. Nassau introduced the Rave Panic Button app to its public schools in 2016, and in July 2018 Suffolk’s county executive signed a $2 million bond to license the same app for public and private schools across Suffolk.

None of those programs installs a camera, but each shapes what a camera system should do. Officers who walk a building every day know its doors; the district’s confidential building-level plan tells them where the recorder is; and a panic-button alert is far more useful when the people responding can be told which camera covers the door it came from. We ask about these arrangements at the first walk-through, so the camera drawing uses the same door names and numbers the police and the safety plan already use.

Most Long Island schools that call us also end up pairing new cameras with door hardware: card readers on staff entrances, a video entry station at the vestibule, and door-position alerts on the doors students prop open. That work lives on our access control installation page, and both trades can share one scope and one schedule.

Your Questions Answered

School Security Camera Installation on Long Island: Questions Answered

These are the questions business officials, facilities directors, technology directors and private-school heads on the Island actually ask before a camera project starts. They are grouped by the stage of the project they belong to.

Bids, cooperative contracts and votes

How much notice do bidders get when a Long Island district advertises a camera bid?

General Municipal Law §103 sets two lines. Purchase contracts — cameras, recorders, licences bought as goods — must be formally bid when the district expects to spend more than $20,000 on that item or commodity group in a fiscal year. Contracts for public work — installation, cabling, mounting — must be bid above $35,000. The State Education Department’s purchasing handbook adds that the advertisement must appear in the newspaper the board has designated, and that at least five days must pass between the first notice and the bid opening, while recommending ten days to two weeks so bidders can prepare.

In practice most Long Island camera jobs cross one or both lines once more than a single building is involved, so the district’s purchasing agent will want a scope that separates equipment from labour and states quantities clearly. Below the thresholds, the district’s own procurement policy under §104-b governs, which usually means written or verbal quotes from several vendors. We write scopes either way and are happy to quote as one of several competing contractors.

What does a BOCES cooperative award actually cover on a camera job?

Often, yes. Nassau BOCES describes its Cooperative Bidding and Purchasing service as an electronic bidding system that participating school districts, other BOCES and municipalities use to meet their purchasing needs; Eastern Suffolk BOCES has run cooperative bidding since August 1981. When a district has signed on to a cooperative bid that covers the equipment it wants, buying from that awarded contract replaces the district’s own sealed bid for those items.

The catch is coverage. A cooperative bid covers exactly what it says — a product list, a service category, a term — and installation labour, cabling and outdoor work are often outside it. Before a board relies on a cooperative contract, the business office should confirm that the camera models and the installation are both inside the award. Where only the equipment is covered, the installation may still need its own quotes or bid, and prevailing wage still applies to it.

Does a camera upgrade need a public vote, or can the board of education approve it alone?

It depends on how it is paid for. Cameras bought inside the annual operating budget are approved when voters pass the May budget, and the board then authorises the specific purchase. A capital construction project financed by borrowing needs voters to approve a specific object and purpose, under Education Law §1718, at the annual meeting or a special meeting called with 45 days’ notice. Smart Schools projects need a board resolution after the plan has been posted for comment and heard in public, but no separate referendum, because voters approved the statewide bond in 2014.

That is why so many Island camera upgrades ride inside larger bonds. When Manhasset voters approved a nearly $44 million bond in December 2022, more than $10 million of it was for safety and security; when Glen Cove passed a $30.5 million bond the same week, $2.2 million was security. Districts with capital reserves already approved by voters can sometimes fund camera work from the reserve with a further vote on its use. We can price to any of these calendars, but we cannot shorten them.

How State Education Department review applies to camera work

Outside New York City, the State Education Department’s Office of Facilities Planning is the code-enforcement authority for public school districts and BOCES, and it approves construction and remodelling plans by issuing a building permit. Low-voltage camera work that involves no construction is lighter: the Smart Schools guidance says high-tech security installations that need no construction, or that capitalise at no more than $100,000, skip the formal capital-project review, though the district must still show the work is code compliant.

The line moves as soon as the job touches walls, ceilings, fire separations, fire alarms or new electrical circuits, or becomes part of a larger capital project. Then the district’s architect or engineer files with the Office of Facilities Planning and the cameras ride on that permit. Our scope flags every item — a new pole base, a cored rated wall, a new circuit — that could push the work over that line, so the district can decide early.

Does the Wicks Law apply when cameras ride inside a large Long Island bond project?

It can. General Municipal Law §101, the Wicks Law, requires separate specifications and separate prime contracts for plumbing, heating and ventilation, and electric wiring when a public building project’s total cost exceeds a county threshold — $1.5 million in Nassau, Suffolk and Westchester, against $3 million in the five boroughs and $500,000 elsewhere. Below the threshold, bidders must list their plumbing, HVAC and electrical subcontractors and the amounts in a sealed list that cannot be changed without the owner’s approval.

Many Island bonds are far above $1.5 million, so camera work bundled into one often sits inside the electrical prime contract or alongside it, and we work under that contractor on the same wage schedule. Districts that want the technology department to choose equipment sometimes keep cameras as a separate, smaller contract instead. The choice should be made when the architect writes the bid documents, not after the electrical contract is awarded.

Smart Schools money and the long wait

Who sits on the Smart Schools Review Board, and why does its schedule matter?

The Review Board is the last stop for every plan. In 2018 the executive director of the state’s school business officials association described it to Newsday as a board with representatives from the State Education Department, the State University of New York and the state Division of the Budget, which meets only when there is a critical mass of projects to consider — so a plan that clears department review just after a meeting can wait for the next one. Islip’s October 2026 presentation sets out the steps before that. The preliminary plan goes to the board of education and is posted on the district website for 30 days of public feedback. After those 30 days the board holds another hearing allowing public comment. The board then approves the final plan by resolution, the superintendent submits it through the State Education Department’s business portal, department staff review it, and the Smart Schools Review Board gives final approval. Only then is the district authorised to start spending and claiming reimbursement.

Patchogue-Medford described the same path in 2017: a preliminary plan approved at a board meeting that doubled as the public hearing, 30 days of community feedback, final board approval, then submission to the department. The practical lesson for a contractor is that nothing should be ordered, and certainly nothing installed, until the district has the state’s approval notice in hand. Equipment bought early may simply not be reimbursed.

Why does Smart Schools money reach the district only after the installer has been paid?

Because the program is built as a reimbursement. Islip’s presentation describes it in one line: funds must first be spent by the district, and the state then reimburses the expenditure. Islip’s first plan shows the timing — 636 Chromebooks and licences bought in July 2026, receipts submitted, and $324,360 then awaited from the state.

For a camera project that means the district pays us on its normal payment terms, from its own cash, and recovers the money later. Business offices plan cash flow around that gap, which is one reason camera phases are often sized to what the district can front in one fiscal year. Our invoices are itemised to match the approved plan line by line, because the reimbursement claim needs receipts that read the same way the plan does.

How should an installer’s quote be written so it survives months of state review?

Islip warned its own residents about this: quotes for its second plan were valid for only 30 to 90 days, prices may rise before orders are placed, and the district may spend only the amount the state approves, so items might have to move to a later plan. A quote that expires in thirty days is nearly useless in that process.

We write school quotes in three layers: a scope that will not change (camera locations, cable routes, closet work), a labour price held for a stated period, and an equipment schedule that names an acceptable alternate for every model in case a product is discontinued during review. Where a manufacturer raises prices, the alternate lets the district stay inside its approved figure without resubmitting the whole plan.

Prevailing wage and the public-work price

Does school camera installation for a public district on Long Island carry prevailing wage?

Yes. Labor Law §220 requires contractors and subcontractors on a public-work contract to pay the prevailing rate of wage and supplements for the locality, and a district contract for camera installation, reconstruction or repair is public work. On Long Island the relevant schedule is District 4, covering all of Nassau and Suffolk.

The 2026 District 4 schedule lists a “Telephone and Integrated Tele-Data System Electrician” rate of $42.93 an hour from July 1, 2026, rising to $44.43 on August 1, and states that the classification applies to all voice, data and video work, excluding fire alarm and energy-management systems, which take the full electrician rate of $66.75. Supplements are paid on top. The schedule asks contractors who are unsure which rate applies to call the Department of Labor’s Nassau or Suffolk district office, and on a mixed job — cameras plus a new circuit — that call is worth making.

Why can the same camera cost a public district more to install than a private school down the road?

The equipment price is identical. The labour is not, for three reasons. First, the prevailing wage schedule sets the floor for installation hours on public work, and private schools are not public work. Second, public contracts carry paperwork that private ones do not: certified payrolls, insurance certificates naming the district, and sometimes bid and performance security. Third, public work often has to happen when students are away, which can mean evening or holiday hours.

That is why the prices on this page — the Brooklyn base times 1.15 or 1.20 — are what a private, parish or charter school will see, while a public district’s figure is built from the wage schedule in force when the work is done. We show both clearly, and we never quote a public district a private-school labour rate it would not be allowed to accept.

Safety plans, privacy and the vestibule

Can residents obtain a school’s camera locations through a Freedom of Information request?

Not if the district files them properly. Under Education Law 2801-a every district and BOCES keeps building-level emergency response plans that are confidential, exempt from Freedom of Information requests, and filed with local police and the State Police within 30 days of adoption. Those plans are reviewed at least once a year, with district-wide plans updated by September 1. If responders are expected to use the camera system — and several Island districts let police view feeds in an emergency — the building plan is the confidential place to record where the recorder is, who can reach it and which cameras cover which doors.

The public, district-wide plan is a different document: it goes out for 30 days of comment and a hearing, so it should describe the camera program in general terms, not list blind spots. We deliver camera drawings as a separate document marked for the building-level team, so the district can attach it to the confidential plan and keep it out of anything posted online.

Does switching to cloud video change what a district must post under Education Law 2-d?

If the vendor receives or stores student data, the district will treat it as a third-party contractor under Education Law §2-d, which expressly includes data management and storage services. The State Education Department’s 2026 quick reference for superintendents lists what follows: a data protection agreement, supplemental information posted on the district website, a Parents’ Bill of Rights, a designated Data Protection Officer and breach reporting.

Whether particular footage counts as student data is a judgment for the district’s Data Protection Officer and counsel, guided by federal FERPA guidance on photos and videos. What we can say is practical: a recorder that keeps footage on the district’s own premises adds no new third party, while a cloud subscription almost always does. We point that out at the start, so the agreement is in place before cameras are switched on rather than discovered at audit.

Where should cameras sit in a newly built entrance vestibule?

Three positions cover almost every vestibule we see on the Island. One camera outside the outer door, at a height and angle that records a visitor’s face rather than the top of a cap. One inside the vestibule looking back at the inner door and the transaction window, so the recording shows what was handed through and who was buzzed in. One in the corridor beyond the inner door, facing back toward it, which the State Education Department’s design guide recommends for every exterior door: interior cameras positioned to face the doors people come in by.

The video entry station at the outer door is a fourth image, and it should be on the same recorder. With so many districts adding vestibules in bond projects — East Meadow’s 2026 bond names them at every building — the cheapest moment to run camera cable is while the vestibule walls are still open. We ask the district’s architect for the vestibule drawings so the cable is in the wall before the drywall closes.

Campus spread: fields, lots and phases

Which parts of a big Island campus should get cameras first when the money covers only one phase?

We rank by what the State Education Department’s design guidance and our own surveys put first. Every exterior door seen from inside. The main entrance and vestibule. The routes from parking fields and the bus loop to those doors. Then the corridors and stairs that link them, the cafeteria, gym and library as assembly spaces, and only after that the far edges: field perimeters, tennis courts, outbuildings and the back of the property.

Phasing that way means every phase leaves the building better covered at its most-used points, rather than half the corridors finished and none of the doors. We also install the backbone — recorder capacity, switch ports and fibre to outbuildings — sized for the final phase in phase one where the budget allows, because pulling cable twice across a campus costs more than pulling it once.

How far from the building can a field camera be before copper cable stops working?

Standard network cable carrying both data and power is limited to 100 metres, about 328 feet, between the switch and the camera, and that includes every foot inside walls and ceilings before the cable even leaves the building. On a Long Island high school, a camera on the far side of a football field or at the end of a parking lot is usually beyond that.

The answer is a small outdoor enclosure closer to the camera, fed by fibre from the nearest network closet, with a hardened switch inside it, and power from a nearby light pole circuit or a new circuit installed by an electrician. One enclosure can serve several field cameras. We mark every such enclosure on the drawing with its power source, because power, not cable, is usually what decides where it can go.

Buses, parish halls and private schools

Who reviews the footage when a Suffolk school bus camera records a car passing?

No. Bus stop-arm cameras on Long Island come from county enforcement programs authorised by a 2019 state law. Suffolk County’s program outfits participating fleets with stop-arm and internal cameras, video recorders, GPS and LTE connections through its vendor, BusPatrol, at no cost to the schools; the county lists dozens of participating districts plus both Suffolk BOCES. Violations are reviewed and tickets are issued through the county, not the district.

The program has had a rocky history: in June 2024 Suffolk dismissed about 90 percent of a backlog of 9,000 contested tickets after an appellate ruling, nearly 3,000 of them from one stop on Jericho Turnpike in Smithtown. Under the county’s process, the bus’s camera box stores the clip with GPS and time data, an encrypted LTE link sends it to the vendor’s cloud, a vendor safety specialist builds an evidence package, and law enforcement decides whether a ticket is mailed — $250 for a first violation, up to $300 for later ones. None of that touches the building system. We do sometimes add cameras at a bus loop or depot so the district has its own view of loading and arrivals, but the bus-mounted equipment belongs to the county program.

Does a private or parish school on Long Island need the State Education Department’s approval for camera work?

Generally not in the way a public district does. The Office of Facilities Planning describes itself as the code-enforcement authority for public schools, BOCES and charter schools chartered after July 31, 2010. A Catholic, Jewish or independent school building falls under the ordinary building-code authority of its town or village, like other private buildings, and that building department is the one to ask whether any part of a camera job needs a permit.

Two exceptions matter. Any new electrical circuit or outlet is line-voltage work for a licensed electrician, under that municipality’s rules. And grants come with their own conditions: the Nonpublic School Safety Equipment grant reimburses eligible religious and independent schools, and a hate-crimes security grant from the state Division of Criminal Justice Services funds interior and exterior improvements for at-risk nonprofits; each has paperwork about what may be bought and how it is documented.

Do BOCES buildings follow the same camera rules as the districts they serve?

Mostly, with one important difference. A BOCES is covered by the same Education Law 2801-a duty to adopt a district-wide safety plan and building-level emergency response plans, the same State Education Department code oversight through the Office of Facilities Planning, and the same public bidding and prevailing wage rules on its own contracts. The 2026 Manual of Planning Standards and the facial recognition ban apply to BOCES classrooms exactly as they do to a high school.

The difference is authorisation. The State Education Department’s guidance on authorizing school construction says a BOCES may authorise a capital project in a facility it owns or leases by board resolution, while voter approval is needed only to acquire buildings, sites or additions. So a camera upgrade at a BOCES technical or special-education centre can move on a board vote, without the referendum calendar a component district would face. Nassau, Western Suffolk and Eastern Suffolk BOCES each run several program sites, and we scope each site as its own building.

Equipment

Types of School Security Cameras We Install

School equipment on Long Island is chosen against three filters before it is chosen for image quality: whether the manufacturer is barred from federally funded purchases, whether the product can be bought through the route the district is using, and whether the district’s IT department can manage it.

Indoor domes and turrets

The workhorse of corridors, stairwells, cafeterias and libraries: small, vandal-resistant housings on drop ceilings or high on block walls, aimed along corridors and back at exterior doors.

Exterior turrets and bullets

Weather-rated cameras on building corners and soffits over doors, walkways and play areas, with infrared or low-light colour imaging for the dark hours after evening events end.

Licence-plate cameras

Narrow-view cameras at driveway entrances and lot gates, set up to read plates on vehicles entering at low speed. The design guide calls for higher-resolution cameras wherever plates or faces must be captured in detail.

Pan-tilt-zoom cameras

For fields, large lots and campus perimeters, where one operator-controlled camera can cover what would otherwise take several fixed ones. They work best with a security desk that actually drives them.

Video entry stations

The camera and intercom at a vestibule’s outer door, which lets office staff see and speak to a visitor before releasing the inner door, and which records to the same system as everything else.

Multi-sensor and wide-view cameras

Several imagers in one housing for gym ceilings, cafeterias and the junctions of corridor wings, reducing cable runs where one mount can see three directions.

Recorders and video management servers

Network video recorders for single buildings; servers and archivers running video management software for districts with many buildings. Islip’s 2026 plan adds a Genetec Streamvault archiver to handle its new cameras, a typical district-scale choice.

Switches, fibre and outdoor enclosures

PoE switches in network closets, fibre runs to outbuildings and field enclosures, and hardened switches inside weatherproof boxes where cameras sit beyond copper distance.

Which brands, and why the list is shorter for schools

Section 889 of the 2019 federal defence authorisation act bars federal money from paying for video surveillance equipment made by Hikvision, Dahua, Hytera and their affiliates. Long Island districts spend federal funds in many programs, so keeping those brands out of a school specification keeps every funding route open. For school projects we therefore start from manufacturers outside that list — Axis, Hanwha Vision, Ubiquiti UniFi and similar lines — and match the district’s existing video management software where there is one. Our Long Island Hanwha price list and Long Island Axis price list show installed figures model by model.

National consumer brands compared with a district system

Ring, SimpliSafe and ADT come up at almost every small-school meeting, usually because a board member has them at home. Consumer cloud cameras are built for one household and one app login: they lack the per-user permissions, audit logs, long retention and on-premises storage a public body needs, and their cloud storage raises the Education Law §2-d question discussed above. ADT does sell commercial systems, and a monitored alarm is a reasonable partner to a camera system, but the decisions that matter for a Long Island school — wage schedules, bid packages, state review, district network rules — are made locally whichever logo is on the panel.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About School Security Camera Installation on Long Island

Search summaries and chatbots answer school camera questions from national material. On Long Island several of their standard answers are incomplete in ways that cost districts time or money. Here are the claims we hear repeated most, and what actually applies here.

“A school can get a camera quote this week and install next month”

For a private preschool, perhaps. For a Long Island public district, the calendar is set by law, not by the installer. Spending above the General Municipal Law thresholds needs either a sealed bid, advertised at least five days ahead, or a cooperative or state contract that already covers the purchase. Capital projects need a voter-approved object and purpose, and a special district meeting needs 45 days’ notice.

Smart Schools projects take longer still. Islip’s 2026 plan lists a 30-day public posting, a second hearing, a board resolution, State Education Department review and Smart Schools Review Board approval before any spending begins. In 2018 the state’s school business officials association called these approval processes slow and cumbersome, with capital reviews taking around nine months.

The realistic timeline for a district camera project therefore runs from the budget or bond cycle, not from the quote date. The useful thing a contractor can do is deliver a scope early enough to fit that cycle, and hold its labour price long enough to survive it.

“Installation costs a few hundred dollars per camera”

National cost guides are written mostly for homeowners, so their per-camera figures assume a house: short cable runs, a few cameras, a single recorder and no public-work rules. A school is commercial low-voltage work, and on Long Island a public school is commercial low-voltage work at prevailing wage.

Our private-school figures start at $518 a camera installed in Nassau and $540 in Suffolk, before recorders and storage. Islip’s October 2026 plan shows what district-scale numbers look like: 29 cameras at Wing Elementary for $54,763.02 with materials and installation, 35 at the middle school for $58,243.67, roughly $1,660 to $2,000 per camera once materials and labour are counted together, with the archiver, access points and door hardware listed separately.

Neither figure is wrong. They describe different jobs. The mistake is applying a homeowner’s number to a district budget line, and then discovering at bid opening that every responsible bidder came in far above it.

“Grants will pay for your school’s cameras”

Some will, on their own terms. Smart Schools money for public districts reimburses spending after approval rather than paying up front. The Nonpublic School Safety Equipment grant reimburses eligible religious and independent schools for certain safety expenses, allocated per student. The state’s hate-crimes security grant funds nonprofits at risk because of their beliefs or mission, through competitive applications.

None of these is free money that arrives before the work. Each needs eligibility, an application or plan, approved items, receipts and, often, waiting. A Long Island school that orders cameras first and looks for a grant afterwards frequently discovers the purchase is ineligible because it came before approval.

The right order is plan, approval, purchase, claim. We write scopes and invoices to fit whichever program the school is using, but the school’s business office or grant administrator owns the application.

“Any licensed security company can install cameras at a public school”

A licence is necessary but not sufficient. On public work the contractor must also pay the prevailing wage and supplements and keep the payroll records that prove it; willful violations can lead to debarment from public work. Districts also check insurance certificates, and many ask how contractor staff working in occupied buildings are screened.

The licence question itself is narrower than people think. In New York, security and fire alarm installers are licensed by the Department of State, and camera work sits alongside that; any new power circuit belongs to a licensed electrician under local rules. A camera contractor who also runs new circuits without an electrician is a problem for the district’s inspector, not just for the contractor.

When a district compares bids, the bid that ignores prevailing wage may look cheapest and be unawardable. We price public work on the schedule from the first draft.

“Modern school cameras use facial recognition to spot threats”

Not in New York. State Technology Law §106-b prohibits schools from purchasing or using facial recognition technology, and the State Education Department repeats the prohibition in its 2026 Manual of Planning Standards. Districts may decide locally about other biometric technology, but only after weighing privacy, civil rights, effectiveness and parental input.

Many current cameras ship with analytics that are not facial recognition: line crossing, loitering, object left behind, people and vehicle classification. These can be useful on a field perimeter after hours. They should still be described plainly in the district’s video surveillance and use policy, so nobody is surprised by what the system does.

When we configure a Long Island school system, any face-matching feature stays switched off and unlicensed, and we list in the handover document which analytics are enabled and where.

“Cloud cameras are simpler for schools”

Simpler to buy, perhaps. A cloud subscription moves video storage to a vendor’s servers, which in a New York school makes the vendor a likely third-party contractor under Education Law §2-d, with a data protection agreement, website disclosures and breach obligations to manage. It also turns a one-time purchase into an annual line that must survive every future budget vote.

On-premises recorders and servers keep the footage in the district’s own closet, under the district’s own retention rules. Cloud management of on-premises systems is a middle path, but even that needs the technology director to agree how remote access works.

Either can be right. The point is that “simpler” depends on the district’s data-privacy workload and budget habits, not on the camera.

“Police can watch school cameras live”

Some can, in emergencies, where the district has arranged it. Nassau BOCES built a fibre network and command centre in 2013 so police could view feeds from participating schools during emergencies; Middle Country told Newsday in 2022 that Suffolk police can access its cameras during an emergency. These are deliberate arrangements, not defaults.

Each one is a district decision with network, privacy and policy consequences, and the details belong in the confidential building-level emergency response plan rather than on a website. A camera system does not become police-viewable just because it is new.

If a district wants that capability, we build the network path the district and the police agree on, and nothing more.

FAQ

Long Island School Security Camera Installation FAQ

Can we see a camera-by-camera drawing before the board votes on the project?
+

Yes, and we recommend it. After the walk-through we produce a drawing for each building showing every proposed camera, its field of view and its cable route, plus a schedule listing model, lens and mounting height. Boards and building-level safety teams can review it before anything is approved. Because the drawing reveals coverage and blind spots, we mark it for the building-level team and suggest it be kept with the confidential emergency response plan rather than posted with public meeting materials; a summary version without locations can go into the public packet.

Will the new cameras work with the video management software our district already runs?
+

Usually. Many Long Island districts already run a video management platform, and Islip's 2026 plan, for example, adds an archiver to its existing Genetec system. We choose cameras the platform supports and check licence counts before quoting, because each new camera normally needs a licence on the server as well as a switch port. Where a district runs an older recorder that cannot take new cameras, we say so in the scope and price the migration as its own line rather than hiding it in camera prices.

Can a district standardise on one camera brand across all its buildings?
+

It can, and many do, but the State Education Department's design guide suggests choosing components by use rather than by manufacturer alone, and stresses that every camera must integrate with the others and with the monitoring system. In practice that means standardising on the video management platform and on a short list of camera models that suit corridors, exteriors, plates and fields, rather than forcing one model everywhere. A standard list also simplifies spare parts and makes later bids easier to write, provided the specification names approved equals where purchasing rules require competition.

Who should manage the camera switches, district IT or the camera contractor?
+

District IT, in almost every case we see on Long Island. The switches that power and connect cameras sit inside the district network, and the technology department is responsible for that network's security, addressing and firmware. We design the camera VLAN, the port assignments and the power budget, install and label everything, and hand the switch configuration to IT, who keep the administrator credentials. Where a district wants us to manage camera switches on its behalf, that is written into a maintenance agreement with defined access, not left informal.

Does a voter-approved camera project count against the district's property tax cap?
+

Usually not, if it is a capital project. Education Law 2023-a lets school districts levy above the tax cap for capital local expenditures, which state rules define to include financing, construction, furnishing and equipping of capital facilities and capital equipment, including debt service, where voters have approved it as required. The expenditure must be for something with a period of probable usefulness under the Local Finance Law, and maintenance and operating costs are excluded. So cameras bought in a voter-approved bond generally fall outside the cap, while annual service, cloud subscriptions and drive replacements come out of the capped operating budget.

Can the camera names match the door numbers in our emergency plans?
+

Yes, and we recommend it. Before programming the recorder we ask for the door and room numbering the district already uses in its building-level emergency response plan and with local police, and we name every camera after the door or space it covers in that same scheme. When an alert comes in, or an officer asks which camera sees the gym's east doors, nobody has to translate between two numbering systems. The same names go on the as-built drawing and on the label at each camera's switch port.

Can a district lease a camera system instead of buying it?
+

Sometimes. New York's competitive bidding law treats true leases differently from purchases, leaving them to the district's own procurement policy, so a leased system may follow a different buying route. But a lease does not change the installation: labour under a district contract is still public work at prevailing wage. Leases and subscriptions also become a recurring operating cost that must survive every future budget vote, and if the vendor stores footage, the Education Law 2-d data agreement applies. District counsel and the business official should review any lease before the board acts.

What should a district put in its video surveillance and use policy before cameras go live?
+

The State Education Department's design guide recommends that districts adopt a video surveillance and use policy. At a minimum it should say where cameras may and may not be placed, how long footage is kept, who may view live and recorded video and for what reasons, how requests for footage are handled, how exports are logged, and that facial recognition is not used. The board adopts the policy; we configure the system to match it, including retention periods and user permissions, and we can provide the technical details the policy needs.

What do you need from the district's IT department before installation day?
+

Four things: the IP address range or VLAN for the cameras, confirmation of which switch ports and how much PoE power are available in each closet, the firewall rules for any remote viewing, and the policy for administrator accounts and passwords. We send a short checklist with the scope so IT can prepare these in advance. When those details are ready, installation days are spent mounting and aiming cameras rather than waiting on network changes.
