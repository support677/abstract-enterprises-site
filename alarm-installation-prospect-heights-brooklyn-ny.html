The Neighborhood

Why Prospect Heights Properties Need Alarm Installation

Wikipedia describes Prospect Heights as relatively small, bounded by Flatbush Avenue on the west, Atlantic Avenue on the north, Washington Avenue on the east and Eastern Parkway, beginning at Grand Army Plaza, on the south. Inside those lines are rows of brownstone-style houses, some built as early as 1890, larger apartment buildings on blocks such as Lincoln Place and St. Johns Place between Underhill and Washington Avenues, a glass condominium tower by Richard Meier completed off Grand Army Plaza in 2008, and, in the north, the Pacific Park towers over the Vanderbilt rail yards. Much of the neighborhood is a city historic district, designated by the Landmarks Preservation Commission on June 23, 2009; the Prospect Heights Neighborhood Development Council counts about 850 buildings in it and notes that it was the largest district created in more than twenty years.

None of that is unusual for brownstone Brooklyn. What is unusual is what happens on the edges. The Barclays Center has been on the northwest corner since 2012, seating 17,732 for basketball and about 20,000 for concerts. In its first winter, residents near Dean Street described concert bass and vibration inside their apartments, packed streets after shows, and buses and black cars idling on Sixth Avenue behind the arena. Vanderbilt Avenue has been an Open Street since May 2020 and, in the city's 2022 plan, closes fully to cars from Friday through Sunday. Every Saturday of the year the Greenmarket runs from 8 a.m. to 4 p.m. at the northwest entrance of Prospect Park. On Labor Day, Grand Army Plaza and the parkway are closed for the parade, and Flatbush Avenue south of the plaza is closed overnight for J'Ouvert.

An alarm that was designed for an ordinary block can behave badly on those days. A shock sensor stuck to a parlor window facing the arena can trip on a concert night. A monitoring call that goes first to someone driving in from New Jersey is no use when the car cannot get past Flatbush Avenue. A restaurant that arms its main area at 1 a.m. while staff are still carrying tables in from the street will either false-alarm or be left unarmed. So the question we ask on every Prospect Heights survey is not only which doors and windows are reachable, but which days the street outside changes, and what the alarm, and the people on its call list, should do on each of them.

Burglary still happens here, of course. The record we checked includes a restaurant office entered through a wall cut on Carlton Avenue at Park Place in May 2015, a 3:30 a.m. front-door break-in at a restaurant at Vanderbilt and St. Marks Avenues in which an ATM holding about $13,000 was taken, a five-week run of smash-and-grab burglaries across sixteen Brooklyn neighborhoods, Prospect Heights among them, in August and September 2026, and a bicycle taken from a residential building near Underhill Avenue and Dean Street on an October afternoon in 2026. The precinct map also splits the neighborhood: east of Vanderbilt Avenue is the 77th Precinct, west of it the 78th, whose station house is at 65 Sixth Avenue.

Arming modes for the calendar

Beyond the usual away and stay settings, we set up a garden-level mode for arena and open-street evenings, a full night mode, and an away mode with a closure-day call list. Each is one button or one fob press.

Door and window contacts

Contacts on every street door, the door under the stoop, garden-level and parlor windows within reach of the sidewalk or stoop, rear windows over yards, and the roof hatch. Original wood sash gets small contacts mounted out of sight on the frame.

Glass-break and interior motion

Acoustic glass-break for storefront glass and large garden-level panes; motion sensors in halls and offices where a person has to pass. We test both before they go on a monitored zone.

Owner and rental areas

For a row house with a garden-level or top-floor rental, the panel is split into areas so each household arms its own floors with its own codes, and shared halls are covered the way the owner chooses.

Cellular plus internet reporting

Every system we install reports over cellular, and on blocks near the arena we add the home's internet as a second path so signals are not held up on a crowded night.

Restaurant and shop alarms

Zones that follow how a Vanderbilt or Washington Avenue restaurant closes: storage room first, kitchen heat detection, cellar, office, then the main room, with an 'armed' text to the owner.

Doorbell cameras that notify, not dispatch

A video doorbell at the stoop can tell your phone that someone is outside without calling the monitoring center, which matters on a block where crowds pass after events.

Takeovers of older systems

Many Prospect Heights houses still have a panel from a previous owner. We test the old wiring and contacts, keep what works, and replace the panel and communicator.

Repairs and false alarms

Battery replacements, sensors knocked out of line, zones that will not clear, and the false alarms that start on event nights. We read the panel's history first, so the fix addresses the cause.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Prospect Heights' Architecture

Most Prospect Heights jobs fall into one of six kinds of building. The opening list is different for each, so the survey starts by placing the house or apartment in one of them.

Italianate and neo-Grec row houses

The bulk of the historic district. Garden level with a door under the stoop, a parlor floor above the stoop, two or three floors above that, a cellar and a roof hatch. Party walls on both sides, a rear yard behind.

Row houses divided into units

Some brownstones now hold an owner's duplex or triplex and one or two rentals, or three or four condominium units. Each household needs its own area and codes; the street door and hall need a decision.

Prewar apartment buildings

Lincoln Place and St. Johns Place between Underhill and Washington Avenues and the large buildings on Eastern Parkway and Plaza Street. The apartment door is the perimeter, plus any fire-escape or low windows.

Glass towers

The 2008 Richard Meier tower at Grand Army Plaza and the Pacific Park buildings north of Dean Street. The building controls the lobby and garage; the alarm covers the unit, its terrace and anything stored in the building.

Buildings on the old grid

Between Underhill and Washington Avenues, some houses and lots still follow the 1830s street plan at about 45 degrees to today's streets, leaving wedge-shaped rooms and a few buildings with no street frontage at all.

Apartments over shops

On Vanderbilt, Washington and Flatbush Avenues, flats sit over restaurants and stores. Rear windows face yards and extensions; the stair door is shared with nobody or with the shop.

Method notes by building type

Row houses in the district. We plan nothing on the outside of the house. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's permit guidebook asks that security equipment not go on or in front of the primary facade unless there is no alternative, that it be small and discreet, and that visible conduit be kept to a few inches; interior work that needs no Department of Buildings permit and does not affect the exterior generally needs no LPC permit at all. Wireless contacts and sensors inside, a panel in a closet or the garden-level hall, and an indoor siren keep the job entirely inside those lines. Where original wood sash or interior shutters survive, contacts go on the frame where they cannot be seen with the window closed.

The garden level and the stoop. In a row house, the floor with the most exposure is the one half below the sidewalk. The door under the stoop, the garden-level front windows behind the areaway railing and the rear door to the yard all sit at standing height for anyone outside. On blocks near the arena and the open street, these are also the openings that crowds walk past late at night. They get contacts, the front windows usually get glass-break as well, and the hall behind the under-stoop door gets a motion sensor, because that hall is the one route every person entering at that level has to cross.

The parlor floor. Tall front windows beside the stoop are reachable from the stoop itself. They get contacts. We do not rely on shock or vibration sensors for windows that face the arena blocks or a busy avenue, because traffic, crowds and concert bass make glass move; residents described exactly that kind of vibration in 2013. On the quieter rear of the house, shock sensors on fixed panes can be useful after testing.

Upper floors and the roof. Rear windows on the second and third floors matter where an extension roof or a neighbor's yard structure puts them in reach; front upper windows rarely do. The roof hatch or bulkhead always gets a contact, because rooflines run continuously along a row.

Divided row houses. We set the panel up in areas, one per household, with a shared area for the street door and common hall if the owners want it covered. In a condominium conversion with three or four owners, the board decides who controls the shared door; each owner's unit stays private to that owner.

Row-house owners who are adding cameras at the same time usually want them under the stoop or inside the vestibule rather than on the facade. Our security camera installation in Prospect Heights page explains how we place them in the historic district.

The Prospect Heights Calendar

An Alarm Planned Around the Days the Street Changes

Most alarm advice assumes the street outside a house is the same every day. In Prospect Heights it is not, and the differences are predictable enough to plan for. We group them into four kinds of day: arena nights, Labor Day, open-street weekends and market Saturdays. A fifth, slower calendar belongs to construction, because the Pacific Park plan published in June 2026 still has seven buildings to come over the rail yard. Each kind of day changes something specific about the alarm: which sensors are trustworthy, which arming mode the household uses, who the monitoring center should call first, and how that person gets to the door.

Arena nights

The Barclays Center opened in 2012 with a sold-out Jay-Z concert and has been home to the Brooklyn Nets ever since; the New York Liberty play there too. Its address is 620 Atlantic Avenue, it has two truck and bus entrances with elevators down to a loading dock, and it has no dedicated parking lot. In January 2013 Brooklyn News Service spoke with residents who had lived through the arena's first months. They described concert bass heard inside their buildings, loud crowds after a reggae concert, buses, black cars and limousines idling or double-parked on Sixth Avenue behind the arena, and dozens of calls to 311 about night construction at Flatbush Avenue and Dean Street. One Dean Street building, they said, stood 247 feet, 6 inches from the arena.

Forest City Ratner later added a green roof to the arena. Brooklyn Paper quoted the company's spokeswoman acknowledging that it would 'probably do something about sound'; Wikipedia records that it was being installed in February 2015. We have no measurement of how much it changed what neighbors feel, and we do not claim one. What matters for an alarm is simpler: on the blocks closest to the arena, glass and window frames move on some nights for reasons that have nothing to do with a burglar.

That shapes three decisions. The first is sensor choice. A magnetic contact reports only that a door or window has opened, so vibration does not bother it; a shock sensor reports that the glass or frame has been struck or shaken, which is exactly what bass and traffic do. On the street side of houses near the arena, contacts come first, glass-break second, and shock sensors not at all unless they pass a test on an event night. The second decision is arming. People are awake and home in the early evening and asleep when the crowds pass, so we give the garden level its own area that can be armed alone before the doors open on an event night, and a night mode that arms the whole perimeter at bedtime. The third is outdoor notification. A video doorbell or a camera over the stoop can tell your phone that someone is on the steps; set to notify, not to sound the siren or call the monitoring center, it lets you see a fan sitting on your stoop at 11 p.m. without anyone being dispatched.

Labor Day

The West Indian American Day Parade marches down Eastern Parkway to Grand Army Plaza on Labor Day, and J'Ouvert runs before it in the early morning. NY1's guide for Monday, September 7, 2026 set out the closures. J'Ouvert closures began at 11:30 p.m. on Sunday and lasted until 11 a.m. on Monday, covering Flatbush Avenue from Grand Army Plaza to Empire Boulevard, Empire Boulevard from Flatbush to Nostrand Avenue and Nostrand Avenue from Empire Boulevard to Rutland Road; spectator access opened at 2 a.m. Parade closures started at 6 a.m. and grew around 10:30 a.m. Near Prospect Heights the whole Grand Army Plaza circle was closed, Flatbush Avenue was closed from the plaza to Caton Avenue, Washington Avenue from Sterling Place to Lincoln Road, and Eastern Parkway's main and service roads from Plaza Street East. Spectators entered through 13 screened entry points; bicycles, scooters, large bags and blankets were not allowed inside them.

Officials expected hundreds of thousands of people. For a house on the southern blocks of Prospect Heights, that means about eighteen hours in which a car cannot reach the front door, the nearest open crossing may be blocks away, and anyone coming on foot may have to go around barriers and checkpoints. A monitoring call that reaches the right person is only useful if that person can get to the house. We therefore write a Labor Day version of the call list for households on these blocks: the first names on it are people who can walk to the door, and the dispatch note tells responders which direction to approach from.

Households that host friends for the parade have the opposite problem: the front door opens dozens of times. For that day we suggest leaving the main area disarmed and the door chime on, while the rear of the house, the upper floors and the cellar stay armed as their own area. The garden-level rental, if there is one, arms on its own schedule. When the last guest leaves, one button returns the house to its normal night setting.

Open-street weekends on Vanderbilt Avenue

Vanderbilt Avenue joined the city's Open Streets program in 2020. NYC DOT's June 2022 presentation for Prospect Heights shows Vanderbilt fully closed to cars from Friday through Sunday, from Atlantic Avenue to Park Place, and Underhill Avenue with limited local access from Monday to Friday. In DOT's 2021 survey of 1,468 people, about two-thirds said they visited nearby restaurants or bars. Brooklyn Paper reported that the Borough President wants Vanderbilt to become a permanent public plaza; DOT had not confirmed that. The Prospect Heights Neighborhood Development Council organizes and maintains the open street.

For a household on Vanderbilt, the open street means foot traffic outside the garden-level windows on three evenings a week from spring into fall, and later nights than on other blocks. The same garden-level mode used on arena nights covers it. For a restaurant, it means tables, chairs, heaters and planters on the roadway, staff carrying them in at closing, and a closing time that changes with the weather. That changes the order in which a restaurant should arm, which is covered in the business section below.

Market Saturdays and museum days

GrowNYC lists the Grand Army Plaza Greenmarket as Saturday only, 8 a.m. to 4 p.m., year-round, at the northwest entrance of Prospect Park, where it has operated since 1989. The Central Library sits at Grand Army Plaza, and the Brooklyn Museum and Botanic Garden face the neighborhood across Eastern Parkway; on the Saturday before Labor Day 2026, the Panorama steel-pan competition at the museum opened its gates at 6 p.m. These days do not close streets in the way the parade does, but they shape household habits. Saturday morning is when many people on the western blocks leave together for the market and the park, often with top-floor windows open in summer. The alarm answer is to arm the perimeter with only the openings that are deliberately left open bypassed, and to have the panel say which ones those are as it arms.

The construction calendar

Pacific Park, first proposed in 2003 as Atlantic Yards, had nine of its fifteen planned buildings finished by December 2023, when the developer defaulted on its loans. A revised plan unveiled on June 29, 2026 adds seven buildings on six sites, platforms over the rail yard and towers up to 799 feet, with construction possible from 2028, according to 6sqft. For an alarm that is a long-range note: blocks next to the rail yard may again sit beside scaffolding and night work, as they did when residents called 311 about the B2 site in 2012, and when that happens we revisit sensor choices and any openings a neighbor's scaffold makes reachable.

The Old Street Grid

Wedge-Shaped Rooms and Buildings With No Street Frontage

Prospect Heights' grid hides an older one. In a 2011 article for Patch, Stephanie Keith traced it back to 1833, when Charles Hoyt and James E. Underhill sold 25-by-100-foot lots on a street plan laid at about 45 degrees to today's streets and oriented to the old Flatbush Turnpike, itself a widened Lenape path. In 1839 the city redrew the grid. Carlton Avenue and Dean Street opened in 1846, Flatbush Avenue moved to its present line in 1852 and the remaining streets followed in the 1850s. Where the new streets crossed the old property lines, the city kept many of the 1834 lot lines, so some buildings still sit at an angle to the street. Patch names 323 Prospect Place as one of them and describes three buildings inside the block that are reached only by an alley and a doorway through another building. The effect is most visible between Underhill and Washington Avenues.

Two alarm problems follow from that history. The first is geometry. A motion sensor is designed to look out from a corner across a room, and a wedge-shaped room has one acute corner that looks mostly at two walls. We put the sensor where it can see the longest stretch of floor that a person entering must cross, usually from the square corner toward the window, and walk-test it from the door to make sure.

The second is finding the building. Emergency responders go to an address. If the alarm account for a mid-block building gives only its address, the first person to arrive may stand in front of the wrong door. The monitoring account therefore carries a dispatch note that says how to reach the building, such as which front door or passage to use, and the call list includes someone who knows the route. The door from the passage gets its own contact, because it is the only way in. We also test the radio signal at the panel's spot: a building enclosed by others on every side can be a poor place for cellular reception, and an outdoor antenna or a second reporting path may be needed.

Buildings like these often share a stair or a passage with a front building. Where the shared door has a buzzer or an old intercom, its condition matters to everyone who uses it; our door buzzer repair in Prospect Heights page covers the work we do on those.

Apartments and Towers

Prewar Buildings on the Parkway and Glass Towers by the Arena

Not everyone in Prospect Heights lives in a row house. The north side of Eastern Parkway is lined with large prewar apartment buildings; Wikipedia compares the parkway between Grand Army Plaza and Washington Avenue to Manhattan's Museum Mile, and the New York Post has written about 135 Eastern Parkway as a grand prewar co-op. Lincoln Place and St. Johns Place between Underhill and Washington Avenues have larger multi-unit buildings, and a February 2023 fire injured four people in a large apartment building at Underhill Avenue and St. Johns Place. To the north, the Pacific Park towers and the 2008 Meier tower at Grand Army Plaza are newer, glass-fronted and run by managing agents.

In a prewar building, the apartment door is the perimeter. We fit a contact on it and on any window onto a fire escape, a courtyard ledge or the street at low floors, put a motion sensor in the hall, and connect smoke and carbon monoxide detection to the panel if the household wants it monitored. Fire-escape windows never get anything that makes them harder to open. On the parkway, a low apartment facing the parade route is worth arming as its own area on Labor Day even with people home, because the street outside is packed from early morning.

In a tower, the building controls the lobby, the elevators, the garage and often the apartment's smart lock. The alarm still has work to do: an entry-door contact, a terrace door if there is one, a motion sensor, sometimes a sensor on a storage locker or bike cage downstairs, and an app the household controls rather than the building. The practical questions are the management office's approval, cellular signal inside the unit and whether the building's Wi-Fi can be used as a second path.

Business Alarm Installation in Prospect Heights

Wikipedia notes that a commercial zone has grown along Vanderbilt Avenue and Washington Avenue since 2000, with new bars, restaurants and specialty shops. Flatbush Avenue on the west edge and Atlantic Avenue on the north carry larger stores and the arena's ground floor. Each corridor raises a different first question for a business alarm.

Vanderbilt Avenue

Restaurants, bars, cafés and shops, with the Open Street outside from Friday to Sunday. The first question is what is still outside at closing and where it goes overnight.

Washington Avenue

Restaurants and shops along the Crown Heights edge, inside the Labor Day closure from Sterling Place south. The first question is who opens and closes on the holiday, and how they get there.

Flatbush Avenue

Storefronts facing Park Slope on a bus route that never really sleeps. Large plate glass, deliveries at the curb, and a street that is busy at 3 a.m. as well as 3 p.m.

Atlantic Avenue and the arena blocks

Ground-floor retail in and around Pacific Park and near the transit hub. Crowds before and after events at the door, and building-managed back-of-house corridors.

Underhill Avenue

Shops and cafés on a street with limited local access on weekdays. Deliveries come to side doors and the curb, so the service door is usually the one that matters.

Eastern Parkway

Institutions and large apartment buildings rather than storefronts. Offices on lower floors and doctors' suites in residential buildings need small, quiet systems.

What a Prospect Heights restaurant alarm needs

The documented break-ins on these avenues do not all come through the front door. In May 2015, DNAinfo reported that a thief at a restaurant on Carlton Avenue at Park Place used a kitchen knife to cut through a wall into the owner's office some time between midnight and 9 a.m., then pried open a file cabinet and took $856 in cash. An alarm built only from door and window contacts would not have noticed. A motion sensor in the office that can see its walls, and in this case its ceiling, would have. We therefore treat any office or room where cash, a safe or records are kept as its own zone with interior motion, whatever the doors look like.

Brooklyn Paper reported a different pattern at a restaurant at Vanderbilt and St. Marks Avenues: on an August 11 (the item gives no year) the front door was broken at about 3:30 a.m. and an ATM holding about $13,000 was taken along with three iPads. And between August 3 and September 10, 2026, according to Hoodline, police were looking for two people who broke into businesses in sixteen Brooklyn neighborhoods, Prospect Heights among them, by throwing an object through the store window and taking cash and merchandise. Both are fast entries through the front. The parts that help are a contact on every door, an acoustic glass-break detector placed to hear the storefront glass, motion in the bar or register area, and a monitoring plan that calls a manager who can actually get there.

The open street adds one more thing. On a busy weekend night, tables, chairs, heaters and planters are on the roadway until closing, and staff carry them in while the last guests leave. If the alarm's main area is armed too early, someone trips it with a stack of chairs; if it is armed too late, the manager has gone home and nobody notices it was never set. We set up the closing in order: the outdoor equipment goes into a storage room with its own contact, the kitchen is shut down with its heat detector still live, the cellar is armed, then the office, then the main room with the closing manager's personal code. If the owner has not received an 'armed' text by an agreed time, the panel sends a 'not armed' reminder instead.

Rules a Prospect Heights business alarm has to respect

How long the siren may sound. Fifteen minutes, then it must cut itself off: that is the rule in § 24-238 of the city's Administrative Code for audible burglar alarms on buildings. We set the cutoff on every panel at installation. Above a restaurant, the tenants upstairs care about that limit at least as much as the owner does.

The installer's license. New York's Department of State licenses alarm installers under Article 6-D of the General Business Law and fingerprints the people they employ. A business owner is entitled to see that license before work starts. Ours, #12000287431, is on the quote.

Landmark rules for storefronts. Inside the historic district, work on a storefront facade, including new signs, cameras or equipment on the front, needs the Landmarks Preservation Commission's review. A business alarm normally lives entirely inside, so it does not. If you want an exterior siren or camera, we raise it before the quote.

Fire and smoke. A burglar alarm can carry smoke and heat detectors for the business, but it does not replace a fire alarm system the building or the code requires. For restaurants we use heat detectors near cooking areas, where smoke detectors would false-alarm.

Restaurants that want their cameras and their alarm on one app usually also need cabling for the recorder, the router and the point-of-sale terminals. We do that work too; see structured cabling installation in Prospect Heights.

Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Prospect Heights: Questions Answered

These are the questions Prospect Heights households and business owners ask us most, in the order the calendar raises them. Each answer is written for this neighborhood.

Event days and closures

On Labor Day morning the plaza, the parkway's service roads and Washington Avenue are closed from before dawn. If our alarm trips then, who can actually get to the house?

Only people who can walk there. For about eighteen hours on Labor Day the southern and western edges of the neighborhood are closed to cars, and checkpoints control foot traffic near the parade. Before the weekend, ask the monitoring center to put a neighbor or a nearby friend first on your call list, add a dispatch note saying which direction to approach from, and switch the list back afterwards. If no one on foot is available, the monitoring center can still dispatch police; your list just decides who meets them.

We host friends for the parade every year and the front door opens all day. How do we keep the alarm doing anything useful?

Split the house. Leave the main floor and the front door disarmed with the door chime on, and keep the rear windows, the upper floors and the cellar armed as a separate area, so a guest who wanders upstairs or out to the yard is noticed. If there is a garden-level rental, it stays on its own codes. When the last guest leaves, a single button or fob press returns everything to the normal night setting, and the panel tells you if any window is still open.

Can the alarm know when there is a game or a concert at the arena and change its settings by itself?

Not directly. Alarm panels and apps do not read the arena's event calendar. What most current systems can do is run a scene or a schedule, so you can set the garden-level mode to start at a fixed time on chosen days, or create a one-press 'event night' scene and a phone reminder for the dates you care about. We usually recommend the one-press scene, because event times change and a schedule that arms while people are still coming home causes false alarms.

After games and concerts, people sit on stoops along our block until late. Can we be told about someone on our steps without the police being called?

Yes. A video doorbell or a small camera over the stoop can send a notification and a short clip to your phone when it detects a person, while the alarm itself stays out of it. We set these devices to notify only: no siren and no monitoring call. That way you can see who is outside, speak through the doorbell if you want, and keep monitored dispatch for an actual opening of a door or window.

Restaurants and shops

Our tables, heaters and planters stay out on the Vanderbilt Avenue roadway until closing. Can the alarm protect equipment that spends the evening outside?

Not while it is outside. Nothing we would put on a public roadway could tell a passer-by from a thief, and a sensor on a heater would trip every time a guest moved it. What works is a place for the equipment overnight: a back storage room or cellar space with a door contact, and a closing order in which the furniture goes in before the main area is armed. A camera on the storage door, set to record, covers the time when it is open.

We live over a restaurant on Washington Avenue. If a fire starts downstairs after it closes, will our apartment alarm know?

Only if smoke or heat reaches a detector connected to your panel. Your system covers your apartment; it has no sensors in the restaurant. A monitored smoke detector in your hall and a heat detector at the top of the stair, if the stair is yours, are the parts that can warn you and call the fire department. Ask the landlord what the restaurant's own fire protection is, and keep the battery smoke alarms the city requires in every unit as well.

Row houses and the old grid

Our back room is wedge-shaped because the house follows an old lot line. Where does a motion sensor go in a room with no square corner?

Usually in the square corner, if there is one, looking along the room's longest diagonal toward the window or the door a person would use. The acute corner is the worst spot because it looks mostly at two walls. We place the sensor, then walk the room from each opening while the panel is in test mode, and move it if any path is missed. A wedge room sometimes needs a contact on its window instead, if no sensor position sees the whole floor.

Our building sits inside the block and is reached through a passage from the street. How would a responder find it?

From a dispatch note on your monitoring account. The note gives the street address of the front building or passage and a plain description of the route, so that police or firefighters do not stop at the wrong door. We also put someone who knows the route near the top of your call list, fit a contact on the passage door, and test the radio signal where the panel will hang, because buildings enclosed on all sides can have weak cellular reception.

We are replacing our front windows with wood replicas approved by the Landmarks Commission. Should the alarm go in before or after?

After, or at least the window contacts should. A contact is aligned to the sash and frame it sits on, and new windows almost always move that line. If you need protection during the work, we can install the panel, the door contacts and interior motion first, then add the window contacts once the new sash is in and has settled. Both visits can go on one written quote.

Our 1890s house still has its original iron areaway gate. Can the gate be on the alarm without drilling the ironwork?

Often, but we rarely recommend it as a monitored zone. A gate swings in the wind and is opened by delivery people and neighbors, so it makes a poor trigger for a siren. If you want to know when it opens, a small wireless contact can be clamped or banded to a plain bar and its magnet to the post without drilling, set to chime or notify your phone. The doors and windows behind the gate are where the monitored protection belongs.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Prospect Heights

We could not open Google's AI Overview or the People Also Ask panels from our research environment, so nothing below quotes them word for word. The claims in quotation marks are the common generic statements we hear repeated about alarm installation, and the answers are our correction for Prospect Heights.

"An alarm system works the same way on any street."

This is the assumption behind most generic advice, and it is the one Prospect Heights most clearly breaks. AI summaries of alarm installation typically recommend a contact on each entry door, a motion sensor in the main room and an outdoor siren, and they treat the street as a constant. Here the street changes several times a week, and a few times a year it closes altogether.

On arena nights crowds pass the garden-level windows late. On open-street weekends Vanderbilt Avenue fills with diners until closing. On Labor Day the plaza, the parkway's service roads and Washington Avenue from Sterling Place south are closed for most of a day. An alarm that ignores these patterns either false-alarms on them or protects badly through them.

The correction is not more equipment. It is a few arming modes set up for the days that recur, a call list that accounts for closures, and sensor choices that suit the vibration and noise of the busiest blocks.

"Shock sensors on windows give you early warning."

Shock and vibration sensors do react before glass breaks, and that is useful on a quiet rear window. The trouble is that they react to anything that shakes the glass. Residents near the arena described bass and vibration in their buildings in its first months, and Flatbush Avenue's traffic shakes storefront glass all day.

A sensor that trips on a concert, a bus or a crowd gets bypassed by a frustrated household within weeks, and then it protects nothing. False alarms also cost money and goodwill with the monitoring center.

On the street side of houses near the arena and on the avenues, we use contacts first and acoustic glass-break second. Shock sensors go only where they pass a test on an actual event night.

"Put the outdoor siren where the neighbors will hear it."

Many AI answers recommend an exterior siren and strobe as a deterrent. In much of Prospect Heights, that advice runs into the historic district. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's guidebook asks that security equipment not go on or in front of the primary facade unless there is no alternative, and that whatever is visible be small and discreet.

There is also a practical point. On a street where crowds are loud after events, an outdoor siren is easy to ignore, and it is limited by city law to fifteen minutes of sound anyway.

Our default in the district is an indoor siren, which is loud enough to make an intruder leave and needs no exterior work. If an owner wants an outdoor sounder, we discuss a discreet location and the LPC review before quoting it.

"The monitoring center will send help, so your call list doesn't matter much."

Monitoring centers do dispatch police when an alarm is not cancelled, but they usually call the people on your list first to verify. Who those people are, and whether they can reach the house, decides what happens next.

In Prospect Heights, the answer changes with the day. On Labor Day a contact who drives in cannot get near the house. On an arena night, parking near the arena blocks disappears. A call list written once and never revisited can send the monitoring center's first call to the one person who cannot help.

We ask households to rank their contacts by who can arrive on foot, write a closure-day version of the list for the weekend that needs it, and add a dispatch note for buildings that are hard to find from the street.

"A building with a doorman or a managed lobby doesn't need an apartment alarm."

Lobbies, cameras and staff reduce some risks, and the newer towers near Grand Army Plaza and the arena have all three. But the building's systems protect the building, not your unit, and they are run by people who also let in couriers, contractors and staff.

Theft also happens inside shared spaces: in October 2026 a bicycle was reported taken from inside a residential building near Underhill Avenue and Dean Street on a weekday afternoon.

An apartment alarm covers the one door that is yours, a terrace if you have one and anything you store in the building, and it reports to you. It is small and inexpensive in a tower, because the perimeter is short.

"Alarm installation costs $1,000 to $2,000, wherever you live."

National averages from sites like Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr are reasonable starting points, but they mix very different jobs: a kit installed in a small apartment and a wired system in a four-story house are not the same work.

Our Brooklyn list, which Prospect Heights uses unchanged, starts at $600 for a basic wireless system and typically runs $600 to $1,200 for an apartment, starts at $2,500 for complete home protection, and runs $2,000 to $6,000 for hardwired full coverage of a larger house. "Anyone handy can install an alarm."

Self-install kits are designed to be put up by their owners, and many people do it well. Installing or servicing alarms for other people is different: in New York State it requires a Department of State license under Article 6-D of the General Business Law, and the license holder's employees must be fingerprinted.

Do the sensors show on original woodwork in a landmarked house?
+

Very little. Wireless contacts are small and go on the frame's inner edge, where they are hidden when the window or door is closed. +

The previous owners left an alarm company's sign in the window and stickers on the glass. Should we keep them up?
+

Only if the system is real and monitored. A sign for a company that no longer serves the house tells anyone who looks it up that nobody is watching. Take them down, or replace them once the new system is monitored; a sticker inside the glass needs no approval in the historic district.
