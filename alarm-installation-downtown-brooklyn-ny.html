The Neighborhood

Why Downtown Brooklyn Properties Need Alarm Installation

Downtown Brooklyn is small on a map and dense on the ground. Wikipedia’s article gives it about 0.433 square miles and 23,493 residents in the 2020 census, and calls it the city’s third-largest central business district, after Midtown and Lower Manhattan. It sits in Brooklyn Community District 2, ZIP code 11201 north of DeKalb Avenue and 11217 south of it, and it is patrolled by two precincts, the 84th and the 88th. The NYPD’s own pages put the 84th at 301 Gold Street, naming Brooklyn Heights, Boerum Hill and Vinegar Hill in its description, and the 88th at 298 Classon Avenue, naming Clinton Hill, Fort Greene Park and Commodore Barry Park. Six subway stations ring the district, from Jay Street–MetroTech and Court Street–Borough Hall to DeKalb Avenue, Hoyt–Schermerhorn, Nevins Street and Atlantic Avenue–Barclays Center.

The rezoning of 2004 is what makes this district different from the brownstone neighborhoods around it. The plan expected new office towers. According to a 2016 report by the Brooklyn Borough President’s office, summarized by Politics NY, it projected about 4.6 million square feet of office space and roughly 1,000 apartments by 2014; about 1.3 million square feet of offices appeared, while more than 11,000 housing units were built or in development. The Architect’s Newspaper, writing about the new skyline that year, quoted an architect at Kohn Pedersen Fox saying the rezoning had so far produced only residential towers, and noted that buildings in Brooklyn had for about eighty years stayed below the 1 Hanson Place tower. The Brooklyner (51 stories, 2009) broke that line; 388 Bridge Street (53 stories, 2014) followed. In 2025, The Real Deal, citing a Downtown Brooklyn Partnership report, counted 26,853 units built since 2004 and more than 3,700 finished in the first six months of that year alone.

The tallest of them is the Brooklyn Tower at 9 DeKalb Avenue, built beside the landmarked Dime Savings Bank. Wikipedia lists it at 1,035 feet to the roof, with roughly 425 rentals and about 150 condominiums and about 120,000 square feet of amenity space, including pools on the old bank’s roof and lounges high in the tower. Around the corner, City Point rose on the site of the old Albee Square Mall: three towers over a retail podium that holds Target, Trader Joe’s, Primark, an Alamo Drafthouse cinema and the DeKalb Market Hall food court. A 2026 renters’ guide from 6sqft describes the same pattern across the district: keyless entry at 300 Ashland Place, a 24/7 doorman at One Boerum Place, an attended lobby at 505 State Street, co-working rooms, pools and roof decks almost everywhere.

For an alarm installer, those towers change the question. In a brownstone the worry is a rear window or a fire escape. In a rental tower forty floors up, the windows are rarely the way in. The front door is, along with the people who have legitimate reasons to come through it: building staff, delivery couriers, movers, a leasing agent showing the unit next door, contractors still finishing a floor upstairs. Terraces on setback floors and private outdoor space beside shared amenity decks add a second opening some apartments have and most do not. And the building, not the tenant, controls the lock, the elevators, the intercom and often the internet. An apartment alarm here is designed to work inside those systems without touching them.

The shops have a different history. The Fulton Mall section of Fulton Street runs from Adams Street to Flatbush Avenue and, according to Wikipedia, holds about 230 stores on a street where only buses, delivery trucks and emergency vehicles may drive. The Brooklyn Daily Eagle’s 2013 account traced it from department-store days (Abraham & Straus, Martin’s, Mays) through the merchants who formed the Fulton Mall Improvement Association in the 1970s to fight vacancies and street crime, to the electronics and jewelry shops of the 1990s and the national chains that arrived after the rezoning. Recent incidents show what a store alarm here is up against. In February 2025, National Jeweler reported that a man stayed inside the Fulton Street Macy’s after it closed, filled suitcases with about $1.5 million in jewelry, and abandoned them when an employee followed him out of the Hoyt Street exit. In March 2024, Brooklyn Paper reported two armed men robbing Contessa Jewelry on Fulton Street of about $91,200 in jewelry; one was arrested that evening and the jewelry was recovered. And in 2007, News 12 reported burglars breaking into a jewelry store on the mall through the roof.

Three of those four lessons are about timing and route rather than force: a thief who is already inside when the doors are locked, a robbery during business hours, a roof instead of the gate. None of this makes Downtown Brooklyn unusual among busy shopping districts. It means the useful alarm features here are specific ones, such as interior protection that is live the moment a store closes, silent duress signals, roof and rear-door coverage on older low buildings, and, in the towers, an entry-door design that tells household members from building staff. Many residents who call about an alarm also ask about cameras, which are covered on our Downtown Brooklyn security camera installation page; both can be done in one visit.

Same-Day Alarm Repair in Downtown Brooklyn: A Keypad Showing Trouble, a Store That Won’t Arm, an Alarm That Stopped Reporting

Plenty of calls from the district are about a system already on the wall. A condo owner moves in and finds a keypad left by the developer’s vendor that no one has a code for. A store’s panel lost its connection when the landlord changed the building network. A tenant’s wireless door sensor started reporting low battery the week the lease was renewed. The faults repeat. A steady beep is usually a weak battery in a sensor or in the panel’s standby battery, or a trouble condition such as lost power. A message that the panel cannot communicate usually means the cellular radio is an outdated model or the internet path disappeared with a router swap. A store alarm that will not arm usually has one zone showing open: a back door that no longer closes fully, a gate contact knocked out of line by a delivery cart, a motion detector blocked by new stock.

Call (347) 934-8335, read us the brand on the keypad and what the screen says, and tell us whether the building’s management controls any part of it. Take the first arrival window offered. If the siren is sounding and nobody has a code, New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires an audible burglar alarm on a building to be capable of shutting off, and to shut off, within fifteen minutes of being set off; one that keeps going is a same-day job. If a panel is too old to repair sensibly, we tell you before any billable work begins.

What We Install

Alarm Installation Services in Downtown Brooklyn

These are priced as separate lines, but most jobs here combine a few. A renter in a tower may need the first two. A condo owner with a terrace needs three or four. A phone store on the Fulton Mall needs a completely different set.

Entry-door alarms for tower apartments

A contact on the apartment door, a motion detector covering the hall behind it, a keypad or fob inside, all wireless and mounted without touching the building’s lock, frame hardware or corridor. Built to tell household members from building staff by code, schedule and app log.

Terrace and balcony doors

Contacts sized for sliding and swing doors on setback terraces and balconies, plus glass-break coverage where a terrace sits beside a shared roof deck or a neighbor’s outdoor space.

Condo systems

Owners can choose more: hardwired contacts while walls are open during a renovation, water sensors under sinks and washers, temperature alerts for an apartment left empty, and integration with lights or a smart-home hub.

Storefront alarms

Gate contacts, rear and roof-hatch contacts, interior motion that becomes live the moment the store is armed, glass-break tuned to the glass actually installed, and opening and closing reports for owners who are not on site.

Duress and hold-up signals

Silent buttons under counters, wireless pendants for staff, and a duress code that disarms normally while sending a silent emergency signal, for jewelry, phone and cash-heavy businesses.

Stalls and counters in larger buildings

Lockable cabinets, counter shutters and stock cages on their own sensors, with motion coverage limited to the stall, for vendors in a food hall or retail podium where the landlord controls the outer doors.

Office suites

Suite doors, server or records rooms, after-hours motion, personal codes for each employee and the cleaning contractor, and a panel that reports by cellular so the landlord’s network is not a weak link.

Takeovers and developer-installed systems

Panels left by a developer’s vendor, a previous tenant or a former store operator, tested device by device, given new codes and a modern communicator, or replaced when that is cheaper.

Monitoring, video and service

Self-monitoring through the app or central station monitoring without a long contract, cameras tied to alarm zones for verification, and repairs on other installers’ equipment.

Tower residents often want the alarm and the building’s door system to work side by side. Where a building owner or board is upgrading the lobby entry or the apartment intercoms, that work is on our Downtown Brooklyn intercom installation page; a tenant’s alarm stays a separate, independent system.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Downtown Brooklyn’s Architecture

Few Brooklyn neighborhoods put so many building types on so few blocks. These are the ones we plan for, with the facts we could check about each.

Glass rental towers built after 2004

Forty to seventy-plus stories, curtain-wall glass, keyless or fob entry, staffed or attended lobbies, and amenity floors with gyms, lounges and roof decks. Tenants rent, so every device must come out cleanly; management controls the door hardware, the intercom and often the Wi-Fi.

Condominium towers and mixed buildings

Some towers hold both rentals and condos; Wikipedia counts about 425 rentals and about 150 condominiums in the Brooklyn Tower. Condo owners can wire, add water and temperature sensors and integrate lighting, within the board’s alteration rules.

Towers over a retail podium

City Point stacks three towers on a podium of shops, a cinema and a basement food hall. Retail tenants control their own space but not the building’s doors, loading areas or after-hours security.

Older commercial buildings on the mall

Low brick and stone buildings from the department-store era line Fulton Street between the towers, many with storefronts below and storage, offices or apartments above, and roofs lower than the buildings next door.

Landmarks converted to apartments

The 1893 Offerman Building, a Romanesque Revival landmark on Fulton Street, was converted into 121 apartments by 2017, according to Wikipedia. Conversions mix big old window openings, new interior walls and modern building systems.

Office campus and college buildings

MetroTech, renamed Brooklyn Commons, covers a 16-acre superblock between Flatbush Avenue Extension and Jay Street, with NYU’s engineering campus and the FDNY headquarters among its tenants. Suites inside sit behind the landlord’s lobby and card readers.

How the install changes from one building type to the next

Rental towers: everything wireless, mounted on removable bases or a single small screw into drywall, never into the door frame, the building’s lock or the corridor. The panel or hub stays inside the apartment away from the door, on a shelf if the lease forbids wall fixings. The communicator is cellular first, because building-managed Wi-Fi often blocks or isolates devices. Programming does the heavy lifting: personal codes, a log of every arm and disarm, and alerts tuned so a scheduled visit by building staff is not mistaken for a break-in.

Condos: the same core, plus the choices ownership allows. During a renovation, contacts can be recessed into door frames and a keypad wired into a wall box. Water sensors go under the kitchen sink, the washer, the dishwasher and any heating or cooling unit, because a leak in a tower travels down through other people’s ceilings. Terrace doors get contacts rated for the door type, and a glass-break detector covers large fixed panes facing a terrace or deck.

Mall storefronts: the gate gets a contact that registers it lifting, not just reaching the floor; the glass behind it gets glass-break detection matched to whether it is tempered, laminated or film-coated; the rear door, any roof hatch and any basement stair get contacts; and interior motion detectors cover the sales floor so the system notices someone who never opened an outer door. Duress buttons and a duress code go where staff stand.

Podium shops and stalls: zones stop at the tenant’s boundary. Motion detectors are aimed so they do not see the public corridor, cabinets and shutters carry their own contacts, and the call list is set up with the landlord’s security desk in mind so an alarm reaches someone who can actually open the outer doors.

Offices and campus buildings: the suite door, the rooms that matter (server, records, a stock cage), and schedules that match how people really work, including cleaners and late staff, with the landlord’s card-access system left untouched.

Tower Apartments

Rental and Condo Towers Since the Rezoning: What an Apartment Alarm Has to Fit Around

Ask most people how a burglar reaches an apartment on the thirty-second floor and they shrug. That is the right instinct about the windows and the wrong one about the door. In a high-rise the realistic risks are a person who gets into the building behind a resident or a courier, a key or code that more people know than the tenant realizes, staff or contractors with legitimate access, and, in apartments that have them, terraces that someone can step onto from a shared deck or a neighboring outdoor space. The alarm is built around the entry door and the few rooms that matter, not around a window count.

The building itself is the second consideration. Most of the towers built since 2004 run their own entry systems: fob or phone readers at the street door and the elevators, apartment locks that management can program, intercoms that ring the tenant’s phone, and building-wide Wi-Fi. A tenant cannot, and should not, wire an alarm into any of that. What the alarm can do is sit alongside it. A contact on the inside face of the apartment door reports every opening regardless of how the lock was released. The app keeps its own record of who armed and disarmed, so if the lock log and the alarm log disagree, you know to ask management a question.

Mail and package security is the complaint that shows up most in resident conversations online. One commenter on r/nyc, in a thread about mail theft, wrote that it was “happening non stop” in the building near Jay Street–MetroTech where they had lived. An apartment alarm cannot guard a building mailroom or a self-serve package room; those belong to management and to the building’s cameras and access control. What it can do is protect what reaches your door: an outdoor-rated or indoor camera aimed at the threshold where couriers drop parcels, linked to the alarm app so a delivery and a pickup both show up on the same timeline. We are honest about that limit at the visit.

The towers also change who is in the apartment. Many tenants share with roommates, travel for work, or move every year or two. Building staff enter with notice for repairs and inspections, and in the first months after a building opens, contractors may still be finishing amenity floors and empty units. Each of those calls for programming rather than more hardware: separate codes that can be deleted the day someone moves out, a temporary code that works only during an agreed repair window, an alert when the door opens while the system is set to Away, and a monitoring call list that knows whether to phone you first or dispatch first.

Condo owners have more choices, and one extra worry. Wikipedia notes that the Brooklyn Tower received its permanent certificate of occupancy in April 2025, years after residents moved in; new buildings routinely open floor by floor while work continues elsewhere. During that period an owner may want contacts on every interior closet that holds valuables and a motion detector in the living room, armed whenever they are out. Later, water and temperature sensors matter as much as burglary sensors, because in a tower one burst supply line can damage several apartments below.

Fulton Mall Storefronts

Fulton Mall Storefronts: The Gate, the Glass and the Hours After Closing

A store on the mall faces a street that is busy from morning to night and, in places, nearly empty an hour after closing. Most stores pull down a rolling gate over the glass. That gate has a legal history worth knowing. New York City’s 2009 law required many businesses to switch to roll-down gates at least 70 percent see-through by July 1, 2026. NY1 reported on July 2, 2026, that the City Council had unanimously voted to repeal that deadline so that a compliant gate is needed only when a business replaces its gate for other reasons, with the bill then heading to the mayor; the article said permit requirements were unchanged. We do not know the final outcome at the time of writing, so check with the Department of Buildings before replacing a gate. Either way, a see-through gate means anyone can look through the glass at night, and the alarm should assume they will.

The gate is the obvious opening. The February 2025 Macy’s case shows the less obvious one. The thief did not break in; he stayed in. The protection that catches that is interior motion coverage that goes live the moment the store is armed, combined with a closing routine that sweeps the fitting rooms, stockroom and restrooms before arming. For larger stores, an exit delay short enough that staff must leave together, and a camera covering the exit door, close the gap between “we locked up” and “we know nobody is inside.”

The March 2024 robbery at a Fulton Street jewelry store is a reminder that an alarm is not only about nights. Two armed men walked in during business hours. No sensor stops that, and staff should never be asked to resist. What an alarm adds is a silent way to call for help without provoking anyone: a fixed button under the counter, a pendant on a lanyard, or a duress code that seems to disarm the system normally while sending a silent emergency signal to the monitoring station. Those signals go to a central station, not just to a phone app, because the person who would read the app may be the one in danger.

Roofs and rears matter on the older, lower buildings. News 12 reported in 2007 that burglars entered a jewelry store on the mall through the roof. Many Fulton Street storefronts sit in two- and three-story buildings with higher towers beside them, so a roof hatch, a skylight or a top-floor window can be reached from next door. Those get contacts, and the ceiling of the top floor can get a motion detector aimed upward toward a hatch. The rear door to a service alley or shared corridor gets a heavy-duty contact and, where deliveries arrive before opening, its own partition so a driver can use it without the whole store being disarmed.

Finally, the mall is a pedestrian street. Wikipedia notes that only buses, delivery and commercial vehicles and emergency vehicles may enter. That shapes deliveries, which come early and through rear doors where buildings have them, and it shapes service: our technicians plan to park on side streets and carry equipment, and we schedule installation before opening or after closing so the store keeps trading.

Inside Bigger Buildings

Podium Shops, Food Halls, Office Suites and Campus-Edge Studios

A growing share of Downtown Brooklyn businesses operate inside a building someone else runs. City Point’s podium holds big-box stores, a cinema and the DeKalb Market Hall, which Wikipedia describes as a 27,000-square-foot food court with about 40 vendors in the basement of City Point II. Brooklyn Commons (MetroTech) has office towers sitting around a 3.5-acre privately owned park, with lobbies, card readers and guards run by the landlord. The colleges, including NYU Tandon, City Tech, LIU Brooklyn, St. Francis College and Brooklyn Law School, rent and own space across the district, and the Brooklyn Academy of Music anchors the Fort Greene side, with smaller galleries and nonprofits around it.

For a tenant in one of these buildings, the alarm question is narrower and more useful: what do you control, and who can respond when it trips at 2 a.m.? A food-hall vendor may control a counter, a cabinet, a walk-in cooler and a register, but no door. A store in a podium controls its own entry and stockroom but not the corridor or loading dock. An office suite controls its door and interior rooms but not the elevator lobby. We design each system to protect only what the tenant controls, and we build the call list with the building’s security desk in mind so that a monitoring station knows who can open the outer doors for police at night.

Office systems add schedules and people. Cleaning contractors, IT staff, late-working associates and early deliveries all need access at specific times. Personal codes for each person, schedules that refuse a code outside its hours, and a report each morning of who opened and when do more for a suite than another motion detector. The communicator should not depend on the landlord’s network: if the building changes providers or segments its Wi-Fi, a cellular path keeps the alarm reporting.

Studios, galleries and nonprofits on the Fort Greene edge have their own pattern: irregular hours, events, borrowed or consigned work, and sometimes a lender or insurer asking for proof of an alarm. For those we can provide a written description of the system and a monitoring certificate, set event schedules, and add contacts to individual display cases or storage racks.

Before the Visit

Four Things to Gather Before a Downtown Brooklyn Alarm Visit

  • The building’s contractor rules. Many towers and office buildings need a certificate of insurance naming the owner and managing agent, a booked service elevator and a work window. Send us the requirements and we send the certificate before we arrive.
  • Your lease or condo alteration clause. Renters usually may install removable wireless devices; some leases ban wall fixings entirely, in which case the panel sits on a shelf and sensors use removable mounts. Condo boards may want a short description of the work.
  • Who should be called. Decide whether an alarm should reach you first, a monitoring station first, or the building’s front desk as well. For stores, list who can come to open the gate at night.
  • The network situation. Tell us whether the building provides managed Wi-Fi and whether you have your own internet. It decides which communicator the panel gets.

Common Fixes

Five Setups We Regularly Correct in Downtown Towers and Stores

1. A motion detector staring at a glass wall. In apartments with floor-to-ceiling glass, a detector aimed straight at the window sees hot glass and moving sunlight patches as changes in heat. We remount it on the window wall looking inward, along the room, where it watches the path a person must take.

2. A panel that only knows the building Wi-Fi. Self-installed systems often connect to whatever network is in the apartment. When management changes providers or passwords, the alarm quietly stops reporting. We add or enable cellular reporting and treat Wi-Fi as a backup.

3. A store that arms with an exit delay long enough to hide in. Some panels leave a minute or more between arming and the interior sensors going live, plenty of time for someone already inside to move to a stockroom. We shorten the delay, add a stockroom door contact and give closing staff a sweep order.

4. Panic buttons that go only to the owner’s phone. A hold-up button that sends a push notification to someone who may be the person being robbed, or asleep, protects no one. Panic and duress signals belong on a central station path with a dispatch instruction.

5. Old codes that never die. Former roommates, sublet tenants, ex-employees and a contractor from a renovation two years ago often still have working codes. We audit and delete them, and set up the app so each person has their own code and their own log entry.

What a Downtown Brooklyn store alarm needs that a home alarm doesn’t

A commercial panel supports more users, more zones and more partitions, sends opening and closing reports, and handles hold-up and duress signals that a residential app-based kit treats as an afterthought. For a store on the mall that usually means a gate zone, a glass zone, an interior motion zone that is live the moment the system arms, a rear-door zone that can be partitioned for deliveries, a stockroom zone, and a separate panic zone that always goes to a monitoring station. Cameras linked to the main zones let the station look before it dispatches.

Four rules that apply to apartments and stores alike

  • Siren cutoff. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building’s audible burglar alarm to be capable of terminating, and to terminate, within fifteen minutes of activation. We set every siren inside that limit, and shorter where apartments are close by.
  • Licensed installers. New York’s General Business Law Article 6-D requires anyone who installs or services security or fire alarm systems to hold a state license from the Department of State. Ask for the number; ours is #12000287431.
  • Exits stay usable. No alarm device or lock we fit may block an exit route, an exit sign, or any door the building’s fire plan depends on.
  • Gate changes need checking. If a store replaces its rolling gate, the gate rules and permit requirements described above apply to the gate; the alarm contacts are then refitted to the new gate.
Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Downtown Brooklyn: Questions Answered

These are questions specific to the towers, shops and offices of Downtown Brooklyn, grouped by building type. Short questions about cost, scheduling and options are in the FAQ further down.

Towers, terraces and building systems

Our terrace sits on a setback a few feet below the building’s shared roof deck. Can someone step down onto it, and how should the terrace door be protected?

Often, yes. Where a private terrace adjoins or sits just below a shared amenity deck, the only things between them may be a planter, a glass railing or a short drop, and anyone using the deck can reach it. That makes the terrace door the second real entry to the apartment, after the front door.

We put a contact on the terrace door matched to its type (sliding doors get a surface or track-mounted contact, swing doors a frame contact) and a glass-break detector covering any large fixed panes onto the terrace. If you leave the door open in good weather, a separate zone lets you arm the rest of the apartment while the terrace door is open, with an alert if it is still open when you set the system to Away.

Window washers on a gondola pass our floor-to-ceiling glass every few months. Will they set off the motion or glass-break detectors?

Not if the detectors are chosen and placed with that in mind. Passive infrared motion detectors respond to heat moving inside the room, and glass is a poor conductor of the infrared they read, so a person outside the window is very unlikely to trigger one; we still aim detectors along the glass wall rather than straight at it, so sunlight and heat from the glass itself do not reach the sensor directly.

Acoustic glass-break detectors listen for the specific sound pattern of glass breaking, not for tapping or a squeegee. We set their sensitivity for the size and type of pane, and building management usually announces facade cleaning days, so you can arm in Stay mode on those days if you are worried.

Our building’s fire system has a speaker or strobe inside the apartment. Could a burglar siren be confused with a building fire announcement?

We avoid that deliberately. A tenant’s burglar alarm is never connected to the building’s fire alarm system, and its siren is set to a tone and pattern clearly different from the building’s evacuation signal. We show every household member both sounds during handover.

When the building tests its fire system, your alarm does not react, because its sensors do not listen to the building’s devices. If you add smoke or carbon monoxide detectors to your own alarm, they report to you and, if you choose, a monitoring station, and they carry their own distinct sound.

Management programmed a keyless smart lock on our door. Can the alarm tell us when that lock is opened, and with whose code?

It can tell you every time the door opens, because a contact on the inside face of the door reports regardless of how the lock was released. What it usually cannot do is read which credential the building’s lock accepted, since that system belongs to management and is not shared with tenant devices.

In practice that is enough. The alarm records the opening, whether the system was armed, and whether someone then entered a valid alarm code or disarmed from the app. If the door opened while you were away and nobody in the household disarmed, you get an alert and can ask management for its lock log for that time.

The building gives us a storage cage in the basement. Can that cage be part of our apartment’s alarm forty floors up?

Sometimes, with a different approach. A wireless sensor in a basement cage will not reach a panel high in the tower through concrete floors, so the cage needs its own small cellular-connected sensor or a second hub that reports to the same app.

Before anything goes in, we check the building’s rules for storage areas, because many managers restrict devices there. Where they allow it, a battery-powered contact on the cage door and a small motion detector inside can send alerts to your phone alongside your apartment alarm.

We moved in while crews are still finishing floors above us. What should the alarm do during that period?

Assume more people are in the building than usual. Arm the system every time you leave, even briefly, and use Away mode with the motion detector live. Put any valuables you leave in a closet or cabinet with its own contact.

Set up an alert for any opening of the front door while armed, and keep the monitoring call list current with your own numbers. Once construction finishes, we can relax schedules and remove extra sensors you no longer want.

Fulton Mall stores

In February 2025 a man reportedly stayed inside the Fulton Street Macy’s after it closed. How does an alarm catch someone who is already inside when we arm it?

With interior protection that goes live the moment the system arms, and a closing routine that checks the hiding places first. Motion detectors covering the sales floor, stockroom corridor and back office will trip as soon as someone moves after the exit delay ends; contacts on stockroom and office doors catch someone who stays put until later.

We also shorten the exit delay so staff leave together, put a camera on the exit door, and give the closing manager a short checklist: fitting rooms, restrooms, stockroom, then arm. An alarm that is armed with someone still inside will usually trip within seconds of that person moving.

Our solid roll-down gate may need to be replaced with a see-through one. Will a new gate mean new alarm sensors?

Usually new gate contacts, rarely anything else. Gate contacts are mounted to suit the gate’s bottom bar and track, so a different gate often needs new brackets or a different contact. The panel, the glass-break detectors and the interior sensors stay as they are.

A see-through gate does change one thing: people can see into the store at night, so we recommend reviewing where stock, registers and display cases sit, and confirming the glass behind the gate is covered by glass-break detection. Book the alarm work for the same day the gate is installed so the store is never unprotected.

If staff are ever forced to open the store or disarm the alarm under threat, can they send a hidden alert?

Yes. A duress code disarms the system as normal, so nothing looks different to the person watching, while sending a silent emergency signal to the monitoring station. Each staff member can have their own duress code, so the station knows who sent it.

Duress codes only work with central station monitoring, and every employee needs to learn it during training. We pair it with a fixed button under the counter and, for staff who move around the floor, a wireless pendant, so help can be called without reaching the keypad.

Our phone store already has alarmed tethers on every display device. What does a burglar alarm add?

Tethers protect the demo phones during the day. They do not protect the stockroom, the safe, the gate or the glass at night, and they do not send a signal to anyone outside the store.

A store alarm covers the building openings and the stock behind the counter, reports to you and a monitoring station, adds duress buttons for staff, and can link to cameras. The two systems work side by side and never need to be connected.

Deliveries reach our Fulton Mall store before anyone opens. Can the driver leave stock in a back vestibule without the whole store being disarmed?

Yes, by partitioning the system. The rear vestibule or receiving area becomes its own area with its own code or a scheduled window, while the sales floor, stockroom and office stay armed. The driver’s code works only during the delivery window and only for that area.

The panel logs when the vestibule opened and closed, and a camera inside the vestibule records the drop. If the driver opens the door into the store itself, the main system trips as normal.

Food halls, galleries and spaces inside larger buildings

Our stall in a food hall has no door or walls of its own. Can it be alarmed at all?

Yes, but the alarm protects things rather than a room. Cabinets, a counter shutter, a cash drawer, a stock cage and a walk-in or reach-in cooler each get their own sensor. A motion detector can cover the inside of the stall if it can be aimed so it does not see the public walkway.

Because the building’s security controls the outer doors, we set up the call list so an alarm reaches you and the landlord’s security desk, and we add a temperature sensor on the cooler so you hear about a failure overnight rather than at opening.

A lender wants proof that our gallery near BAM has an alarm before it ships work for a show. What do you provide?

A written description of the installed system (devices, zones, what is monitored, how alarms are handled) and, if you take central station monitoring, the station’s monitoring certificate. We can also add contacts to individual display cases or storage racks holding loaned work.

Read the lender’s or insurer’s requirements carefully, because some ask for specific features such as monitored glass-break or a minimum response arrangement, and send them to us before the visit so the system matches what they ask for.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Downtown Brooklyn

Our build environment could not retrieve Google’s AI Overview for Downtown Brooklyn alarm searches, so the claims below are not quotations from it. They are common generalizations found in instant answers and on national cost and advice sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each is followed by what we see in Downtown Brooklyn towers, stores and offices.

“Apartments on high floors don’t really need alarms.”

The idea is that height protects you, because nobody climbs thirty stories. That is true of windows.

It ignores how high-rise apartments are actually entered: through the front door, by someone who got into the building, someone who has a code or a key, or someone with legitimate access. Terraces beside shared decks are a second route in some units.

A tower alarm is smaller than a house alarm, often a door contact, one or two motion detectors and perhaps a terrace contact, but it is not pointless. It is what tells you the door opened while you were away. In a building where dozens of people hold some kind of access, from porters to package couriers, that single fact is worth more than a window sensor on the fortieth floor ever could be.

“A smart lock and a video doorbell are basically an alarm system.”

Smart locks log who unlocked the door, and doorbells record who stood outside. Both are useful.

In many Downtown rental towers the lock belongs to management and the tenant cannot see its log, and a doorbell is often not allowed on a corridor door at all. Neither one notices someone who is already inside, a terrace door opening, or a store gate lifting at 3 a.m.

An alarm adds the parts they lack: sensors inside, a siren, a duress option, and a monitored path to someone who will act. The lock and the doorbell can still feed into the same app where the hardware allows it, so nobody has to choose one system over the other.

“New luxury buildings have security built in.”

New towers do have staffed or attended lobbies, fob readers, cameras and intercoms. 6sqft’s 2026 renters’ guide lists those features across many Downtown buildings.

Those systems protect the building and its common areas. They are not watching your apartment door, your terrace, your storage cage or your store, and the front desk is not notified when your door opens.

Building security and an apartment alarm complement each other. One guards the lobby; the other guards what is yours. When the two are planned together, the alarm’s monitoring notes can even tell responders how to reach the front desk and the floor.

“A heavy rolling gate is enough to secure a store overnight.”

A gate slows down a smash-and-grab through the glass, and many Fulton Mall stores rely on one.

It does nothing about someone who stayed inside after closing, entry from a roof or a rear door, or the hours when the gate is up. The cases we cite on this page involved each of those situations, not a forced gate.

The gate is one layer. Interior motion, rear and roof contacts and a duress signal for business hours are the others. With a see-through gate now the long-term direction of City rules, the layers behind it matter more, not less, because the inside of the store is visible all night.

“Glass-break sensors work the same on any window.”

Advice sites often treat glass-break detectors as universal: stick one on the wall and every window in the room is covered.

Acoustic detectors are listed for particular glass types and distances, and laminated or film-coated glass, common in new storefronts and towers, breaks with a different sound. Large single panes may be better served by a shock sensor on the glass itself.

We identify the glass first, then choose the detector, then test it with a simulator after installation. If a store later replaces its window with a different type of glass, the detector choice should be revisited at the same time.

“Alarm systems are for when the business is closed.”

Most alarm marketing pictures a dark store and a burglar at night, so the alarm is something to set at closing and forget.

In Downtown Brooklyn, a 2024 jewelry robbery happened during business hours, and a 2025 theft began with a man who stayed in a store after closing. Both scenarios start while staff are present.

A good store alarm has daytime functions: hold-up buttons, pendants, duress codes, a stockroom that stays armed while the floor is open, and cameras linked to the panel. Training matters as much as hardware here: staff who know the duress code and the closing sweep are part of the system.

“A home alarm costs a few hundred dollars plus a monthly fee.”

National cost sites quote ranges based on self-install kits and long monitoring contracts, and instant answers repeat them.

Our Brooklyn list for a professionally installed basic residential system starts at $600 with no long-term contract, and a commercial system starts at $1,500. A store with gate, glass, rear-door, interior and panic coverage costs more than a one-bedroom apartment, and the written figure comes after a visit.

The useful question is not the national average but what your specific apartment or store needs, which is why we price from the Brooklyn list after seeing it. Monitoring, if you want it, starts from $19.99 a month through our partners, with no long-term contract to sign.

Day to Day

Living With the System: Arming Habits for Tower Residents and Store Managers

An alarm earns its keep in ordinary weeks, not on the night something happens. These are the routines we set up at handover, adjusted to the way people in the district actually live and work.

In a tower apartment

  • Leaving for work: Away mode from the app or the fob as you walk to the elevator. The entry-door contact and the hall motion detector are both live; the terrace door, if closed, is too.
  • Going down to the gym or the roof deck: the same Away mode. It takes two seconds, and most of the time people are out of the apartment inside the building, not out of the building, which is exactly when an unlocked door matters.
  • At night: Night or Stay mode, which arms the door and terrace contacts but leaves the motion detector off so you can walk to the kitchen. A keypad by the bed lets you check status without getting up.
  • Scheduled building visits: when management gives notice of a repair or inspection, create a temporary code for that window, or simply watch the door-open alert arrive and confirm it matches the notice.
  • Travel: Away mode, water and temperature sensors on, and the monitoring call list checked before you leave, with a neighbor or friend who can be reached if the building calls.

In a store on the mall

  • Opening: the first employee disarms with a personal code; the panel logs who and when, and an alert goes to the owner if the store has not been opened by the scheduled time.
  • During the day: the stockroom and office stay armed as separate areas; staff carry pendants or know where the counter buttons are; the duress code is the one thing every employee is trained on in the first week.
  • Deliveries: the rear vestibule area is disarmed only for the delivery window, by the driver’s code or by a scheduled rule.
  • Closing: a short sweep of fitting rooms, restrooms and the stockroom, then arm. If nobody arms by the scheduled closing time, the owner gets a notice.
  • Staff changes: delete a departing employee’s code the same day from the app, without a service call.

None of this needs extra equipment. It is set up in the programming step of the installation and explained to every person with a code, which is why we ask at the visit who will be using the system and when.

Downtown Brooklyn Alarm Installation FAQ

What does a storefront alarm on the Fulton Mall cost on your Brooklyn list, and what pushes it higher?
+

A standard small-business alarm on our Brooklyn list runs $1,500 to $3,000: a commercial panel, door and gate contacts, motion and glass-break detectors, keypads and monitoring setup. Larger floors, a second level or basement stockroom, extra partitions for deliveries, several hold-up buttons or pendants, and cameras (which add $1,000 to $2,500) move a store toward the top of that range or beyond. Sales tax is added, and the written figure follows one visit.

Do glass-break detectors work on the laminated or film-coated glass used in newer storefronts and towers?
+

Some do and some do not. Each acoustic detector is rated by its maker for particular glass types and distances, and laminated or filmed glass breaks with a different sound from plain or tempered glass. We identify the glass before choosing a detector, use a model rated for it, and test it with a glass-break simulator after mounting. Where no acoustic model suits a large pane, a shock sensor fixed to the glass is the alternative.

Can the alarm dial 911 itself when it goes off?
+

We do not set panels to call 911 directly. A recorded call from a panel gives the dispatcher no way to ask questions, and the useful alternatives are better: either the alarm alerts you and the people on your list, and you decide whether to call 911, or a central station receives the signal, tries to verify it, and dispatches police with your address, floor and access notes. For duress and hold-up signals, central station monitoring is the only option we offer.

Does the alarm work with Apple Home, Google Home or Alexa?
+

Many current panels and their apps can link to Google Home and Alexa for status and arming by voice, and some support Apple Home; which ones depends on the panel brand and the monitoring platform. We tell you at the visit what the chosen panel supports. For security, disarming by voice is usually limited or requires a spoken PIN, and we recommend leaving it off.

Should an alarm be inspected every year, and what does a check cost if nothing is wrong?
+

A yearly check is sensible for stores and offices and worth doing every year or two in an apartment. The technician tests every sensor and siren, checks battery health and signal strength, confirms the monitoring account details and deletes stale codes. Faults in AESS-supplied parts are handled under the terms of the service agreement; otherwise a check is a service visit at $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum ($585), so many owners combine it with an upgrade or added devices.

What is two-way voice, and is it worth having on an apartment or store panel?
+

Two-way voice lets the monitoring station speak through the panel and listen after an alarm, so an operator can ask who is there and hear the answer. For a tenant who sets off the alarm by mistake, it can clear a false alarm in seconds. In a store it lets the station hear what is happening. It requires a panel with a speaker and microphone and central station monitoring; most of the self-contained panels we use in apartments have it.

What happens if the phone with our alarm app is lost or stolen?
+

The alarm keeps working; the app is just one way to control it. Log in to the alarm account from another device or call us, and the lost phone’s session can be signed out and its login removed. Every user should have their own login with a strong password and two-step verification where the platform offers it, and the keypad and key fobs still arm and disarm in the meantime.

Can the monitoring station tell police how to get past our lobby and up to our floor?
+

Yes. Monitoring accounts carry premises notes, and we fill them in with the building’s address and entrance, the front desk’s phone number, the floor and apartment or suite number, and any instructions about access after hours. The station passes those details on when it dispatches, which saves time in a building where responders would otherwise wait at a locked lobby.

Can a second keypad go by the bedroom so we can arm at night without walking to the front door?
+

Yes. A wireless keypad can sit on a nightstand or by the bedroom door, and the app and key fobs do the same job. In a long apartment it is the easiest way to arm in Stay mode at bedtime and to silence a false alarm without walking down the hall.

Will the alarm notice if someone covers, moves or opens a sensor?
+

Most of the devices we install have a tamper switch that reports when the cover is opened or the device is pulled off its base, and wireless devices report if they stop checking in. A motion detector that is deliberately covered while the system is disarmed is harder to catch, which is one reason we place detectors where visitors do not stand next to them and pair stores’ key zones with cameras.
