Why Nassau County Properties Need Video Wall Installation

Nassau is a county of houses and of businesses spread along roads rather than stacked in towers. Census figures for 2024 count 1,392,438 residents and 480,668 homes, and 79 percent of those homes are single-unit structures; 81 percent are owner-occupied. The commercial life of the county runs along its corridors: department stores and specialty shops around Roosevelt Field on Old Country Road, car dealerships and shops along Northern Boulevard through Great Neck and Manhasset’s Miracle Mile, office buildings in Uniondale and Garden City, and schools, gyms and houses of worship in every village. That is where video walls go here: into glass showrooms, mall storefronts, office-park lobbies, school gymnasiums, security desks and finished basements, not into the high-rise lobbies that dominate a page about Manhattan.

The second thing that makes Nassau different is who is in charge. The State describes the county as two cities, three towns, sixty-four incorporated villages and more than sixty unincorporated hamlets. A hamlet such as Elmont, Uniondale or Hicksville is governed by its town; a village such as Garden City, Mineola or Floral Park typically has its own building department, its own forms and often its own rules about what a business may display in its window. A wall that needs no filing at all in one place can need a permit, an electrical inspection and a sign review a mile away. Nobody can give a single Nassau answer to “do I need a permit?”; the honest answer always starts with the address.

Building Specialists

Video Wall Installation for Nassau County's Architecture

Nassau’s buildings are low and wide, and almost every video wall here goes onto one of six kinds of wall. Each one changes the mount, the cable route and who has to say yes.

Glass-front car showrooms

Tall glazing, polished floors and high ceilings. The issues are sunlight, reflections and reaching a high ceiling for cable, often from a lift. Walls go on the solid back or side walls, rarely on glass.

Mall and strip-center tenant spaces

Steel-stud demising walls and open or lay-in ceilings, all under a landlord’s alteration rules. The tenant’s space usually ends at the demising wall, so backing and cable routes have to stay inside it.

Office buildings and office parks

Lobbies with stone or panel cladding over steel studs, lay-in ceilings on the floors above, and a network closet per floor. Lobby walls sit on public paths, so depth and accessibility count.

Schools and gymnasiums

Concrete block walls, high open ceilings and hard use. Mounts go into block with the right anchors, high enough or guarded against balls, and cable runs in conduit where it is exposed.

Houses of worship

Sanctuaries with high walls, stained wood and stone, and congregations that dislike visible cables. Walls are placed for sightlines from the back pew and cable is routed to avoid historic finishes.

Single-family homes

Basements of poured concrete or block, upstairs walls of wood studs, and family rooms in Capes, ranches, split-levels and colonials. The wall type decides the mount and the anchors, and heat has to leave the room.

What each wall is made of, and what that means for the mount

Steel studs and drywall. This is the most common commercial wall in Nassau office buildings and tenant spaces. Drywall carries almost nothing on its own, and a steel stud is thin metal, so a heavy wall mount is fixed into blocking or a backing plate set between the studs, or into a frame that spreads the load across several studs. In a finished wall that means opening it, adding backing and patching; in a fit-out it means putting backing in before the drywall goes up, which is cheap if somebody remembers.

Concrete block. Schools, gyms, warehouses, the backs of many strip-center units and a good number of basements are block. Block holds well if the anchors go into solid sections or are designed for hollow cores; the wrong anchor in a hollow cell can pull out. We drill test holes, choose sleeve or adhesive anchors to suit, and keep away from mortar joints for the main fixings.

Poured concrete. Basement walls in many Nassau houses and the structure of some commercial buildings are poured concrete. It is strong but hard to run cable inside, so the usual answer is a furred-out wall or a recessed box set into a stud wall built in front of the concrete, with the cable coming from the ceiling.

Wood studs. Most upstairs walls in Nassau houses are wood studs at regular spacing. A video wall mount spans several studs, and the recessed box for power and signal goes between them. Older plaster on wood lath, found in some early houses, cracks if drilled carelessly, so we score and drill slowly.

Stone, tile and wood paneling. Lobbies and sanctuaries often have finishes that cannot be repaired invisibly. We fix through joints where possible, use a floor-standing frame where not, and agree any visible change with the owner in writing before drilling.

Nassau houses: where a home video wall usually goes

Most Nassau homes are single-family houses, and most home video walls end up in one of three places: a finished basement, a den or family room off the kitchen, or a converted garage or attic room. Basements are popular because the walls are long and the light is easy to control, but they are often poured concrete or block, and the ceiling is crowded with ducts and pipes. We check where the cable can come down, whether a furred wall is needed in front of the concrete, and how the room will be ventilated once a wall of panels is running for hours.

Upstairs, wood-stud walls make the mount simpler, but finished rooms mean patching and painting after the recessed box goes in. Houses with plaster walls need slower drilling. Split-level and raised-ranch layouts sometimes put the best wall on the far side of a staircase from the outlet, which is where an electrician’s new circuit or a relocated outlet comes in.

Because work on a house of up to three families is home improvement under county law, the homeowner should see the contractor’s county license details before signing, and the written contract should describe the work, the price and the payment schedule. We put the mount, the box positions and the equipment list on a simple drawing so the family knows exactly what will change in the room.

Compliance callouts for Nassau commercial walls

Local building and sign rules. The village, city or town where the business sits decides whether a filing is needed and whether a display visible from the street is a sign. There is no county-wide sign code to rely on. We ask the right office in writing when it matters and keep the answer with the job file.

Electrical work by a licensed electrician. New circuits, outlets and disconnects are done by an electrician licensed for that municipality and inspected where required. Electric signs, including LED signs, fall under their own article of the National Electrical Code, and inspectors look for listed equipment.

Accessibility. Under the 2010 ADA Standards, wall-mounted objects whose leading edge is between 27 and 80 inches above the floor may project no more than 4 inches into a circulation path. Lobby, corridor and store walls are designed to that limit or recessed.

Old equipment. Since January 1, 2015, New York State has banned putting covered electronic equipment in the trash. Old screens taken down for a new wall go to a recycler or a manufacturer’s take-back program, which is free for most small businesses, nonprofits and schools.

Insurance and landlord sign-off. Malls, office buildings and districts all ask for a certificate of insurance naming them before work starts, and most ask for drawings and hours. We prepare both with the design so they do not hold up the date.

Your Questions Answered

Video Wall Installation in Nassau County: Questions Answered

Below, the Nassau questions are sorted by the decision each one feeds: who approves the wall, how a store or showroom uses it, how offices, gyms and control rooms differ, and how to live with it for years. The answers lean on this county’s own governments, buildings and records.

Villages, Towns and Who Signs Off

Most of what makes a Nassau job different happens before the first panel is unboxed. These are the questions owners and managers ask once they realise the county is many governments, not one.

How do I find out whether my building answers to a village or to the town?

Start with the tax bill or the property record: it names the town and, if there is one, the incorporated village. Nassau County is made up of two cities, three towns and sixty-four incorporated villages, with more than sixty unincorporated hamlets in between, according to the State’s county page. A hamlet such as Uniondale, Elmont or Hicksville has no building department of its own; work there goes to the Town of Hempstead or the Town of Oyster Bay. A village such as Garden City, Mineola or Great Neck Plaza typically runs its own building department with its own forms and inspectors.

For a video wall the answer matters because two different offices can be involved: the building department for any structural or electrical work, and, if the screen is visible from the street, whoever enforces the sign rules. Glen Cove and Long Beach are cities and are separate again. The county’s own building-and-zoning page simply lists the local offices and sends you to them, which tells you everything about where the decisions are made.

We ask for the address before we quote and look up the jurisdiction ourselves. If the job needs a filing, the written proposal says which office and who files it, so the owner is not discovering it the week the panels arrive.

Does Nassau County itself issue a permit for a video wall?

Generally not. The county’s building-and-zoning page does not issue building permits for local projects; it lists the city, town and village offices and sends people to them. Building, electrical and sign questions for a wall are answered by whichever of those offices covers the address.

Where the county does come in is licensing. Its Department of Consumer Affairs licenses home-improvement contractors under Chapter 21 of the Administrative Code, which matters when the wall goes into a house. County agencies are also customers in their own right, as the police department’s Intelligence Center wall shows, but that is a buyer’s role, not a permitting one.

So the useful first call is never “the county.” It is the village, city or town office for the address, and, for a home, a check of the contractor’s county license.

Is a living-room video wall covered by Nassau’s home-improvement license law?

Very likely, yes. Nassau’s home-improvement law, Local Law 6 of 1970 in Chapter 21 of the county Administrative Code, defines home improvement as repair, remodeling, alteration or addition to a building used as a private residence for not more than three families, and the county’s Consumer Affairs department licenses contractors who do that work. Cutting into a wall, adding backing and building a recessed box in a family room is exactly that kind of work.

The same law exempts people licensed under another law, such as electricians, when they work only within their own trade, and it does not reach commercial buildings at all. So a showroom wall in Westbury is outside it, while a basement wall in Massapequa is inside it. Licenses issued under the law run for two years.

Our advice to a homeowner is simple: ask any installer, us included, for the license number the county issued, and look it up on the county’s license search before you sign. Our NYS license #12000287431 covers security and fire alarm installation statewide; it is not a substitute for the county’s home-improvement license on residential work.

Our building has four apartments — does the county home-improvement law still apply?

Not by its own definition. Nassau’s Local Law 6 of 1970 defines home improvement as work on a building used as a private residence for not more than three families, plus related work on the land next to it. A one-, two- or three-family house is inside that line; a four-unit building is outside it, much as a store or office is.

That does not make a four-unit job unregulated. The village, city or town building rules still apply, any electrical work still goes to a licensed electrician, and the owner will usually want the same written scope and insurance as on any commercial job.

For a two-family house where the owner lives upstairs and wants a wall in the downstairs unit’s den, the county law does apply. When in doubt, count the dwelling units on the property record before choosing who to hire.

Showrooms, Storefronts and Mall Tenants

Retail and automotive walls are where most Nassau video walls end up, and each comes with a landlord, a sun angle and a sales floor that cannot close for long.

Which wall in a glass-fronted showroom should carry the screens?

Usually a solid back or side wall that never takes direct sun, and only then is brightness chosen for it. Car showrooms such as the dealerships along Northern Boulevard in Great Neck are built around big windows, and a standard indoor LCD panel that looks bright in a conference room washes out when direct sun lands on it. The options are a high-brightness panel rated for window-facing use, direct-view LED, which is far brighter, or moving the wall to a side that never takes direct sun.

We visit at the hour that matters: late afternoon on a west-facing wall, morning on an east-facing one. We check where the reflections land, because a glossy panel facing the glass can show the parking lot better than the content. Matte-finish panels and a slight tilt can fix reflections; nothing but brightness fixes direct sun.

Brighter panels draw more power and run warmer, so the decision feeds straight into the electrical and ventilation plan. That is why we settle it at the site measure, not after the order.

Do the cables above our drop ceiling need a special rating?

Often, yes. In many Nassau offices, schools and stores the space above the lay-in ceiling also carries return air to the heating and cooling system. Cable run through an air space like that has to carry the plenum rating the electrical code requires, which burns with less smoke than ordinary cable.

We check the ceiling at the site measure: whether it is a return-air plenum, where the existing cable trays and hangers are, and where the light fixtures and sprinkler pipes run. Wall cable is hung on its own supports, never laid on the tiles, and labeled at both ends so the next person can follow it.

The price table already assumes the right cable. If a building’s own rules ask for conduit instead, that is quoted as its own line.

How do we keep a showroom wall from looking dated when the models change every year?

Treat the wall as a channel, not a poster. The hardware lasts for years; what dates it is content that was made once for the opening and never changed. A showroom wall works best with a few templates that the sales or marketing team can update themselves: new model photos, a monthly offer, a service-department message, an event night.

We set the content player up with those templates and a simple schedule, and we show the person who will update it how to swap images and change the order without calling anyone. Manufacturer brand guidelines often supply approved video and photography; we size the wall’s canvas so those files fit without awkward cropping across the seams.

Once a year it is worth a short service visit to recalibrate brightness and color, clean the panels and update the player. A wall that is bright, even and showing this month’s cars looks new long after the install.

When does a storefront need direct-view LED instead of tiled LCD panels?

When the wall faces sun, needs to be seen from far away, or has to be truly seamless. Tiled LCD panels are sharp and affordable, but every panel has a thin border, and those lines run through the picture. Direct-view LED has no borders and is much brighter, but it costs more per square foot and its fineness depends on pixel pitch, the spacing between LEDs: the closer viewers stand, the finer the pitch has to be.

For a store where customers stand a few feet away and the wall faces into the shop, LCD usually wins on cost and sharpness. For a showroom wall seen from the road, a large wall over a sales floor, or a design that cannot tolerate lines across faces and logos, LED is the better tool. Our Nassau price table shows both, so the comparison is in dollars, not adjectives.

One more check: the wall behind LED has to be flat and strong, and the cabinets need service access from the front or the back. We confirm which before the order.

Office Parks, School Gyms and Operations Rooms

The other half of Nassau’s walls go into lobbies, gyms and rooms where people watch screens for a living. Each has a different failure to design around.

Does a high showroom or gym wall need a scissor lift on site?

Often, yes. Showroom ceilings and school gyms are usually too high for a step ladder to reach the cable route safely, and a wall whose top edge sits well above head height has to be aligned from a stable platform. A small electric scissor lift fits through a standard double door on most commercial sites.

We include the lift in the quote when the site measure shows one is needed, and we check the floor first: polished showroom tile and gym floors need protection under the wheels, and some floors have load limits the facilities office will want to see.

In stores and offices with lay-in ceilings at normal height, ladders are usually enough, which keeps the cost down. The quote says which access equipment the job assumes.

What does a school gymnasium wall need that a lobby wall does not?

Protection, height and sound. A gym wall takes balls, so it is mounted high, behind a guard, or both, and the mount has to stay rigid when something hits it. It is seen from bleachers across the floor, so it is usually larger and coarser than a lobby wall, and it is almost always paired with a sound system, because a picture without audible commentary in a gym is half a system.

Nassau districts are buying these. In July 2026 the County Legislature approved a $66,350 community revitalization grant toward a new sound system and video wall for the Hempstead High School gymnasium, and the superintendent described the space as one for athletic programs, events and community gatherings. Can the conference-room wall work with Teams or Zoom without an AV technician in the room?

Yes, if the wall is designed as a meeting room rather than a display. That means a camera placed where it sees the table rather than the back of people’s heads, microphones that pick up the far end of the table, speakers that do not echo off glass partitions, and one way to connect a laptop or start a call that works the same every time.

On a video wall the extra question is where the call appears. A layout that puts the remote participants across the middle of the wall, with shared content to one side, keeps faces away from the seams. Controllers and some meeting-room systems can switch to that layout automatically when a call starts.

We test it with the people who will use it: a call from the room, a laptop shared, the far end asked whether they can hear the person at the end of the table. A conference wall that needs a technician to start a meeting will not be used.

How is an operations-room wall different from a wall built for advertising?

An operations wall is a tool that people use all day, so reliability, layout flexibility and heat matter more than brightness. Nassau has a well-documented example: the Nassau County Police Department’s Intelligence Center, opened in October 2021 in the David S. Mack Center for Training and Intelligence on the Nassau Community College campus, is built around a Planar LCD video wall in an 8-by-8 layout nearly 32 feet long and 18 feet high, integrated by a Long Island AV firm. Its users describe splitting the wall between daily work and an active case, and switching between one large image and many small ones.

Two design choices in that project apply to any security desk or dispatch room: the processing lets operators place any source anywhere on the wall from preset layouts, and the panels’ power supplies were moved to a rack room, taking heat, noise and electrical outlets away from the wall itself. A small office will not build anything that size, but the same thinking — presets, remote power, sources mapped to roles — works on a 2-by-2.

An advertising wall, by contrast, plays scheduled content for people walking past. It needs a content player and a schedule more than a switching controller. Knowing which one you are buying keeps money out of the wrong box.

Sizing a Nassau wall: distance, height and the picture itself

The right wall starts from the people looking at it. In a reception area where visitors stand a few steps away, a 2x2 of 55-inch panels fills the view and every detail is sharp; the same wall at the back of a gym or across a showroom floor looks small. Measure from where the nearest regular viewer stands and from where the farthest one sits, and size the wall for the far viewer while choosing a pixel pitch or panel resolution that still looks clean to the near one. The finer the LED pitch, the closer people can stand before they see individual dots, and the higher the price per square foot.

Height matters as much as size. A wall hung too high in a conference room gives everyone a sore neck; a wall hung too low in a store disappears behind shoppers and displays. In lobbies we usually keep the bottom edge above the heads of people standing in front of it and the center near the eye line of someone walking in; in gyms the bottom edge goes above the reach of a bounced ball; in homes it sits where a seated viewer looks slightly up, not sharply.

Finally, design the content for the wall, not for a single TV. A 2x2 of full-HD panels is a 3840 by 2160 canvas with lines through it at the center; text that crosses a seam is harder to read, and faces split by a bezel look odd. We send the owner’s designer the exact canvas size and the seam positions before the first file is made, so the opening-day content fits the wall it plays on.

Video wall terms, in plain English

  • Bezel. The frame around each LCD panel. Two bezels meet at every seam; “narrow-bezel” panels keep that line thin, but it never disappears.
  • Pixel pitch. On an LED wall, the distance between the centers of neighboring LEDs, in millimeters. Smaller pitch means a finer picture at close range and a higher price.
  • Brightness (nits). How bright a panel can get. A conference room needs far less than a wall facing showroom glass; too much indoors wastes power and tires the eyes.
  • Controller or processor. The box that treats the wall as one screen, places several sources on it and recalls layouts from presets.
  • Content player. A small computer that plays scheduled content from a local copy, managed through software from anywhere.
  • Calibration. Matching brightness and color from panel to panel so the wall reads as one picture instead of a checkerboard.
  • Pop-out mount. A mount that lets each panel be pulled forward for service without taking the wall apart.
  • Remote power supply. A design, used in larger LCD walls, that moves the panels’ power supplies to an equipment room, taking heat and noise away from the wall.

Online kit, national AV chain or local installer?

An online kit gets panels and a mount to your door for the lowest price, and that is all it does. Somebody still has to find out what the wall is made of, add backing, run cable from the closet, bring in an electrician, align the panels and set up the player. Kits are a good fit for people who already have that somebody.

A national AV chain brings standard designs and purchasing scale, which suits a brand rolling out the same wall to dozens of stores. The local questions — this village’s sign rules, this landlord’s hours, this lobby wall — still have to be answered on the ground, often by a subcontractor.

A local installer should be able to answer those local questions before quoting, show a license and insurance, put the price in writing from a published list, and come back next month when a panel goes dark. “A video wall costs $5,000 to $15,000”

“Any installer can hang a video wall — it’s just TV mounting”

A single television is one heavy object on one mount. A video wall is several panels that must line up to within a millimeter or two, sit flat as one surface, share a controller or player, and match in color. A crooked panel or a gap is visible from across the room.

The mount is different too: video-wall mounts let each panel slide forward for service without taking the wall apart, and the whole assembly has to reach studs, blocking or a frame that carries several hundred pounds in total. Professionals on AV forums describe quoting LED walls per tile or per crew-day for exactly this reason.

“You don’t need a permit for a TV on the wall”

For a single TV on a wall in a house, that is usually true. For a commercial wall it can be wrong in Nassau, where sixty-four villages, three towns and two cities each enforce their own building and sign rules. A screen that faces the street may be a sign; a new circuit needs an electrician and, often, an inspection.

The AI answers we tested treat Nassau as a single place, or answer for a different state. None mentioned that a hamlet answers to its town while a village answers to itself.

The safe approach is to look up the jurisdiction from the address and ask the right office before ordering. That is part of our design step, and the proposal names the office.

“LED is always better than LCD”

LED has no seams and goes far brighter, and for a showroom wall seen from the road it often is the better choice. But fine-pitch LED that looks sharp at conversation distance costs much more per square foot than LCD, and coarse LED looks grainy up close.

For a reception area or conference room where people stand a few feet away and the light is controlled, tiled LCD is usually sharper for the money. The seams are the trade-off, and narrow-bezel panels keep them thin.

Our price table puts both side by side at Nassau prices, which makes the choice a budget conversation instead of a technology argument.

“Consumer TVs work fine as a video wall”

Consumer TVs are cheaper per inch, and the advice to use them shows up often. In a grid, though, their thick, uneven borders break the picture, their colors differ from set to set, and most are not rated for twelve or more hours a day.

Commercial video-wall panels are built for tiling, carry signal from one panel to the next, and can be calibrated to match. They also come with commercial warranties that cover business use.

There are places where separate TVs make sense — a menu board, a row of sports screens with gaps between them — and we will say so. A seamless wall is not one of them.

“A video wall is plug and play”

Panels power up out of the box, but a wall is not finished until the inputs are mapped to the right panels, the content player starts the right playlist after a power cut, the brightness is set for the room, and someone other than the installer can log in.

What happens to the old displays we take down?
+

They cannot go in the dumpster. New York State has banned the disposal of covered electronic equipment in the trash since January 1, 2015, and manufacturers must run free recycling programs for households, smaller businesses, nonprofits, schools and government offices; larger companies may be charged. We can stack and label the old screens for a recycler, or bring them to a collection program, and note which route was used for your records.
