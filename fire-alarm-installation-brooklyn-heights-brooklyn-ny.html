The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Heights Properties Need Fire Alarm Installation

Brooklyn Heights looks settled, but for fire detection it is one of the more complicated neighborhoods in the borough. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Brooklyn Heights Historic District on November 23, 1965, the first historic district in New York City, and the Historic Districts Council describes it as known for its 19th-century brownstones. Those houses were built long before anyone imagined a smoke alarm. Their walls are plaster on brick or on wood lath, their floors are thick, their stairs are open from the parlor to the top floor, and many of them have been divided, combined, divided again and renovated by owners who each solved the alarm question their own way. When we walk a Heights house for the first time, we often find three generations of devices on the same ceilings: a hardwired alarm from one renovation, battery units from another, and a carbon monoxide alarm plugged into whatever outlet was handy.

The apartment buildings are just as varied. There are prewar co-ops in former townhouses and small apartment houses, postwar elevator co-ops such as 100 Remsen Street, built in 1950 with eight floors and 78 apartments, and 60 Remsen Street, built in 1956 with ten floors and 78 apartments. There are rental buildings such as the six-story building at 75 Pierrepont Street, and there are the pieces of the old Hotel St. George complex on Clark and Henry Streets, now apartments and student housing. Each of these has its own mix of in-unit alarms, hall detection and building panels, and each has a different person responsible: the owner who lives in the house, a landlord, a co-op board and its managing agent, or a university or a school.

Recent events have made the stakes concrete. On January 5, 2026, a fire in a third-floor apartment at 75 Pierrepont Street, between Henry and Hicks, injured seven people and left several upper-floor residents waiting on the fire escape in freezing weather until firefighters reached them. Just over a year earlier, during Thanksgiving week of 2024, an electrical fire in a manhole on Remsen Street spread to the fourth floor of an apartment building, and firefighters found high carbon monoxide levels in two neighboring buildings. Neither event had anything to do with a gas stove or a furnace inside the buildings affected. Both are reasons we look at a Heights building as part of a block, not just a list of rooms.

Then there is the calendar. Under Local Law 102 of 2025, HPD's June 2026 bulletin says owners of multiple dwellings must provide and install at least one approved and operational natural gas detector by January 1, 2027, wherever there is gas piping. As of this page's review that is about seven weeks away. A neighborhood full of brownstones divided into rental apartments, nearly all of them with gas ranges, has a lot of apartments to visit in a short time. At the same time some Heights buildings are moving the other way: Habitat reported in November 2024 that the 22-unit co-op at 205 Hicks Street replaced its oil boiler and steam heat with electric heat pumps. A building's fuel decides which detectors it needs, so that change matters for the alarm plan as much as for the heating bill.

Six Brooklyn Heights facts that shape a fire alarm plan before any device is chosen

  • The whole neighborhood sits in a historic district. The Brooklyn Heights Historic District was designated on November 23, 1965. LPC's permit page says an LPC permit is needed for any interior work that requires a Department of Buildings permit, any interior work that affects the exterior, and any work in a designated interior landmark. Most alarm work inside a house touches none of those, but some does, and we check before we start.
  • Some interiors are landmarks too. The Historic Districts Council lists the Brooklyn Trust Company Building, exterior and interior, designated in 1996, and the interior of the Long Island Historical Society, designated in 1982, among the Heights' individual landmarks. Work inside spaces like those is reviewed by LPC whatever its size.
  • Apartment buildings range from 1950s co-ops to old hotels. 100 Remsen Street (1950, eight floors, 78 units, part-time doorman and live-in super) and 60 Remsen Street (1956, ten floors, 78 units, full-time doorman) are typical postwar co-ops. St. George Towers at 100 Henry Street, a student residence in three towers, keeps a 24-hour security desk at its main entry.
  • The gas detector deadline is close. HPD's June 2026 bulletin sets January 1, 2027 for at least one approved natural gas detector in multiple dwellings with gas piping, allows a combined smoke, CO and gas device, requires posting notices, and exempts buildings with no gas piping.
  • Older battery alarms in apartment buildings have rules of their own. Local Law 112 of 2013, in effect since April 1, 2014, requires owners of multi-family buildings to replace non-working or expired battery smoke alarms with sealed ten-year-battery alarms that give an end-of-life signal. It does not apply to hardwired alarms. A First Alert summary of the law noted that buildings from before 1982 are the ones most affected.
  • The Heights has lived through a famous fire. On August 26, 1995, a 16-alarm fire began at 3:31 a.m. in the vacant Clark Building of the Hotel St. George complex at 51 Clark Street and burned into seven apartments of the occupied 31-story Tower Building. Fire Engineering's account of it is still one of the most useful documents for anyone planning detection in a converted hotel.

Building Specialists

Fire Alarm Installation for Brooklyn Heights's Architecture

Six kinds of building make up nearly all the fire alarm work we see in Brooklyn Heights. They differ in who is responsible, who sleeps there, what the walls and floors are made of and which rules reach them, and those differences decide the plan well before a model number comes up.

Single-family townhouses

Four- and five-story brownstone and brick houses with a garden floor, a parlor floor and bedrooms above, owned and lived in by one household and built of plaster, brick and old-growth timber.

Brownstones divided into rentals

Houses split into two, three or more apartments, sometimes with the owner on one floor, sharing one stoop door and one stair, each apartment with its own kitchen and gas range.

Prewar co-ops in small apartment houses

Buildings of a few to a few dozen apartments, such as the 22-unit co-op at 205 Hicks Street, often with old dumbwaiter shafts, a cellar boiler room and a board that turns over every few years.

Postwar elevator co-ops

Concrete and masonry buildings from the 1950s such as 100 and 60 Remsen Street, with elevators, laundry rooms, storage, a doorman for part or all of the day, and a building panel in the lobby or cellar.

Converted hotels and student housing

The old Hotel St. George buildings on Clark and Henry Streets and other former hotels, now apartments, co-ops and student residences, with long halls, duplex apartments and a staffed desk.

Rental elevator and walk-up buildings

Five- and six-story rental buildings like 75 Pierrepont Street, with in-unit alarms in each apartment, a fire escape at the windows and whatever hall detection the building already has.

Single-family townhouses: one family, five floors, one open stair

A Heights townhouse lived in by its owners is the one building type where the law asks the least of the household and the building asks the most. The kitchen is often on the garden floor, a half-level below the sidewalk. The living rooms are on the parlor floor. Bedrooms are on the two or three floors above, and children may sleep on the top floor, three flights above the kitchen. The stair runs open through all of it, which carries smoke upward quickly. The FDNY's own guidance for homes is simple to state and hard to meet in a house like this: a smoke alarm on every level, including the basement, a smoke alarm within 15 feet of all sleeping areas, carbon monoxide alarms on each floor and in bedrooms where people sleep with the door closed, and a test of every alarm at least once a month.

Our plan for a townhouse starts at the bottom, where the boiler, the water heater and the gas meter usually sit, and works up. We put CO detection in the cellar, more than five feet from the fuel-burning equipment, as the FDNY asks, and on each floor above. Smoke alarms go on every level, at the top of the stair, outside every sleeping area and, for a family that sleeps with doors shut, inside the bedrooms too. Then we link them, by cable where the house allows it and by tested wireless interconnect where it does not, so an alarm that starts in the garden-level kitchen sounds in the top-floor bedroom. The last step is a listening test: every bedroom door closed, a window air conditioner running if there is one, and an alarm sounded two floors down.

Brownstones divided into rentals: several households, one stair

Once a brownstone has three or more apartments it is a multiple dwelling, and the owner carries HPD's detector duties for every unit: smoke and carbon monoxide detection in each apartment, natural gas detection by January 1, 2027 where there is gas piping, notices to the occupants and a record of what was installed and when. A Heights house divided into rentals also tends to have a mixed history. One apartment was renovated recently and has hardwired alarms; another still has battery units that went up a decade ago; the garden apartment has a CO alarm that a past tenant bought. Local Law 112 of 2013 requires owners of multi-family buildings to replace non-working or expired battery smoke alarms with sealed ten-year models, so every expired battery unit we find gets replaced, not just reset.

The shared spaces get their own look. Everyone in the house leaves by the same stair and the same stoop door, so we look at whether the stair and entry hall have any detection and, if they do, whether it is part of a system or just battery alarms someone added. We do not decide whether an apartment layout is legal, and an alarm does not change that. What we do is make sure each household has detection that will wake it, that the owner has the paperwork HPD asks for, and that a tenant who calls about a dead alarm gets an answer within the week, not the season.

Prewar co-ops in small apartment houses: shafts, plaster and a board decision

Small prewar co-ops are where cable routing gets interesting. Many of these buildings kept the dumbwaiter shafts, light wells and pipe chases they were built with, and some boards have already found new uses for them. When the 205 Hicks Street co-op switched to electric heat pumps, Habitat reported that the refrigerant lines were routed up two old dumbwaiter shafts, and the condensers went behind the roof bulkhead where they cannot be seen from the street, with the Landmarks Preservation Commission approving the work. For fire alarm cable, the same shafts can be a clean way to rise through the building without opening a single plaster ceiling, as long as the shaft is examined first, the cable is the type the use calls for, and every penetration out of the shaft is closed again.

The decision that matters most in a small co-op belongs to the board, not to us. Depending on the proprietary lease and the house rules, in-apartment smoke and CO alarms may be each shareholder's to maintain, while anything in the halls, stairs, cellar and roof belongs to the corporation. A board that wants every apartment brought to one standard, gas detectors done before the deadline, and one set of records kept for the whole building, does better to hire one contractor for all of it than to leave each shareholder to call their own. We price either way and say plainly which parts need a design professional.

Postwar elevator co-ops: a building panel, a part-time desk and 78 apartments

The 1950s co-ops on Remsen Street and the streets around it were built of masonry and concrete, with elevators, laundry rooms, bike and storage rooms, and lobbies that were staffed for part or all of the day. A building like 100 Remsen Street, with eight floors and 78 apartments, a part-time doorman and a live-in super, usually has a panel in the lobby or the cellar and detection in some of the common spaces, while each apartment has its own smoke and CO alarms. The questions there are practical. Can the super read the panel and tell an alarm from a trouble signal? Is the panel monitored, and what happens to a trouble signal at three in the morning when the desk is empty? Are the apartment alarms near their ten-year date, and is anyone keeping a list?

In a concrete building, cable does not fish through hollow ceilings, so we plan the route before we price it: along existing conduit where it is there, in surface raceway painted to match where the board allows it, and through the few new openings that are unavoidable. Work inside an apartment is scheduled with the managing agent and the shareholder, and anything that changes the building system goes through the design professional and the FDNY before it is touched. For a building of this size, we often recommend doing the apartment alarms and the gas detectors floor by floor in one campaign, so every shareholder is visited once and the records come out complete.

Converted hotels and student housing: long halls, duplexes and a lesson from 1995

The Hotel St. George complex was made up of nine attached buildings, and over the years most of it was converted to apartments, with only the 100 Henry Street building still a hotel at the time of the 1995 fire. Fire Engineering's account of the August 26, 1995 fire describes what made that fire so hard: the Tower Building, a 31-story fire-resistive building from 1929, had only two stairways, long and dead-end hallways, multilevel duplex apartments, and apartment numbering that was inconsistent from one floor to the next. In a vacant neighboring building, large holes had been cut in the floors to remove furniture and fixtures, and hoistway doors had been taken off, so fire traveled straight up through the building and across into the next.

For anyone installing detection in a converted hotel today, those details are a checklist. Duplex apartments need detection on both levels, not just near the entry door. Every device label and every line on the panel's zone list has to match the apartment numbering as it actually exists on that floor, so a firefighter or a technician reading the panel goes to the right door. Every opening our cable makes through a floor or a fire-rated wall is closed again before we leave. And in a building with a staffed desk, such as St. George Towers at 100 Henry Street, the desk needs to know what each signal from the panel means. Most of that work runs through the building's design professional and its fire alarm company of record; our part is to install, label and test it so the records match the walls.

Rental elevator and walk-up buildings: what the Pierrepont Street fire showed

Five- and six-story rental buildings are where a fire in one apartment most quickly becomes everyone's problem. The Brooklyn Eagle reported that the January 5, 2026 fire at 75 Pierrepont Street started in a third-floor apartment just after 3:30 in the afternoon, that 21 units and 79 fire and EMS personnel responded, and that several upper-floor residents were trapped on the fire escape in freezing temperatures. News 12 reported that the Department of Buildings issued a partial vacate order and that the Red Cross registered five households for help. No report we found says whether the smoke alarms or any building alarm sounded, and we do not claim either.

What a building like that can control is what each household knows and when. We start with the apartment alarms, because they are the owner's legal duty and the first warning anyone gets: a working smoke detector near every sleeping room, CO where the law and the fuel call for it, and gas detection by January 1, 2027. Then we look at the halls and the stair, and at whatever system the building already has. A like-for-like repair of an existing system is ordinary service work. Adding a system where there was none, or moving a panel, is a design change that a registered design professional has to put on drawings for the FDNY before any device goes up.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Read the Shafts, Count the Floors, Then Shut Every Bedroom Door

Find the fuel first. In a Heights house or small co-op, the first stop is the cellar: the gas meter, the boiler or furnace, the water heater, a dryer, and then every gas range on every floor above. We write each one down by room, because from January 1, 2027 every rented apartment with gas piping needs a gas detector, and the CO plan depends on where combustion happens. If the building has already gone electric, as 205 Hicks Street did, we write that down too, and note anything still burning fuel, such as a fireplace, an old range or a neighbor's boiler vent near a window.

Look for the old ways up. Before we think about fishing cable through plaster, we look for paths the house already has: a dumbwaiter shaft, a closet stacked above a closet, a pipe chase beside the chimney breast, an abandoned speaking tube or an old bell-wire run. A Heights house was usually built with at least one of them, and using it can mean no new holes in the parlor ceiling at all.

Count the floors people sleep on. In a townhouse, the answer is often three: the parlor floor guest room, the second-floor main bedroom and the children's rooms at the top. Each needs its own alarm, linked to the kitchen and the cellar. In a divided house, we count households instead and check the count against what the owner expects.

Close the doors and listen. The last test of any Heights job is the same: every bedroom door closed, a window air conditioner running if there is one, and an alarm sounded two floors below. If it cannot be heard clearly in the farthest bedroom, the plan needs linking or another device, whatever the minimum says.

Seven Weeks to January 1, 2027

Gas Detectors in Brooklyn Heights Rentals and Co-ops: What Still Has to Happen Before January 1, 2027

HPD's June 2026 bulletin to property owners puts it in one sentence: under Local Law 102 of 2025, owners must provide and install, by January 1, 2027, at least one approved and operational natural gas detector. The same bulletin says a building with no gas piping can be exempt, that owners may install a single device combining smoke, carbon monoxide and gas detection, and that a posting notice is required for these devices. As of this page's review on November 13, 2026, that leaves about seven weeks, and the weeks around the holidays are the hardest ones in the year to get into every apartment of a building.

Who it reaches in the Heights. The requirement reaches the buildings that hold most of the neighborhood's renters and many of its co-op shareholders: brownstones divided into three or more apartments, small prewar co-ops, the postwar elevator co-ops and rental buildings, and converted hotels with apartments. Almost all of them still cook with gas, even where the boiler has been replaced. A building that has removed every gas line, which is rare but no longer unheard of after conversions like the one at 205 Hicks Street, can be exempt, and the exemption has to be on file rather than assumed.

What a visit involves. In each apartment we find every appliance the gas feeds, mount the detector where the room and the manufacturer call for it, test it, and record the install date and the end of its useful life. Because getting into the apartment is the slow part, we check the smoke and CO alarms in the same visit: an expired battery smoke alarm in a multi-family building has to be replaced with a sealed ten-year unit under Local Law 112, and a CO alarm past its useful life gets replaced too. One visit, one notice to the occupant, one updated record.

What the board or owner keeps. The posting notice goes up in the building. The owner keeps, for each apartment, the device type, its location, the date it went in and the date it will need replacing. HPD also requires owners of multiple dwellings to post a Suspected Gas Leaks Notice, and the bulletin warns that HPD will issue a violation for failing to post it. We hand over a spreadsheet the managing agent can keep, organized by apartment, not by the order we happened to work in.

If you are behind. Send us the number of apartments, how many have gas ranges, and whether any shareholders or tenants are away. We will tell you honestly how many visits it takes and what can be done before the first of the year.

Inside the Historic District

Fire Alarms Under Landmarks Rules: What the LPC Reviews in Brooklyn Heights, and What It Leaves Alone

People in the Heights often assume that anything done to a house in the historic district needs Landmarks approval. For fire alarm work, the truth is narrower and more useful. LPC's page on permit types says an LPC permit is required for three kinds of interior work: any interior work that requires a permit from the Department of Buildings, any interior work that affects the exterior of the building, such as louvers and vents, and any work affecting interior spaces that are designated interior landmarks. A staff preservationist decides which type of permit a project needs.

What usually needs nothing from LPC. Replacing battery smoke alarms with sealed ten-year models, adding plug-in or battery CO and gas detectors, and swapping a failed hardwired alarm for an equivalent one are household work inside the walls. LPC's Health, Safety and Utility guidebook also exempts the ordinary maintenance, repair and retrofitting of health, safety and utility equipment. None of that touches the facade, a designated interior or a DOB filing.

What can bring LPC in. Work that is part of a larger renovation filed with the Department of Buildings comes to LPC along with the rest of that filing, because the DOB permit triggers the LPC permit. A building system whose design shows a horn, strobe, bell, annunciator or antenna on the outside of the building affects the exterior. The guidebook says equipment should stay off a primary facade unless there is no other option, and then go somewhere discreet, such as beneath a band course, under the stoop or behind an areaway wall, fastened at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork and never through cast iron or decorative surfaces, with conduit concealed. And any device in a designated interior, such as the banking hall of the Brooklyn Trust Company Building or the interior of the Long Island Historical Society, is reviewed regardless of size.

How we plan around it. On a Heights job we ask early whether the alarm work is part of a bigger renovation with an architect and a DOB filing. If it is, we give the architect our device list and cable routes so they can go into the same applications, rather than becoming a second round of review later. If it is not, we keep every device and every run inside the building, which almost every household alarm job can do. Where a building system truly needs something outside, the design professional who files it with the FDNY also handles the LPC application, and we install what both agencies approve.

Compliance notes for Brooklyn Heights businesses and buildings

  • Plans before devices. FDNY Fire Code Chapter 9 requires fire alarm design and installation documents to be submitted to the FDNY for review and approval before installation. A filed system is designed by a registered design professional; we install, label and pre-test to the approved drawings.
  • Repair is not redesign. Replacing detectors, pull stations or a control panel with identical parts and no design change counts as a repair, according to ASM Integrators' summary of the code. Moving a control panel, or replacing it with something different, needs new plans filed with the FDNY.
  • Small buildings have an exception. The 2022 NYC Building Code, as published by UpCodes, does not require a manual fire alarm system in buildings of two stories or fewer where each dwelling unit is separated by one-hour fire partitions and has a direct exit to a public way, exit court or yard. Few Heights buildings are that low, and whether one qualifies depends on how it is actually built.
  • Landmarks where it applies. LPC review comes in when alarm work is part of a DOB-permitted job, touches the exterior, or happens in a designated interior landmark, as described in the section above.
  • Unnecessary alarms cost money. The Habitat Group reported an owner fined $2,500 after the FDNY issued two violations for failing to maintain a fire alarm system that sounded unnecessarily twice, once because of construction work. The Environmental Control Board upheld the fine on appeal (ECB App. No. 1101382, April 26, 2012). Dust from a Heights renovation is exactly that kind of risk, and it is planned for before the crew starts sanding.
  • Carbon monoxide and gas in mixed-use buildings. HPD's rules for apartments above a business still apply to the apartments, and from January 1, 2027 gas detection goes in each rented unit with gas piping. A restaurant's own kitchen and gas equipment are a separate question for the business's system and its designer.
Your Questions Answered

Fire Alarm Installation in Brooklyn Heights: Questions Answered

These are situations Brooklyn Heights owners, shareholders, boards and tenants bring to us, many of them shaped by what has happened on these blocks. Each is answered fully here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

What the recent fires here teach

After the Pierrepont Street fire, a resident said he did not know how bad it was until he opened his door. How should our household find out before that point?

By having warning outside your apartment as well as inside it, and a habit for the moment it sounds. Inside, a working smoke alarm near every bedroom and on each level of a duplex tells you there is smoke in your home. It does not tell you there is a fire in the hall or the apartment below, and that is the situation the resident described to News 12. In a building with hall detection that sounds on every floor, the alarm itself carries that warning; in a building without it, a smoke alarm just inside your entry door picks up smoke coming under the door sooner than one in a back bedroom. The habit matters as much as the device: when any alarm sounds, feel the door and the knob with the back of your hand before opening it, and if it is hot or smoke is coming in around it, keep it shut, seal the gap, and call 911 from inside. We help owners and boards work out which of those layers a building has, and add the in-unit part on the same visit.

Carbon monoxide got into buildings on Remsen Street from a manhole fire. Our building has no gas and no oil, and HPD says buildings like ours can be exempt from CO detectors. Should we put them in anyway?

We think so, and we say that knowing it may not be a legal duty. HPD's bulletin says a building can be exempt from carbon monoxide detecting devices if it has no fossil fuel burning devices and no enclosed parking, and an all-electric building may qualify. But the CO that firefighters found in two buildings near the Remsen Street manhole fire in 2024 came from outside, from an electrical fire under the street, and it did not care what fuel the buildings used. A plug-in or sealed-battery CO alarm in the cellar near where utility lines enter and one on each sleeping floor is a small cost against that risk. If the exemption is filed, keep the alarms as an owner's choice, and keep the paperwork that says why they are there.

Old houses, converted hotels and co-ops

Our townhouse still has its old dumbwaiter shaft, running from the cellar kitchen to the top floor. Can fire alarm cable go up through it instead of through our plaster?

Often, yes, and it is one of the best paths a Heights house can offer. Habitat reported that the 205 Hicks Street co-op used two old dumbwaiter shafts to carry refrigerant lines for its heat pump conversion, which shows how well those shafts can serve as a vertical route. For alarm cable, we first open the shaft and look: what else runs in it, whether it is lined or open to the floors, and whether it vents to the roof. The cable has to be the right type for a shaft, it is fastened rather than left hanging, and every opening from the shaft into a room is small and closed again so the shaft cannot carry smoke between floors. Where the shaft works, linked hardwired alarms on every floor become possible without opening a single ornamental ceiling.

We bought a duplex apartment in one of the old hotel buildings on Clark Street. The previous owner left one smoke alarm by the front door. What does the upper level need?

Its own detection. The FDNY's guidance asks for a smoke alarm on every level and within 15 feet of all sleeping areas, and in a duplex the bedrooms are usually on the level that has nothing. Fire Engineering's account of the 1995 Hotel St. George fire singled out multilevel duplex apartments as one of the features that made the Tower Building hard to search. We put a smoke alarm at the top of the internal stair and outside or inside each bedroom upstairs, CO detection on both levels if the apartment or building has fuel-burning equipment, and link the levels so an alarm downstairs is heard in a closed bedroom upstairs. If the building has a system with devices in the apartment, we coordinate with the board before touching it.

Our 1950s co-op's apartments have hardwired smoke alarms that went in during a renovation years ago. Does the sealed ten-year battery law mean they all have to come out?

No. Local Law 112 of 2013 is about battery-operated smoke alarms: it requires owners of multi-family buildings to replace non-working or expired battery alarms with sealed ten-year models with an end-of-life signal, and the summary we rely on says plainly that it does not apply to hardwired alarms. Hardwired alarms still wear out, and the FDNY's guidance is to replace smoke alarms at least every ten years or when the end-of-life warning sounds. So the question for your building is age, not type. We check the date on the back of each unit, replace the expired ones with compatible hardwired alarms, and give the board a list by apartment of which ones are coming due next.

Gas, electrification and the 2027 date

Our co-op is moving from gas to electric over the next two years, but most kitchens still have gas ranges today. Do those apartments need gas detectors by January 1, 2027?

Yes, as long as the gas piping is there. HPD's bulletin ties the requirement to gas piping, and its exemption applies to a building that has none, not to a building planning to remove it. Until the last gas line is capped and the exemption is properly on file, the rented or co-op apartments with gas need an approved, working detector. The practical answer for a co-op in the middle of a conversion is a plug-in or sealed-battery gas detector, often a combined smoke, CO and gas device, which can be removed cleanly from each apartment when its range is replaced and the line is capped. Keep the record of each removal with the same care as each installation.

Our managing agent's memo says Local Law 102 of 2025, but our neighbors keep talking about Local Law 157. Are these two different gas detector rules?

They are two stages of the same requirement. Local Law 157 of 2016 created the requirement for natural gas detectors in residential buildings, and its start date was pushed back while the detector standards and rules were settled. HPD's June 2026 bulletin now cites Local Law 102 of 2025 as the law under which owners must install at least one approved and operational natural gas detector by January 1, 2027. For an owner or a board, the point is the date and the duty: an approved detector, installed and working, in each dwelling where there is gas piping, with the posting notice and records HPD asks for.

Landmarked interiors

Our office is inside a designated interior landmark, under an ornamented ceiling. Can detectors be added there at all?

Yes, but not without the Landmarks Preservation Commission. LPC's permit page lists work affecting designated interior landmark spaces as one of the kinds of interior work that need an LPC permit, whatever its size. In practice that means the design professional prepares drawings that show exactly where each device and each length of cable will go, chosen to avoid painted, gilded or carved surfaces and to use existing openings, moldings and service spaces; LPC staff review them; and the FDNY reviews the same design for the fire alarm. We install only what both have approved, mounting through plain surfaces and existing joints, and we photograph every location before and after for the record.

What AI Gets Wrong About Fire Alarm Installation in Brooklyn Heights

"Anything in a historic district needs Landmarks approval, so alarms will take months"

Ask an AI assistant about fire alarms in a landmarked Brooklyn Heights house and it may warn you to expect a Landmarks review. That overstates it for most households. LPC's permit page limits interior permits to work that needs a DOB permit, work that affects the exterior and work in designated interior landmarks.

Sealed-battery smoke alarms, plug-in CO and gas detectors, and like-for-like replacement of hardwired alarms fall outside all three, and LPC's guidebook also exempts ordinary maintenance, repair and retrofitting of health and safety equipment.

Where LPC does come in, through a DOB-filed renovation, an exterior device or a designated interior, the fix is planning, not delay: put the alarm devices into the same drawings the architect is already preparing.

"Brownstones are single-family homes, so landlords have nothing to do"

Many Heights brownstones are single-family homes, but many others are divided into three or more apartments, which makes them multiple dwellings. Answers that treat every brownstone as a private house skip HPD's duties for those buildings.

An owner of a divided brownstone provides smoke and CO detection in each apartment, natural gas detection by January 1, 2027 wherever there is gas piping, the required notices and records, and sealed ten-year replacements for expired battery alarms under Local Law 112.

The house may look like one home from the stoop. What matters is how many households live behind the door, and we count them on the first visit.

"An all-electric building never needs carbon monoxide alarms"

AI summaries often say CO alarms are only needed where something burns fuel. HPD's exemption reflects that logic for buildings with no fossil fuel devices and no enclosed parking, and some Heights buildings converting to heat pumps may qualify.

The Remsen Street manhole fire showed the gap. Firefighters found high CO in two nearby buildings from an electrical fire under the street, and FDNY Deputy Chief Stephen Corcoran called one building's level "extremely high."

An exemption describes what the law requires, not what can happen. A CO alarm in the cellar and on each sleeping floor costs little and covers the risk the building cannot control.

"There's still plenty of time before the gas detector rule"

Because the natural gas detector requirement under Local Law 157 was postponed, older articles and AI answers can leave the impression that it is still years away. HPD's June 2026 bulletin settles it: under Local Law 102 of 2025, the date is January 1, 2027.

On the day this page was reviewed, that was about seven weeks off, with Thanksgiving and the December holidays in between, when shareholders and tenants travel and apartments are hardest to get into.

A Heights building with dozens of gas ranges should be scheduling visits now, and combining them with any smoke and CO replacements that are due, so each apartment is opened once.

"Wireless alarms solve every old-house problem"

AI answers often suggest wireless interconnected alarms as the answer for any older home. They are often the right tool in a Heights townhouse, because they spare the plaster, but brick party walls, thick floors and the depth of a five-story house can weaken the signal between floors.

We test wireless linking in place before relying on it, sounding one alarm on the garden floor while someone listens in each top-floor bedroom with the doors closed.

Where the signal fails, an existing shaft or chase often lets us run cable for that one link. The best plan in an old house is usually a mix, decided by testing rather than by the box.

"A co-op's building system protects every apartment"

Some AI answers imply that a building with a lobby panel has detection everywhere. In many Heights co-ops, the panel watches the halls, stairs, cellar and some common rooms, while each apartment relies on its own smoke and CO alarms.

Those in-unit alarms may be the shareholder's responsibility under the proprietary lease and the house rules, and they are the ones most likely to be expired, missing or covered during a renovation.

Boards that want certainty do best with a building-wide check of every apartment's alarms and a single list of dates, done in the same campaign as the 2027 gas detectors.

"A false alarm is just an annoyance"

AI answers tend to treat nuisance alarms as a comfort issue. For a building with a fire alarm system, they can be a legal one. The Habitat Group reported an owner fined $2,500 after the FDNY issued two violations for a system that sounded unnecessarily twice, once because of construction.

The Environmental Control Board upheld the fine on appeal in 2012. In a neighborhood where gut renovations of townhouses and co-op apartments go on all year, dust from sanding and demolition is a predictable cause.

The answer is planning with the contractor and the building's fire alarm company before work starts, so detection stays in service in occupied areas while the work area is handled under a written arrangement, never by quietly bagging detectors.

FAQ

Brooklyn Heights Fire Alarm Installation FAQ

Does the gas have to be shut off in our building while you install the natural gas detectors?
+

No. A natural gas detector is a sensing device, not part of the gas piping, so installing one never involves opening a gas line or turning off service. Plug-in units go into an outlet, sealed-battery units are fastened to the wall or ceiling, and hardwired units connect to an electrical circuit an electrician provides. Ranges, boilers and water heaters keep running the whole time. We do not work on gas piping at all; that belongs to a licensed plumber and Con Edison.

What happens if your technician smells gas while installing detectors in our apartment?
+

The work stops. The technician tells everyone in the apartment to leave without switching lights or appliances on or off, leaves with them, and calls 911 and Con Edison from outside. Nothing is mounted or tested until the utility or the FDNY has checked the building and said it is safe. HPD requires owners of multiple dwellings to post a Suspected Gas Leaks Notice in the building, and that notice is worth reading before the visit so everyone knows the same steps.

Is HPD's Suspected Gas Leaks Notice the same thing as the notice for the new gas detectors?
+

No, they are separate. HPD's June 2026 bulletin says owners must post a Suspected Gas Leaks Notice, and that HPD will issue a violation for failing to post it. The same bulletin says a posting notice is also required for the natural gas detecting devices. A Heights building that has one up should check that it has the other, and keep a record of when each was posted.

Can a carbon monoxide alarm warn us about a natural gas leak?
+

No. Carbon monoxide comes from burning fuel, and natural gas is the fuel itself, so they are different gases and need different sensors. A CO alarm can stay silent while gas collects in a kitchen. That is why the 2027 rule calls for a natural gas detector. If you want fewer devices on the ceiling, a combined unit that lists smoke, CO and natural gas among its functions can cover all three, as long as it is placed where each function is needed.

What listing should be printed on the smoke and CO alarms we buy for our townhouse?
+

Look for a mark from a recognized testing laboratory, such as UL, on the alarm itself. Smoke alarms for homes are tested to UL 217, and carbon monoxide alarms to UL 2034, and combination units carry both. Check the manufacture date on the back too, since the FDNY says to replace smoke alarms at least every ten years. If you plan to link alarms, buy them from one product line so they can talk to each other.

We sleep with our bedroom doors closed. Does each bedroom need its own carbon monoxide alarm?
+

The FDNY's guidance says yes: CO alarms on each floor of the home and in bedrooms or sleeping areas if you sleep with the doors closed. A closed door slows how fast CO reaches an alarm in the hall, and the people most at risk are asleep. In a townhouse with bedrooms on two or three floors, that can mean one CO alarm in each bedroom plus one on every level, linked where possible.

When you drill through a floor to run alarm cable, how is the hole closed afterward?
+

Every hole is kept as small as the cable allows and is filled again before we leave that floor. Openings through fire-rated floors and walls in apartment buildings are sealed with a listed firestop material, and openings in a townhouse are closed so smoke cannot use them as a chimney between floors. We note each one on the device list. We do not patch or paint plaster finishes; if a hole has to show, we tell you before drilling.

A bell on the front of our building has failed. Can it be replaced with the same kind of bell without going to Landmarks?
+

Often, yes, but confirm it first. LPC's Health, Safety and Utility guidebook exempts ordinary maintenance, repair and retrofitting of safety equipment, and a like-for-like replacement in the same spot usually fits that description. A larger device, a new location, new visible conduit or a different finish can change the answer, so for anything other than an identical swap we check with LPC staff before mounting it.

Con Edison's Clean Heat program helped pay for our co-op's heat pumps. Can it also cover the new detectors?
+

Ask Con Edison directly, because we do not know of incentives for detectors. Habitat reported that Clean Heat incentives covered $215,694 of the 205 Hicks Street co-op's $900,000 heat pump project, which shows the program's focus is heating equipment. What we can do is price the detector work separately and in writing, so a board can see exactly what an electrification project adds for alarms and gas detectors.

For a divided brownstone or a small co-op, usually all of it: a few apartments a day, each with gas detection and any overdue smoke or CO replacement in one visit. For a building of 70 or more apartments, it depends on how quickly residents give access. Send the apartment count and a gas appliance photo, and we will lay out a written schedule. Anything that cannot be finished by the first of the year should be booked, with dates, before it.
