The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Heights Buildings Need Fire Alarms Built Around Their History

Brooklyn Heights buildings need commercial fire alarm systems that respect what the buildings are, because almost none of them were designed for one. It is bounded roughly by Cadman Plaza West on the north, the Brooklyn-Queens Expressway and the Promenade on the west, Atlantic Avenue on the south, and an irregular line along Clinton and Henry Streets and Court Street on the east. When the Landmarks Preservation Commission designated it on November 23, 1965, it became the city's first historic district, and the Commission's report praised a place whose architecture was "a remarkable example of stylistic development, rarely found in any American city." That protection is why a fire alarm here cannot be a simple box-and-wire job.

The buildings that need a full commercial fire alarm under the NYC Building Code are mostly not the row houses people photograph on Willow Street. They are the larger structures among them: elevator co-ops, grand hotels turned into apartments, dormitories and a senior residence, nineteenth-century churches and schools, a library and a history museum, and the store and office buildings along Montague and Court Streets. Each was built for one use and now serves another.

The neighborhood's fire history keeps that in view. The Hotel Margaret on Columbia Heights burned in February 1980 during its conversion to condominiums. In August 1995 a 16-alarm fire tore through the vacant Clark Building of the former Hotel St. George. In December 2021 a four-alarm fire burned through the upper floors of 132 Montague Street above a cafe and an eyewear shop. And on January 5, 2026, a third-floor fire at 75 Pierrepont Street injured seven people; one resident told CBS New York that the building's fire alarms went off. Renovation, vacancy and stacked uses recur in every one of those stories.

So what is a commercial fire alarm system in a Brooklyn Heights building? It is the control panel and its power supplies, the smoke and heat detectors, the pull stations at exits, the horns, strobes and speakers, the modules that recall elevators, close held-open doors and stop fans, and the communicator that reports to an FDNY-approved central station, all shown on sealed drawings and accepted by the FDNY. How does a commercial fire alarm system work? Supervised circuits let the panel tell a fire from a broken wire or a closed sprinkler valve; on a fire signal it sounds the building, runs its outputs and calls for help. When does a building need a fire alarm system? Section 907 of the NYC Building Code answers by occupancy and size: schools, assisted living and hotels across the board, assembly spaces of 300 or more, offices above certain occupant loads, and apartment buildings with units three or more stories above the exit or with 16 or more units, among other triggers.

Why is my fire alarm panel showing trouble? Because the panel has found a problem with itself or its wiring, not a fire: low or failed batteries, a device that stopped answering, a broken circuit, a ground on a cable, or a lost central station connection. Building Types

Commercial Fire Alarm Systems for Brooklyn Heights' Building Types

Commercial fire alarm systems in Brooklyn Heights follow the building type, and the neighborhood has a handful of types that recur block after block. Each brings its own code trigger, its own construction and its own approval path inside the building, which is often a co-op board or a congregation rather than a single owner.

Pre-war and post-war elevator co-ops

Apartment co-ops are the Heights' most common fire alarm clients. 105 Montague Street became a co-op in 1980, when its owner converted it and added an elevator; 205 Hicks Street is a 22-unit pre-war co-op; 100 Remsen is an eight-floor, 78-unit co-op from 1950 and 60 Remsen a ten-floor, 78-unit building from 1956. Apartment building fire alarm installation and multifamily building fire alarm installation here cover cellars, boiler and compactor rooms, stairs, corridors, lobbies and elevator controls, while apartment smoke and carbon monoxide alarms remain a separate requirement. In a co-op the corporation is the owner of record, so every decision runs through a board vote and every apartment entry through the managing agent.

Hotels that became homes

No neighborhood in the city has converted as many grand hotels into apartments, dormitories, condominiums and senior housing. A hotel's fire alarm was designed for transient guests and a front desk; each new occupant type asks different things of the same corridors, as the section on converted hotels below explains.

Churches, chapels and parish halls

Plymouth Church at 57 Orange Street, built in 1849 and 1850 and a National Historic Landmark since 1966, connects to a church house at 75 Hicks Street and to Hillis Hall. Other congregations nearby pair a tall, decorated sanctuary with offices, classrooms and a rented hall. Often the hall or school, not the sanctuary, triggers the fire alarm requirement.

Independent schools in historic buildings

The Packer Collegiate Institute's Gothic Revival building at 170 Joralemon Street was designed by Minard Lafever, and its chapel holds nine Tiffany Studios windows; in 1969 the school bought the former St. Ann's Church next door, which became its middle school in 2003. Two blocks away, Saint Ann's School teaches in the former Crescent Athletic Club at 129 Pierrepont Street, a 1906 Frank Freeman building whose double-height windows hide twelve floors and which once held a swimming pool, a bowling alley and a top-floor gymnasium; the Episcopal church behind the school bought it in 1966 for classrooms. Every educational occupancy needs a fire alarm, and turning a church into classrooms sends drawings back to the FDNY.

Libraries and museums

The Center for Brooklyn History at 128 Pierrepont Street protects one of the city's designated interiors, while the Brooklyn Heights Library, reopened in June 2022 as a 26,000-square-foot branch at the base of the 38-story One Clinton tower, is brand-new construction with 134 condominiums above it.

Montague Street store buildings

Montague Street, which the historian Martha Foley called "once the principal commercial artery of the independent city of Brooklyn," is lined with four- and five-story buildings that have shops and restaurants on the ground floor and offices or apartments above. Mixed use building fire alarm installation here serves both the store and the people upstairs from one panel the owner controls.

Court Street offices and the edges

At the eastern edge, the taller office buildings around Court Street and Borough Hall have their own landmark layer: the LPC created the Borough Hall Skyscraper Historic District in 2011 to protect 21 buildings near Borough Hall, though the north side of Montague Street between Cadman Plaza West and Clinton Street was left out. Beyond the offices, the surrounding blocks hold the occasional parking garage under a residential building, storage rooms in co-op cellars and a few server and telecom rooms in professional buildings, which bring parking garage fire alarm installation, storage facility fire alarm installation and data center fire detection installation into scope. Medical office fire alarm installation applies to doctors' and dentists' suites on Remsen and Montague Streets, and high rise fire alarm installation to the towers along Cadman Plaza West. Warehouse, industrial and manufacturing buildings are scarce inside the historic district, so warehouse fire alarm installation, industrial fire alarm systems, industrial fire alarm installation, industrial building fire alarm installation, manufacturing facility fire alarm installation and industrial fire detection system installation are work we do elsewhere in Brooklyn rather than on these streets; hospital fire alarm installation and shopping center fire alarm installation likewise apply to buildings outside the neighborhood. Business fire detection systems and commercial fire detection systems in every type above follow the same FDNY path.

Corridors

Business Fire Alarm Installation From Montague Street to the Promenade

Business fire alarm installation in Brooklyn Heights is concentrated on a few streets. Naming a building here does not mean we have worked in it.

Montague Street, Court Street to the Promenade

Montague runs from the office buildings near Court Street past restaurants, cafes, salons and shops in four- and five-story buildings to the Promenade. The Montague Street Business Improvement District works from an office at 129 Montague Street. Its buildings show the risk clearly: in December 2021, a fire at the rear of 132 Montague Street, near Henry Street, burned through the second to fifth floors above a cafe and an eyewear shop, four firefighters were hurt, and FDNY Chief of Operations John Hodgens told reporters, "It's a tough time for a fire like this, we have a lot of businesses on the block." Commercial building fire alarm installation on Montague is mostly about stacked uses: a restaurant kitchen under offices, a salon under apartments, and an owner who must keep one system coherent while storefront tenants come and go.

Pierrepont, Remsen and Joralemon Streets

South of Montague, the cross streets hold the Center for Brooklyn History, Saint Ann's School, the Packer Collegiate Institute and co-ops and rentals of six to fourteen stories, including 75 Pierrepont Street, where the January 2026 fire began.

Clark, Pineapple and Henry Streets, the hotel blocks

The northern blocks are the old hotel district, with the St. George complex above the Clark Street station and the former Leverich Towers, now a senior residence, at 21 Clark Street.

Columbia Heights, Hicks Street and the bluff

Along the bluff, Columbia Heights and Hicks Street mix row houses with apartment buildings that look out over the Promenade and the harbor. The Hotel Margaret stood here until its 1980 fire, and the former Engine 224 firehouse, a 1903 Adams & Warren building, still stands on Hicks between Joralemon and State Streets. Buildings on the bluff contend with exposed roof bulkheads and narrow streets for staging work.

Landmarks

Fire Alarms in the City's First Historic District: Landmarks Review and Hidden Wiring

In Brooklyn Heights a fire alarm project usually needs two approvals, not one: the FDNY approves the system, and the Landmarks Preservation Commission approves how it touches a protected building. The LPC's own permit page lists "any interior work that requires a permit from the Department of Buildings" and any interior work that affects the exterior among the work that needs an LPC permit, and a filed fire alarm job is a permitted job. The good news is that the Commission says 95 percent of its approvals are issued at staff level, without a public hearing, when the work follows its rules.

What the LPC rules say about safety equipment

The Commission's Permit Guidebook chapter on health, safety and utility equipment spells out the approach. Ordinary maintenance, repair and retrofitting of health and safety equipment does not need a permit. New equipment cannot go on or in front of a primary facade "unless no other option exists, i.e., no feasible alternative exists or the location is mandated by law." Where it must go on the facade, it belongs in a discreet spot, such as under a band course, beneath a stoop or behind an areaway wall. Equipment is attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, never through cast iron or decorative surfaces. Conduit is concealed through plain masonry behind the device, and if that is impossible, "staff may approve a maximum of approximately ten inches of visible conduit," painted to match.

For fire alarm work, that means exterior appliances drawn small and kept off decorative stone, conduit run through the wall behind each device, and an LPC submission prepared alongside the FDNY drawings so neither agency sees a plan the other would reject.

Inside: plaster, moldings, shafts and closets

Most of the work is indoors, and indoors the constraint is the building's fabric. Lobbies have plaster cornices, hotel corridors coffered ceilings, churches painted walls. Fire alarm conduit installation in these buildings favors existing vertical paths: abandoned dumbwaiter shafts, old chimney flues where they are clean and approved for reuse, pipe chases, closets stacked floor over floor, and the space behind elevator machine room walls. The 205 Hicks Street co-op, in an LPC-approved heat pump project, ran its refrigerant lines through two old dumbwaiter shafts, the same kind of path that can carry a fire alarm riser. Horizontally, cable uses ceiling voids, removable molding covers and the cellar.

Interior landmarks are a separate case

A handful of interiors in and around the neighborhood are designated in their own right; the Center for Brooklyn History's interior at 128 Pierrepont Street has been a city landmark since 1982. Any work that affects a designated interior needs an LPC permit, so device placement in those rooms is designed with the Commission's staff, often using beams or air sampling instead of spot detectors.

None of this changes the FDNY process; it adds a parallel track that costs little time if it starts with the FDNY filing.

Converted Hotels

From Grand Hotels to Co-ops, Dormitories and Senior Living

A former hotel in Brooklyn Heights needs its fire alarm re-engineered whenever its occupants change, because the code asks different things of guests, residents, students and people in assisted living. The neighborhood has four very different examples within a few blocks of each other, and their histories explain most of what we look for in a converted building.

The St. George: one block, many buildings

When its last expansion opened in 1930, the Hotel St. George was New York City's largest hotel, with 2,632 rooms on the block bounded by Clark, Hicks, Pineapple and Henry Streets, in eight attached buildings around a courtyard. The Tower and Grill buildings became apartments by 1984 and are owned by a cooperative; other wings have been student housing since the late 1990s. The Clark Street subway station's only entrance is still through the hotel's ground floor.

On August 26, 1995, a 16-alarm fire broke out in the vacant Clark Building at the corner of the complex. Fire Engineering's account describes a building that had stood empty for years, where fire spread through holes cut in floors, through stairways and through elevator shafts whose hoistway doors had been removed, and where a vandalized standpipe in the basement meant, in one firefighter's words, "The water never made it to the interior crews." Wikipedia attributes the start to a man scavenging copper. No one died, but the Clark Building was demolished.

The lesson is about the edges of a complex: where one owner's building adjoins another's, or a vacant wing adjoins occupied floors, the design professional has to decide how each system sees the other and who is responsible for the empty space.

The Bossert: hotel to condominiums

The Hotel Bossert at 98 Montague Street opened in 1909 to designs by Helmle and Huberty, fourteen stories in the Renaissance Revival style, with the ship-themed Marine Roof restaurant on top. The Jehovah's Witnesses used it to house visitors until 2012. On September 1, 2026, the LPC approved a fifteenth-floor rooftop addition, and SomeraRoad and Marriott then announced 62 Ritz-Carlton-branded condominiums with a restaurant by Union Square Hospitality Group at the base. "The Hotel Bossert has long stood as one of Brooklyn's most iconic buildings, and we are honored to help write its next chapter," said Ian Ross of SomeraRoad. Construction is expected to begin late in 2026 and finish in 2029.

We are not involved in that project; it simply shows the sequence every hotel conversion follows. New apartments, a restaurant with a commercial kitchen and a new top floor in a landmarked building normally mean a new design, a new TM-1 filing and a new acceptance test.

The Leverich Towers: hotel to assisted living

The Leverich Towers Hotel at 21 Clark Street opened in 1928, sixteen stories tall. The Jehovah's Witnesses bought it in 1975 and used it as a residence for about 1,000 volunteers. After a 2017 sale it reopened as The Watermark at Brooklyn Heights, with 275 senior apartments: 29 for independent living, 204 for assisted living and 42 in a memory care wing with 24-hour care.

Assisted living and memory care change the fire alarm more than any other conversion. Residents may not wake to a horn, may need staff to help them move, and in memory care may wander toward exits; notification must reach private rooms, door hardware must release correctly on alarm while still protecting residents, and staff need to know exactly where the alarm started. That is the territory of assisted living fire alarm installation and the related nursing home fire alarm installation requirements, and it is a very different design from the hotel the building once was.

The Hotel Margaret on Columbia Heights, which burned in 1980 while being turned into condominiums, is the neighborhood's reminder that a building is most exposed while it is being changed, so we plan impairments and fire watches during conversions as carefully as the finished system.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Panel in the Old Coat Room

This describes a common Brooklyn Heights situation rather than one particular job. A pre-war co-op calls because the panel in a former lobby coat room has shown a ground fault on and off for months, always worse after rain. A shareholder's renovation upstairs may also have touched a corridor detector.

The survey finds three things. A roof bulkhead junction box lets in water, which is why the ground fault follows the weather. On the renovated floor, a corridor detector is still in place, but the contractor installed a new soffit that now sits between the detector and part of the hallway it is meant to cover. And the building's most recent drawings show a different panel from the one on the wall, which means a past replacement was never documented.

The roof box is the easy part. The soffit is a design question for an engineer, because moving or adding a device changes an approved system. The missing records are the board's real problem: before anyone can plan an upgrade, the building needs to know what the FDNY last approved. In these buildings the history matters as much as the hardware, and only a survey that looks for it will find it.

Your Questions Answered

Fire Alarm Installation in Brooklyn Heights: Questions Answered

Questions from first calls, grouped by topic.

Cost and budgeting

Our co-op's sponsor still owns several unsold apartments on Remsen Street. Does the sponsor share the cost of a new fire alarm?

Usually through maintenance like any shareholder. A building-wide fire alarm is a corporation expense, so it is paid from the budget or an assessment by share count, and the sponsor's shares carry their portion. Work inside individual apartments may be treated differently under the proprietary lease, which the board's attorney reads before the proposal is approved.

Why does a fire alarm proposal for a 1920s Brooklyn Heights building include plaster repair and painting?

Because concealing cable in an occupied pre-war building means opening some walls and ceilings and closing them again. Where a shaft or a ceiling void is available, patching is small; where cable has to cross a finished lobby or a corridor with original moldings, a plasterer and painter are part of the job.

Trust and paperwork

Why would a fire alarm for a former hotel cost more per floor than one for a newer apartment building of the same size?

Because of what the floors hide. Long hotel corridors carry more devices, old room partitions were moved when apartments were combined, ceilings were lowered or restored, and the original cable paths rarely match the new layout. Concealing new cable and restoring finishes in that kind of building takes more labor than running it through a modern drop ceiling.

A company has offered to install our new panel without filing anything because it is "the same as the old one." Can that be right?

Sometimes, but rarely. A true like-for-like repair is treated differently from a replacement; the Department of Buildings' 2024 bulletin on fire alarm work lists a panel replacement with different technology, a relocated panel or a change of occupancy among the changes that need new plans. Ask the company to put in writing why no filing is needed, and have your engineer check it.

Who may do the work

The company bidding on our panel replacement also wants to sell us the central station monitoring. Is bundling those a problem?

Not by itself, but keep them separable. The monitoring account should be in the owner's or board's name with its own term and cancellation clause. The FDNY's bulletin on central station connections says changing central stations requires reinspection, so a monitoring contract that is hard to leave can tie the building to an installer it no longer wants.

Can the engineer who sealed our building's fire alarm drawings years ago update them, or do we need a new one?

The original design professional can often update the drawings if still practicing and licensed, which saves time because they know the building. If they are unavailable, a new engineer or architect surveys the building and takes responsibility for the design going forward.

Technical

Our former hotel's panel still lists old hotel room numbers. Does relabeling it for apartment numbers involve the FDNY?

Relabeling is programming work by the certified company and should match the approved drawings, which may need updating too. If the conversion changed occupancy or layout, the relabeling is part of a filed alteration anyway.

A shareholder's new soffit now hangs between a corridor detector and part of the hallway. Is the detector still doing its job?

Possibly not. Beams and soffits that project down from a ceiling change how smoke travels, and NFPA 72 spacing assumes the detector can see the space it protects. The engineer reviews the new ceiling; the fix may be an added device or a relocated one, done by the fire alarm company and shown on revised drawings.

Our former hotel has long corridors with doors held open on magnets. What happens to those doors in an alarm?

The fire alarm releases them. Magnetic door holders are wired to a relay so that, on an alarm, power to the magnets drops and the doors close, dividing the corridor into smoke compartments. Each release is shown on the drawings and demonstrated at the acceptance test, and in senior housing it is coordinated with any secured doors.

Owners, boards and occupants

We lease the second floor of a Montague Street building for a dance studio. Can we ask the landlord for a copy of the fire alarm approval?

Yes, and you should before you invest in the space. The FDNY Letter of Approval, the approved drawings and recent inspection reports show whether the system covers your floor as it will be used. What AI Answers Get Wrong About Fire Alarms in Brooklyn Heights

AI summaries and national lead sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr answer fire alarm questions for the whole country at once. For a landmarked co-op or a converted hotel in Brooklyn Heights, those answers can be confidently wrong. These are the ones we hear most often on first calls.

"Wireless devices mean you never have to open a wall."

Wireless fire alarm devices exist and have a place, so it is natural to hope that they solve the problem of wiring a historic building.

In practice, a code-compliant wireless system still needs wired panels, power supplies, translators or gateways, notification appliances with power, and connections to elevators, fans, doors and sprinklers. Each device needs battery changes, and the design professional must accept the approach.

Wireless devices can shorten cable runs in a decorated room or an interior landmark, but they rarely remove the need to route some cable through the building, so the honest plan uses them where they help most.

"Landmark status doesn't affect interior work."

Many owners believe the Landmarks Preservation Commission cares only about facades, and some AI answers repeat it.

The Commission's own permit page says an LPC permit is needed for any interior work that requires a Department of Buildings permit, for interior work that affects the exterior, and for any work in a designated interior landmark. A filed fire alarm job in the Brooklyn Heights Historic District therefore involves the LPC, even if most such permits are issued at staff level.

The fix is simple: start the LPC application alongside the FDNY filing, and design exterior devices and conduit to the Commission's published rules from the first drawing.

"Fire alarm installation cost is a price per device."

Directory sites often quote a cost per detector or per square foot, as if a co-op were a large house.

A commercial system in New York City starts with sealed drawings, goes through an FDNY plan examination and ends with a witnessed acceptance test. In a pre-war Brooklyn Heights building, routing cable through shafts and plaster, opening apartments with a board's cooperation and repairing finishes can outweigh the cost of the devices themselves.

That is why we do not publish prices and quote each building in writing after a survey. The useful answer to a cost question is a description of the stages and what in your building drives each one.

"Converting a hotel to apartments makes the fire alarm simpler."

It sounds reasonable: hotels have strict requirements, so apartments should need less.

In reality the building gets a different set of requirements, not necessarily a smaller one. Corridors, apartment entrances, elevator lobbies, a restaurant at the base or an assisted living floor each have their own rules, and the Department of Buildings treats a change of occupancy as a change that needs new fire alarm plans. The neighborhood's own conversions, from the St. George apartments to the senior residence at 21 Clark Street to the planned condominiums at 98 Montague Street, all went or are going through new approvals.

Plan the fire alarm as part of the conversion design, not as something inherited from the hotel.

"Any licensed electrician can install a commercial fire alarm."

National guides often say so, and a renovation contractor may say the same.

The FDNY's guidance describes the installer as a NYC licensed electrician or a fire alarm vendor holding an FDNY Fire Alarm Company Certificate, and the company certificate requires a principal holding an S-97 certificate of fitness, which in turn requires NICET Level II or higher or a NYC master or special electrician license. An S-97 or S-98 holder must be present at the acceptance test.

Ask for those names and documents in writing before any work begins. A company that cannot produce them cannot carry your building through the FDNY.

"A fire alarm installation takes a few days."

The physical work on one floor can take a few days, and boards sometimes plan around that.

The FDNY estimates about 20 to 24 weeks from plan submission to final approval, with the initial plan review alone taking up to eight weeks, and design comes before submission. An LPC permit and a board vote add their own steps.

A realistic Brooklyn Heights timeline is measured in months.

"Apartment smoke alarms are the building's fire protection."

Some answers treat the smoke and carbon monoxide alarms in each apartment as the building's fire alarm.

Those alarms warn the people in one apartment. A building fire alarm, where the code requires one, watches the cellar, stairs, corridors, compactor and boiler rooms, warns the whole building and calls for help through a central station. One does not replace the other.

Rarely as it stands. A change from transient lodging to apartments is a change of occupancy, which the Department of Buildings lists among the changes that need new fire alarm plans. Can a slim remote annunciator go in a landmarked Brooklyn Heights lobby while the main panel sits somewhere less visible?
+

Often, yes. The design professional can place the control panel in a back-of-house room and put a small annunciator at the entrance firefighters use, as long as the FDNY accepts the location on the drawings. That keeps a large red cabinet off a restored marble or plaster wall.

Does assisted living in a converted Brooklyn Heights building need a different fire alarm design from ordinary apartments?
+

Yes. Assisted living and memory care residents may not hear a horn, may need staff to help them move and may need secured doors, so the design adds notification inside rooms, careful door-release logic and clear staff signaling. It is a separate occupancy with stricter rules than a rental or co-op building.

Does running a fire alarm riser through an old dumbwaiter shaft in a Brooklyn Heights co-op affect the shaft's fire protection?
+

It must not. A dumbwaiter shaft is often a convenient path from cellar to roof, but every penetration in and out of it has to be firestopped, and the engineer confirms the shaft's separation before choosing it. The route is shown on the drawings and the as-built set so later trades know what is inside.

How much visible conduit will Landmarks staff accept on a Brooklyn Heights facade?
+

Very little. The LPC's guidebook says conduit should be concealed through plain masonry behind the device, and where that is impossible staff may approve about ten inches of visible conduit, painted to match and secured in mortar joints rather than on decorative surfaces or cast iron.

Can a fire alarm in a Brooklyn Heights chapel be designed so its stained-glass windows are never touched?
+

Yes. Devices and cable can be kept off the window walls and openings entirely, with detection placed on ceilings or opposite walls, or provided by beams or air sampling where the room is tall. Any work near historic glass is planned with the congregation, and with the LPC where its review applies.

What did the 1995 St. George fire show owners of partly vacant Brooklyn Heights buildings?
+

That empty space next to occupied space is dangerous. Fire Engineering reported that the fire in the vacant Clark Building spread through holes cut in floors and open elevator shafts and that a vandalized standpipe left crews without water. Vacant wings need their fire separations, valves and detection checked, not forgotten.

Who should be the impairment coordinator when part of a Brooklyn Heights co-op's fire alarm is out of service?
+

Someone the owner designates who is in the building and reachable, usually the super or the managing agent's building manager. That person makes sure covers come off at the end of each day, tells residents and the central station what is out of service, and arranges a fire watch when the FDNY requires one.

Can fire alarm cable in a Montague Street store building run through the cellar instead of across the sales floor?
+

Often, yes. Most Montague Street buildings have a cellar under the store, and cable can travel there and rise through closets or chases to the floors above. That keeps the store open during the work and keeps conduit off the shop's finished ceiling, as long as the engineer approves the route.

What should a Brooklyn Heights co-op board ask for before approving a shareholder renovation near a fire alarm device?
+

A drawing showing every fire alarm device in and outside the apartment, a statement that none will be moved, covered or blocked without the building's fire alarm company, and, if a device must move, the engineer's revised drawing. At the end, the board should receive updated programming labels and as-built drawings.
