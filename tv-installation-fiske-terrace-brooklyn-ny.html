The Neighborhood

Why Fiske Terrace Properties Need TV Installation

Nobody who built a house in Fiske Terrace between 1905 and 1914 was thinking about a 65-inch screen. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's designation report describes a planned suburb of roughly 250 freestanding houses, put up by the T.B. Ackerson Company and a handful of other builders on land that had belonged to the oil merchant George P. Fiske. The houses are foursquares, gable-front temple houses and bungalows, dressed in Arts and Crafts, Colonial Revival and Dutch Colonial Revival styles, with a few Tudor and stucco half-timber designs mixed in. They were laid out around a parlor, a dining room and a stair hall, and in a great many of them the parlor was organized around a fireplace. Those rooms are lovely to live in and awkward to put a television in, because the walls that look biggest are usually the ones with a chimney inside, a radiator under them or a doorway cut through them.

The walls themselves are the second reason. A wood-frame house of this age was finished in plaster over wood lath, and a century later that plaster has been patched, painted many times over, skim-coated and sometimes covered with drywall in one room but not the next. A stud finder rarely gives a clean answer on it. The lath strips read as a continuous surface, nails from earlier repairs set off a metal scanner, and the framing behind it was not always placed at the even 16-inch spacing that a modern bracket's instructions assume. In the Brooklyn homeowner discussions we drew on for this page, one Brownstoner forum member noted that on a non-party wall "you can often screw through plaster into wood lath," and another warned that a TV over a mantel sits high enough to strain your neck. Both are true, and neither is enough on its own to hang a heavy screen safely. Finding the framing, fixing into it and keeping the plaster around the holes intact is the actual job.

The third reason is the way the TV signal arrives. For decades these houses got television over a roof antenna, then cable, and their phone lines famously reached them across back yards rather than from the street. When Verizon brought its FiOS fiber service to the area, it did so with tall equipment poles that the neighborhood did not expect. CBS New York reported on June 7, 2011 that a Verizon FiOS tower more than 20 feet tall had gone up in a single day in Midwood Park and Fiske Terrace, and that Verizon described it as "a safer alternative to ground-level terminals that have been subjected to graffiti and other forms of vandalism." Residents were not convinced. "We don't want to see high-tech poles in our neighborhood," Fiske Terrace Association co-president Nancy Beranbaum told the station. Today most households here stream as much as they watch cable, and where the router, the fiber or cable box and the TV sit in relation to each other matters as much as the bracket.

The fourth reason is the district itself. Fiske Terrace is a historic district, and that makes some owners nervous about drilling anything. The Commission's own permit-types page is clear that interior work needs an LPC permit only when it needs a Department of Buildings permit, affects the exterior of the building, or touches a designated interior landmark. A TV bracket inside your parlor is none of those. A TV on the porch, a dish on the roof or a cable drilled through the siding is a different matter, and we explain that line in its own section below. Most Fiske Terrace households need a TV installer who understands old plaster, respects original woodwork, hides wires the legal way, and knows where the landmark rules start and stop.

Six Fiske Terrace facts that change a TV job before we pick a bracket

  • The houses are early and freestanding. The LPC report dates most of the district's roughly 250 houses to between 1905 and 1914. Freestanding means no party wall, so nearly every TV wall is either an exterior wall of a wood-frame house or an interior partition, and both are plaster over lath unless someone has renovated.
  • Builders avoided identical neighbors. The report notes that the builders deliberately avoided putting identical houses side by side. Two houses on the same block may have their chimneys, stairs and parlors in different places, so a neighbor's TV layout is not a guide to yours.
  • The district has edges where the housing changes. Ocean Avenue frontages were left out of the district, and the only apartment house inside it is the six-story 1936 building at 800 East 17th Street. Apartment walls there and on Ocean Avenue are built differently from the houses, and we plan them differently.
  • The fiber arrived with a fight. CBS New York and Brownstoner both reported in 2011 on the Verizon FiOS pole on East 18th Street between Foster Avenue and Glenwood Road, and the LPC told Brooklyn Paper that work was postponed "until Verizon obtains approval." Many homes now have fiber or cable equipment that was added long after the house was wired, often in the cellar.
  • The paint is old. The EPA's Renovation, Repair and Painting rule applies to paid work that disturbs paint in housing built before 1978, with an interior threshold of more than six square feet of painted surface. Every Fiske Terrace house is far older than 1978, so we treat every hole as a dust-control job.
  • A falling TV is a real hazard. The Consumer Product Safety Commission's 2023 report counted 217 tip-over deaths reported from 2013 to mid-2023, and 103 of them, 47 percent, involved a television. In houses where grandchildren visit, a TV on a dresser or stand needs a strap or a bracket, not just a level surface.

Most Fiske Terrace households that call us about a TV also ask about the network behind it, because the router sits in the cellar and the screen sits in the parlor. We cover that below, and many pair the TV job with a properly run data line so the streaming box is not fighting thick plaster for a Wi-Fi signal.

Same-Day Repair in Fiske Terrace

Need Repair in Fiske Terrace? A Bracket Working Loose From Lath, a Screen Drooping Over the Mantel, or a Coax Cable Nobody Can Trace

The TV calls that cannot wait in old Flatbush frame houses tend to come in three kinds. The first is a bracket that is pulling away from the wall. On plaster over lath, the usual cause is that the bracket was fixed with fasteners that only reached the lath: wood screws biting thin strips of wood, or hollow-wall anchors that expanded in the gap behind the lath rather than behind solid framing. Lath is held to the studs with small nails, and every time someone pulls an arm-mounted TV out to plug something in, the strips flex a little more. Eventually the plaster keys break, a crack appears in a fan shape around the bracket, and the top of the screen starts to lean forward. If you can see that crack, the bracket is telling you the fixings are moving.

The second kind is a TV above a fireplace that has dropped or tilted. Chimney breasts in houses from this period are brick inside a plaster finish, and an earlier installer may have used plastic plugs in the mortar joints, or drilled into soft old brick with anchors meant for concrete. The third kind is the mess left behind when an old system comes out: a coax cable poking out of the baseboard that may or may not go anywhere, an old antenna lead running up the outside of the house, or a splitter in the cellar feeding rooms that no longer have TVs. We trace those with a tone generator before we cut or reuse anything.

If a screen is leaning, do not push it back against the wall. Unplug it, and if two adults can lift it safely, take it off the bracket and lay it flat on a blanket. Then call (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list. If the same TV goes back up on a properly fixed bracket in the same visit, it is priced as a new mount on that wall type, with the take-down added. If the problem turns out to be in the wall itself, such as plaster that has come away from its lath over a large area, we say so plainly so you can bring in a plasterer before anything heavy goes back on it. We do not patch or paint walls, and we would rather tell you that up front than leave you with a surprise.

Building Specialists

TV Installation for Fiske Terrace's Architecture

The LPC report sorts the district's houses into a few basic forms, and the form tells us more about a TV job than the style does. A foursquare and a bungalow can both be Arts and Crafts on the outside and still put the TV in completely different places inside. These are the six situations we plan for.

The foursquare

A two-and-a-half-story box with a hipped roof, four rooms to a floor and a straight or turning stair. The parlor usually has the chimney on one side and wide openings on another.

The gable-front temple house

Narrow to the street, deep to the back, with the gable end facing the sidewalk. Long side walls, but windows and the stair often break them up.

The bungalow

A low house with a broad roof and rooms tucked under it. Ceilings slope upstairs and the main floor has fewer full-height walls than you expect.

The Dutch Colonial and Tudor

Gambrel or steep roofs, dormers, stucco or half-timbering outside. Upstairs walls are short and angled; downstairs walls are often thicker than they look.

The porch and sunroom

Open front porches and porches enclosed as sunrooms, with windows on three sides. The light is beautiful and the glare on a screen is serious.

Apartments at the edges

The six-story building at 800 East 17th Street and the elevator buildings along Ocean Avenue, where walls are masonry or block and the building has its own rules.

The foursquare: a big parlor with very little blank wall

The foursquare is the house most people picture when they think of Fiske Terrace, and it is the one that most often surprises owners when they try to choose a TV wall. The parlor is square and generous, but it was designed with a fireplace on one wall, a wide cased opening to the hall or dining room on a second, and windows on the other two. The only long uninterrupted surface may be the chimney breast, and that brings the fireplace question with it. Where there is a stretch of plain plaster between a window and a corner, it is often narrower than a modern screen. We measure the clear width between trim pieces before anything else, because a 65-inch TV is about 57 inches wide, and window casings in these houses are wide and proud of the wall.

Behind the plaster, a foursquare's exterior walls are framed in wood with sheathing on the outside. That gives us something solid to fix to, but the framing spacing was set by a carpenter more than a century ago, not by a modern standard, and a bracket's slots may not line up with two framing members. We find the framing with a scanner and a small test hole hidden where the bracket plate will cover it, then choose a wall plate wide enough to reach two members, or fix a bracket that spans the gap. Plaster adds $45 to the $179.99 base. If the chosen wall is the chimney breast, the fireplace position adds $130 and brick adds $65, and we look at the mantel shelf depth before agreeing the height.

Foursquares also have a hall stair, and in many of them it turns at a landing. A 75-inch carton is long and floppy, and it does not like a turn with a newel post at the bottom. We carry large screens with two people, wrap the newel and the balusters, and if a carton cannot make the turn, we take the TV out of its box downstairs and carry it in the manufacturer's foam.

The gable-front temple house: long walls with something in the way

The gable-front house turns its narrow end to the street, so its rooms run front to back along a long side wall. On paper that is ideal for a TV. In practice, the side wall carries the windows that light the house, and in many of these houses a radiator sits under one of them. A steam or hot-water radiator under a TV is not a safety problem in itself, but it puts warm air across the back of the set, and the supply pipe often rises inside the wall where a bracket fixing might want to go. We check for that pipe before we drill. Our scanner picks up metal, and on an older wall we also look in the cellar to see where the pipes come up.

The other feature of the gable-front plan is the stair, which often runs up along one side of the house and makes the wall it backs onto an interior partition. Interior partitions in these houses can be studs with plaster on both sides, and sometimes they are thinner than the exterior walls. They are fine for a TV, but they carry sound. If the stair is on the other side of the TV wall, a soundbar fixed to that partition will be heard on the stair and in the room above. We usually suggest the console for the soundbar in that case, at $79, rather than fixing it to the wall at $129.

The bungalow: sloped ceilings and short walls

The bungalows in the district are lower and broader than the foursquares, and the upstairs rooms sit partly under the roof, so a wall may be full height for three feet and then slope. A TV on a sloped wall needs a tilting or articulating bracket that can bring the screen back to vertical, and the fixing points need to land in rafters or collar framing rather than in the plaster between them. Downstairs, a bungalow's living room often opens straight onto the porch and the dining room, so there may be only one wall that is not a window or an opening. That wall is often where the fireplace is.

Because bungalow ceilings are lower, the height question is easier: there is simply less room to mount a screen too high. The concern is the other way. A TV hung low over a built-in bench or bookcase can make the room feel crowded. We tape the outline on the wall first, at seated eye level, and you look at it from the sofa before we drill.

The Dutch Colonial and Tudor: dormers, gambrels and thick walls

The Dutch Colonial Revival houses have gambrel roofs, and the Tudor and stucco half-timber houses have steep roofs with dormers. Upstairs, both give you knee walls and dormer bays rather than long flat walls, and a bedroom TV usually ends up on the one gable wall or on the inside wall shared with the hall. Downstairs, the stucco houses can have a heavier wall build than the frame houses beside them, and a test hole tells us whether we are into wood framing, a masonry backing or a thick lime plaster that needs a different bit.

These are also the houses where original woodwork is most likely to have survived intact: beamed ceilings, built-in cabinets, wainscot. We do not fix brackets into original paneling or casings. If the only wall you want is paneled to chair height, the bracket goes into the plaster and framing above the paneling, and the cables come down behind a cord cover painted to match or through an in-wall kit that comes out above the wainscot cap.

The porch and sunroom: glare, cold and the landmark line

Many Fiske Terrace houses were built with front porches, and over the decades a good number were enclosed with windows to make sunrooms. A sunroom is a pleasant place to watch television on a winter afternoon, but it is also a room with glass on three sides, and the solid wall is usually the original front wall of the house, with the front door and a window in it. Glare from the opposite windows is the main problem, and a tilting bracket or a different seat position solves more of it than a brighter TV does. In winter, enclosed porches that are not heated as well as the house can get cold, so we suggest you check the operating temperature range printed in your TV's manual before choosing that room.

An open porch is outdoors, and in a historic district that matters. The LPC's guidebook says permits are needed for work that affects the exterior of a building in a historic district, even on parts not visible from the street, and for work in front, side and rear yards. A bracket bolted into a porch wall or a cable drilled through the siding is exterior work. We do not fix anything to the outside of a Fiske Terrace house until the owner has checked with the Commission. A TV on a rolling stand that is wheeled out to the porch for a game and back in afterward needs no permit at all.

Apartments at the edges: masonry walls and building rules

The six-story building at 800 East 17th Street, built in 1936 according to the LPC report, is the only apartment house inside the district, and the buildings along Ocean Avenue were left out of the district boundaries. These buildings are masonry, and their partitions may be block, clay tile or plaster on metal lath rather than plaster on wood lath. That changes the anchor. We confirm the wall with a test hole and use masonry or toggle fixings sized for what is actually there. Brick adds $65 to the base, plaster adds $45.

Apartment buildings also come with a management office or a board. Some want to know in advance what will be opened in a wall, and some ask for a certificate of insurance before a contractor works in the building. We can provide one when the building asks. If you rent, check your lease for any clause about alterations before we drill, because a TV on its stand at $119.99 may be the better answer if the lease is strict.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The First Twenty Minutes Inside a 1910 Fiske Terrace Parlor

These notes describe how our technicians approach a house of this age. They are our standing practice, not an account of one particular address.

Start at the stair, not the wall. Before the TV comes in, the technician walks the route from the curb to the room: the driveway, the porch steps, the front door, the hall and, if the TV is going upstairs, the stair and its landing. Blankets go over the treads and the newel post. Original stair parts in these houses are not something anyone can replace from a catalog.

Read the room's history. A room that has been renovated tells on itself. Drywall sounds hollow and flexes slightly under a knock, plaster sounds dense and does not flex. A wall with modern square-edged trim may have been opened and rebuilt; a wall with its original wide casings probably has its original plaster. Outlets that sit in the baseboard were usually added later, and where the old wiring runs matters when we choose where to cut an in-wall kit.

Look for the picture rail. Many rooms of this period have a picture rail moulding a foot or so below the ceiling. It was made for hooks and wire, not for a 50-pound screen, and it also tells us roughly where the top plate of the wall framing is. Nothing hangs from it.

Find what is in the wall. The scanner goes over the area the bracket will cover, then a little beyond it. We are looking for framing, but also for radiator pipes rising from the cellar, old knob-and-tube or armored cable, gas pipes that once fed wall lights, and the cavity of a pocket door. A pocket door slides into the wall beside its opening, and that wall has no framing where the door lives.

Drill one hole where nobody will see it. Once we have a likely framing member, a small test hole inside the outline of the bracket plate tells us what the bit actually meets: plaster, then lath, then wood, or plaster straight onto brick. Only then do we choose fixings. The vacuum attachment is on the drill for that first hole and every hole after it.

Tape the screen on the wall. The outline of the TV goes up in painter's tape at the height we think is right, and you sit where you will watch. If you want it lower or off to one side, it moves now, before the bracket is fixed. Then the work starts.

Signal and Network

Fiber Poles, Cable and Streaming: Getting the Picture From the Cellar to a Fiske Terrace Parlor

A TV is only as good as the signal that reaches it, and in Fiske Terrace that signal has an unusual history. The houses were built before radio, let alone television, and every service since then has been added to them from the outside: roof antennas, then telephone and cable lines, then fiber. Brooklyn Paper reported that Verizon put up a 20-foot pole on East 18th Street between Foster Avenue and Glenwood Road without the permits it needed, saying the poles protect access points to its underground fiber network. Lisi De Bourbon, a spokeswoman for the Landmarks Preservation Commission, told the paper: "Work on the tall pole in Midwood has been postponed until Verizon obtains approval." Brownstoner reported on August 1, 2011 that the Commission was working with Verizon on pole designs "consistent with the character of New York City's historic districts," and that plans for more poles in the district were on hold. By December 2012, Bklyner was reporting that FiOS had come to Ditmas Park and was available in select areas from Prospect Park to Avenue J.

What that history leaves behind, inside the house, is a set of services that arrived at different times and ended up in different places. It is common to find the fiber or cable entry in the cellar, a router on a cellar shelf or in a back room, an old coax splitter feeding cables to rooms that no longer have a TV, and the parlor TV two floors or two walls away from all of it. Plaster on lath, especially plaster on metal lath where a room has been repaired, is hard on Wi-Fi. Owners tell us their new streaming TV buffers in the evening, and the TV is not the problem.

There are three ways we deal with that, and we tell you which one fits before we start. The first is to move the streaming to a wired connection: a data line from the router to the TV wall, run through the cellar ceiling and up inside a wall or through a closet, terminated on a wall plate behind the screen and tested. The second is to move the router or add a properly placed access point, which is often the cheaper fix when the router is simply hidden in the wrong corner. The third, for a TV that will only ever stream, is to leave the wiring alone and set up the TV and device carefully on the existing Wi-Fi, which is what the setup-only visit at $195 for the first hour covers. Data lines and access points are quoted separately on the call once we know the route, because the distance from a Fiske Terrace cellar to a second-floor bedroom varies a lot from house to house.

One thing we do not do in this district is run new cable along the outside of the house to save time. The LPC's guidebook treats work affecting the exterior of a building in a historic district as needing a permit, including parts not visible from the street. Cable on the siding, a new antenna on the roof or a dish on a rear wall all fall on the outside of that line. Cables that need to travel from the cellar to an upper floor go up inside the house, through closets, chases or the wall cavity, and that is something we plan when we look at your photos.

What the cable box, fiber box and streaming stick each need from the TV wall

Before we choose a bracket, we ask what will be plugged into the TV, because it decides what has to come through the wall. A streaming stick plugs into the back of the TV and needs only power and, ideally, a wired network line. A cable or fiber TV box usually needs a coax or network connection from the provider, power, and an HDMI cable to the TV, and it needs to sit somewhere with air around it, not inside a closed cabinet with the door shut. A game console needs the same, plus room for controllers and a short cable to a soundbar if the household plays with sound. A soundbar needs power and one cable to the TV.

If the box and console sit on a console table under the TV, the in-wall kit carries the HDMI, network and power cables from behind the screen down to just above the console, and nothing shows. If they live in a cabinet across the room, the cables have to travel farther, and we discuss whether a longer run through the wall is worth it or whether the box should move. In every case, the power for the TV goes through the kit's own recessed receptacle and inlet. We do not run the TV's own power cord inside the wall.

Walls and Woodwork

Plaster, Lath, Picture Rails and Pocket Doors: What Holds a TV in a Fiske Terrace House

A plaster wall over wood lath has three layers that matter. On the outside is a hard finish coat of plaster, often less than half an inch thick in total with the coats beneath it. Under that is the lath, narrow strips of wood nailed across the framing with gaps between them so the plaster could push through and grip. Behind the lath is the framing. The plaster's strength depends on those little knobs of plaster, called keys, that hold it to the lath. Old keys break with age, with vibration and with careless drilling, and a wall can look perfect while the plaster in one area has come loose from the lath behind it. Press gently with the flat of your hand: if the plaster moves or sounds different in one patch, that patch is loose, and nothing heavy should be fixed through it.

That is why we fix a TV bracket into the framing, not into the plaster, and why plaster costs $45 more than drywall on our list. The extra time goes into finding the framing reliably, drilling plaster without cracking it, using bits that cut cleanly rather than punching, and setting fasteners long enough to pass through plaster and lath and still bite deeply into the wood. Where the framing does not line up with the bracket, we use a wall plate or bracket design that reaches it rather than trusting hollow-wall anchors in the gaps.

Original features add their own rules. A picture rail is decorative and lightly fixed; nothing hangs from it, and the bracket goes below it. A pocket door slides into a hollow pocket beside its opening, so the wall on that side has no framing for several feet, and a bracket cannot go there at all. A plate rail or chair rail can push a bracket away from the wall unless the screen sits entirely above or below it. Built-in cabinets and window seats are not structural, and we fix to the wall behind them only if the cabinet is not in the way of the bracket plate. A chimney breast is brick under its plaster, which takes masonry anchors well if they go into the brick and not the mortar, and that is the $65 brick add-on.

Old paint and the dust from every hole

Every house in Fiske Terrace is many decades older than 1978, the cutoff year in the EPA's Renovation, Repair and Painting rule, and old interior walls often carry many layers of paint. The EPA rule applies to paid work that disturbs more than six square feet of painted surface on the interior of a pre-1978 home, and the EPA states that "No firm may perform, offer or claim to perform renovations covered by the RRP Rule without certification from EPA." A TV installation sits well below that size. A bracket needs a handful of holes, and an in-wall power and cable kit needs two openings about the size of a junction box. Small is not the same as harmless, though, especially in a house with young children or a pregnant member of the household.

So we drill every hole with a vacuum attachment held at the bit, lay a drop cloth under the work, cut the kit openings with a hand tool that makes chips rather than fine dust where we can, and wipe down the floor and trim before we leave. If you want a TV wall rebuilt, a large area of plaster removed, or a room repainted around the new TV, that is renovation work beyond a TV installation, and it is a job for a firm set up for it under the EPA rule. We do not patch or paint walls, and we will tell you before we start if the job you are describing crosses that line.

Hiding cables without breaking the electrical code

The most common shortcut we see in Brooklyn houses is the TV's own power cord pushed into a hole behind the screen and pulled out of another hole down by the outlet. It looks tidy and it is not allowed: a flexible cord is not permitted as a substitute for fixed wiring through a wall. In a StreetEasy discussion about mounting a TV in a New York City apartment, one homeowner wrote that "Electrical outlets cannot be hidden behind drywall," and another added that metallic armored cable is what belongs in New York City walls. The point both were making is the same: power inside a wall has to be done with the right components.

An in-wall power and cable kit is how we do it. It puts a recessed receptacle behind the TV and a matching inlet near the existing outlet below, connected with cable that is rated to be inside a wall, plus a separate open pass-through for the low-voltage HDMI and network cables. It is $169 on our list. In a plaster wall the openings are cut carefully so the plaster around them does not crack, and the kit's frames grip the wall from behind. If the wall cavity is blocked by a fire stop or a horizontal framing member halfway down, we tell you before cutting the second opening, and a paintable cord cover at $59 is the fallback. Historic District

Inside Is Yours, Outside Is the Commission's: TV Work in the Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park Historic District

The Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park Historic District was designated on March 18, 2008, after a public hearing in October 2007, and it covers about 250 houses from Foster Avenue to Avenue H between the Brighton line and East 19th Street. Owners sometimes assume that landmark status means they need permission to hang anything. For a TV inside the house, that is not how the rules read. The LPC's permit-types page lists the interior work that needs a permit: interior work that requires a Department of Buildings permit, interior work that affects the exterior of a building, such as louvers and vents, and work in spaces that are themselves designated interior landmarks. A TV bracket in a parlor, a soundbar in a bedroom and an in-wall power and cable kit between two rooms are none of those.

The exterior is different. The LPC's Permit Guidebook says permits are required for work affecting the exterior of a building in a historic district, "even on parts of the building that are not visible from the street, as well as work in front, side, and rear yards." For TV work, that covers more than people expect: a satellite dish on a rear wall or roof, an outdoor antenna, a TV bracket fixed to the wall of an open porch, a cable drilled through siding or a window frame to reach a garage, and a TV mounted on the outside of a detached garage facing the yard. None of those is impossible. Each is a question for the Commission before a contractor touches the building.

Here is how we keep a job on the right side of that line. We work inside the building envelope. Cables travel inside walls, closets and the cellar, not along the outside. If a customer wants a TV on an open porch, we can set it up on a stand that is moved in and out, which changes nothing on the building. If a customer wants something fixed outside, we explain that the owner needs to clear it with the Commission first, and we do the work once they have. And when we drill an interior hole near a window or an outside wall, we make sure it does not come through the other side.

Many Fiske Terrace owners also belong to the Fiske Terrace Association, which LPC's report describes as founded in 1907, or the Midwood Park homeowners' group. We are not connected with either. Fireplaces, plaster and what can go wrong

We still light the parlor fireplace a few evenings every winter. Will the heat rising up the chimney breast harm a TV mounted above the mantel?

It can, and a working fireplace is the one case where we slow the conversation down. Heat and smoke rise up the face of the chimney breast and collect under the mantel shelf and above it, exactly where the TV would hang, and TV manufacturers publish operating temperature limits for a reason. The simple test is your hand: after the fire has been going for an hour, hold your palm flat against the plaster where the bottom of the screen would be. If it is uncomfortable to keep it there, the TV should not be there either. A deep mantel shelf helps by deflecting heat outward, and a tilting bracket that hangs the screen a little higher and farther out helps more. If the fireplace is used often, we usually suggest a different wall for the TV and the chimney breast for the art. If you go ahead, the fireplace position is $130 and the brick is $65 on top of the $179.99 base.

What happens if the hundred-year-old plaster cracks while you are drilling?

The honest answer is that old plaster can crack, and our process is built to find out whether yours will before the bracket goes up. The test hole is drilled first, inside the bracket outline where it will be hidden, and it tells us whether the plaster is well keyed to its lath. If the plaster around it is loose, sounds hollow over a wide area or flexes when pressed, we stop and show you, and we suggest either a different spot on the wall or a plasterer before anything heavy is hung there. Where the plaster is sound, we drill with sharp bits at low speed without hammer action, and we pre-drill through plaster and lath before driving fasteners so that the fastener does not wedge the plaster apart. A hairline crack at the edge of a hole is sometimes unavoidable in very old plaster, and the bracket plate covers it. We do not patch or paint, so if you are worried about the surface, it is worth knowing that before we start.

We want TVs on both sides of the same inside wall, one facing the parlor and one facing the dining room. Can one wall carry both?

Yes, if the two brackets are planned together. An interior partition in these houses is framing with plaster on each face, and both brackets can fix into the same framing members, but they should not share the same bay of the wall if both TVs also get in-wall power and cable kits. Two kits in one bay would put two sets of openings in the same narrow cavity, and the second kit's cables would have to pass the first one's frames. We offset the TVs by at least one framing bay, or stack the kits so they use separate bays. Sound is the other issue: two TVs on one thin partition will hear each other, so we usually suggest that only one of them gets a wall-mounted soundbar. Each TV is priced separately on the list, from the $179.99 base plus $45 for plaster.

Boxes, antennas and the signal

We would like no boxes at all in the parlor. Can the cable box and the router stay in the cellar, with only the TV upstairs?

Often, yes. A streaming TV needs only power and a network connection, so if the router stays in the cellar, a tested data line from the cellar to the TV wall gives the TV a steady connection with nothing visible but a wall plate behind the screen. A cable or fiber TV box is harder, because it has to send its picture to the TV over HDMI and receive the remote's signal. Long HDMI runs need the right kind of cable or an extender, and the remote needs either a repeater or a box that responds to the TV's own remote through HDMI control. Some providers also offer an app on the TV that replaces the box entirely. We tell you which route your provider and your house allow after looking at the photos, and the cable run is quoted separately because a run from a Fiske Terrace cellar to a parlor varies with the house.

There is still an old TV antenna on our chimney from the 1960s. Can our new TV use it?

Sometimes. Over-the-air broadcasts still exist, and an old antenna can pick them up if its lead-in cable is intact and the antenna itself has not corroded or turned. What we can do at the TV end is test the old lead-in, connect it through a modern coax run to the TV, and scan for channels so you can see what comes in. What we do not do is climb onto the roof, repair or re-aim the antenna, or replace it. Anything on the roof or chimney is on the outside of the building, and in the historic district a new or replacement antenna is a question for the Landmarks Preservation Commission before anyone installs it. If the old antenna gives a poor picture, an indoor antenna placed near an upstairs window is the low-effort alternative, and that stays entirely inside the house.

We turned the old detached garage at the end of our driveway into a workshop. Can a TV go out there, and how does it get a signal?

The TV inside the garage is interior work, the same as a TV in the house, and we treat the garage wall like any other: we find out whether it is framed, stud and siding, or masonry, and fix the bracket accordingly. Getting a signal there is the real question. Running a new cable from the house to the garage across the yard is exterior work in the yard, which the LPC's guidebook includes among the things that need a permit in a historic district. So we look first at whether the garage already has power and a cable or phone line, and whether a Wi-Fi signal from the house reaches it or can be reached with an outdoor-rated access point mounted inside a window. If the garage gets cold in winter, check that your TV's manual allows it to be stored and run at those temperatures, because many sets are not meant to sit in an unheated space.

Households, children and moving on

Our grandchildren visit most weekends and climb on everything. If we do not want to drill, is a TV on a low dresser safe?

Not without a strap. The Consumer Product Safety Commission's 2023 tip-over report counted 103 deaths involving a falling television out of 217 tip-over deaths reported from 2013 to mid-2023, and children under 18 were 155 of the 217. The danger is a child pulling on the TV or climbing the furniture under it. If you do not want a bracket in the plaster, the TV can go on its stand on the dresser for $119.99, and we recommend an anti-tip strap from the back of the TV to the wall behind it, plus a furniture strap on the dresser itself. A strap needs only one small fixing into the wall's framing, much less than a bracket. The other option is a wall mount, which takes the TV off the furniture altogether. Either way, keep remotes and toys off the top of the dresser, because those are what children climb to reach.

We are selling the house in the spring. Should the TV bracket come off before the listing photos, and what is left on the wall afterward?

That depends on whether the TV is staying with the house. If it is going with you, it often looks better in photos to leave a well-placed TV on its bracket than to leave a bare bracket or a set of holes. When the time comes to move, taking down the TV is $85 on our list, and the bracket and its fasteners come out with it. What is left is a set of fixing holes and, if there was an in-wall kit, two framed openings with their covers. Those can stay as they are, since many buyers want a TV in the same spot, or a plasterer or painter can close them. We do not patch or paint walls, so if you want the wall restored before closing, book the plasterer after our visit, not before it.

Our family has had the house since the 1950s, and there is wallpaper over the plaster in the living room. Does the wallpaper change anything?

A little. Wallpaper hides the state of the plaster under it, so a crack or a loose patch may not show until we drill. We knock and press over the area first, then make the test hole. Where we drill through paper, we start each hole with a sharp bit so the paper does not tear or wind around it, and where an in-wall kit opening has to be cut, we score the paper cleanly along the outline before cutting the plaster, so that the kit's frame sits on a clean edge. Old papers are sometimes layered several deep, and the paste behind them can make the surface feel softer than plain plaster. None of that stops the job. It just means the first twenty minutes take a few minutes longer.
