The Neighborhood

Why Weeksville Properties Need Security Camera Repair

Weeksville began in 1838, when James Weeks, a free Black stevedore from Virginia, bought land in what was then the countryside east of Brooklyn, and by the 1850s it was one of the largest free Black communities in the country. Today it has no official boundary. Most maps fold it into the eastern end of Crown Heights, some older ones into Bedford-Stuyvesant, and the city's commercial district study pairs it with Ocean Hill. For this page we mean, roughly, the blocks from Atlantic Avenue south to Eastern Parkway between Troy Avenue and Ralph Avenue, with the corridors just beyond them. That is the history in brief. For a camera repair, what matters is what got built here afterward and who ended up owning each system.

The city's lot records give a clear picture. Inside those four streets, MapPLUTO lists 2,089 lots with apartments. 1,079 of them are one- and two-family houses holding 2,033 homes; 642 are buildings with three to seven apartments; 220 have eight to nineteen; and 148 have twenty or more, together holding 7,040 apartments. Seventy-four lots with three or more apartments were built in 2010 or later, adding 1,194 homes. In plain terms, about half the lots are owner-run houses where the person with the camera problem is usually the person who paid for the cameras, but most of the apartments are in larger buildings where the cameras belong to a landlord, a co-op, a manager or a housing agency. That split shapes almost every repair call: before we talk about the fault, we have to know whose system it is.

The houses came in layers. Brownstoner's history of the area describes how the original wood-framed houses were replaced by masonry row houses as the street grid pushed east in the 1880s and 1890s, often running straight through older homes. A handful of the frame houses survive, including the four Hunterfly Road houses on Bergen Street, restored again in a $4 million project that finished in February 2026, and a few private ones such as the two-story shingled house at 1718 Dean Street, seventeen feet wide, that one family held from 1897 to 2001. On houses held that long, camera systems tend to be layered too: a coax DVR from one decade, a Wi-Fi doorbell from the next, and a phone app on an account set up by a relative who has since moved away. A repair here often means working out which of those pieces is still connected to what before anything is replaced.

The public housing came next. Kingsborough Houses, finished in 1941 between Ralph and Rochester Avenues and Pacific and Bergen Streets, has 16 six-story buildings and 1,148 apartments, plus a senior Extension added in 1966. Weeksville Gardens, two buildings with 256 apartments between Pacific and Dean Streets and Schenectady and Troy Avenues, opened in 1974 and moved from NYCHA to private management through the city's PACT program in February 2020, followed by a large repair program. Citywide, NYCHA told the City Council in April 2025 that it repairs most cameras in-house and aims to fix a reported camera within 24 to 72 hours, and that spray paint and cracked housings are the most common damage. A month earlier, the Department of Investigation reported that at 44 of 63 NYCHA senior buildings it checked, the cameras could not be viewed remotely, citing "outdated or incompatible software or hardware causing login, connection, and data storage issues." That report did not name Kingsborough, and NYCHA's own cameras are NYCHA's to repair, but its findings describe the same three failures we see in private Weeksville buildings every month: logins that no longer work, recorders that no longer talk to their cameras, and drives that are full or failing.

The newest layer is the one owners often assume is too new to break. Since 2010, buildings have gone up on lots that sat empty for decades: Weeksville Place, eight stories and 45 apartments on Prospect Place at Buffalo Avenue, finished in early 2025; the 199-apartment lottery building at 1921 Atlantic Avenue, whose lottery closed in January 2023; and others closer to Ralph Avenue. A planned 13-story senior building called Weeksvillage on Bergen Street at Kingsborough was shaped by a 2019 community visioning that asked for "video intercom and security cameras" along with better lighting. Buildings like these come with network cameras, a video intercom and a recorder in a locked room. Repair guides put the typical life of a recorder's hard drive at three to five years of nonstop recording, which means the first wave of these buildings is reaching the age when drives, power supplies and the original installer's logins all start to cause trouble, often after the installer's warranty period has ended.

Running through all of this is the power supply. In May 2025 a manhole fire on Albany Avenue cut power to about 200 Con Edison customers in Crown Heights, with a cellar fire in a house on Lincoln Place between Albany and Troy Avenues the same night. During the June 2025 heat wave, Con Edison shut off almost 900 customers temporarily in Central Brooklyn to prevent longer outages and lowered the voltage near 1414 Eastern Parkway. Those reports name Crown Heights and Central Brooklyn rather than particular Weeksville blocks, so we do not claim which houses lost power. What we can say is what happens to camera systems in events like these: a recorder comes back with its clock reset, a PoE switch port no longer powers its camera, or a drive that was already worn stops mounting. Those failures often go unnoticed for weeks, until the day someone needs to play something back.

Need Repair in Weeksville? A Recorder That Stopped Saving, a Hallway Gone Dark, or a Lobby System Nobody Can Log Into

Urgent calls from Weeksville usually arrive in one of three forms. The first is the empty playback: something happened on the stoop or at the store counter, a detective from the precinct or a neighbor asks for the video, and the recorder shows live pictures but nothing saved for that day, or footage stamped with a date years in the past. The second is the dark hallway: one or several cameras in a walk-up or a newer building have been out for weeks, tenants have complained, and the owner or manager needs them back before the next incident. The third is the locked system: the cameras work, but the person who set them up is gone, the password is unknown, and nobody in the house or the building can see or export anything.

If footage from a particular day matters, do not unplug, restart or reset the recorder. Recorders overwrite their oldest video once the drive fills, so time is already working against you, and a restart can clear the log that shows when a camera dropped or the drive stopped writing. Text us the date and time you need, the camera it should be on, and a photo of the recorder's front or its screen. We plan the visit so the clip is copied before anything else is touched, and we can tell you over the phone whether the recorder is likely to still have it.

For everything else the path is short. The $150 Brooklyn service call books a two-hour arrival window. The technician finds the fault, tells you what failed and why, and writes down the price from our Brooklyn list before any part is replaced. Most single-fault repairs are finished on the same visit, and before we leave we play back a recording from each repaired camera so you can see that it is saving as well as showing a live picture.

Recording and playback repair

Recorders that show a live picture but save nothing, keep only a day or two, or stamp footage with the wrong date. We read the drive's health, the recording schedule and the clock, fix what is wrong, and play back a test clip from every camera before we leave.

Hard drive and recorder replacement

Drives worn out by years of nonstop recording, recorders that restart, beep or freeze on start-up, and boxes too old to support current phone apps. We copy what can be copied, then replace the drive or the recorder and carry the camera names and schedules over where the model allows.

Outage and surge recovery

Systems that came back wrong after a power cut or a voltage drop: a lost clock, a switch port that no longer powers its camera, a plug-in supply that died, a drive that will not mount. We find every part that did not survive and add protection where it is worth the money.

Dead camera diagnosis

One camera or several dark at once. Power, switch port, connector, cable, camera and recorder channel are tested in that order, so you pay for the part that failed and not for the three around it.

Coax and connector repair

The BNC connectors, splitter cables and plug-in power adapters of older analog and HD-over-coax systems, which corrode at the facade and come loose in the cellar. Reterminated or replaced along the existing path wherever the old cable still tests good.

Network camera and PoE repair

Network cameras that drop off the recorder in newer buildings and upgraded houses: failed PoE ports, overloaded switches, address conflicts, firmware that no longer matches the recorder, and cable damaged above a ceiling.

Login and phone access recovery

Lost admin passwords, apps tied to the email of a relative, a former super or an installer who has moved on, and phone viewing that stopped after an internet provider change. We recover access, remove old users and give each person who should have access a login of their own.

Picture repair by day and night

Fogged or scratched domes, infrared bouncing off a nearby wall, cameras blinded by a bright sidewalk, and night pictures that went to grain when a light nearby changed. We separate a failing camera from a changed scene and fix whichever it turns out to be.

Footage export for incidents

Three- to nineteen-apartment buildings from the early twentieth century, including small co-ops. Hallway, entrance and cellar cameras on one recorder in a locked room, owned by a landlord or a board.

Masonry row houses and two-families: the recorder nobody has looked at in years

The typical Weeksville row-house system was bought as a kit or put in by a local installer: four to eight coax cameras, a DVR on a cellar shelf or in a closet, and a plug-in power box with a bundle of thin cables coming out of it. It works for years without anyone touching it, and that is the problem. The drive inside has been writing video every minute of every day, the clock has drifted or reset after a power cut, and the phone app that used to show the cameras was replaced by a new version that no longer talks to the old recorder. When the owner finally needs a clip, the recorder turns out to have stopped saving months ago.

The repair usually begins at the recorder, not at the camera. We read the drive's health and its oldest and newest recordings, check the date and time, and look at the recording schedule. If the drive is failing, it is replaced and the recorder is set to overwrite properly and to send an alert if it stops recording. If the recorder itself is too old for current apps, we explain the choice between a hybrid recorder that keeps the existing coax cameras and adds room for network cameras later, and a straight replacement of the same type. On a house with two or three apartments, we also look at where the system draws its power, because a camera box plugged into a tenant's outlet goes dark the day that tenant's breaker trips or their account closes.

Facade work is the other common cause on these houses. Pointing, cornice repair, new windows and new doors all happen around cameras, and the cable is often cut or pinched in the process. Where the old cable path still works, we reuse it and replace only the damaged section and the connectors; where it does not, we price a new run along the same path and show you the difference before we start.

Surviving wood-frame houses: small walls, soft wood, and water on the cable

The frame houses that survive in Weeksville are small. Brownstoner described the shingled house at 1718 Dean Street as about seventeen feet wide and roughly 1,020 square feet over two floors, with the kitchen in a rear extension. On a house like that, every camera is close to a window or a door, and every cable goes through wood or siding. The usual failure is water. If the hole beside a camera was not sealed, or the cable was not given a drip loop below the entry, rain runs along it into the wall and into the connector, and the camera dies months later with green corrosion inside the junction box.

We repair these by checking the wall around the hole as well as the camera, sealing what we open, and replacing corroded connectors with weatherproof ones rather than wrapping old ones in tape. Mounts are fixed into solid framing, not just into siding that moves with the seasons. Where siding is old cement shingle, we work with the existing holes and mounting points rather than drilling new ones, and we tell you before the visit if a repair would need the siding disturbed.

Small walk-ups: three to nineteen apartments, one recorder, one person with the key

MapPLUTO counts 862 lots inside our outline with three to nineteen apartments, holding 4,868 homes. A walk-up like the four-story, seventeen-unit co-op that StreetEasy lists at 578 Ralph Avenue, built in 1922, typically has a camera at the street door, one in the vestibule or by the mailboxes, one or two on the stairs, and one in the cellar or the back yard, all on a recorder in a locked room. Tenants notice when a camera goes dark; the owner, the board or the super is the one who can let a technician in and approve the bill.

Two things decide most walk-up repairs. The first is the stair run: cameras on upper landings are fed by cable that climbs the stairwell in molding, conduit or the old dumbwaiter shaft, and a fault anywhere on that climb can take out every camera above it. The second is the recorder room: heat from a boiler room next door, a dusty cellar shelf or a closet with no ventilation shortens a recorder's life. We test the run floor by floor, fix the break where it is, and move or ventilate the recorder if heat is the cause. If the building also has an intercom with a camera at the door, we check that the two systems are not fighting over the same power supply.

Public and PACT housing: knowing which cameras are yours

Kingsborough Houses is a NYCHA development, and NYCHA told the City Council in 2025 that it repairs most of its cameras in-house. Weeksville Gardens has been run by a private manager under PACT since 2020, so its building cameras are the manager's. In both cases, the lobby, elevator, hallway and grounds cameras are not ours to touch, and a private company should not be working on them without the agency's or manager's written direction. What we can repair is the equipment residents own themselves: a doorbell camera or an indoor camera inside an apartment, a small camera facing the apartment door from inside, or the phone app and account behind them.

For residents, the practical help is telling the two apart and pointing the building's problems to the right office. If the building camera by your mailboxes has been dead for weeks, the report goes to the development's management office or, for NYCHA, through its own repair system. If your own camera inside the apartment stopped connecting, it is usually the apartment's Wi-Fi, a changed router password or an app account in someone else's name, and that is a repair we can do in one short visit. NYCHA's resident rules call for written consent before residents mount equipment on building surfaces, so we keep resident cameras on shelves, inside door frames or on removable mounts unless the resident has that consent.

New affordable and lottery buildings: young systems, first failures

The buildings that have gone up since 2010 tend to have the best equipment on the block and the least documentation. A typical system has network cameras on every floor and at each entrance, PoE switches in a telecom closet, a network video recorder and a video intercom whose screens or phone app show the lobby camera. When the building opened, the installer held the logins and the warranty. A few years later the warranty period has ended, the installer's number rings out, and the managing agent has a dead camera on the fourth floor and no password.

Most of these repairs are not exotic. A PoE switch port fails, a camera's firmware no longer matches the recorder after an update, a drive that has recorded thirty cameras around the clock for four years starts throwing errors, or a camera above a dropped ceiling was knocked loose during a plumbing repair. We work with the managing agent or the board, recover or reset the administrator account with proof of ownership, document which port feeds which camera, and leave the building with its own administrator login rather than a contractor's. If the system is still inside an installer's warranty, we tell you so before we touch anything, because opening it could complicate that claim.

Churches and storefront buildings: two systems, two owners, one door

The city's Ocean Hill–Weeksville commercial study counted 79 houses of worship, 65 salons and barbershops and 52 delis and bodegas among 560 storefronts on its corridors, and 83% of the merchants it surveyed rent their space. That mix produces a familiar repair call: a store with its own cameras on its own recorder, apartments above with the landlord's cameras on another, and a church or community room sharing a wall with both. When the camera over the shared doorway goes dark, the first question is whose it is.

Congregations bring their own patterns. Berean Missionary Baptist Church on Bergen Street, founded in 1850, uses its 1894 building as a fellowship hall beside the newer church it built in 1961, with a parsonage behind; many congregations here run more than one building and more than one recorder, often set up by volunteers. On church and store repairs we confirm who owns the system and who pays, put the recorder and logins in the organization's name rather than a former volunteer's or a previous tenant's, and schedule around services or business hours.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Why We Ask Weeksville Callers to Photograph the Recorder First

This is how our technicians handle the most common Weeksville call, written as our working method rather than a story about any one house. Someone says "the cameras stopped working." Before we book, we ask for two pictures: the label on the back or bottom of the recorder, and a screenshot of whatever the screen or the phone app shows right now. The label tells us the brand, the model and the age of the system, and that tells us which generation of system it is. A coax DVR from the early 2010s, a network recorder from a building that opened in 2023, and a Wi-Fi camera with no recorder at all fail in completely different ways and need different parts in the van.

The screenshot tells us the rest. If every camera is dark at once, the fault is usually power, the recorder or the switch, not the cameras. If one camera is dark and the others are fine, it is usually that camera's connector, cable or power. If the live view is fine but the playback is empty, it is the drive, the schedule or the clock. If the app shows nothing but the recorder's own screen in the cellar is fine, it is the network or the login, and that may not need a visit at all. Sorting this out costs nothing, and it means the technician arrives with the right drive, supply or camera, or tells you before the visit that no part is needed.

Fault by Fault

Worn Drives, Stranded Logins, Outage Damage, Water at the Facade and Dark Corridors: How Weeksville Camera Systems Go Wrong

Worn drives: the part that never gets a day off

A recorder's hard drive works harder than any other part of a camera system. It writes video from every camera, all day and all night, and on most systems it starts overwriting the oldest video the moment it is full. Repair guides commonly put the replacement point for a recorder drive at three to five years, depending on how much it records, and forum veterans report DVRs as a whole lasting five to seven years, often with only the drive dying.

In Weeksville this shows up two ways. Row-house systems installed a decade ago are often on their original drive, with no alert set to say it has stopped writing. And the first wave of newer buildings is reaching three to five years of service with dozens of cameras recording to one recorder. The symptoms are the same in both: playback with gaps, a recorder that beeps or restarts, a "disk error" or "HDD abnormal" message, or a system that seems fine until someone asks for last Tuesday. The fix is a surveillance-rated drive of the right size, a check that the recorder overwrites properly, and an alert that tells someone the next time it stops.

Stranded logins: systems nobody can get into

The Department of Investigation's 2025 report on NYCHA senior-building cameras is a useful warning for every building owner: at 44 of 63 buildings, the cameras could not be viewed remotely, mostly because software and hardware were outdated or incompatible, causing login, connection and storage problems. Private systems strand themselves the same way. A phone app is replaced by a new version that will not connect to an older recorder. An internet provider swaps the router, and the recorder's network settings no longer fit. The admin password belongs to an installer who has closed shop, a relative who moved south, or a super who left the job.

Recovering access is one of the most common things we do in Weeksville, and one of the cheapest when it goes smoothly. With proof that you own or manage the property, most recorders can be reset or recovered through the manufacturer's process without losing recorded video, though some models erase settings or footage in a reset, and we tell you which kind yours is before we start. Afterward, the owner gets the administrator login, each family member, tenant leader or staff member who should see the cameras gets an ordinary account, and anyone who should no longer have access is removed.

Outage damage: what does not come back after the power does

Crown Heights and Central Brooklyn lost power several times in 2025, from the May manhole fire on Albany Avenue to the June heat-wave shutoffs and voltage reductions, and News 12 reported that winter snowmelt mixed with road salt can seep into underground electrical equipment. Water at the facade: connectors, holes and cables that drink rain

On masonry fronts and frame walls alike, water gets into camera systems at three places: the camera housing if its seal has aged, the connector where the camera's pigtail meets the cable, and the hole where the cable enters the wall. A fogged lens, a picture that rolls or flickers in wet weather, or a camera that drops out every rainstorm and comes back a day later all point here. Taping a corroded connector buys a few weeks. What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Weeksville

Search summaries, chat assistants and home-service sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr repeat a set of general claims about camera repair. Some are fine as general advice. Here are seven we hear from Weeksville callers who read them first, and what our repairs on these blocks actually show.

"A good security camera system lasts about ten years"

Cameras can. NYCHA told the City Council in 2019 that it expects its cameras to last about ten years. But a camera system is not one part, and the part that fails first is the recorder's drive, which repair guides put at three to five years of nonstop recording.

That gap matters in Weeksville because so many row-house systems were put in once and never looked at again. The cameras still show a picture, so the owner assumes the system is fine, while the drive behind them stopped saving long ago.

Plan on the cameras lasting and the drive not. Check playback once a month, turn on the recorder's drive alert, and budget for a drive well before the cameras need replacing.

"If the cameras stopped recording, the hard drive is dead"

Sometimes. But one repair company's answer to this question puts it plainly: "Running out of storage is the most common reason" cameras stop recording. A full drive that is not set to overwrite looks dead from the outside, and so does a recording schedule that was switched off or a clock that reset after an outage.

Replacing a drive in those cases costs money and leaves the real fault in place. The new drive fills up, and the recorder stops again.

We read the drive's health first, then the overwrite setting, the schedule and the clock. Only a drive that fails its health check gets replaced.

"After a power outage, just restart everything"

A restart is the instinct, and sometimes it works. But one recovery guide lists what usually goes wrong after an outage: the router, switch and recorder "started in the wrong order", the drive "reports an abnormal state", or the time "was lost". It also warns that "repeated power cycling can worsen storage and power faults."

In Central Brooklyn, where Con Edison shut off almost 900 customers during the June 2025 heat wave and a manhole fire cut power on Albany Avenue that May, owners who pulled the plug three or four times trying to fix a system sometimes turned a recoverable drive into a dead one.

If cameras do not come back, restart once, in order: internet equipment, then the switch, then the recorder. If that does not work, stop and call, especially if you need footage from before the outage.

"'Video loss' on the screen means the camera is broken"

Reolink's own list of causes for video loss starts with insufficient power and a faulty PoE switch or injector, then long cable runs, loose connectors, infrared or filter faults, a recorder that cannot decode the camera's resolution, and address conflicts. The camera itself is only one item.

On Weeksville row houses, the most common cause is a corroded connector at the facade or a tired multi-camera power box in the cellar. In the newer buildings, it is usually a switch port.

We test in order from power to camera, and we replace the camera last, only once everything in front of it has passed.

"Hikvision and Dahua are banned, so those systems can't be fixed"

The FCC's November 2022 rules stop new equipment authorizations for covered Hikvision and Dahua products. According to the Security Industry Association's summary, they do not affect products already authorized and on the U.S. market.

Many Brooklyn systems run on recorders from these makers or on equipment built from them. Those recorders and cameras can still be repaired, and drives, supplies and cables are not brand-specific.

What changes is what you can buy new to match, so when a matching camera is hard to find, we fit a compatible one that works with the recorder you already have.

"Your landlord has to show you the building's footage"

Not in general. Brick Underground's answer to a tenant asking how to see footage of a package theft explains that landlords are not obliged to show tenants footage outside a legal process, although attorney Catherine Grad told the outlet: "I don't think that it's such an arduous duty to impose on the landlord."

In Weeksville, where most apartments are in buildings someone else runs, this is a frequent source of frustration. A dead camera is the bigger problem, though: no footage exists to argue about.

What tenants can do is report the dead camera in writing to the owner or manager, and ask that the clip be saved for the police if a crime is reported. What owners can do is keep the cameras working so the question can be answered.

"Police can get your doorbell video through the app"

That was true for Ring's Request for Assistance tool, but Ring ended it in January 2024, as News 12 reported. Police can still obtain video with a warrant, and owners can still choose to share it.

So if your doorbell or camera is the only one that saw something on your block, the clip reaches the 77th Precinct or a neighboring precinct because you export and hand it over, not because an app request arrives.

That makes the recorder's settings matter more: enough days kept, the right clock and a camera that actually records the street. Those are the things we check on a repair visit.
