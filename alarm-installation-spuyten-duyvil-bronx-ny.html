The Neighborhood

Why Spuyten Duyvil Properties Need Alarm Systems

Most of Spuyten Duyvil is not houses. It is a small, steep piece of the northwest Bronx where the apartment buildings that went up between the 1950s and the 1980s now hold most of the people. Wikipedia’s count for 2011 was about 10,279 residents on less than half a square mile. The Henry Hudson Parkway splits the neighborhood into an east side and a west side, Brick Underground notes, and on both sides the typical front door is a lobby door: a postwar co-op of six to twenty floors, often with a hundred or more apartments behind it. The old houses that remain, the 1920s villa buildings on Palisade Avenue and a few newer townhouses are the exceptions. An alarm here is usually an alarm for one apartment inside a large building, and that changes almost every decision.

In a house, the alarm protects the whole envelope and the owner decides everything about it. In a Spuyten Duyvil co-op, the street door, the lobby, the elevators, the corridors, the garage and the storage rooms belong to the building. What belongs to you is one door, a set of windows, perhaps a terrace or a fire-escape window, and the rooms in between. The building’s own defenses, a locked lobby, an intercom, sometimes a 24-hour door attendant, sit between you and the street. Whether those defenses are awake at three in the morning is the first thing we ask, because it decides what your own alarm has to do on its own.

The reports we could find from these blocks show why the question matters. In September 2023, Norwood News reported that a resident on the 3500 block of Netherland Avenue came home around 1 a.m. to find a glass window by the door broken and his laptop, jewelry, sunglasses and baseball cards gone; police had no suspects. In the spring of 2024, the same paper reported that someone pried open the lobby door of a building near Corlear Avenue and West 231st Street with a crowbar, at about 3 a.m. on March 25 and again at 2:52 a.m. on April 5, and left on foot both times without taking anything. That building is in Kingsbridge, just east of the neighborhood, in the same 50th Precinct. In March 2019, NBC New York reported a man who climbed fire escapes into two apartments on different floors of one building near Johnson Avenue and West 236th Street, a few minutes apart, shortly after 8 p.m.; NBC filed it under Riverdale, at the northern edge of Spuyten Duyvil.

None of those reports says whether an alarm was present, and we do not guess. What they share is timing and place: each happened where the people who might notice were asleep, away or one floor removed. A quiet neighborhood makes that more likely, not less. Brick Underground’s guide says plainly that the neighborhood “does not have a nightlife”, and its one grocery, Ben’s Market, and the take-out places on Knolls Crescent are not open all night. On a hill of dead-end streets and stairways, very few people walk past after dark. A siren in a corridor or a broken pane by a door may go unheard until morning.

So the design question here is less “how many sensors” and more “who finds out, how fast, and what can they do”. For an apartment in a building with a staffed desk, the alarm can lean on the attendant for the first look. For an apartment behind an unstaffed lobby, it has to reach you, a neighbor you trust and a monitoring station on its own, by cellular signal, with enough detail that someone can decide whether to send police. For a house on a step street, there is no lobby at all, and the gate on the steps is part of the perimeter. The rest of this page works through each of those cases.

Most of the neighborhood’s apartments arrived in a short postwar burst; the alarm questions on this page follow from that.

Spuyten Duyvil in six facts that shape an alarm

  • Size and place: about 0.47 square miles in Bronx Community District 8, bounded by Riverdale to the north, Kingsbridge to the east, the Harlem River to the south and the Hudson to the west; ZIP codes 10463 and 10471 (Wikipedia).
  • Housing: apartment high-rises went up from the 1950s through the 1980s, and many became co-ops and condominiums (Wikipedia). Brick Underground calls co-ops the most available kind of home, single-family houses scarce and dating from the 1900s to the 1920s, with some townhouses from the 1960s, 1970s and early 2000s.
  • Scale: 60 Knolls Crescent has 240 apartments on ten floors (CityRealty, built 1953); the Winston Churchill at 2500 Johnson Avenue has 339 on twenty (Brick Underground, built 1967).
  • Terrain: steep hills and twisting streets, step streets between Irwin and Edgehill Avenues and between Kappock Street and Knolls Crescent, and stretches of Johnson Avenue where private houses have gates opening straight onto the steps (Forgotten New York, 2019).
  • Police: the 50th Precinct at 3450 Kingsbridge Avenue covers Spuyten Duyvil with Riverdale, Fieldston, Kingsbridge and Marble Hill (NYPD). Wikipedia lists 126 burglaries for the precinct in 2022 and a 69.9 percent fall in total crime from 1990 to 2022.
  • Getting around: no subway stop in the neighborhood; the 1 train in Kingsbridge is a 15 to 20 minute walk, and Metro-North’s Hudson Line stops at Spuyten Duyvil station, about 25 minutes to Manhattan by one broker’s estimate (Brick Underground).

We quote the red rows to you and the grey rows to the board. The fire escape is the building’s, but the windows that open onto it are yours to protect.

The building’s layers can be strong or weak depending on staffing; the apartment alarm cannot see them, so the call plan has to account for them.

Attended buildings. A 24-hour desk is a real layer. The attendant sees the street door, knows who belongs, and can walk police to your floor. An apartment alarm in a building like this does not need to replace that, but it should use it. With your agreement, the desk can be on the call list for the hours it is staffed, and the alarm can be programmed so that a door forced at 3 a.m. reaches you first and the desk second, while an alarm during a building fire drill does not. What we will not do is assume the desk is watching. Attendants sign for packages, take calls and step away; they cannot hear a siren behind an apartment door ten floors up.

Buildings with no one in the lobby at night. Some buildings have a doorman for only part of the day or none at all, a super who may live on site but sleeps like everyone else, and an intercom panel at the street door. When the lobby door was pried twice near Corlear Avenue in 2024, nothing was taken, and the police described it as a pattern and asked the public to help identify the man. For an apartment in a building like that, the alarm has to carry the whole job of noticing: a cellular communicator that does not depend on building wiring or the internet, a door contact and a hallway motion detector inside your apartment, video at your door so a remote person can see what tripped, and a call list that includes someone who is physically in the building.

Small buildings. The villa co-ops on Palisade Avenue are a different world: Villa Charlotte Brontë, built in 1926, is 17 apartments in two buildings linked by staircases, walkways and stone arches, about 100 feet above the river, according to Wikipedia and a 2024 Substack essay by Rob Stephenson. Netherland Terrace, opened in 1988, has 15 apartments. In buildings that small, neighbors usually do know one another, windows along outdoor stairs are effectively street-level, and a shared alert between two or three households can work better than any siren.

Houses. Houses on these hills often have a level that touches the street and a level that touches a stair, a slope or a back yard below. Atlas Obscura describes the West 230th Street step street, 295 feet long and 38 feet high, passing houses with entrances directly onto the steps. For a house like that, no layer stands between the stair and your gate. The alarm’s first job is to treat every level as ground level until proven otherwise.

The rest of the design follows from which row you are in. It is also why two neighbors in the same building can end up with quite different systems: one whose bedroom window opens onto the fire escape, and one on the river side whose only way in is the apartment door.

The buildings named in our sources fall into six groups. The alarm for each is different, and so is what we need to see before we quote.

Large 1950s–1960s elevator co-ops

One door, a hallway, sometimes a terrace; the fire-escape question depends on the line you live on.

Mid-size postwar co-ops

The Presidential at 601 Kappock Street (1960, 100 apartments, six floors), Tangiers (1958, 73 apartments, eight floors), 2601 Henry Hudson Parkway (1954, 69 apartments, nine floors), 3512 Oxford Avenue (1962, 45 apartments, six floors). Lower buildings mean more apartments within reach of the ground, a roof or a neighboring roof.

1920s villa co-ops on Palisade Avenue

Villa Charlotte Brontë at 2501 Palisade Avenue (1926, 17 apartments) and Villa Victoria at 2475 Palisade Avenue, Tudor style. Outdoor stairs and walkways, casement windows and thick masonry walls; wireless devices are tested one by one.

Small late co-ops

Netherland Terrace (opened 1988, 15 apartments, six floors). Few apartments per floor and neighbors who know each other; a shared alert between households is worth considering.

New rental buildings

The Henry at 2395 Palisade Avenue, a six-story building with 55 rental apartments and sub-grade parking for about 30 cars, built where Villa Rosa Bonheur stood. Renters need equipment that leaves with them and nothing that touches building systems.

Houses and townhouses on the hills

Single-family houses from the 1900s to the 1920s and townhouses from the 1960s, 1970s and early 2000s (Brick Underground), many on steep lots, some with gates onto step streets such as the one on Johnson Avenue.

The stock is not going to change much. Brick Underground points out that Spuyten Duyvil lies in a Special Natural Area District, which limits new development on its slopes; the Henry on Palisade Avenue is the only new residential building the guide mentions. For alarm planning that is useful news. The buildings described here will still be the buildings in ten years, so a system designed around how your particular building is staffed, where its fire escapes run and how its hillside meets each wall will stay right for as long as you live there. When we survey, we write those building facts into the account notes, so that whoever services the system later starts from the same picture.

How the install differs by building type

Postwar co-ops (1950s–1960s). Postwar apartment buildings commonly have plaster or block walls and apartment doors hung in steel frames, and the building rather than the owner controls the corridor side of the door, so we use wireless contacts and detectors and avoid drilling into the door or frame unless you ask for a hardwired contact. The panel and its backup battery go in a closet away from the door, and the cellular communicator is tested in that exact spot, because a closet on the rock side of a hillside building can have a very different signal from a room facing the river. Nothing goes in the public corridor, on the outside of the door or on the building’s intercom wiring.

Villa buildings. Thick masonry, steel or wood casements and outdoor circulation change three things: we put contacts on each casement leaf that opens, we treat any window beside an outdoor stair or walkway as ground level, and we check wireless range room by room because old walls absorb radio signal unevenly. Exterior devices are kept off the facade of buildings people care about; anything outside is small, low and removable.

New rentals. We use adhesive-mounted contacts and a tabletop or shelf panel, no holes in walls, doors or frames unless your lease and your landlord allow them, and we write down what to remove and how when you move out. The building’s smart locks, cameras and Wi-Fi stay the building’s; your alarm runs on its own cellular connection.

Houses on slopes. We walk every level from the outside first, from the street and from the lowest ground touching the house, because a basement door on the downhill side can be the most private door on the lot. Gates onto public steps get a contact or a camera; doors on every level get contacts; motion detectors cover the stairs between levels so a single detector catches a person moving up or down the house.

Business Alarm Systems in Spuyten Duyvil

Spuyten Duyvil has very little commerce, which is part of why each business here matters to the people around it. Brick Underground describes Knolls Crescent as a small commercial corner with take-out restaurants and calls Ben’s Market the neighborhood’s only grocery. Beyond that, most shopping is in Central Riverdale, Kingsbridge and Inwood. These are the places we see as business alarm work in and around the neighborhood:

Knolls Crescent

The neighborhood’s commercial corner, with take-out places and a pharmacy. Norwood News reported that in October 2022 a group walked into the pharmacy at 21B Knolls Crescent around 8 a.m. and left with merchandise; two men were arrested. An alarm does not stop a theft during open hours, but it does protect the same store after closing.

Johnson Avenue toward West 235th Street

On the Riverdale end of the avenue, an undated Riverdale Press crime report described three businesses at 3530 and 3532 Johnson Avenue and 560 West 235th Street broken into overnight between 4 p.m. Sunday and 8:30 a.m. Monday, two of them through the rear; at one, the thief took the security system and the safe.

Ground floors of residential buildings

Shops and offices tucked into the ground floors of apartment buildings share walls and sometimes doors with the residents above. The alarm has to cover the business’s own openings without reacting to the building’s traffic.

Cultural and community buildings

Edgehill Church, built in 1888–89 and a city landmark since 1980, has housed the Kingsbridge Historical Society since the church closed (Wikipedia). Buildings like this are open a few hours at a time and hold irreplaceable things.

Clubs and amenity spaces

Brick Underground notes that The Club of Riverdale, a fitness center with an indoor pool in The Century at 2600 Netherland Avenue, is open to members who do not live in the building. Spaces shared with the public need alarms that know opening hours.

Home-based work

In a neighborhood of apartments, a lot of small business happens at home. A home office in a co-op apartment is protected by the apartment alarm, with a separate code for anyone who works there.

Business alarms start at the back of the shop and at whatever would keep it closed tomorrow.

Compliance callouts for businesses

  • Siren limit. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building burglar alarm to be capable of shutting off, and to shut off, within fifteen minutes of activation. We set every siren inside that limit.
  • Licensed installer. Alarm installers in New York State are licensed by the Department of State; our license number is NYS #12000287431, printed on every quote and invoice.
  • Fire alarms are separate. A burglary panel is not a code-compliant fire alarm system. Businesses that need an FDNY-approved fire alarm get one designed for that purpose, quoted separately.
  • Accessible keypads. Where a keypad serves employees with disabilities, we mount it at a reachable height beside the door, not above a counter.
  • Landlord equipment. If the landlord’s building systems run through your space, we do not tap into them; your alarm runs on its own power supply and communicator.

If your shop rolls down a gate at night, the gate is not the alarm. We put contacts on the doors and glass behind it, motion along the aisle and a glass-break detector by the front, so the alarm still knows what is happening if the gate is cut or lifted.

Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Spuyten Duyvil: Questions Answered

These are the questions we would want answered if we lived in one of these buildings. Each begins with something reported on or near these blocks, and each answer is written for this neighborhood. Questions answered elsewhere on the page are not repeated here.

Small numbers, plotted only to show the pattern the page is built on: the break-ins in these reports happened when nobody nearby was likely to be awake. The Corlear Avenue building is in Kingsbridge and the Johnson Avenue building was reported as Riverdale; both are in the 50th Precinct.

When nobody is in the lobby

Near Corlear Avenue in 2024, someone pried a building’s street door with a crowbar at about 3 a.m., twice in eleven days, and left without taking anything. If that happened at our unattended co-op, would anyone upstairs know before morning?

Only if somebody planned for it. An apartment alarm sees your door and your rooms, not the lobby, so in most buildings a forced street door at 3 a.m. reaches nobody until the super finds the damage. There are two honest ways to close that gap. The building can put its own contact or door-forced sensor on the street door and send that one event, by text or app, to the super, a board member and any residents who opt in. Or, if the board is not ready, you can put a small camera on your side of your apartment door so that if the person comes up, you see it and the monitoring station sees it.

What we will not do is wire a single apartment’s alarm to a building door it does not own. A building-level sensor is a board decision, and we quote it to the board. For your apartment, the useful lesson from Corlear Avenue is the pattern: the same door, at almost the same hour, eleven days apart. If something like that is reported on your block, it is worth asking us to shorten your entry delay and move a neighbor in the building higher up your call list for a few weeks.

If one apartment in our building is broken into, can its alarm warn the other apartments on the same line?

Not automatically, and it should not. Each apartment alarm reports to its own owner and its own monitoring account. But neighbors can agree to warn one another, and we can make that easy. In the 2019 case near Johnson Avenue and West 236th Street, NBC New York reported that the burglar went into a second apartment on a different floor about five minutes after the first. Five minutes is enough for a text.

The simplest setup is a shared group on a messaging app plus an alarm app that lets each household add a neighbor as a contact who receives alarm notifications for your apartment, with no ability to arm or disarm it. When your alarm trips, your neighbor’s phone buzzes too. For buildings where several households want this, we can set it up the same week for each of them, each on its own account. Nobody shares codes, and nobody’s alarm can see anyone else’s rooms.

Shorter is safer, as long as everyone can reach the keypad in time; on handover day each person times it from their own door.

Windows that touch the outside world

The 2019 burglar near Johnson Avenue and West 236th Street went in through fire-escape windows on two different floors. We live several floors up. Which of our windows can actually be reached?

Any window that opens onto a fire escape, at any height, because a fire escape is a staircase. A person who gets onto it from the street, a roof, a neighboring building or another apartment can reach every window on it. Height protects street-side windows, not fire-escape windows. Then there are the in-between cases: windows beside a setback roof, a terrace that a neighbor’s terrace adjoins, or the top floor under a roof that people can get onto.

On a site visit we stand at each window, look down and sideways, and mark every one that a person could step to. Those get a contact (and a glass-break detector if the window is large), with the window still free to open in a fire, and the hallway motion detector backs them up. Windows that can only be reached through your front door do not need contacts; the door contact and motion detector cover them. On a hillside lot we count floors from the lowest ground next to each wall, since the “fourth floor” on the street side can be the second floor at the back.

In September 2023 a resident on the 3500 block of Netherland Avenue came home around 1 a.m. to a broken window by the door and missing belongings. If our alarm trips while we are out, what will tell us afterward exactly what happened, and when?

The panel’s event history. Every modern panel we install keeps a time-stamped log of each sensor that opened, every arm and disarm with the user code that did it, every alarm and every report sent to the monitoring station. The app shows the same list. If the glass beside your door is broken at 12:40 and the door opens at 12:41, the log shows a glass-break event, then the door, then the motion detector in the hall, each to the minute, and whether police were requested.

That record matters twice. First, it tells you before you open the door whether someone went inside, so you can wait downstairs for police instead of walking in. Second, it helps the police report and your insurer. If you add a door camera, a short clip is attached to the alarm, so the log has a picture as well as a time. We show every household how to export the log on the day we hand the system over.

Houses on the stairs and the slopes

Our house has its front door on the street and a back gate that opens onto a public step street, and we use both every day. How do we arm a house with two everyday entrances on two different levels?

With two keypads, or a keypad and a key fob, and an entry delay on both doors. Atlas Obscura describes houses along the West 230th Street step street with entrances opening directly onto the steps, and Forgotten New York describes gates on Johnson Avenue that open straight onto stairs. If the family really uses both, both have to be places where you can disarm. A keypad at the top door and a fob for whoever comes up the steps works well; so does a second keypad inside the lower door.

The gate itself is outside the house, so it gets a contact that chimes indoors when it opens during the day and can be included in the alarm at night. Because a public stair has foot traffic at all hours, any camera on the gate is aimed at your gate and your path only, not at the steps the public uses. And because a house with an entrance on a stair can be hard for police to find, the monitoring station’s notes for your account say which door faces which street.

Gate positions are examples to show the idea: on a public stair, a house gate is the first thing a passer-by can touch.

In our co-op the indoor garage connects to the building by an inside door near the elevators. Does that change anything for an apartment alarm on an upper floor?

It changes the call plan more than the sensors. Several buildings here have indoor parking: a Riverdale Press listing for 750 Kappock Street mentions deeded indoor spaces, Brick Underground lists indoor parking at Tangiers, and the building at 2395 Palisade Avenue was planned with sub-grade parking for about 30 cars. A garage gives a second way into the building that skips the lobby, so in a building with a desk, the desk may not see everyone who comes up.

For your apartment, that is one more reason not to rely on the lobby: keep your entry delay short, put the motion detector where every route from your door has to cross it, and if you have a door camera, let it record whenever the alarm is armed. For the building, garage doors and the inside door from the garage are common-area openings a board can protect with its own sensors. We do not put apartment devices in the garage, and we do not alarm a deeded parking space.

Keys, stores and the morning after

We bought into a big co-op where apartments change hands often, and we have no idea how many keys to our door are still around. Should the alarm come first, or new locks?

New lock cylinders first, if you can only do one this week; the alarm the week after. Brick Underground notes that the large co-ops here see a lot of turnover, and keys travel: previous owners, their contractors, cleaners, relatives, sometimes the building. A new cylinder costs much less than an alarm and closes the quietest way in. Check your building’s rules first, since many co-ops keep a key on file for emergencies.

The alarm then covers what locks cannot. If someone does have a key, the door contact still opens the entry delay, and only a valid code or fob stops it. Give every person their own code so the event log shows whose code disarmed the system. If you inherited an existing panel, change every code on day one, including the installer and master codes; if nobody knows them, the panel needs a reset or a replacement, and we price that on the Bronx list.

The neighborhood has only one grocery and a few small shops. If our store were broken into overnight, what should the alarm protect first so we can still open in the morning?

The things that keep you closed if they are damaged: the back door, the register and safe area, and the refrigeration. The undated Riverdale Press report from the Johnson Avenue end of the neighborhood is a good guide: two of the three businesses were entered from the rear, overnight, and at one of them the thief took the security system and the safe. So we start with a contact and a shock sensor on the rear door, motion along the aisle toward the back, and a contact on the safe or the cabinet it sits in.

Then we make sure the alarm survives the break-in. The panel goes out of sight, not under the counter; the communicator is cellular, so cutting a phone line or a router does nothing; and any recorder is locked away from the panel, because a thief who pulls one box should not silence both. For a store with coolers or freezers, a temperature sensor can text the owner when a compressor fails overnight, which keeps stock you can still sell in the morning.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Systems in Spuyten Duyvil

We could not open Google’s AI Overview, People Also Ask, Answer the Public, Bing, DuckDuckGo or Yelp from our research environment for this page, so nothing below is quoted from them. These are claims that cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, product pages and general advice repeat about alarms, set against what we see in Spuyten Duyvil buildings.

“An apartment in a doorman building does not need an alarm.”

This is the most common thing we hear, and it rests on a picture of a lobby that is staffed every hour and watches every door. Some buildings here do advertise a 24-hour door attendant. Many others have a desk for part of the day, or an intercom panel and no one. The 2024 Corlear Avenue reports describe a lobby door forced twice in the small hours; nobody stopped it at the time.

Even a good desk cannot see a fire escape, a terrace or a back stair, and a garage door may let people reach the elevators without passing the desk at all. The attendant protects the front door of the building, not the windows of your apartment.

What we suggest instead is simple: let the desk be one layer, write it into the call plan for its hours, and let the apartment alarm cover what the desk cannot see.

“Upper floors are safe; only ground-floor apartments get broken into.”

Height protects windows that face the street. It does nothing for windows on a fire escape, which is a staircase by design. NBC New York’s 2019 report from a building near Johnson Avenue and West 236th Street described a burglar entering two apartments on two different floors through fire-escape windows within minutes.

On a hillside lot the idea of a “ground floor” is shaky anyway. A building that meets the street at its lobby may meet a yard, a parking lot or a slope several floors lower on its other side.

So we judge every window by what is next to it, not by the floor number: fire escape, setback roof, terrace, stair or nothing.

“An alarm system costs about $750 installed.”

HomeAdvisor’s national average for an alarm system is $747, with a wide range around it. National averages blend a two-sensor kit in a small house with a monitored system in a large one. They say nothing about the Bronx, about co-op apartments, or about whether a fire-escape window is involved.

Our Bronx list is published on this page. A basic wireless system with four to six contacts and a motion detector is $600 to $1,200 all-in; a co-op apartment with one door, a hall detector and a single fire-escape window usually sits at the lower end of that range.

The number that matters is the one written on your quote, device by device, before work starts. Ours comes from the same list every time.

“A loud siren is the best deterrent.”

A loud siren works when there are people around to hear it and a burglar who cares. In a large co-op with thick walls and carpeted corridors, a siren behind an apartment door may barely reach the hallway, and New York City law requires it to stop within fifteen minutes anyway.

On a quiet hill at 3 a.m., the thing that changes the outcome is how fast someone who can act finds out: you, a neighbor two doors down, a monitoring operator with a video clip.

We set apartment sirens loud enough to startle and fast reporting to do the real work. Houses on the slopes get a louder outdoor siren if the family wants the neighbors to hear.

“Wi-Fi alarms are as reliable as anything else.”

A system that reports only over home Wi-Fi depends on the router, the internet service and the building’s power, all of which a burglar, a storm or an outage can take away. Building internet in older co-ops can also be shared or managed by others.

A cellular communicator does not need any of that. It has its own battery backup and its own path to the monitoring station.

Signal does vary in these buildings. A closet against the rock on the uphill side can read differently from a room facing the river, so we test the communicator in the exact spot the panel will live before we fix it there.

“Renters cannot have a real alarm.”

Renters can have a full alarm; they just need one that leaves no trace. Adhesive-mounted contacts, a shelf or tabletop panel and a cellular communicator need no holes in walls, doors or frames.

New rental buildings here, such as the Henry at 2395 Palisade Avenue, come with building systems of their own. Those stay the building’s; your alarm runs alongside them without touching them.

When you move, the equipment comes with you, and we can reinstall it in your next apartment at the labor-only rate.

“Any alarm company can install anywhere in the city the same way.”

The devices are the same. The neighborhood is not. Spuyten Duyvil has step streets, dead-end avenues, a parkway down the middle and a station reached by a staircase, and police and monitoring operators need directions that fit.

For houses with entrances on two levels or on a public stair, we write the account notes to say which door faces which street. For apartments, the notes give the building entrance that is actually used at night.

An installer who knows the neighborhood also knows what not to install: nothing on a building’s facade, corridor or intercom wiring without the board, and nothing aimed at a public stair.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Counting Floors From the Low Side

This is how we survey on a hill, written as a method rather than a story about any one customer. In most of the Bronx you can count a building’s floors from the sidewalk and be done. On these streets we walk around first. Forgotten New York notes that one side of Netherland Avenue sits higher than the other, held up by a median or a stone wall, and Brick Underground describes buildings set on a hill over the river. A building on a slope meets the ground at more than one level, so the floor number in your address tells us very little about which of your windows can be reached.

So the first ten minutes of a Spuyten Duyvil survey are spent outside and at the windows. We find the lowest ground that touches each wall: a parking lot, a yard, a path, a set of steps. We look for anything a person can stand on near a window: a fire escape, a setback roof, a terrace, a neighboring roof, a wall top. Then we go inside and stand at each window and look down and to both sides. Only after that do we count devices.

The second habit is asking about the night before we ask about the budget. Who is at the desk at 3 a.m., if anyone? Who lives on the floor? Who could knock on your door within two minutes? Those answers decide whether an apartment gets a modest siren and a fast call list, or a louder siren and a camera at the door. They also decide whether we should be talking to you alone, or suggesting that you bring a lobby-door idea to your board.

The third habit is leaving the building exactly as we found it. Nothing goes on the corridor side of your door unless the building has agreed, nothing is tied into the intercom or the building’s phone lines, and every device we mount inside can come off with a screwdriver, leaving only small screw holes or adhesive marks.
