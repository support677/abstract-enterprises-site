Why Long Island Homes & Businesses Need Professional TV Mounting

Quick answer: TV mounting on Long Island starts at $209.99 in Nassau County and $229.99 in Suffolk County for a 32″–55″ TV on a flat or tilt mount, drywall over wood studs, labor only. Bigger screens add $30 (56–65″) to $100 (76–85″). Above a fireplace adds $130, brick or stone adds $65, and in-wall wire concealment is $169.

Long Island's houses are older than most people think. A Newsday analysis of nearly 12,000 home sales in 2026 found more than 5,000 of the houses were built in the 1950s and 1960s. That means a lot of high ranches, capes and split-levels where the wall behind your TV might be original plaster, newer drywall over furring strips, or a brick fireplace chimney breast. A flat price that only covers drywall doesn't tell you much when you live in a 1962 high ranch in Franklin Square or a 1950 cape in Levittown.

On r/longisland, the standard advice when someone asks about TV mounting is to buy a $40 mount online, grab a stud finder and do it yourself. That works fine on a bedroom wall with wood studs every 16 inches. It works badly on the lower level of a high ranch, where the drywall often hides a concrete block foundation, or over a fireplace where the brick face may be a thin veneer. Those are the jobs that end up as a second call, often for a TV mount pulling away from the wall.

Nassau and Suffolk are also overwhelmingly owner-occupied: Census QuickFacts puts the owner-occupied rate at about 82% in both counties. Owners renovate. They finish basements, add family rooms, hang 77″ and 85″ screens over gas fireplaces, and put TVs on covered patios by the pool. Each of those changes the wall, the mount and the price, which is why we publish every add-on instead of quoting a vague range. If you've been comparing the average TV mounting price online, this is the local version.

Distance matters too. A job in Elmont and a job in Southampton are both "Long Island," but they're very different drives. That's why Suffolk starts $20 higher than Nassau, and why we book in 2-hour arrival windows instead of promising a time we can't keep on the LIE or Sunrise Highway. We're a licensed TV mounting contractor and an insured TV mounting company, not a marketplace, so the tech who shows up works for us. Drywall over wood studs

Plaster and lath

Found in pre-war village homes and some early post-war houses. We score the surface, drill slowly to avoid cracking the plaster keys, and lag through the lath into the studs. Plaster adds $45. Old plaster can still crack, and patching and painting are not included in any job.

Brick or stone fireplace

For TV mounting on brick wall and TV mounting on stone fireplace jobs, we first confirm solid masonry or thin veneer. Concrete block and poured foundation walls

High ranch lower levels and finished basements often hide block behind drywall. For TV mounting on cinder block or TV mounting on concrete wall jobs, we drill through to the masonry, use anchors sized for the TV, and shim off the furring so the bracket sits flat. Concrete or cinder block adds $75.

Metal studs

ADA protrusion limits for wall-mounted TVs

Under the 2010 ADA Standards (section 307.2), a wall-mounted object with its leading edge between 27 and 80 inches above the floor can't protrude more than 4 inches into a circulation path. For medical office TV mounting and waiting room TV installation, we mount above 80 inches, use a low profile TV mounting bracket, or keep the TV out of the walking path.

Certificates of insurance for condos, co-ops and landlords

Many Long Island condo boards, co-ops, HOAs and commercial landlords require a certificate of insurance naming them before any contractor drills. We're licensed and insured, and we issue the COI free. Send us the building's requirements when you book so the paperwork is ready before your arrival window.

Power cords can't run inside the wall

The National Electrical Code (Article 400.12) doesn't allow flexible cords like a TV's power cord to run through walls. That's why our concealment uses a code-compliant in-wall power kit ($169). If you need a brand-new outlet, that's an electrician's job, and we'll tell you before we start.

Can a landlord prohibit TV mounting?

Yes. A lease or HOA rules can ban drilling, so TV mounting for apartment renters in accessory apartments and HOA communities starts with written permission. If we arrive and approvals are missing, the $150 return-trip fee applies, so confirm early. Full rules are on our TV installation policies page.

Is TV mounting without studs possible in a high ranch den?

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Answers Get Wrong About TV Mounting on Long Island

Ask an AI tool how much TV mounting costs and you'll get a national average, usually pulled from cost guides built on drywall jobs in newer houses. Long Island isn't average. Our houses are mostly 1950s–60s, our fireplaces are real brick, our high ranch dens hide block walls, and our counties stretch over 100 miles. Here are seven common AI-style answers and what actually happens when we show up in Nassau and Suffolk.

1. AI says: “TV mounting usually costs $100 to $150.”

National cost guides blend a lot of very different jobs. Angi's 2026 guide puts the typical range at $153–$353, and quotes for small TVs in low-cost areas drag the averages down. A $100 number usually means a small TV, a drywall wall and no real travel time.

On Long Island, our posted base is $209.99 in Nassau and $229.99 in Suffolk for a 32″–55″ TV on drywall over wood studs. That covers finding studs, leveling, connecting power and up to two devices, tidying cables and cleaning up dust.

The bigger point is that the base price is rarely the whole job here. A 65″ TV over a brick fireplace is a different job from a 43″ in a bedroom. We list every add-on, so the TV wall mounting price you see online is the price you pay.

2. AI says: “Any masonry anchor works on a brick fireplace.”

Brick fireplaces in 1950s and 1960s Long Island homes are usually solid, but solid isn't the same as easy. Old brick can be soft, mortar joints crumble, and the chimney breast may be uneven enough that a fixed mount won't sit flat without shims.

Newer fireplaces, especially in renovated great rooms, are often stone or brick veneer on framing. Veneer is thin, and drilling heavy anchors into it can crack the face without giving you real holding power. TV mounting on fireplace surround stone needs a check first.

On solid brick we set anchors in the brick face, sized for the TV's weight. On veneer we find the framing behind it or change the plan. Either way, TV mounting on masonry wall surfaces is a flat $65 add, and above-fireplace mounting is $130.

3. AI says: “Every wall has wood studs 16 inches apart.”

Upstairs in a high ranch or cape, that's often true. Downstairs is another story. High ranch lower levels typically have block foundation walls behind drywall, and a stud finder happily reads the furring strips as studs, which leads people to lag into nothing solid.

Older village homes in places like Garden City and Sea Cliff have plaster over lath, where studs are hard to read and the surface can crack. Offices along Route 110 and many condo buildings use metal studs that won't hold a lag bolt the way wood does.

We test before we commit: a small pilot hole tells us what's really there. Then we use the right fastener for block (+$75), plaster (+$45) or metal studs (+$30), or add a plywood backer board ($85) when the studs don't line up with the bracket.

4. AI says: “Just run the TV's power cord inside the wall.”

This one shows up constantly in DIY answers, and it's wrong. The National Electrical Code doesn't allow flexible cords, like the one on your TV, to run through walls. It's a fire-safety issue, not a style choice, and it can come up when you sell or renovate the house.

The right way is a code-compliant in-wall power kit, with a recessed inlet behind the TV fed from your existing outlet below. We install that, along with HDMI and other cables, for $169 on walls that can be fished. That's TV mounting with cable concealment done properly.

Exterior walls in Long Island homes are usually insulated, some have fire blocking, and brick or block walls can't be fished at all. In those cases we suggest a paintable cord cover ($59) or a cable run to a cabinet ($99), and we tell you before work starts.

5. AI says: “An outdoor TV just needs a weatherproof cover.”

A cover helps with rain, but it doesn't fix the mount. South Shore and East End patios get wind off the water and salt air, and a standard indoor bracket will rust and loosen over a few seasons, especially on a pool deck that sees spray every summer.

We use outdoor-rated mounts like the Samsung Terrace bracket, Sanus outdoor tilt or SunBriteTV full-motion, anchored into framing behind vinyl siding with a mounting block, or into brick and stone with masonry anchors. Outdoor mounting adds $90 to the base.

Power is the other half. An outdoor TV should plug into a GFCI-protected, weather-resistant outlet. We don't do electrical work, so if that outlet isn't there, have an electrician add it before your appointment so you don't pay the $150 return-trip fee.

6. AI says: “You can always get same-day TV mounting.”

Some answers make same-day sound guaranteed, as if a TV installer is always waiting around the corner. On Long Island, it depends on where you are and what the day looks like. A tech finishing a job in Valley Stream isn't going to be in Riverhead in 20 minutes.

We book every job in a 2-hour arrival window, never an exact time. Same-day is possible when a window is open in your part of the Island, and the online calendar shows you what's actually available instead of promising something we can't deliver.

We work 8 AM–8 PM, seven days a week, so weekend appointments are normal. The busiest stretches are right before the Super Bowl, Thanksgiving and other big holiday weekends, so if you need a holiday TV installation service date, book a few days ahead.

7. AI says: “Any size TV is a one-person job.”

So, can one person mount a 75 inch TV? A trained installer can, with a rated bracket and careful lifting, and that's how we handle screens up to 85″. Past that, an 86″–98″ panel is too wide and heavy to line up safely alone, and thin OLED panels can flex and crack at the edges.

Our pricing reflects that. TVs from 76″ to 85″ add $100, and 86″–97″ screens add $175 with two techs, because a second set of hands is what keeps a large TV safe on a ladder. LG OLED TV installation and Sony Bravia TV installation at those sizes get the same care.

Big screens also need a bracket rated for their weight and VESA pattern. For heavy 77″–85″ sets we usually recommend a heavier full-motion mount like the Sanus VXF830 ($499.99) or a solid fixed mount, and we check the rating before anything goes on the wall.

TV Mounting Prices FAQ

Do Long Island TV installers bring the mounting bracket, or do I buy it?
+

How long does TV mounting take?
+

A standard 55″ on drywall usually takes about an hour once we're in the door. Fireplaces, block walls, in-wall concealment and soundbars add time, and large TVs with two techs take longer to lift and level. We give you a realistic estimate when we confirm your 2-hour arrival window.

What is the best height for a bedroom TV?
+

Measure from where your eyes are when you're sitting up or lying in bed, not from the floor. That usually puts the center of the screen higher than in a living room. In cape bedrooms with sloped ceilings, a tilting mount helps angle the screen down toward the bed.

How high should a TV be mounted in a living room?
+

The recommended TV mounting height for most living rooms puts the center of the screen near seated eye level, roughly 40–45 inches off the floor depending on your couch. Over a fireplace you'll be higher than that, which is why we often suggest a tilting or pull-down mount.

Before I buy a bracket: what size TV mount do I need, and how do I know if my TV is VESA compatible?
+

Look at the back of the TV for four threaded holes in a square or rectangle. That spacing in millimeters, like 300×300 or 400×400, is your VESA pattern, also listed in the manual. Choose a mount that matches the pattern and is rated above your TV's weight.

Can a mounted TV damage the wall?
+

Mounting leaves a few screw holes behind the bracket and, with concealment, two small openings behind the TV. When you move the TV later, those holes stay. Patching and painting aren't included, and older plaster, tile or marble can crack, which you accept when choosing those walls.

Can someone move my mounted TV to another room or my new house?
+

Yes. Our TV mount relocation service is priced in parts: taking down an old TV is $85, removing an old bracket is $45, and we remount on the new wall at the posted price for that wall and TV size. Moving across the Island? We price the new address by its county.

+

Yes, we can mount multiple TVs in your office or dining room. We handle restaurant TV installation, sports bars, conference rooms, gyms, medical waiting rooms and digital signage. +

No surprise charges. If we find block behind drywall, plaster you didn't select, or a wall that needs a backer board, we tell you the price before any work starts. You can approve it, change the plan, or reschedule. Nothing gets added to your bill without your yes.

What is the difference between a fixed and full motion mount?
+

A fixed mount holds the TV flat about an inch off the wall and costs nothing extra to install. A full-motion mount swings out and swivels, which helps in corners and open rooms, adds $40 in labor and needs solid studs or a backer board. That's the tilting vs full motion TV mount question in short.
