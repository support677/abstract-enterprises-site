The Neighborhood

Why Westchester Properties Need Hikvision Security Camera Installation

Westchester is a county of mixed buyers on the same street. Drive Central Park Avenue from Yonkers through Greenburgh toward White Plains — about eight and a half miles of shopping centers — and the camera owners change every few hundred feet: a franchise store, a town park, a school district office, a medical suite, a house of worship. Each of them bought cameras for the same reason, which is to see what happened in their lot last night. Since July 16, 2026, the reason no longer settles the brand. The FCC's import and marketing prohibition on previously authorized Hikvision equipment applies to public safety, security of government facilities and other national security purposes, while the Federal Register notice says plainly that consumers “may continue to use any device or equipment that they currently possess” if it was legally bought. On Central Avenue, the store and the medical suite are on one side of that line and the town park and the district office are on the other.

The public side is unusually large here. The county describes itself as six cities, nineteen towns and twenty villages (other counts, which treat Harrison, Mount Kisco and Scarsdale as both town and village, reach twenty-three). Southern Westchester BOCES lists thirty-two component school districts, and Putnam/Northern Westchester BOCES serves eighteen more across the two counties. The county owns Westchester County Airport on the Harrison, North Castle and Rye Brook line. The federal courthouse on Quarropas Street in White Plains sits a few blocks from the Metro-North station. New York City's Kensico Reservoir in Valhalla receives the water of the Catskill and Delaware systems before it heads south. Every one of those is a building where a Hikvision camera now raises questions that a private kitchen camera does not, and every one of them has private neighbours whose cameras look over the same fence.

There is a second Westchester wrinkle. The county police run a Real Time Crime unit out of Hawthorne that connects more than a hundred local, state and federal agencies and describes itself as monitoring and evaluating crime “in real-time through surveillance cameras, fixed and mobile license plate recognition (LPR) systems” and other tools. When it launched in 2017, the county said it would lean on public-private partnerships, and its early wins included images of people and vehicles captured by security cameras. That is valuable to a store owner who wants a theft solved. It also means a privately owned Hikvision feed can drift toward a public-safety use if it is wired straight into a police system rather than exported on request — a distinction we explain in the questions below.

The private side is where most of our Westchester Hikvision work still happens: houses on wide northern lots where Bedford's four-acre zoning and Pound Ridge's three-acre minimum put the gate a long way from the kitchen; pre-war houses and apartment buildings in the river towns and the Sound Shore; strip and big-box retail on Central Avenue and the Boston Post Road; and office parks along the I-287 Westchester Avenue stretch that brokers call the Platinum Mile. On those properties Hikvision remains a legal, capable choice, and our job is to install it so it is well aimed, locked down and easy to hand to police when something happens. Owners who are also replacing an old gate keypad or an office door reader should look at our Westchester access control installation page first, because one trench or one ceiling pull can carry both cables.

How the building changes the method

Stone and stucco. On a Larchmont or Bronxville stucco house or a fieldstone wall in Katonah we drill only where a crack would not spread, use sleeved anchors, and seal the cable entry with a compatible sealant. Where the wall is too precious to drill, a camera goes on the soffit or a wood trim board instead.

Long drives and gates. Ethernet stops at about 100 metres per run. Past that, the gate camera gets a small weatherproof enclosure with its own PoE switch fed by fibre or by an outdoor-rated network extender, and a dedicated power source at the gate. Any trench gets an 811 call at least 48 hours before digging.

Apartment common areas. Cameras cover lobbies, mail rooms, laundry doors, garages and the exterior, never a view into an apartment door or window beyond what the board has approved. The recorder sits in a locked room the managing agent controls.

Office suites. Ceiling cameras ride the existing drop-ceiling grid, cable runs in plenum-rated jacket where the ceiling is a return-air space, and the suite recorder lives on the tenant's network, not the landlord's.

Retail pads. Exterior cameras on landlord poles or fascia go up only after the landlord's written approval, and the lot cameras are aimed at shared lanes, not at a neighbouring tenant's door.

Owners of estate houses often pair the perimeter cameras with a gate intercom or a keypad. If that is on the list, mention it at the walkthrough so one trench serves both.

Why the line is drawn at use rather than at brand

The FCC added Hikvision to the Covered List in 2021 with a qualifier: its video surveillance equipment is covered “to the extent it is used for the purpose of public safety, security of government facilities, physical security surveillance of critical infrastructure, and other national security purposes.” The June 2026 public notice (DA 26-635) kept that structure. For equipment covered only for certain purposes, the new import and marketing prohibition “will not apply to importation and marketing for non-‘covered’ uses.” Hikvision USA now asks dealers to put a use-restriction label on boxes saying the product may not be used for those purposes. A camera in a Scarsdale back yard is therefore not the same regulatory object as an identical camera on a village DPW garage.

The fourth prong — critical infrastructure — is on hold. The D.C. Circuit in 2024 found the FCC's earlier definition “arbitrarily broad” and sent it back, and the 2026 notice suspends that part of the prohibition until a new definition is adopted. That matters in Westchester because so much infrastructure sits beside private property: the county airport, the Kensico Reservoir and the aqueduct structures around it, rail yards and power substations. Until the FCC writes a definition, we treat private neighbours of those sites as ordinary private sites but avoid aiming any camera across the fence line.

Why money and contracts can matter more than the building

Two other federal rules follow the money rather than the address. 2 CFR 200.216 bars federal grant and loan recipients from spending those funds on covered video surveillance equipment, naming Hikvision. NDAA Section 889 Part B, in force since August 13, 2020, bars federal agencies from contracting with companies that use covered equipment. A Tarrytown biotech supplier, a White Plains engineering firm on a federal task order and a Yonkers temple with an NSGP award can all be private, and still have a reason to keep Hikvision off their walls.

Dense, older and mostly rented. Yonkers alone has about 83,851 housing units, with more renter-occupied than owner-occupied homes, and Mount Vernon's housing has a median build year of 1952. Older houses, village downtowns along the Post Road, and water. In August 2026 News 12 reported a rise in car thefts and break-ins in Larchmont and nearby, which is why driveway and curb views are the first upgrade most Sound Shore owners ask about.

Central Westchester: White Plains, Greenburgh, Harrison

The county seat, the courts, the office parks and the big retail. This is where the use test does the most work, because private tenants, public offices and federal contractors share buildings, lots and even lobbies. Large lots, stone walls, long drives, and a different utility: NYSEG's tariff lists Bedford, Lewisboro, North Salem, Pound Ridge and Somers among its Westchester towns, so outage patterns and restoration contacts differ from Con Edison's south county. Jobs here are gate links, barns, caretaker logins and PTZs over open ground, with battery backup sized for the recorder and the gate switch. Hikvision is usually still a fine choice on these private properties; the challenge is distance, not regulation.

Your Questions Answered

Hikvision Security Camera Installation in Westchester: Questions Answered

These are the questions Westchester owners, boards and facility managers bring us about Hikvision in late 2026. The general federal background is set out on our Hudson Valley and Long Island pages; the answers here are about how it plays out in this county's buildings.

Public Buyers, Grants and Police Links

Our school district's buildings-and-grounds office asked whether the Hikvision cameras at the bus garage can stay. What should the board hear?

Three things, in this order. First, nothing in the FCC's June 2026 notice orders anyone to remove cameras already in service; it prohibits further importing and marketing for covered uses. Second, a public school district is a government body, so new Hikvision purchases for district buildings sit on the wrong side of the “government facilities” purpose, and much district security spending touches federal or state grant money with its own rules. Third, the cameras that keep running still need current firmware, unique passwords and no internet-facing ports, because the Hikvision flaws on CISA's exploited list were found on exactly this kind of long-untouched equipment.

What we give a district is an inventory — every camera and recorder by model, location and firmware — plus a replacement order that starts with the least defensible devices, such as anything bought with grant funds or facing a public entrance. The board can then decide timing with its counsel and its BOCES purchasing contacts. We do not tell a district its existing cameras are illegal, because the rule does not say that.

A Westchester village sent a camera bid that names Hikvision models. Will AESS bid it?

Not with Hikvision. A village hall, public works yard or police building is a government facility or public-safety site, and since July 16, 2026, previously authorized Hikvision equipment may not be imported or marketed for those purposes. We would ask the clerk whether the specification will accept an “or equal” non-covered line and, if so, bid the same camera count, resolution and recording days in that line.

Many older municipal specifications were written around a Hikvision catalogue because that was what the previous integrator stocked. Rewriting the spec in brand-neutral terms — resolution, field of view, low-light rating, ONVIF support, retention — usually costs the village nothing and protects it from buying equipment it may later have to defend.

If our store gives the county police a live link to its cameras, does that change anything for Hikvision?

It can. Westchester's Real Time Crime unit in Hawthorne describes monitoring crime in real time through surveillance cameras and license plate readers, and it was set up around public-private partnerships. Handing a detective a clip after a theft is an ordinary private use. Wiring a permanent live feed from your Hikvision recorder into a police monitoring system starts to look like equipment used for public safety, which is one of the covered purposes. The FCC has not ruled on that exact arrangement, and we are not going to pretend it has.

Our practice: if a Westchester business wants its cameras on a standing police feed, we design that system in a non-covered brand. If it only wants to cooperate when asked, Hikvision is fine, and we set up named cameras, a synced clock and a tested USB export so the clip is ready in minutes without opening the recorder to anyone outside.

Our temple applied for New York's hate-crimes security grant, not the federal one. Do the same Hikvision limits apply?

The federal limit in 2 CFR 200.216 attaches to federal grant and loan money, so it governs Nonprofit Security Grant Program awards. New York's Securing Communities Against Hate Crimes program is a state program — up to $250,000 per organization in the 2026 round, with applications due July 8, 2026 — and its own request for applications and contract set the equipment rules. Read the award terms, or ask the Community Security Initiative or your grant writer, before any camera is ordered.

Whatever the state terms say, we quote grant-funded camera work for houses of worship and schools in a non-covered brand. Many organizations hold both grants over time, and equipment bought under one often ends up listed in the application for the other; a single brand list that satisfies both is less trouble than explaining a mixed one to an auditor.

Our public library is chartered separately from the village. Is it a “government facility” for Hikvision purposes?

Possibly, and that uncertainty is the point. Hikvision's own reading of the FCC's footnote describes government facilities as federal, state, local and tribal government buildings, including general-use offices. A public library in New York may be run by a separate board, funded by a village, town or school district tax levy, and housed in a municipally owned building. Whether that counts is a question for the library's attorney, not for a camera installer.

Our practice is to treat public libraries the same way we treat village halls: no new Hikvision, and a written inventory and replacement plan for any Hikvision already there. A library that later learns it was a private use loses nothing by having chosen a non-covered brand; one that guessed the other way may have to explain the purchase.

Our private school takes no government money. Can it still choose Hikvision?

If the school is genuinely private, owns or leases its own buildings and spends no federal grant money on the cameras, it is a non-covered use under the FCC's purpose-based listing, and Hikvision can be installed. The two things that change that answer are money and links: a federal Nonprofit Security Grant for the cameras brings in 2 CFR 200.216, and a live connection to a police monitoring system starts to look like a public-safety use.

Many Westchester private and religious schools apply for security grants every few years. If yours might, choosing a non-covered brand now saves replacing cameras later to keep a future grant budget clean. We quote both so the board can weigh it.

We are a private business next to Westchester County Airport. Does that make our cameras a “critical infrastructure” use?

Not by being next door. The covered purpose is surveillance of critical infrastructure, and the FCC's definition of that term was vacated by the D.C. Circuit in 2024 and is still being rewritten; the 2026 notice suspends that part of the prohibition until a new definition exists. Your cameras watching your own loading dock and lot are a private use.

What we avoid is aiming any camera across the fence at airport property, fuel areas or approach paths, both because it is not your property to record and because it could make the system look like something it is not. The same rule applies to private sites beside the Kensico Reservoir lands, rail yards and substations elsewhere in the county.

Our engineering firm holds a federal task order and leases a Platinum Mile suite where the landlord's lobby cameras are Hikvision. Whose Section 889 problem is that?

Section 889 Part B asks whether your company uses covered equipment; the landlord's lobby system is the landlord's equipment, not yours, though a contracting officer may still ask about it. What you control is inside the suite: any Hikvision camera or recorder your firm bought, leases or relies on for its own security belongs on your compliance inventory.

The cleanest arrangement is a suite system in a non-covered brand on your own network, with no connection to the building's recorder, and a short letter from the landlord confirming you have no access to or control of the lobby system. We can supply the suite inventory and design; your contracts counsel decides how to answer the representation.

Licences and Paperwork in Westchester

Does a camera installer in Westchester need the county alarm licence, the home improvement licence, or both?

It depends on what the job touches. Westchester's Chapter 863, Article XVI home improvement licence covers work on private residences of up to three units, and the county raised the application fee to $750 from January 1, 2026. Article XV is the county's alarm business and alarm agent licence; town codes such as Cortlandt's and Mamaroneck's point to it for anyone installing or servicing police or fire alarm devices. Work on an alarm system by a county-licensed alarm business is exempted from the home improvement article.

On top of the county rules, New York State requires a Department of State licence for anyone installing or servicing a video surveillance system used to detect or monitor intrusion or theft. Before you sign, ask any installer which licences cover your specific job — a camera-only house, a camera system tied into a burglar alarm, or a commercial building — and check the numbers yourself on the county's online licence lookup and the state's records.

Our town makes us renew an alarm user permit every year. Does adding Hikvision cameras to the alarm change the permit?

The permit stays. In the Town of Mamaroneck, for example, any owner or tenant with an alarm device must hold an alarm user permit from the town clerk, valid for a calendar year. Cameras that only record do not create a new permit by themselves, but cameras integrated with the alarm — video verification, a camera that triggers the siren — are part of the alarm system that the permit covers.

If the alarm company monitors the cameras as well, keep the permit details current with the town, because Mamaroneck's code, for one, lets its Alarm Review Board deny, suspend or revoke a permit for violations of the chapter. We keep camera-only systems separate from the alarm unless you ask for integration, which keeps the paperwork simple.

Estates, Gates and Long Drives

Our Bedford gate already has a keypad and a lamp post. What else has to be there before a camera can go up?

Four things: steady power suitable for a small PoE switch and a camera heater, a weatherproof enclosure to hold the switch and any fibre converter, a data path back to the house, and a mount that will not move. A lamp post usually has power but often on a photocell or a timer, which is useless for a camera that must run all day; a licensed electrician puts in an always-on circuit or a separate feed.

The keypad tells us there is already a conduit or cable path from the gate, which may carry our cable too if it is large enough and not shared with line voltage. The mount goes on the gate pier or a dedicated post rather than on the moving gate leaf, so the view does not swing every time a car comes in. We list each of these on the quote so nothing is discovered on install day.

Can a Hikvision camera at our gate read licence plates at night?

Yes, if it is the right camera in the right place. A plate needs a narrow view, a fast shutter and controlled infrared, which means a varifocal or dedicated plate-capture camera aimed at the point where cars slow for the gate, not a wide camera trying to show the whole entrance. Headlights blooming in the lens are the usual failure, so the angle matters as much as the model.

We pair the plate camera with a separate wide camera that shows the car, the driver's side and the gate, because a plate with no context rarely answers what happened. Keep in mind that the county's own plate readers are a police tool; your gate camera's job is to record your entrance for you.

The caretaker lives in the cottage. Can he see the gate and barn cameras without seeing the cameras around the main house?

Yes. Hikvision recorders let us create a user who can view only the cameras you choose, with or without playback and export rights. The caretaker's phone gets the gate, the drive and the barns; the family's phones get everything; nobody shares the administrator password.

When a caretaker leaves, the account is deleted, not just the app removed from a phone. We also set the recorder to log logins, so you can see who looked at what after the fact.

When is a PTZ camera worth it on a large northern Westchester property?

When one view has to cover a field, a paddock or a long run of fence that fixed cameras would need four or five positions to watch. A Hikvision PTZ on the barn or the house can patrol presets and zoom on movement, so one camera does work that would otherwise need more poles and trenches.

It is never the only camera. A PTZ looking at the paddock is not looking at the gate, so fixed cameras still cover the entrances, doors and the drive. And PTZs have motors that wear, so we mount them where a ladder or lift can reach them for service.

Stores, Plazas and Offices

Our Central Avenue store shares a parking lot with three other tenants. Who should own the lot cameras?

Usually the landlord, with tenants given view access. A single owner of the lot cameras means one maintenance schedule, one place police go for footage and no argument about whose camera failed on the night of a fender-bender. Tenants then put their own cameras on their doors, registers and stockrooms.

If the landlord will not do it, a tenant can mount lot cameras on its own fascia with the landlord's written permission, aimed at the lanes in front of its own store. What we will not do is put a tenant's camera on a landlord pole without that letter, or aim it into a neighbour's storefront.

Should our suite cameras be on the office-park landlord's Hikvision recorder, or our own?

Your own. Putting suite cameras on the landlord's recorder hands your footage, your user list and your retention decisions to someone else, and ties your system's security to how well they maintain theirs. It also blurs any Section 889 or insurance question about whose equipment is whose.

A small PoE recorder in your own IT closet, on your network or a separate camera network, costs little and gives you control. If the landlord wants a view of your entrance for building security, we can share one camera stream with them rather than putting your whole system on their box.

Our medical office in a converted Platinum Mile building wants cameras at reception. What should they not see?

Screens, paperwork and patients' faces at the check-in counter in detail. Hikvision cameras let us draw privacy masks that black out fixed regions in the recorded image, so a camera can cover the waiting room door and the corridor while the computer screens and the intake clipboard stay blank. We aim reception cameras at entrances rather than across the desk.

Exam rooms and changing areas get no cameras at all. Audio recording stays off unless your counsel approves it, and the recorder's retention is set to what your practice policy says rather than to whatever fills the drive.

Running a Hikvision System Well

Why do our Hikvision clips show a time an hour off every spring?

The recorder's daylight-saving setting is off or set to the wrong rule, or it is not synchronizing with a network time server. The cameras stamp the time the recorder gives them, so the whole system shifts by an hour at the change. A clip handed to an insurer or a detective with the wrong hour invites questions you do not need.

We set the time zone to Eastern with daylight saving on, point the recorder at a reliable time server, and make the cameras take their time from the recorder. Then we check it against a phone at handover and note it on the sheet.

Can our Hikvision recorder keep a copy of the footage somewhere other than the building?

Yes, in a few ways. A second recorder or network storage in another building on the same property can hold a copy; important clips can be exported on a schedule to a computer elsewhere; and some sites use a cloud service for selected cameras. Each copy is another place footage lives, so each needs its own password and access list.

For most Westchester houses the practical answer is simpler: hide and lock the recorder so a burglar does not walk out with it, keep the drive healthy, and export anything important the same day. For multi-building campuses we often put a second recorder in the furthest building as a mirror.

Our committee wants to keep the Hikvision cameras for now. What does a sensible two-year plan look like?

Year one: firmware updated, passwords replaced, ports closed, every camera tested and inventoried, and a non-covered model chosen as the standard replacement for any camera that fails. Year two: replace the cameras that matter most to the organization's risk — public entrances, anything grant-linked — and, if the recorder is old, swap it for one that records both brands during the transition.

That keeps recording continuous, spreads the cost over two budgets, and means the committee never has to make an emergency purchase when a camera dies. We write the plan with prices per stage so it can go straight into a budget request.

Living With the System Day to Day

How many days does a Hikvision installation on a large Westchester property usually take?

A four-to-six-camera house is normally one day. An estate with a gate link, two outbuildings and ten to sixteen cameras is usually two to four days on site, and the calendar is set less by the cameras than by the trench, the electrician's outlet at the gate and any fibre splicing. Stone walls and ledge can add a day on their own.

We sequence it so recording starts early: the recorder and the house cameras go live on day one, then the gate and outbuildings join as their links are finished. You are never left with an empty recorder overnight because the last run is not done.

The snowplow keeps hitting the camera post at the edge of our drive. Where should that camera go instead?

Off the plough line. Contractors push snow to the edges of a drive and into the first few feet of lawn, so a short post near the curb is the most exposed camera on the property. We move driveway cameras onto the house, the garage, a gate pier or a taller pole set well back from the edge, and aim them down the drive instead of across it.

Where a low camera is unavoidable, such as for a plate reader at the gate, it goes on the gate pier itself or behind a bollard, and the cable is buried deeper than a plough blade or a frost heave will reach. Marking the pole with a reflective stake before the first storm also helps.

Can we watch the cameras on the kitchen TV without opening the phone app?

Yes. A Hikvision recorder has a video output, so a TV or monitor can show a live grid of chosen cameras directly. If the TV is far from the recorder, we run an HDMI extender over Cat6, or set up a small decoder or streaming box on the TV that pulls the cameras over your home network.

We set the screen to show only the views that are useful in the kitchen — the front door, the drive, the back yard — rather than every camera at once. Sixteen tiny tiles on a TV across the room tell you very little.

How long should outdoor Hikvision cameras last on a Westchester house?

Hikvision does not publish a single service life, and neither do we. In practice, outdoor cameras that were sealed properly, mounted on junction boxes and kept on current firmware tend to keep working for many years; the ones that fail early usually have water in the cable splice, a cracked dome from ice or a lens fogged from a missing gasket.

The more useful question is how long a camera stays good enough. A 2015-era camera may still record, but if it cannot show a face at the door at night, it has reached the end of its useful life even if the LED is green. Our yearly check compares each view against what it is supposed to show.

We are buying a house in Scarsdale that comes with Hikvision cameras. What should the purchase contract say about them?

Three things are worth getting in writing before closing: that the recorder, cameras and drives stay with the house; that the seller will hand over the administrator password or reset the recorder to factory settings; and that the seller will remove the system from their Hik-Connect account so you can bind it to yours. Your attorney decides the wording.

Without those terms, buyers often find a recorder they cannot log into and an app binding still on the seller's phone. Both can be fixed after closing, but it takes a service visit that the seller could have avoided with ten minutes of work.

Our apartment building's Hikvision recorder sits in the boiler room. Is that a problem?

Usually, yes. Boiler rooms run hot and dry in winter and damp in summer, and recorder hard drives are sensitive to sustained heat. Dust from the burner and the room's role as a utility space that many contractors enter also make it a poor place for the one device that holds every recording.

We move recorders into a ventilated, lockable cabinet in a cooler room — a super's office, a storage room or a locked closet near the lobby — and extend the camera runs to reach it. If the boiler room is truly the only option, the recorder goes in a ventilated enclosure on the wall farthest from the boiler, off the floor.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Hikvision Security Camera Installation in Westchester

We could not load Google's live AI Overview from our build environment, so none of the lines below is a quotation from it. They are seven claims about Hikvision and Westchester camera work that turn up in search summaries, cost guides, forum threads and sales pitches, each checked against the county code, federal rules and the other sources listed at the end of this page.

“Westchester has banned Hikvision cameras”

Our research turned up no Westchester County law that bans Hikvision on private property, and the county's consumer-protection pages say nothing about camera brands. The restrictions come from federal rules, and they turn on purpose: public safety, government facilities, national security and (once defined) critical infrastructure.

What is true is that Westchester's public buyers — villages, school districts, the county itself — are covered by those purposes, so the brand is disappearing from public buildings. A shopper on Central Avenue sees fewer Hikvision cameras on the town hall than on the store next door, and draws the wrong conclusion.

For a private house or business in the county, buying and running Hikvision remains lawful as of this update. Whether it is wise for your building is the question we answer at the walkthrough.

“Any licensed electrician can install your cameras”

Westchester does require plumbers and electricians to hold a county trade licence, and an electrician is exactly who should add an outlet at a gate. But New York State separately requires a Department of State security licence to install or service video surveillance used to detect intrusion or theft.

Camera work also touches the county's alarm and home improvement articles depending on the building and whether an alarm is involved. One licence rarely covers everything.

Ask any installer which licences apply to your specific job and look the numbers up yourself. Ours is NYS #12000287431.

“Grant money can buy any security camera”

Not federal grant money. 2 CFR 200.216 bars recipients from spending federal grant or loan funds on covered video surveillance equipment, and it names Hikvision. Nonprofit Security Grant Program awards are federal funds, and the lawmakers who pushed to release them in 2025 listed CCTV cameras among the things the program pays for.

New York's Securing Communities Against Hate Crimes program is state money with its own terms, so its equipment rules have to be read from its own documents rather than assumed.

The safe course for any Westchester house of worship or school is a grant budget written around a non-covered brand from the start.

“Sharing your cameras with police is always a good idea”

Cooperating with an investigation is a good idea, and Westchester's county police have long used security-camera images to solve cases. Handing over a clip on request keeps your system a private one.

A permanent live connection is a different thing. It gives an outside agency standing access to your premises, and for a Hikvision system it pushes the equipment toward a public-safety purpose that the 2026 federal rules single out.

If you want to be part of a live-feed program, choose a non-covered brand for those cameras and read the program's data terms first.

“Wireless cameras are the easy answer for a big lot”

A Reddit thread in r/Westchester about a camera install drew the advice to look at wired PoE cameras, and that matches what we see on large northern properties. Wi-Fi rarely reaches a gate several hundred feet from the house reliably, and battery cameras miss events while they wake up.

Wired cameras need cable, and on a three- or four-acre lot that can mean a trench or fibre. That is real cost, but it buys continuous recording, power over the same cable and no batteries to climb up for in January.

Where a trench is impossible, a point-to-point wireless bridge to a wired camera beats a Wi-Fi camera every time.

“Professional installation runs about $1,500”

That figure is Angi's New York average for surveillance-camera installation of any kind, about $1,509, and HomeAdvisor's national average is $1,289. Both describe small systems of mixed brands, often wireless, not a wired Hikvision system on a Westchester house.

On our list, a four-to-six-camera Hikvision system in Westchester starts at $2,250 with recorder, cable and setup, and a large estate or commercial job starts at $10,000. Service on an existing system is billed hourly.

Treat the national figure as a floor for a tiny wireless setup. A wired Westchester system is priced from your own drive lengths and walls.

“A Hikvision camera with old firmware is fine if it still records”

Recording and being secure are different things. CVE-2021-36260 let attackers run commands on affected Hikvision devices without a password, and CVE-2017-7921 was added to CISA's exploited list in March 2026, nine years after disclosure, because unpatched devices were still being found.

Westchester has a lot of systems installed during the brand's peak years and never touched since, often by a contractor who has since closed.

A recorder that has sat untouched since installation, especially one ever opened to outside access, needs new firmware first, fresh passwords second and its router rules closed third.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Walking a Westchester Gate Before Quoting It

On a northern Westchester property the first walk is down the drive, not around the house. We pace or wheel the distance from the house's utility room to the gate, because that number decides whether the gate camera is a simple Cat6 run, a fibre run with a media converter, or a wireless bridge. Anything past roughly 100 metres of cable gets one of the latter two, and we say so on the first page of the quote rather than finding out with a cable tester.

Next is power at the gate. Many gates already have a low-voltage operator or a lamp post; few have an outlet suitable for a switch and a heater-equipped camera. If one is needed, a county-licensed electrician adds it before we arrive. Then the ground: stone walls, ledge and old drainage lines determine whether a trench is a day's work or a week's, and a call to 811 goes in at least 48 hours ahead.

Last is the view. We stand where a car stops for the gate, at night if possible, and look at what headlights do to the lens. Plates are read at the stopping point, faces at the keypad, and the wide camera is set back far enough to show the whole car. Only after that do we choose a camera model, and we write down which Hikvision model and which non-covered alternative would do the same job, in case supply changes before installation day.
