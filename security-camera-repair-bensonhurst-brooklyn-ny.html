The Neighborhood

Why Bensonhurst Properties Need Security Camera Repair

Bensonhurst is a neighborhood that already has cameras. Walk any side street between 18th Avenue and Bay Parkway and you will see them on the porches of semi-detached houses, over the garage doors of the brick rowhouses, under the eaves of two-families and above the roll-down gates of the shops on 86th Street. The city put its own up too. In 2013 the Brooklyn Eagle reported that the NYPD had approved ARGUS camera sites on 86th Street at 20th Avenue, at Bay Parkway, at 23rd Avenue and at 25th Avenue, along with Stillwell and Benson Avenues, after Councilmember Domenic Recchia secured $400,000 for eleven new cameras in the 86th Street shopping area and other parts of southern Brooklyn. In 2020 Councilmember Justin Brannan set aside $275,000 for ten more NYPD cameras across a district that takes in Bay Ridge, Dyker Heights and parts of Bath Beach and Bensonhurst. So the question here is rarely whether a property should have cameras. It is whether the ones on the wall still work, and whether anybody would notice if they had stopped.

That second question is the one that brings most repair calls. A private camera system fails quietly. The recorder keeps its lights on, the app still opens, and the tiles on the screen look normal right up to the day someone needs a clip and finds the drive stopped writing three weeks ago, or the camera over the side alley has been showing a frozen frame since a storm. Brannan described the thieves his cameras were aimed at as people who "walk up and down the block and wiggle every door handle until they get lucky," and the same article reported police saying that most crimes in the 62nd and 68th Precincts involved items taken from unlocked vehicles. A camera that is supposed to cover the car in your driveway or at the curb only earns its keep if it is recording, if its clock is right, and if its picture at night shows more than a bright smear. That is the work on this page: finding which of those has failed and fixing it.

Robberies were up, at 115 against 106, and grand larcenies were flat at 488 against 490. Over the long run burglary in the precinct is down 92.5 percent from 1993. None of that makes a working camera less useful. When the Brooklyn District Attorney's office announced a ten-year sentence in 2024 for a man behind seven burglaries that took more than $150,000 in jewelry and cash, the targets included private homes on 76th Street and 72nd Street, grocery stores on 17th Avenue, Bath Avenue and New Utrecht Avenue, and a vape shop on 18th Avenue, and the office said cell phone data and video evidence linked the suspects to the crimes. Need Repair in Bensonhurst? A Dark Camera, a Recorder That Stopped Saving, or a Picture That Goes White After Sunset

Urgent camera calls from Bensonhurst usually fall into three groups. The first is something just happened, a car was opened on the block, a package went missing from the porch, a register was short at closing, and the owner opens the app to find the camera that should have seen it is blank or the recording for that hour is missing. The second is a recorder that has started beeping, shows a hard drive error, or reboots every few minutes, which in our experience is most often a failing drive or a recorder power adapter on its way out. The third is a picture that looks fine by day but turns white, flickers or drops to black every evening, which in houses and shops alike points at a power supply that cannot carry the extra load when every camera's infrared switches on at dusk.

If something has happened and you need footage, the most useful thing you can do before we arrive is nothing. Do not unplug the recorder, do not reset it, and do not let anyone "try a few things" with it, because a reset or a drive format can erase the very hour you need. Call (347) 934-8335, tell us the date and time window you need, and we will book the visit with saving that clip as the first task. If the footage exists, we export it to a USB drive in the recorder's own format and in a common video format, with the recorder's timestamp, so it can be handed to the 62nd Precinct or an insurer. Only after that do we start diagnosing what failed. If the recording does not exist because the drive stopped writing, we tell you that straight away, and then we make sure it cannot happen silently again.

For everything else, the usual path is a $150 service call booked within 2 hours of your request, a two-hour arrival window, and a written price for the fix before any part changes. Most single faults, a connector re-terminated, a power supply replaced, a camera body swapped, are finished on that first visit when the part is on the truck. When a part has to be ordered, such as a camera that must match an older recorder, we tell you that before you pay for it, and the repair is booked once it arrives.

Building Specialists

Security Camera Repair for Bensonhurst's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for nearly every Bensonhurst camera call. They differ in where the recorder ends up, how the cables reach the cameras, and who besides the person calling has to agree to the work. Those three things decide how long a diagnosis takes and what the fix is likely to be.

The semi-detached house

Two houses sharing one wall, with cameras on the open side, the front porch and the rear yard. Cables usually run down the outside brick and into the basement through a window frame or a drilled hole.

The two-family house

One system for two households. The recorder sits on one floor, often in the basement or the owner's closet, and the tenant may live with the cameras but not hold the login.

The rowhouse with a ground-floor garage

Three- and four-story brick rowhouses with the garage at street level. The recorder and power box often live in the garage, which runs hot in July and cold in January.

The detached house on a wider lot

Fewer of them, often on bigger lots, with cameras spread over the driveway, side yards and rear. Long cable runs and more exposure to weather.

The six-story apartment house

Lobby, vestibule, mailroom, laundry and hallway cameras wired back to a superintendent's room or basement office, often installed in stages by different contractors.

The storefront with apartments above

The typical building on 18th Avenue, 86th Street, Bath Avenue and Bay Parkway: a shop on the ground floor, cameras inside and over the gate, a recorder in a back room or under the counter.

The semi-detached house: cables on the brick and a recorder below grade

City Planning's description of Bensonhurst puts semi-detached homes across a large share of the side streets, and they produce one of the most predictable repair patterns we see. The cameras are almost always on the outside of the house: one at the porch, one or two down the open side covering the alley or driveway, one at the back facing the yard and the garage. In older installs the cables were run on the face of the brick, clipped every foot or two, and brought in through a basement window frame or a hole drilled at the sill. That outside run is where most of the trouble starts. Ten summers of sun can crack the jacket of a cheap cable, water follows the crack to the connector, and the camera at the end starts flickering in wet weather before it goes dark for good. On analog systems the fault often sits in the twist-on BNC connector at the camera; on network systems it is the plug at the camera end that corrodes. We check the far end first because it is the most exposed and the easiest to fix, and only replace a run when a meter or analyzer shows the cable itself has failed.

The shared wall matters too. A camera mounted on the corner of a semi-detached house often looks across the neighbor's side of the front yard, and when the neighbor complains, the camera gets nudged, taped or turned. If your camera's view has drifted, we re-aim it, tighten the mount and, where the recorder supports it, set a privacy mask so the picture never shows the neighbor's windows. That is a short adjustment, not a new camera.

The two-family: one recorder, two households and a login nobody shares

A two-family house usually has one camera system for the whole building, bought by whoever owned it at the time. The recorder ends up in the basement, in the owner's hall closet or, quite often, in the downstairs apartment because that is where the cables came in. Years later the owner lives upstairs, the tenant lives with the recorder, and the phone app is on the phone of an adult child who set it up and has since moved to another borough. When a camera fails, three people may need to be involved: the owner who pays, the tenant who has to let us in to the recorder, and the person who knows the password. We sort that out by text before the visit. If nobody has the recorder password, we can usually recover access on site with the owner present; the brand decides how, and some methods reset settings, which we explain before doing anything.

The garage rowhouse: heat, cold and a power box on the wall

The three- and four-story rowhouses with ground-floor garages that City Planning described were built into the 1980s, and many of them put the camera recorder and its power supply on a shelf or wall in the garage. Garages in Brooklyn are not heated or cooled, and a recorder's hard drive does not like either extreme. A recorder that reboots on the hottest afternoons of July, or a drive that starts reporting errors after a cold winter, is a common call from these houses. Part of the repair can be moving the recorder to a closet inside the living space, which usually means extending the cables through the garage ceiling into the floor above.

The detached house: long runs and weather on every side

City Planning noted detached houses "often on large lots" in part of the rezoned area. Those properties tend to have more cameras spread farther apart, a camera on a garage at the back of the lot, another on a fence post or a pole by the driveway. Long runs mean voltage drop on analog systems powered from one central box, so the camera at the far end is the first to flicker at night. Exposure on all four sides means wind-driven rain reaches connectors that would be sheltered on a semi-detached house. On these jobs we test the far cameras at the camera, not just at the recorder, because the reading at the recorder end can look fine while the camera is starved.

The apartment house: cameras added by different contractors over the years

City Planning described "corridors of six-story and taller apartment houses" on some of the larger avenues, and those buildings tend to have the most complicated systems: a lobby camera from one installer, hallway cameras from another, a laundry room camera somebody's nephew added, and a recorder in the superintendent's room that has been replaced twice. A CBS New York report from neighboring Gravesend in 2021 showed why laundry and mailroom cameras matter: video showed a burglar covering the camera before drilling a washing machine's lock. In buildings like these, the repair often starts with simply finding out which cable goes where, then labeling it, then fixing the actual fault. We work with the managing agent or board, and we only take instructions from whoever is authorized to give them.

The storefront with apartments above: inherited systems and the gate at closing

Most of the 1,265 storefronts the city counted on Bensonhurst's corridors are ground-floor shops in buildings with apartments above. The camera system usually belongs to the business, but it may have been left by the last tenant, and the recorder is often in a back room or under the counter where it collects dust and heat. The camera over the entrance has to see a face through the gap of a half-raised gate at closing, and the cameras inside the window fight reflection from the glass at night. We cover those businesses in detail in the commercial section below.

Field Notes

Field Notes: How a Technician Works Through a Bensonhurst Camera System in the First Half Hour

These notes describe how our technicians approach a camera repair in houses and shops like Bensonhurst's. They are our standard practice, not a story about any one address.

Start at the screen, not the ladder. The technician looks at the recorder first: which channels show video, which show "no video" or a frozen frame, whether the drive is listed as normal, and what the recorder's clock says. A clock that is hours or years off tells us the recorder has lost power or its clock battery, and that any clip from it will need a note explaining the time difference.

Group the dead cameras. If the dark cameras all hang off one power box, one switch or one side of the house, the shared part is tested before any single camera. If one camera alone is out, the technician goes to that camera's cable and connector at the recorder end, then at the camera.

Measure, then touch. Voltage at the camera, PoE power on the port, and a continuity or analyzer test on the cable settle most arguments in minutes. Nothing is unplugged or reset until any footage you asked for is saved.

Price before parts. Once the fault is found, you get the cause and a written price. The technician does not swap parts to "see if it helps," because that is how a $150 problem becomes a $600 bill.

Leave it labeled. Every cable we traced is marked at both ends we can reach, so the next repair, ours or anyone's, starts with a map.

Fault by Fault

Power, Cable, Recorder or Camera: Where Bensonhurst Systems Actually Fail and What Each Fix Involves

A camera system is a chain of four links: power, cable, the recorder or switch, and the camera itself. When one link fails, the symptom on the screen often looks the same, a black tile or a frozen picture, which is why so many owners replace a camera that was never broken. Brinks Home's own troubleshooting guide puts it bluntly: "One of the main reasons that a camera fails to work is an insufficient power source." Western Digital, which makes hard drives sold for surveillance recorders, says much the same: "An inadequate power supply is perhaps the most common reason your CCTV camera may not work." Here is how each link fails in the kinds of buildings Bensonhurst has, and what fixing it involves.

Power: the central box, the wall adapter and the PoE port

Analog systems, the kind with round BNC connectors on the back of the recorder, usually power their cameras from either a central power box with a row of fused outputs or a separate plug-in adapter for each camera. Both age. A central box can keep the cameras alive by day and then sag at dusk when every camera's infrared lights switch on at once and the load jumps. The result is cameras that reboot, flicker or go black only at night, which owners often read as a night vision problem. A single adapter in a damp basement or a hot garage can simply die. Network systems power cameras through the data cable from a PoE switch or from ports on the back of the recorder; a port can fail on its own, or a switch can hit its total power limit when a camera is added.

Fixing power is usually quick and usually inexpensive compared with replacing cameras: a new power supply sized with headroom for the night load, or a switch or port change. On our Brooklyn list power supply repair typically runs $150 to $400, and a reterminate or PoE port swap $150 to $350. We also recommend a small battery backup for the recorder in houses where the lights blink during storms, because a recorder that loses power mid-write is the one whose drive starts reporting errors.

Cable: sun, water, rodents and renovations

Cable faults in Bensonhurst follow the buildings. On semi-detached and detached houses, cable run on the outside of the brick is exposed to sun and rain for years. On storefronts, a renovation by a new tenant can cut a run in the ceiling or behind a new wall without anyone noticing until the camera goes dark. In basements and garages, rodents chew whatever is accessible. Most of the time, though, the cable is fine and the connector at the end of it is not. CCTV Camera World notes that copper-clad aluminum cable, damaged connectors, corrosion and old power wiring cause problems "even when the cable looks usable," and that is the order in which we test: connector first, cable second.

When a run really has failed, the repair depends on the route. An outside run on brick can often be replaced along the same path. A run inside a finished wall or ceiling may be replaced by a new path, sometimes in a neater surface raceway, which we agree with you before starting. A full rewire of a house or a large partial rewire is priced separately and starts at $1,500 on our Brooklyn list, and we tell you on the first visit if that is where things are heading.

Recorder: the hard drive, the clock and the software

The recorder is the part that fails most quietly. A drive that has developed bad sectors may keep recording in fits and starts, leaving gaps the owner discovers only when they look back for something. A recorder that has lost its clock setting stamps every clip with the wrong date. Older recorders may stop working with current phone apps after a firmware or service change. The fixes range from a drive swap ($200 to $450 depending on capacity) to a firmware update and settings review, to a replacement recorder. A DVR that will not work averages $300 to $700 on our Brooklyn list including the drive and labor; an NVR that is not recording averages $400 to $900 depending on the model; and a full DVR or NVR replacement with a drive and reconfiguration runs $700 to $1,500.

Before replacing a recorder, we check whether your existing cameras will work with the new one. CCTV Camera World makes the point that "often times good existing cable can stay in place while you change the equipment on either end of the cable," and many newer recorders handle several analog and HD-over-coax formats. That matters in a two-family or semi-detached house where the cables are buried in brick and plaster: keeping them saves the most expensive part of any job.

Camera: water, impact and age

Cameras themselves turn out to be the failed part less often than owners expect, but they do fail. Water gets in through a cracked housing or a poorly sealed cable entry. Two-families, tenants and who can book the repair

I rent the upstairs of a two-family off 20th Avenue, and the landlord's camera over our shared door stopped working. Can I book the repair myself?

You can start the request, but the system belongs to the owner, so we need the owner's go-ahead before we open the recorder or change anything. In practice the quickest route is to send us the photos and the owner's contact, and we arrange the visit with them by text, including who pays. If the owner agrees to let you handle it, we take their written OK by text or email and book with you. What we will not do is reset or reconfigure a recorder on someone's say-so when they are not the owner or authorized by the owner, because that recorder may hold footage the owner needs.

The recorder for our whole house is in my tenant's basement apartment. Can it be moved somewhere I can reach it?

Usually yes. Moving a recorder means extending or re-routing the camera cables from where they come together now to the new location, often a closet or utility space on the owner's floor or a common hallway. Analog coax and network cables can both be extended, though very long analog runs need care to keep the picture clean. We look at the route on the service call and give you a written price for the move as its own item. In many two-families the move costs less than owners expect, because the cables already pass through the basement ceiling and only the last stretch has to change.

Pictures that fail after dark

Every evening around sunset, three of my cameras on the side of my semi-detached house start rebooting or go black, but they're fine all day. What's happening?

That pattern almost always points at power, not at the cameras. When it gets dark each camera switches on its infrared lights, which draws noticeably more current, and if the cameras share one central power box that is old or undersized, the voltage sags and the cameras reset or drop out. The fact that it hits a group of cameras at once, all on one side of the house, makes a shared supply the prime suspect. The fix is normally a new power supply sized for the night load, which on our Brooklyn list typically runs $150 to $400. We confirm it with a meter at dusk before replacing anything.

Can a camera inside my shop window on 86th Street still see the sidewalk after the gate comes down at night?

It can, but usually not the way it is set up now. A camera looking out through glass at night sends its infrared light straight into the window, which reflects it back into the lens and produces a white or grey wash. The gate a few inches beyond the glass makes it worse by bouncing even more light back. The usual fix is to turn off the camera's own infrared and rely on the street and storefront lighting, to press a camera designed for it right up against the glass, or to move the camera outside under the sign where it can see the doorway directly. Under the elevated tracks, where light is uneven, the outdoor position often gives the cleanest picture. We test the options on the visit before suggesting a part.

My driveway camera on 81st Street shows the car but never the license plate after dark. Can the camera I have be adjusted to read it?

Often it can. A plate is made to reflect light back at its source, so a camera with its own infrared pointed straight at the back of a car gets a glowing rectangle instead of readable characters. Turning down the infrared, shortening the exposure, changing the angle so the plate is not square to the camera, or narrowing the zoom on the plate area can all help, and on many recorders those are settings, not parts. If your camera simply does not have the zoom or the settings to do it, we tell you, and the answer may be one dedicated camera aimed at the driveway rather than a whole new system. Since police in the 62nd and 68th Precincts have said most crimes there involve items taken from vehicles, it is worth getting right.

Inherited, mixed and do-it-yourself systems

We put in a box-store camera kit ourselves a few years ago. Do you charge more to fix a system you didn't install?

No. The service call is the same $150, and the repair is priced by what failed. Self-installed kits do tend to have the same few weak points: plug-in adapters in damp places, extension cables joined with tape outdoors, and recorders tucked into closets with no airflow. We find those quickly. If a part from the kit has failed and the same model is no longer sold, we tell you what will work with the recorder you have before you spend anything.

My apartment building's cameras were added by three different companies over the years and some don't show up on the recorder. Can they all go on one system?

Often, yes, but it depends on what each camera is. Network cameras from different brands can usually be added to one recorder if they support the common ONVIF standard, though some features such as smart alerts may only work brand-to-brand. Analog cameras need a recorder that supports their specific signal type. On the first visit we identify every camera, label every cable, and tell you which can join the main recorder as they are, which need a setting change, and which would have to be replaced. That inventory alone usually explains why some cameras "disappeared" from the screen.

We bought a store on Bath Avenue that came with cameras and a recorder. What has to happen before that system is really ours?

Three things: confirming the equipment was sold with the business, getting control of the recorder, and putting the accounts in your name. Control is the technical part, and the method depends on the brand and model: some recorders allow a reset with a code from the manufacturer tied to the serial number, some need a physical reset, and a few need a firmware procedure. Most of these reset the old settings and accounts, which suits a new owner who wants a clean start. We confirm the equipment came with the business, set up new accounts in your name, put the app on your phone, and write down the details for you to keep somewhere safe.

Tampering, privacy and the neighbors

Someone keeps turning my porch camera toward the wall. How do you stop it happening again?

There are three parts to the fix. First, height and reach: a camera mounted where a person can touch it from the porch rail will be moved, so we raise it or relocate it out of reach. Second, the mount: a vandal-resistant dome or a bracket with a locking adjustment is much harder to push. Third, alerts: many recorders can warn you on your phone when a camera's view suddenly changes or goes dark, which is exactly what the Gravesend laundry-room burglar reported by CBS New York did when he covered the camera. We set that up if your recorder supports it.

My neighbor says my camera can see into his yard. Can you fix the view without taking the camera down?

Yes. On semi-detached and closely spaced houses, a camera on the corner almost always takes in part of the house next door. We re-aim it to cover your own door, yard and driveway, and where the recorder supports privacy masking, we black out the part of the picture that shows the neighbor's windows or yard so it is never recorded. It is a short adjustment, usually covered by the service call when no part is needed, and it tends to settle the disagreement for good.

Footage, clocks and evidence

My recorder's clock was off by a few hours when I pulled a clip for a car break-in. Is the footage still useful?

Usually it is, as long as you can explain the difference. Write down the time the recorder showed and the real time when you noticed, so anyone reviewing the clip can line it up with other evidence. Then get the clock fixed, because the gap tends to grow. A recorder loses time when its clock battery is dead or when it is not set to take the time from the internet, and both are quick to correct. We set the recorder to keep time automatically and confirm the time zone and daylight saving settings, which are a common source of a one-hour error.

Can you join my shop's cameras to the NYPD's camera-sharing program?

We can get your system ready for it, and the registration itself is your decision and your application. The pilot the Mayor's Office announced in 2024 lets retail businesses register their cameras with the NYPD and choose whether to share live feeds, at no cost to the business. For that to be useful, the recorder needs current firmware, a stable internet connection and correct time, and the cameras need to cover what matters, the door, the register and the sidewalk. We handle those parts; you decide whether and how to sign up.

Heat, cold, damp and power cuts

Our recorder sits in the garage of our rowhouse and keeps restarting on hot summer afternoons. Do we need a new one?

Not necessarily. Recorders and their hard drives are made for indoor temperatures, and a sealed garage in July can run far hotter than that. A recorder that restarts in the heat may be perfectly healthy in a cooler room. We check the drive's health and the recorder's own logs; if both are fine, the cheapest fix is often moving the recorder into the house or giving it ventilation. If the drive has already been damaged by heat, we replace it, which runs $200 to $450 on our Brooklyn list depending on capacity.

The lights blink during storms and afterwards my cameras take ages to come back. Should the recorder be on a battery backup?

For most houses and shops, yes. A small uninterruptible power supply keeps the recorder and, on network systems, the PoE switch running through brief outages and lets them shut down cleanly in a long one. That protects the hard drive, which is the part most likely to be damaged when power is cut mid-write, and it keeps the cameras recording during the very events, storms and outages, when people are most likely to try doors. We size the battery to the recorder and switch you have and can add it on a repair visit.

A ball from the playground down the block cracked the dome on our front camera. Do we need a whole new camera?

Often not. On many dome cameras the clear bubble or front cover is a separate part, and when only the cover is cracked or scratched, replacing it brings the picture back for a $30 to $80 part plus labor. The thing to check is whether water got in through the crack before it was noticed. If the picture shows spots, the infrared has stopped working, or the camera reboots in wet weather, the electronics have probably been reached and the camera itself should be replaced, which runs $250 to $600 on our Brooklyn list including the camera. Near parks and schoolyards, a vandal-rated dome is worth the small difference next time.
