The Neighborhood

Why Bergen Beach Properties Need Alarm Installation

Bergen Beach is one of the most suburban corners of Brooklyn, and that is exactly why an alarm here has to be planned differently from one in a rowhouse neighborhood. Wikipedia describes it as a residential neighborhood on a peninsula against Jamaica Bay, bordered by Mill Basin, Flatlands, Paerdegat Basin and the Belt Parkway, with a sub-neighborhood called Georgetown. Brooklyn Community Board 18 describes both Bergen Beach and Georgetowne as zoned for predominantly one- and two-family homes with small commercial overlays and recreational waterfront uses. There is no subway, the buses are the B3, the B41 and the BM1, and most households drive. In practice that means many houses stand on their own lot or in a pair, with a driveway, a garage, side yards and a back yard, and every one of those sides is a way in.

An attached rowhouse has two faces to protect, the front and the back. A detached house in Bergen Beach has four, plus a garage, plus a basement, and the two side yards are often the least watched ground on the property. A narrow passage between a house and its neighbor, screened by a fence and a car in the driveway, is where a person can try a side door or a basement window without being seen from the street. That is why our surveys here start with a full walk around the house rather than a look at the front door. The front of a Bergen Beach house, up its short flight of steps, is usually the most visible part of it. The side door off the driveway, the back slider to the yard and the door from the garage into the house are where the plan has to be strongest.

The second thing that makes this neighborhood different is the ground itself. Bergen Beach was an island in Jamaica Bay until it was joined to the rest of Brooklyn by landfill early in the twentieth century, and more fill was added in the 1980s. According to Wikipedia, that fill was unstable, parts of the neighborhood have been sinking, and many homes were built so that extra steps could be added if the ground in front of the house sank. A 1988 New York Times report, quoted in the same article, put it bluntly: everything not on piles driven down to bedrock, everything except the houses, was breaking and buckling. For an alarm, that matters in a way most installers never think about. A house on piles stays put while its stoops, driveways, garage slabs and back patios move around it. Doors that meet those surfaces, especially garage doors and patio doors, change their fit over the years, and a door contact that was perfectly aligned on install day can start reading open after a cold winter. We choose and place contacts on those doors with that movement in mind.

The third is water. The Canarsie Courier reported that a storm on April 30, 2023, which brought 2.32 inches of rain to Brooklyn after two wet days before it, caused extreme flooding in Bergen Beach, with streets and homes between Avenue U and Avenue T and East 69th Street and Bergen Avenue flooded, and many basements filled through shower drains and toilets. The president of the Bergen Beach Civic Association said about 67 houses were affected. During Hurricane Sandy in 2012, a New York State Public Service Commission filing records some areas of Bergen Beach reporting six to nine feet of flood water. Brownstoner, reporting on the city’s updated hurricane evacuation map in 2021, listed Bergen Beach among the neighborhoods in Zones 1 and 2. An alarm panel and its battery mounted in a basement next to the boiler, which is where many older systems were put, can be lost in exactly the event the owner most wants to be warned about. So on this page the panel goes upstairs, and the basement gets water sensors instead.

Burglary here is low by any long-term measure, and we will not pretend otherwise. The NYPD’s CompStat report for the 63rd Precinct, which patrols Bergen Beach along with Marine Park, Mill Basin and part of Flatlands, counted 1,119 burglaries in the whole of 1990 and 67 in the whole of 2025. This year has been a little worse than last: 65 burglaries through November 8, 2026, against 57 in the same period of 2025, a rise of 14 percent, even as the precinct’s grand larcenies of autos fell from 134 to 109. Those are precinct-wide numbers across four neighborhoods, so they say nothing about any one house. What they do say is that break-ins are rare enough here that a household can go years without thinking about one, which is exactly the situation in which a basement window or a side door gets left unlatched, and in which nobody would notice for hours if it were forced.

One more local detail shapes how a Bergen Beach alarm should report. Wikipedia notes that because of the 63rd Precinct’s distance from Bergen Beach, some residents have complained that there is not enough police presence in the neighborhood; the station house is at 1844 Brooklyn Avenue, well to the northwest. We cannot change how quickly a patrol car arrives, and we will not promise a response time on anyone’s behalf. What an alarm can do is make sure the right people know within seconds: the household on their phones, a neighbor or relative who lives nearby, and, if the owner chooses monitoring, a central station that has the cross streets, the driveway and the door to use written into its dispatch notes. In a neighborhood where houses sit apart and the nearest neighbor may not hear an indoor siren, the alert matters more than the noise.

Alarm Installation for Bergen Beach’s Architecture

Bergen Beach was built late. Wikipedia records a few bungalows and vacation houses before the First World War, fourteen single-family houses in the 1940s, suburban growth from the 1950s, and a surge after more marsh was filled in the 1980s, with much of the neighborhood not fully developed until the 1990s. Georgetowne, Community Board 18 says, was developed slowly from the 1960s through the 1980s. The result is a neighborhood of comparatively young houses in a few recognizable types, and each one asks something different of an alarm.

The detached 1980s and 1990s house

Brick or brick-and-siding one-family homes on their own lots, built after the last fill, often with a front stoop, a driveway along one side, an attached garage and a finished basement. Four exposed walls, more doors than a rowhouse, and a back yard that faces other back yards.

The Georgetown semi-detached pair

Two-story houses joined in pairs, some of them the first houses built for Georgetowne Greens in the 1960s, and split-levels such as the all-brick semi-detached multi-family home a recent listing described on East 69th Street. One shared wall, three open sides.

The two-family with a rental unit

A house with the owner on one floor and a tenant on another, sometimes with the rental at garden level. Separate entrances, a shared basement or boiler room, and two households that need separate codes.

The gated condominium

Mill Harbor, which Wikipedia calls one of the few gated communities in New York City, with homes built around 2005, assigned carports and a clubhouse pool according to one listing, and a four-story building of 208 units on Royce Street with an intercom to the front desk.

The low-rise condo of Georgetowne

The small condominium buildings Community Board 18 mentions among Georgetowne’s one- and two-family homes: a common entrance run by the association, and apartments whose owners want only their own doors and windows on an alarm.

The waterfront and basin-side house

Houses on the blocks nearest Jamaica Bay and Paerdegat Basin, with decks, sliders and big rear windows facing the water and open ground beyond the back fence. The most glass and the least overlooked back yards in the neighborhood.

The detached house: a walk around all four sides

The survey of a detached Bergen Beach house always follows the same route, and it starts outside. We stand at the curb and look at the front: the door at the top of the stoop, the windows on either side of it, and the windows above. Then we walk up the driveway. The driveway side is the one most families use every day, with a side door into the kitchen or a mudroom, the garage door, and often a basement window or two at the foot of the wall. It is also the side with the most cover: a parked car, a fence, a gate, and the wall of the next house a few feet away. Then we go round the back, where there is usually a slider or a pair of French doors onto a patio or deck, the kitchen and family-room windows, and sometimes a basement door down a short flight of outside steps. Last comes the far side, the narrow strip between your house and the neighbor’s, which nobody walks except the meter reader and is screened from the street at both ends.

The list that comes out of that walk is longer than a rowhouse list, and we would rather say so than pretend otherwise. A typical detached house here has four or five exterior doors once the garage door and the door from the garage into the house are counted, and more ground-floor windows than a basic system’s four to six sensors will cover. That is why most detached houses land in the $600 to $1,200 wireless range, or in complete home protection from $2,500 once glass-break, smoke, carbon monoxide and water sensors are added, rather than on the basic starting price. What brings the number down is judgment about the windows. A ground-floor window you can stand outside and reach needs protection; a second-floor window with nothing beneath it except lawn usually does not, unless a deck, a porch roof, a garage roof or a trellis puts it within reach. We mark each one and say why.

Inside, the motion detectors go where the routes from those doors meet. In a center-hall house that is usually the hall at the foot of the stairs; in a side-hall or split-level layout it may be the landing between levels. A detector there sees anyone coming from the garage, the basement, the back door or the front, and it does not need to see the whole house. Bedrooms stay out of motion coverage so the family can arm at night and still walk to the kitchen, with the doors and windows still protected.

The Georgetown semi-detached house: three open sides and a shared wall

A semi-detached house is a detached house with one side removed, and the alarm follows that logic. The shared wall needs nothing; nobody comes through a party wall. The open side, the back and the front get the same treatment as a detached house, and the garage, often tucked under the house or set at the end of a driveway between pairs, gets its own contact. Split-level layouts, which are common in this part of the neighborhood, add a twist: the lowest level, often half below grade, has windows close to the ground and sometimes its own door to the yard, while the main floor is a half-flight up. The lowest level is where we put the first motion detector, because it is the easiest level to enter and the one most often empty.

The siren needs more thought in a pair than in a detached house. A loud exterior bell on a semi-detached house sounds through the shared wall as clearly as through your own rooms, and a household that sets off its siren twice in a month by mistake soon hears about it from next door. We fit an interior siren loud enough to drive out an intruder, keep any exterior sounder modest, set the cutoff well inside the city’s fifteen-minute limit, and make sure the people on the other side of the wall know whose alarm it is and who to call.

The two-family house: one building, two households

Bergen Beach’s zoning allows two-family houses, and many owners live on one floor and rent the other. The question in these houses is not which doors to protect but whose alarm it is. A partitioned panel gives each household its own area and its own codes on one system. The owner’s floor arms on the owner’s code, the rental floor on the tenant’s, and the shared spaces, usually the basement, the boiler room and the door from the garage, sit in a common area that is armed only when both households have armed their own. The tenant decides whether to use their part of the system. The owner can see whether the shared doors are protected, but not when the tenant comes and goes, unless both agree otherwise. When a lease ends, the old tenant’s code is deleted in a minute.

Two-family owners should also know that the alarm does not change their legal duties about detectors. HPD’s rules require carbon monoxide detectors, as well as smoke detectors, in one- and two-family homes that are not owner-occupied and have fuel-burning appliances, within fifteen feet of the entrance to each sleeping room. Monitored smoke and carbon monoxide detectors on the alarm are a good addition, but they sit alongside the detectors the law requires rather than replacing them.

The gated condominium: inside your own door

A gate and a front desk change who can walk up to your door, but they do not watch the inside of your home. At Mill Harbor, where one listing describes an association fee covering common-area and exterior maintenance, the gate, the grounds, the carports and the building systems belong to the association, and an owner’s alarm should never be wired into any of them. What we install is a tenant-style system inside the unit: a small cellular panel in a closet, contacts on the unit’s entry doors and on any slider or window that can be reached from the ground, a patio or a low roof, one motion detector across the main room, and a keypad by the door. Everything is wireless, nothing is fixed to the outside of the building, and anything that might touch a common element, such as a camera at the front door, waits for the board’s approval.

Condo owners at Mill Harbor and in Georgetowne’s low-rise buildings also tend to be away for long stretches, whether for the winter or for family visits, and an alarm that only makes noise in an empty unit does little. These are the systems where we most often recommend central station monitoring or, at minimum, alerts to a relative or neighbor who has a key, plus a water sensor under the kitchen sink and by the water heater, since a leak in a unit that sits empty for weeks becomes the neighbors’ problem too.

The house nearest the water: glass, decks and open ground

The blocks closest to Jamaica Bay and Paerdegat Basin have houses built to look at the water, with decks, sliders and large rear windows. From an alarm’s point of view that is a lot of glass facing ground that nobody overlooks, especially in winter when the water’s edge is empty. Contacts on every slider and door, glass-break detectors in the rooms with the largest panes, and a motion detector that covers the route from the back of the house inward are the core. We also look hard at the deck itself, because a raised deck puts second-floor windows within easy reach, and at the lighting, because a dark back yard on open ground is exactly where a floodlight camera earns its place.

Built on Fill, Next to the Bay

Planning an Alarm for a House Whose Ground Moves and Whose Basement Floods

Most alarm advice assumes a house that stays still and stays dry. Bergen Beach houses mostly do stay still, because, as the 1988 Times report put it, it is everything except the houses that breaks and buckles on the sinking fill. But the driveways, stoops, garage slabs and patios around them move, and Wikipedia notes that some houses were built so extra steps could be added at the front as the ground dropped. For an alarm, the doors that meet those moving surfaces are the ones to worry about, and the garage door is the first of them.

An overhead garage door is usually alarmed with a contact on the floor and a magnet on the bottom edge of the door. That works on a slab that stays level. On a slab that has settled or cracked, the door may stop an inch higher on one side than the other, or the gap may change between a warm afternoon and a frozen night, and the contact reads open when the door is shut. The family notices that the alarm will not arm, bypasses the garage, and soon stops arming at all. We avoid that in two ways. We use wide-gap overhead door contacts designed for a garage door’s movement, mounted where the door’s travel is most consistent, and where the slab is badly out of level we use a tilt sensor on the top panel of the door instead, which reports when the panel swings up rather than measuring a gap at the floor. Either way, we test the door closed several times before we leave, and the follow-up call after the first cold snap asks specifically about the garage.

Patio sliders and French doors onto a sinking patio raise the same problem more slowly. A slider whose track has dropped at one end will still close and lock, but the leading edge may sit a little differently in its frame each season. A contact with a generous gap rating, mounted at the top of the door rather than the bottom, where the frame is fixed to the house rather than the ground, stays aligned far longer. Where a door has already moved enough to bind, we say so on the written price and suggest the owner have it adjusted first, because an alarm cannot make a sticking door secure.

Water is the other half of the problem. The April 2023 storm flooded basements in a block of Bergen Beach from below, through shower drains and toilets, and the civic association president told the Canarsie Courier that his own house was spared by a backflow valve. We cannot stop a sewer backup, and we will not claim an alarm can. What a water sensor on the alarm can do is tell you about it within seconds, wherever you are, rather than when you come down the stairs in the morning. That is why our water sensors go at the lowest points where water arrives first: the basement floor drain, the floor beside a basement shower and toilet, under the boiler and the water heater, and beside the washing machine. Each one reports as its own zone, so the alert tells you which drain is backing up.

The same thinking decides where the alarm itself lives. Many older systems in this neighborhood have their panel, transformer and backup battery in the basement, often beside the boiler, because that is where the phone line came in. In a house that took water during Sandy or in 2023, that is the worst possible place for it, because the alarm fails in the very event it should be reporting. On every Bergen Beach job we put the panel and its battery on a main floor, usually in a hall closet or a utility cupboard off the kitchen, with a cellular communicator so it does not depend on a phone line or a basement router. If the basement floods and the power to the house is cut, the panel keeps running on its battery and keeps reporting over cellular.

Two last points come from the same ground. First, the flood sensors are only useful if they are tested, so on the visit we show the household how to trip one with a damp cloth and see the alert arrive, and we suggest doing it before the start of each hurricane season. Second, an alarm cannot be a substitute for the city’s evacuation advice. In a coastal storm the right use of the system is to arm it, leave, and watch it from wherever you have gone, with the panel upstairs and the battery charged, not to stay in the house because the alarm is on.

  • Fire alarms are a separate system. A burglar alarm is not a code fire alarm system.   • Detectors in rental housing. HPD requires smoke detectors in each unit of a multiple dwelling and carbon monoxide detectors in non-owner-occupied one- and two-family homes with fuel-burning appliances. Adding monitored devices to an alarm does not replace those duties.
  • Condominium and shopping-center rules. Flooding, rain and storms

Our alarm’s panel and battery were in the basement when the April 2023 storm pushed water up through the drains. Which parts of a flooded system can be kept, and which have to go?

Anything that sat in the water goes; anything above it can usually stay. A panel circuit board, a transformer and a backup battery that were submerged, even briefly, should be replaced rather than dried and switched back on, because flood water, and especially the sewage-laced water the Canarsie Courier described coming up through shower drains and toilets in 2023, corrodes the board and can damage the battery in ways that only show up weeks later. Sensors and detectors on the main floor and upstairs that never got wet are normally fine, and so is the wiring that runs through the walls above the water line. Wiring that ran along a flooded basement ceiling may also be sound, but wiring that lay in water, or connections in a junction box that flooded, we test and usually replace. On most of these jobs the answer is a new panel with a cellular communicator on a main floor, connected to the old hardwired sensors upstairs, plus wireless sensors in the basement and water sensors at the drains, which is the panel replacement line on our price list, $800 to $1,500.

Rain ponds in our street and against the front of the house after every heavy storm. Can standing water at a basement window or under the garage door trip the burglar sensors?

It should not, if the sensors are chosen and placed for it. A magnetic door or window contact only reports whether a magnet has moved away from its switch; water on its own does not open a window or lift a garage door, so a properly mounted contact ignores it. The trouble comes from contacts mounted where water collects. A garage door contact screwed to the floor in a low corner of a settled slab can sit in a puddle, corrode and fail open; a basement window contact at the bottom of a window well that fills in heavy rain can short. Wikipedia notes that in parts of Bergen Beach minor rain or snow melt is enough to cause ponding, so on every survey we look at where water stands against the house. Garage contacts go on the side track or the top panel rather than the floor where the slab is low, and basement window contacts go at the top of the sash rather than the bottom. If water is getting into the house through a window well, that is a job for a waterproofing contractor, and a water sensor on the alarm will tell you when it happens.

If the city orders our evacuation zone to leave before a hurricane, what should we do with the alarm on the way out?

Arm it fully, check the app, and leave. Brownstoner’s report on the city’s 2021 evacuation map listed Bergen Beach among the neighborhoods in Zones 1 and 2, and an alarm is never a reason to stay behind. Before you go, look at the app and confirm that every water sensor reads dry and the panel shows mains power and a charged battery, so that any later change is meaningful. Arm in the away mode, which covers motion as well as doors and windows, because in an empty, storm-hit neighborhood the risk is someone walking in, not someone breaking a window while you sleep. Make sure the people on your alert list know you have left and where you are, and, if you have monitoring, tell the station that the house is empty so it does not waste a call trying to reach you at home. Afterwards, expect power to go and water alerts to come in; the panel on a main floor, with its battery and cellular communicator, will keep reporting for as long as the battery lasts, and the alerts tell you what you will face on your return.

Our neighbor’s backflow valve kept their basement dry in 2023 and ours flooded. Can the alarm tell us whether our own valve has closed?

Not directly, and we would rather send you to a plumber than pretend otherwise. A backflow valve on a house sewer line is a plumbing device, usually a flap that closes when sewage tries to flow back toward the house; the Bergen Beach Civic Association president credited one with keeping his house dry in 2023. Whether you have one, whether it is the right type and whether it is working is a licensed plumber’s question. What the alarm can do is put a water sensor right beside the cleanout or the lowest floor drain, which is the first place water appears if the valve fails or is missing, and another beside the basement shower and toilet, which is where the Courier said water came in for many homes. If water reaches any of them, you get an alert naming the spot within seconds, which may be enough time to stop using water upstairs and get valuables off the basement floor.

Settling ground and the outside of the house

The 1988 Times report said the houses here stand on piles while the ground around them sinks. Does that mean sensors on the house itself stay put and only outdoor devices move?

Mostly, yes, and that is a useful way to plan. A contact screwed to a door frame and a window sash that are both part of a house on piles moves with the house, so it stays aligned. Devices fixed to things that rest on the fill are the ones that drift: a contact on a garage door whose floor is a slab poured on the ground, a sensor on a gate post set in a footing, an outdoor beam on a fence post, a camera on a free-standing pole in the yard. Doors are the in-between case, because the house holds the frame but the stoop, patio or driveway outside may have dropped, so a storm door, a slider or a garage door can change how it closes. That is why we mount as much as possible on the house itself, use contacts with a generous gap on doors that meet the ground, prefer tilt sensors to floor contacts on garage doors where the slab has moved, and keep outdoor detection to cameras mounted on the house wall rather than posts in the yard. If your front steps have been extended over the years, as Wikipedia says many Bergen Beach houses were designed to allow, that tells us the ground at your house is one of the places it has moved.

We have an in-ground pool behind the house and a gate from the side yard. Can the pool gate be on the alarm so we know when it opens?

Yes, and for many families with young children or grandchildren that matters more than the burglary side. An outdoor-rated wireless contact on the pool gate, set as its own zone, can chime inside the house every time the gate opens, day or night, whether or not the alarm is armed, and can send a notification to your phone if you choose. We set it to chime rather than sound the siren, because the people opening it will usually be family. Two practical points. The gate post must be solid, because a contact on a gate that sags on a settling post will report open when it is closed; if the post moves, it needs fixing before the sensor goes on. And wireless range from the yard to a panel inside the house is usually fine for a typical Bergen Beach lot, but we test the signal from the gate before we fix anything and add a repeater if the back of the lot is far from the panel. A pool gate contact is a convenience and a warning, not a substitute for the latching gate and fence that pool safety requires.

Semi-detached houses and condos

The family on the other half of our semi-detached house already has an alarm. Can two wireless systems on either side of one wall interfere with each other?

No, not in any way that matters. Every wireless sensor in a modern system carries its own identity, and a panel only listens to the sensors that have been enrolled on it, so your neighbor’s door opening cannot appear on your keypad and yours cannot set off theirs, even if both panels use the same brand and sit a few feet apart on either side of the party wall. The two systems share the radio band in the same way two cordless phones do, and the protocols are designed for crowded places like apartment buildings where dozens of panels work side by side. What does need coordinating is the siren. If either household uses a loud exterior sounder, both will hear it through the shared wall, so it helps for each family to know whose alarm is whose and to have a phone number for the other. Some pairs also agree to put each other on their alert lists, which costs nothing and gives each house a neighbor who will look out of the window when an alert comes in.

Usually only from inside your own home. “Put the alarm panel in the basement or a utility closet, out of the way.”

On a rowhouse, that is close to true, because a rowhouse has only a front and a back. What moves a Bergen Beach quote up or down

  • Sides, not square feet. A semi-detached house with three open sides usually needs fewer sensors than a detached house of the same size with four, and a condo unit needs fewer still.
  • The garage. An attached garage adds the overhead door and the door into the house, and sometimes a motion detector inside; a garage door on a badly settled slab may need a tilt sensor rather than a floor contact.
  • Glass at the back. Sliders, French doors and big rear windows facing the yard or the water usually call for glass-break detectors as well as contacts, which pushes a house toward complete home protection.
  • The basement. A finished basement with a bathroom, a laundry and a boiler needs wireless contacts on its windows and door and water sensors at each drain, each one a line on the quote.
  • Old wiring above the water line. Sound hardwired sensors on the main floors can usually be kept with a new panel moved upstairs, which is the cheapest upgrade on the list.
  • Store glass. Each large pane of storefront glass needs a glass-break or shock sensor, so a shop with a long glass frontage costs more than one with a narrow door.

Field Notes: Start in the Side Yard, Finish at the Floor Drain

This describes how our technicians survey a Bergen Beach house, not a story about one particular job. The visit starts at the curb with a look at the front steps, because steps that have been extended or rebuilt tell us the ground here has moved. Then the technician walks up the driveway, opens and closes the garage door twice and looks at the gap at the floor on both sides, and tries the side door. He walks the narrow strip on the other side of the house, looking at every basement window and asking whether anyone ever goes down there. At the back he opens the slider or the French doors, checks how they meet the frame, and looks at what a person could stand on to reach an upstairs window: a deck, a porch roof, the garage roof, a trellis.

Inside, the first question is where the old panel is, and in many houses here the answer is the basement. So he goes down, finds the floor drain, the shower, the toilet, the water heater, the boiler and the washer, and asks the question that decides half the plan: did water come in during Sandy, or in April 2023, and where did it come from? Then he chooses a main-floor closet for the new panel, walks the routes from each door to the bedrooms to place the motion detector, and asks about the household: who comes in through the garage, who is home in the day, whether anyone rents a floor, and whether the house is left empty for weeks. FAQ

Bergen Beach Alarm Installation FAQ

If a water sensor goes off at 3 a.m., can we silence it without disarming the burglar side of the alarm?
+

Yes. Water sensors sit on 24-hour zones that work whether the alarm is armed or not, and acknowledging a water alert at the keypad or in the app silences that alert only. The doors, windows and motion detectors stay armed exactly as they were, so you can go down to the basement to deal with the water while the rest of the house is still protected.

Does a flood sensor keep working once it is sitting in water?
+

It reports at the moment water first touches its probes, which is the alert that matters, and it keeps reporting while it stays wet. Most wireless water sensors are not built to sit submerged for days, so after a real flood we test each one and replace any that fail. The panel itself stays safe because it is mounted on a main floor, not in the basement.

Can thunder or heavy rain against the windows set off a glass-break detector?
+

Not a properly chosen and placed one. Modern acoustic glass-break detectors listen for the particular combination of a low thud and the high frequencies of breaking glass, which thunder and rain do not produce. We mount them on an inside wall facing the glass, test them with a simulator, and avoid placing them where a slamming storm door or a dropped pan sounds right beside them.

When the monitoring station receives a water alarm instead of a break-in, who does it call?
+

You and the people on your call list, not the police. Water and temperature alarms are set up as supervisory signals that the station handles by phoning the household and your chosen contacts, while burglary, panic, fire and carbon monoxide signals follow the emergency procedure you agreed. We write the call list and the order with you on install day.

Is there a minimum number of sensors before a system can be connected to central station monitoring?
+

No. Even a small condo system with an entry door contact, a motion detector and a keypad can be monitored, from $30 a month through our partners. What we do insist on is that every sensor on a monitored system is tested and named, so that when the station calls, it can tell you exactly which door or zone reported.

Will the app warn us about a weak sensor battery before the keypad starts chirping?
+

Yes. The panel reports a low battery on any wireless sensor as soon as it detects one, and the app shows which sensor it is, usually weeks before the battery is flat. Replacing it then, at a convenient hour, avoids the overnight chirping that happens when a low-battery warning reaches the keypad and nobody has dealt with it.

Can the alarm app show whether the garage door is up and let us close it from our phones?
+

Showing whether it is open, yes, through the garage door contact or tilt sensor, and the app can remind you if it has been left open after dark. Closing it remotely needs a compatible garage door controller connected to the opener, which we can quote as an add-on after checking the opener’s make and age on the survey.

Can we turn off the basement sensors while a plumber is working down there and keep the rest of the house armed?
+

Yes. Any zone can be bypassed for one arming period from the keypad or the app, so the basement door, windows, motion detector and water sensors can be switched off while the plumber works and the rest of the house stays protected. The bypass clears itself the next time the system is disarmed, so nothing is left off by mistake.

How long do wireless door and window contacts last before they need replacing?
+

The contacts themselves usually last many years; it is their batteries that need changing, typically every few years depending on the model and how often the door is used. Contacts on doors that meet settling ground may need re-aligning sooner than they need replacing, which is why we check the garage and slider contacts on the follow-up call.

Will a storm that knocks out local cell service stop the alarm from reporting?
+

The panel keeps working on its battery, the siren still sounds, and the communicator keeps trying to send its signals until the network comes back. Where an owner wants a second path, the panel can also report over the home’s internet when that is working. No system can promise to report when every network is down, and we will not claim otherwise.

We close on a Bergen Beach house next month. How soon after closing can the alarm go in?
+

Requests are booked within 2 hours, and we can survey the house as soon as you have access, even between contract and closing if the seller agrees. The written price comes from that visit, and installation follows once it is agreed. Many buyers have the alarm in before they move furniture in, so the empty house is protected from the first night.

If we redo the kitchen or finish the basement later, can the wireless sensors be moved?
+

Yes. Wireless contacts, detectors and water sensors come off and go back on in minutes, and the panel simply keeps them enrolled. Take them down before the contractor starts, keep them together, and call us when the work is done. We refit them, add any new doors or windows the renovation created, update the zone names and re-test everything.
