Elevator Access Control Explained for a Staten Island Building Buying It for the First Time

Most people who call us from Staten Island about an elevator have never bought this kind of system before. A co-op board on the North Shore has been told by a resident that strangers ride up to the top floor. A landlord on Hylan Boulevard has a small elevator building with offices upstairs and a store at street level, and wants the store's customers to stop wandering to the second floor. A property manager at a senior residence wants to know who is using the elevator at three in the morning. None of them want a sales pitch; they want to understand what they are buying. This section is written for them.

An elevator, left alone, will take anyone anywhere. Push a button in the lobby and the car comes. Step inside and push any floor and the car goes there. The building's only protection is whatever happens at the front door. If the front door is propped open for a delivery, if a visitor follows a resident in, or if a side door off a parking lot does not latch, every floor of the building is one button away from a stranger. Elevator access control changes that one fact. It makes the elevator ask, in effect, "who are you?" before it moves.

It does that with a reader. The reader can sit inside the car, on or beside the panel of floor buttons, or it can sit outside the car at a landing, next to the call button. A rider presents something the building has issued: a key fob, a card, a code typed on a keypad, a phone app, or, in some buildings, a face or a fingerprint. The reader passes that credential to a small access control panel, usually in the machine room or a nearby closet. The panel checks it against the list of approved people and their permissions. If the person is allowed, the panel closes a relay, which is just an electrical switch, and the elevator controller receives a signal that says "this request is permitted." If the person is not allowed, nothing happens, and the attempt is written into a log with the time and the credential that was tried.

That is the whole idea. Everything else, from the type of reader to the software the property manager uses, is a choice about how strict the building wants to be and how convenient it needs to be for the people who live or work there. On Staten Island, those choices tend to land in a different place than in Manhattan or Downtown Brooklyn, because the buildings are different. You are more likely to have one car than four. You are more likely to have five or six floors than thirty. You are more likely to have a board that runs the building itself, or a small managing agent, than a large management company with a security department. And you are more likely to have residents who drive to the building and walk in from a parking lot than residents who arrive on foot from the subway.

So the first thing we tell a Staten Island building is that elevator access control is not one product. It is a ladder with three rungs, and most buildings here never need to climb past the first one:

  • Level 1: a reader in the car. The car will not take a floor call until a valid credential is shown. Once it is, the rider can choose any floor the car serves. For a small co-op or a one-tenant office building this is usually all that is needed, because the question is "should this person be in our elevator at all?" rather than "which floor is this person allowed on?"
  • Level 2: biometrics. The same idea, but the credential is a face, a fingerprint or an iris rather than a fob. It suits buildings where lost or shared fobs are a real problem, and it comes with privacy duties in New York City residential buildings that we explain further down.
  • Level 3: floor-by-floor control and everything around it. Each person reaches only their own floors. Readers can be added at the lobby or garage call buttons. Floors can open and close on a schedule. The intercom can send a visitor to one floor only. This is what a medical building, a multi-tenant office building or a large North Shore rental usually needs.

Two more facts help a first-time buyer. The first is that the access control company does not rebuild your elevator. Our equipment sits beside it and talks to it through a small number of wires. The elevator's motor, brakes, doors and safety circuits stay exactly as they are, maintained by your elevator company. The second is that New York treats the final connection inside the elevator controller as elevator work, which must be done by a licensed elevator contractor. That is why every honest elevator access quote on Staten Island has two names on it: the security contractor's and the elevator contractor's. We explain who does which part in its own section below.

The established buildings usually have older elevators, often hydraulic in the lower buildings and traction in the taller ones, maintained under a long-standing contract with one elevator company. Their boards want something reliable that the super or the managing agent can run without a computer science degree. The new buildings usually have a cloud platform at the front door already and want the elevator to join it. We plan the two very differently, and most of this page explains how.

The small number of elevator buildings has one more consequence that a first-time buyer should know about. Fewer local buildings means fewer local installers with real elevator experience. Plenty of companies install door readers on Staten Island. Far fewer have coordinated a controller connection with an elevator contractor, tested fire recall with a reader in the car, or written a permission chart for a co-op board. When you interview contractors, ask about the elevator side specifically, not about access control in general.

How It Works

How Elevator Access Control Works Inside a Staten Island Elevator, From Fob to Floor

It helps to follow one ride from start to finish. Picture a resident coming home to a six-story co-op on Grymes Hill with a single elevator. She parks in the building's lot, uses her fob at the side door, and walks to the elevator. This is what happens next in a building with a Level 1 reader in the car.

  1. She calls the car. In a Level 1 building the lobby call button still works for anyone, so the car comes down. If the building has a landing reader, she would have to show her fob at the call button first.
  2. She shows her fob inside the car. The reader is mounted on the car's front return panel or on the side wall near the buttons, at a height a seated rider can reach. It reads the fob's number over a short radio field, a few centimeters at most.
  3. The reader passes the number on. The signal travels down a pair of conductors in the traveling cable, the flexible bundle of wires that hangs from the bottom of the car and moves with it, to the machine room or controller closet.
  4. The panel decides. The access control panel checks the fob against its list. Is this fob active? Is it allowed in this car at this hour? If the building uses a cloud platform, the panel already holds a copy of the list, so the decision does not wait on the internet.
  5. A relay closes. If the answer is yes, the panel closes a relay for a set number of seconds. That relay is wired to a point in the elevator controller that the elevator contractor has chosen and connected.
  6. The elevator accepts her floor. For those few seconds the car will take a floor call. She presses five, the button lights, and the car goes. When the time runs out, the buttons go quiet again until the next valid credential.
  7. The ride is logged. The panel records the fob, the car and the time. In buildings with a button-sensing board it can also record which floor she chose.

Now picture a stranger who slipped in behind a delivery. He calls the car, steps in and presses the top floor. Nothing happens. He has no fob, so no relay closes and the button never lights. He can take the stairs, which must always remain available as an exit, but he cannot quietly ride to the roof door or the top-floor hallway. The attempt shows up in nothing more than an unanswered button press, which is exactly the point.

A Level 3 building adds one layer. Instead of one relay that enables the whole car, the panel controls one relay per floor. When the resident shows her fob, the panel closes only the relays for the lobby and her own floor. She can still press any button she likes, but only the ones her permission covers will respond. A visiting doctor at a medical building gets the floors of the practice he works for. A cleaner gets every floor, but only from six in the evening to six in the morning. That is what people mean when they talk about elevator floor access control, and it is described in its own section below.

Three details matter more on Staten Island than people expect:

  • The traveling cable. The reader in the car needs a few conductors to reach the machine room. Many older elevators have spare conductors in the traveling cable that the elevator company can assign to us. If they do not, a new cable or a wireless link has to be arranged, and that is a real cost. It is the first thing we look for on a survey, and the reason no honest contractor gives a final price on the phone.
  • The machine room location. In the low hydraulic buildings that are common here, the machine room is often at or below the lobby level. In a borough where Sandy pushed water into ground floors and basements, that affects where we put the panel and its battery.
  • The front door system. The cheapest and most convenient elevator system is one that uses the same fob, card or app as the building's entrance, so residents carry one credential and the manager keeps one list. We always check what the door runs before recommending anything for the car.
Small Buildings

One Car, Two to Six Floors: Why a Level 1 Reader Is Often Enough on Staten Island

A large share of the elevators on Staten Island serve small buildings. Think of a three-story professional building on Victory Boulevard with a dentist on the second floor and an accountant on the third. A four-story mixed-use building on Bay Street with apartments above a shop. A five-story senior building with one car. A six-story prewar co-op like the elevator buildings on Hart Boulevard in Silver Lake that the Staten Island Advance described in its market reports. In every one of these there is a single car, a handful of floors and usually one entrance most people use.

In a building like that, the security question is simple: should this person be riding our elevator at all? Floor-by-floor control answers a different question, which is: which floor is this person allowed to reach? In a six-story co-op where every resident has a reason to be on every hallway, for the laundry room, the storage room, the trash chute and their neighbors, restricting each person to one floor causes more trouble than it prevents. What the board really wants is to stop people who do not live there from riding up at all. A Level 1 reader in the car does exactly that, at the lowest price on the ladder: from $1,650 per car on Staten Island.

Here is how we usually set up a Level 1 system in a small Staten Island building:

  • The lobby stays free. Anyone in the lobby can call the car. That keeps the elevator usable for a resident's guest who is waiting for them downstairs, for the mail carrier and for emergency crews.
  • The car needs a credential to go up. One reader in the car, at accessible height. Residents tap a fob, enter a code, or use a phone. Some buildings choose a video keypad station, like the one in the photo at the top of this page, so that a resident who forgets a fob can still type a personal code.
  • Going down is free. We almost always program the lobby floor so it never needs a credential. Nobody should ever be stuck on an upper floor because their phone died.
  • Staff and services get their own credentials. The super, the porter, the cleaning company, the oil delivery driver who needs the basement, a home health aide who visits every morning. Each gets a credential that can be turned off without affecting anyone else.
  • The super has an override. A key switch or a command in the software frees the car for a move, a delivery or an emergency, and every use is recorded.

When is Level 1 not enough in a small building? Three cases come up on Staten Island again and again. The first is a mixed-use building where the ground floor is a business open to the public and the upper floors are private; if the elevator also serves a basement or a roof, the owner may want those landings locked even to some credentialed riders, which is Level 3. The second is a professional building where different tenants want their own floors closed to each other's staff and patients after hours. The third is a building with a garage or lower parking level, where the real problem is people calling the car from the garage, and the answer is a reader at that landing.

Even then, it is worth starting with the simplest version that works. A Level 1 reader installed today can usually grow into Level 3 later: the panel, the cable and the credentials stay, and the floor relays and landing readers are added when the building is ready. We design the first stage so the second stage does not mean starting over.

Floor Control

Elevator Floor Access Control: Restricting Certain Floors in a Staten Island Building

Elevator floor access control means the elevator decides floor by floor, and person by person, where a rider may go. Every floor button is locked by default. When a rider shows a credential, the access control panel unlocks only the buttons that rider's permission covers, for a few seconds. Everyone else's floors stay dark. It is the most precise kind of elevator security, and the most involved to install, which is why it is Level 3 on our price ladder and is quoted per building after a survey.

On Staten Island, the buildings that genuinely need floor-level control fall into a few clear groups:

  • Medical and professional buildings. A building near one of the borough's hospitals may have a radiology practice on one floor, a group of physicians on another and administrative offices or records storage on a third. Patients need to reach the waiting rooms. Staff need to reach back offices. Nobody from outside should be able to ride to the floor where files or medications are kept.
  • Multi-tenant office buildings and business parks. In the Teleport campus in Bloomfield, the Corporate Park of Staten Island, or a mid-size office building on the North Shore, tenants usually lease one or more floors and want those floors closed to everyone else's staff, especially after hours.
  • Larger rental buildings with amenity floors. In a North Shore rental with a gym, a co-working mezzanine, a roof deck or a package room on a separate level, floor control becomes amenity control: residents reach their own floor and the amenities they pay for, and the roof closes at night.
  • Buildings with mechanical, roof or storage floors. Even a small co-op may want one thing: the roof landing and the basement boiler room locked to everyone except staff, with every other floor left open. That is a narrow Level 3 job, often only two relays, and it is one of the most common floor requests we hear on the island.
  • Senior and supportive housing with staff floors. A building with offices for case workers, a clinic room or a staff-only floor can let residents ride freely to their own floors and the community room while keeping staff areas closed.

So yes, you can restrict access to certain elevator floors, and you can do it in very different ways. The question at the survey is always which floors, for whom, and at what hours. We turn the answer into a permission chart: a simple table with groups of people down one side, floors across the top, and hours in each cell. The board, owner or each tenant approves the chart before we program anything.

In-car floor control versus landing control

There are two places to enforce floor rules, and they answer different questions. In-car control, described above, decides which floor buttons work once someone is inside. Landing control, sometimes called hall-call control, puts a reader at a call button outside the elevator, so nobody can even summon the car from that floor without a credential. On Staten Island, landing readers make the most sense at parking levels, basements with a back door to the street, and the lobby of a building that has no doorman and a door that is often propped. Many buildings use both: a landing reader at the garage and in-car control upstairs.

What floor control does not do

Floor control does not stop someone from riding along with a resident to that resident's floor, and it does not stop anyone using the stairs. Stairs are exit routes and must stay usable. A building that wants to stop people moving between floors by stair needs a separate plan for stair doors, which falls under fire and building code rules and is a different job. We say this plainly at the survey, because a board that expects the elevator alone to seal every floor will be disappointed.

How many relays a Staten Island building needs

Installers who work on large systems point out that the parts count grows as floors times cars times call stations. A six-story, one-car co-op that wants every floor controlled needs roughly six floor relays and one reader. A two-car building with eight floors and a garage landing reader needs sixteen floor relays, two car readers and a landing reader, plus two controller connections. Staten Island's buildings are small enough that the numbers stay manageable, which is one reason Level 3 here is often less involved than people fear, but it is also why we never quote it without counting.

Building Types

Staten Island Building Types and the Elevator Access Control Each One Usually Needs

Because the borough's elevator buildings are few and varied, the best way to explain the options is building by building. These are the types we see most, with the design that usually fits. Every building is surveyed before anything is decided, so treat this as a starting point for the conversation.

Mid-rise co-ops on the North Shore hills and around the parks

The Staten Island Advance's co-op and condo reports name the buildings that most islanders picture when they hear "co-op": the high-rise pair at 1000 and 1100 Clove Road beside Clove Lakes Park, the garden and elevator co-ops of Grymes Hill around Howard Avenue, Seth Court and Narrows Road North, the seven-story buildings on Austin Place in Tompkinsville, and the prewar six-story elevator condos on Hart Boulevard in Silver Lake. They were mostly built between the 1920s and the 1960s, they are run by boards, and their elevators have usually been modernized at least once. Several have doormen for part of the day, laundry rooms in the basement, and parking lots with a side entrance that sees more traffic than the front door.

For these buildings we usually recommend a Level 1 reader in each car on the same fob as the front and side doors, programmed so the lobby and basement laundry floor are always reachable, plus two specific Level 3 additions where the board wants them: a locked roof landing and, if the parking lot entrance is the weak point, a reader at the side-door landing. A two-car co-op starts at two times the per-car price, because each car needs its own reader and its own controller connection. If the board is worried about older shareholders who do not want to carry a fob, a keypad code or a video station gives them a choice.

Condos and co-ops along Hylan Boulevard and the East Shore

From Rosebank at the foot of Hylan Boulevard down through South Beach, Dongan Hills and Grant City, the East Shore has smaller elevator condos and co-ops, often four to six floors, some within sight of the water. Many of them sit in neighborhoods that took on water during Sandy. For these buildings the design starts with where the equipment will live. We place the access panel, its power supply and its battery above the floors that flooded, run cable down to the machine room only for the connection point, and choose readers rated for the conditions in a lobby that faces the shore. The access design itself is usually Level 1, sometimes with a code keypad for residents who leave a car at the curb and walk in with their hands full.

New North Shore rentals from St. George to Stapleton

The new buildings near the ferry are a different world. Urby in Stapleton was described at opening as two buildings with 571 apartments, an open mezzanine leading from the elevators to the residential areas, and a residents-only zone behind both entrances. Lighthouse Point beside the St. George terminal combines 115 rental homes with commercial tenants, a childcare center and 274 parking spaces. Buildings like these usually already run a cloud access platform or a cloud video intercom at the doors, with phone credentials, package room access and a leasing office that adds and removes residents every week.

Here the job is almost always Level 3: extending the platform the doors already use into the cars, so a resident's phone or fob unlocks the lobby, their own floor, the gym and the roof deck at the hours the building allows. Garage levels get landing readers so a car parked downstairs cannot become a stranger's route to the apartments. Shared commercial areas stay on their own schedule. We also plan for turnover: in a rental, the leasing team must be able to remove a departing resident's credentials from the elevator and the doors at the same moment, and the system should make that one action, not two.

Senior and supportive housing

Staten Island's senior buildings range from low-rise buildings like the 90-home senior building planned at Hillside Grove on Jersey Street to the 12-story building at 475 Bay Street, where 138 of 270 apartments house formerly unhoused seniors with supportive services. These buildings have the hardest balance to strike. Residents need to move freely and safely, often with walkers or wheelchairs. Aides, nurses, case workers and family visit at all hours. Staff need to know who is in the building at night. And a resident who loses a fob should never be stranded in the lobby.

Our usual design is a Level 1 reader in each car with a choice of fob, card or code for each resident, longer unlock windows for riders who move slowly, readers mounted at a height reachable from a wheelchair, and staff floors closed with a small Level 3 addition. Visitors are handled through the intercom or the front desk rather than by handing out fobs. We avoid biometrics in senior residential buildings unless the operator has a proper consent process under Local Law 63, which is explained below.

Medical and professional buildings near the hospitals

Staten Island University Hospital and Richmond University Medical Center anchor clusters of medical practices, labs, therapy offices and professional buildings in their neighborhoods, and the borough's office parks hold more medical space; the Corporate Commons Three building at the Teleport was announced with a fifth of its space set aside for medical offices. We do not name or imply any hospital as a client. What we can say is how these buildings generally work. Patients and visitors need open access to public floors during office hours. Staff need access to clinical and back-office floors at all hours. Records rooms, medication storage and administrative floors should never be reachable by a member of the public. After hours, the whole building should usually go to credential-only.

That is a classic Level 3 design: floor-by-floor control, with schedules that open public floors during posted hours and close them in the evening, a landing reader at any parking level, and practice-by-practice permission groups that each practice manager can understand. Where a building already issues employee badges, we look for readers that accept the badge format the practices already carry.

Office buildings, business parks and the St. George civic district

Staten Island's office space is concentrated. The Port Authority's Teleport in Bloomfield covers 100 acres with five Class A office and specialized buildings totaling about 700,000 square feet, and the Corporate Park of Staten Island markets space for offices, medical users and schools. In St. George, the courthouses, Borough Hall and city offices draw law firms, title companies, insurance agencies and other professional tenants into the private buildings nearby. Government buildings are run by their own agencies and their own security rules, and they are not what this page is about. The private office and professional buildings around them are.

In a multi-tenant office building, the landlord owns the elevator and sets the rules. Each tenant wants its floor closed to the others after hours and to visitors who have not checked in. Safety and Storms

Fire Recall, Power Failures and Flood Risk in Staten Island Elevator Buildings

An elevator access system must never make the elevator less safe. Three situations decide whether that promise holds: a fire, a power failure and, on an island with a long shoreline, a flood.

Fire: the elevator stops listening to us

New York City elevators follow the ASME A17.1 safety code as modified by the city's building code, including firefighters' emergency operation. When a smoke detector in an elevator lobby or machine room trips, or firefighters turn the recall key, the cars return to the designated landing and leave normal service. The Department of Buildings made the rule for added devices explicit in Buildings Bulletin 2022-010: anything added to a car or a hall station must not impede normal or emergency operation and must become inoperative when Phase I or Phase II emergency operation begins. We design and test every system so the elevator controller, not our panel, is in charge the moment fire service starts. During testing, the elevator contractor triggers recall with our reader active, and we confirm the reader has no effect.

Power failure: decide the answer before it happens

Every access panel we install has a battery, sized to keep it deciding through ordinary outages. But the building still has to choose what the elevator does if our panel goes dark completely. Most Staten Island residential buildings choose to keep the lobby and the floors staff must reach always enabled, so nobody is stranded, with the rest of the building returning to normal locked behavior when power returns. Some commercial tenants make the opposite choice for sensitive floors. Either way, the decision is written into the scope and tested, not discovered during a storm.

Flood: put the brains above the water line

Hurricane Sandy pushed water into ground floors and basements across the East and South Shores and into parts of the North Shore. In April 2013 the Staten Island Advance reported ten residents of a St. George co-op suing over flood damage to their first-floor apartments. Many low elevator buildings on the island keep their machine rooms, electrical rooms and storage at or below the lobby. For a building in a flood-prone location, we mount the access panel, power supply and battery on a higher floor or high on a wall, bring only the connection cable down to the controller, and choose a design that can be brought back quickly once the elevator company has dried out and restored the elevator. Nothing we install can make an elevator safe to run after flooding; that judgment belongs to the elevator contractor. Our goal is that our equipment is not the reason the elevator stays out of service longer.

The override and accessibility

Every system has a documented override, a key switch or a software command that frees all floors for a move, a delivery, an ambulance crew or an emergency, and every use is logged. Readers go at a height a seated rider can reach, with an unlock window long enough for someone who moves slowly, and with a code or phone option for a resident who cannot easily handle a small fob. None of this is legal advice; your elevator contractor and, where needed, a design professional have the final word on code compliance for the elevator.

Resident Privacy

Local Law 63 for Staten Island Apartment Buildings Adding Elevator Fobs or Biometrics

Staten Island is part of New York City, so Local Law 63 of 2021, the Tenant Data Privacy Act, applies here exactly as it does in the other boroughs. As HPD summarizes it, residential multiple dwellings that use smart access, meaning fobs, cards, phones or biometrics, must give tenants a plain-language privacy policy, get express consent before collecting biometric reference data, use the data only for granting access and monitoring entrances and common areas, protect it, and destroy a departed tenant's reference data within 90 days. Elevators are common areas reached through the access system, so the elevator belongs in the same policy as the front door.

The difference on Staten Island is who has to carry that out. Many co-ops and condos here are run by volunteer boards, sometimes self-managed, sometimes with a small managing agent. They do not have a compliance department. So we keep it simple. We set the elevator log to record only what the building needs, which is usually the credential, the reader and the time. We recommend fobs, cards, codes or phones in residential buildings rather than faces or fingerprints, unless the board has a consent process it can actually run. We give the board a one-page procedure for deactivating a departing resident's credentials and clearing their data on time, and we hand over the platform's own privacy documentation so the board can attach it to the policy it gives residents. Office, medical and other commercial buildings are not covered by this particular law, but most of them ask for the same discipline: short retention, named administrators and an audit trail.

Credentials

Fobs, Cards, Codes, Phones and Faces: Picking an Elevator Credential for Staten Island Riders

The credential is the thing a rider carries, types or is. It is also the part residents and staff notice every day, so it deserves more thought than the brand of panel in the machine room. On Staten Island the choice is shaped by who the riders are: long-time co-op shareholders, ferry commuters, seniors, medical staff on rotating shifts, and visitors who arrive by car.

  • Key fobs and cards. The workhorse. Cheap to replace, easy to cancel, and usually the same credential the front door already uses. We recommend encrypted fob and card formats over the old easily copied ones, because a fob that can be cloned at a kiosk is a weak key to every floor.
  • Keypad codes. Useful as a backup for residents who forget a fob and for buildings where some riders cannot manage a small fob. Codes are easier to share, so we usually pair them with a fob rather than use them alone.
  • Phones. Mobile credentials using Bluetooth or NFC suit the new North Shore rentals, where residents already open the front door with an app. Every phone user should also keep a fob on the same account, because phones die and get replaced.
  • Video keypad stations. A station with a camera, a keypad, a reader and a call button, like the Akuvox unit in the hero photo, combines a credential reader with an intercom. In a small building it can be the only device in the car.
  • Biometrics. Face, fingerprint or iris readers are Level 2, from $2,750 per car on Staten Island. They end lost and shared fobs, which some medical and office buildings value, but in residential buildings they bring Local Law 63 consent duties, and some residents simply will not enroll.

Most buildings use more than one. A typical Staten Island co-op ends up with fobs for everyone, codes for a few residents who asked for them, and phone credentials for those who want them. The system treats them all as one person with several keys, so cancelling a person cancels every key at once.

Visitors and Staff

Guests, Aides, Deliveries and Parking-Lot Arrivals in Staten Island Elevator Buildings

The hardest part of any elevator system is not the residents. It is everyone else. Staten Island buildings have their own mix of non-residents, and each needs a rule before the system goes live:

  • Visitors who drive. Many guests arrive by car and walk in from a lot or a garage. In a building with a landing reader at the garage, they should be met or buzzed in at the main entrance, not given a way to call the car from the parking level.
  • Home health aides and nurses. In senior buildings and co-ops with older shareholders, aides visit daily. Each aide gets a named credential tied to the resident's floor and the hours they work, so it can be cancelled the day the agency changes staff.
  • Deliveries. Package carriers and food deliveries usually stop at a package room or the lobby. Where a building wants carriers to go upstairs, the intercom can release the car for one ride to one floor.
  • Contractors and movers. The super uses the override or a temporary credential that expires automatically at the end of the job.
  • Patients and clients. In medical and professional buildings, public floors stay open during posted hours, so patients never need a credential at all.

The intercom link deserves a word. When a visitor calls a resident from the lobby station and the resident opens the door, a properly integrated system can also enable the elevator for that resident's floor for a minute or two. The guest rides up without a fob and reaches only the right floor. Whether that works depends on the intercom brand and the access platform, so we confirm it before promising it.
