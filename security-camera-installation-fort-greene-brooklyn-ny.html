The Neighborhood

Why Fort Greene Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Fort Greene is a small neighborhood with an unusually wide range of buildings packed close together. Wikipedia puts it at about half a square mile, bounded by Flushing Avenue and the Navy Yard to the north, Flatbush Avenue Extension and Downtown Brooklyn to the west, Atlantic Avenue to the south and Vanderbilt Avenue to the east. Inside that line sit hundreds of mid-1800s brick and brownstone rowhouses, most of them inside the Fort Greene Historic District, designated in 1978 along with the separate BAM Historic District. North of Myrtle Avenue stand the Walt Whitman and Ingersoll Houses, built for Navy Yard workers in 1944, with the Whitman campus alone holding 1,659 apartments in fifteen buildings of six and thirteen stories. West and south, along Ashland Place, Flatbush Avenue Extension and the edge of Downtown Brooklyn, towers of thirty and fifty floors have gone up in the last two decades. And threaded through it all are the commercial streets, Myrtle Avenue, DeKalb Avenue, Fulton Street and Lafayette Avenue, lined with cafés, restaurants, groceries, bars and shops, most with apartments upstairs.

Each of those building types asks something different of a camera, and the neighborhood's own police and housing records show where cameras here have worked and where they have let people down. The Brooklyn Paper's 88th Precinct reports, which cover Fort Greene and Clinton Hill, are full of break-ins that ended in an arrest because a camera caught a face: a lobby package thief on Clinton Avenue identified on the building's cameras the day after he pried the front door at 6:30 in the morning, two armed men recorded by the cameras at the Casa Blanca hotel on Park Avenue after they forced their way into a room at 2:30 a.m., and the teenager identified on surveillance footage after a run of early-morning break-ins at Hungry Ghost Coffee, Heavenly Market, SmashBurger and Ya Ya Tea. The same reports are just as full of the opposite: checks stolen from the blue mailbox at Ashland Place and Willoughby Street with no cameras available, a motorcycle taken off Adelphi Street near DeKalb Avenue between 8 and 11 p.m. with no camera mentioned, and a man who broke a Cumberland Street building's glass front door with a brick at about 5 a.m. to go through the packages in the lobby, with no suspect found.

The misses are rarely about the camera being cheap. They come from where it was put. NYCHA told News12 in October 2024 that it has spent more than $900,000 on cameras at the Whitman Houses since 2013 and runs 149 cameras there, and residents still questioned whether the system works after a sixteen-year-old was killed outside 32 Auburn Place. News12 found that one of the four cameras visible at that spot was partly blocked by tree cover. Ten years earlier, DNAinfo reported that the staff at Myrtle Grocery near Ryerson Street put up a "no hoodies" sign after a masked gunman robbed the store and the security footage never showed his face. "Cameras are no good," one worker said. We understand why he felt that way, but the camera did exactly what it was placed to do: it watched the room from up high, and from up high a hood and a mask hide everything.

So a Fort Greene camera plan is less about how many cameras and more about which few positions actually identify people at this address. On a rowhouse, that usually means a face-height view at the top of the stoop or inside the vestibule, a view along the sidewalk in the direction people walk off, and a view of the rear windows and garden door, which is where DNAinfo reported two homes near Fort Greene Place and Hanson Place were entered one September night in 2014, through unlocked back windows at about ten o'clock. On a storefront, it means a camera at the glass that sees the person before the glass breaks and a camera at the register and back office. In a walk-up, it means the street door, the lobby where packages land and the mailboxes. And on every block, it means walking the view in summer and winter, because Fort Greene's street trees are part of what makes the neighborhood what it is, and they grow.

Two more things set Fort Greene apart from most of Brooklyn. First, most of its rowhouses are inside a landmark district, and the Landmarks Preservation Commission has written rules for cameras on those fronts: limits on how many per 25 feet of frontage, a strong preference against equipment on the primary facade, and a cap of about ten inches of visible conduit. Second, many of its houses have been divided over the years into an owner's duplex with a rental below or above, and many of its walk-ups have a shop at street level and two or three apartments overhead. When more than one household or business shares a front door, the question of who controls each camera and who sees each recording matters as much as where the camera goes. This page works through both.

Fort Greene facts that shape a camera plan

  • Landmark fronts have a camera count: the LPC Permit Guidebook lets staff approve up to two security cameras for every 25 feet of street frontage on small residential and commercial buildings, and up to three on large ones, as long as they are well spaced and the conduit is concealed.
  • Public cameras do not watch your door: the NYPD announced four pole cameras for Myrtle Avenue between Carlton Avenue and St. Edwards Street and at a Fulton Street intersection in 2011, recording for 30 days; they watch intersections, not stoops or lobbies.
  • Housing cameras exist and still have gaps: NYCHA reports 149 cameras at Whitman Houses and over $900,000 spent there since 2013; Ingersoll's resident association said in 2016 the development had long been "overlooked as far as cameras and lights."
  • Early mornings are a pattern for shops: the café and shop break-ins in the 88th Precinct report happened shortly after 6 a.m. on one morning and around 5:30 to 6 a.m. two days later, before staff arrived.
  • Lobbies get hit for packages: the 88th Precinct reports a 5 a.m. brick through a Cumberland Street building's glass door and a 4:45 a.m. package theft from a Grand Avenue foyer, both by breaking the street door.
  • Cars are targets at the curb: Brooklyn Paper counted car burglaries and thefts across more than a dozen Fort Greene and Clinton Hill streets in one ten-day July stretch, plus 20 cars with smashed windows on Park Avenue in a single early morning.

What Fort Greene owners often add next. Once the stoop and the vestibule have face-height cameras, owners of divided brownstones usually ask whether the doorbell can show the same picture to each household, and shopkeepers on Myrtle and DeKalb ask whether a camera alert can be tied to an alarm so a broken window at 6 a.m. reaches a phone right away. We leave a labeled spare port on the recorder and, where an intercom, buzzer or alarm upgrade is likely, a spare cable to the entrance, so plaster and molding only get opened once.

What Blinds a Camera Here

Leaves, Stoops, Sheds and Hoods: Why Fort Greene Cameras Miss, and How We Plan Around It

Every failure in the local record above comes down to a handful of causes. None of them is exotic, and every one of them can be checked before the drill comes out. This is the checklist we walk on a Fort Greene job, in roughly the order we find problems.

Street trees: the view that disappears every May

The camera at 32 Auburn Place that News12 found partly blocked by tree cover is the clearest local example of a problem we see on block after block. A camera mounted in February looks perfect. By late spring the tree in the pit out front fills in, and the camera that used to see the stoop, the gate and the sidewalk now sees leaves for six months of the year. The fix starts at the survey. We ask when the photos were taken, we look at the trees themselves rather than the bare branches, and we aim the street camera below the lowest limbs or from the side of the tree that stays clear. Where a tree simply sits between the door and any good mounting spot, we move the identifying camera inside the vestibule glass or onto the door frame, and let the high camera handle the wide view. Street trees in New York belong to the city, and pruning them is the Parks Department's job, so a plan that depends on trimming a tree is a plan that will fail. We would rather design around it.

The stoop: a staircase that hides whoever is under it

A Fort Greene rowhouse stoop puts the parlor door eight or ten steps above the sidewalk and tucks the garden-level door underneath, often behind a gate in the areaway railing. A single camera above the parlor door sees the top of the stairs and the tops of heads coming up. It sees almost nothing of the space under the stoop, which is where a package left for the garden apartment usually sits and where someone can stand out of sight of the street. We usually split the job: one camera at face height beside the parlor door, set to look slightly down the stairs, and a second small camera under the stoop or inside the garden-level vestibule that covers that door and the areaway gate. On a district front both of those positions are within or beside a door opening, which also keeps them out of the open facade.

Sidewalk sheds and scaffolding

Facade work is a constant in a neighborhood of 150-year-old brick and brownstone, and so are the sidewalk sheds that go up for it. Brooklyn Paper's 88th Precinct report described five people hiding under scaffolding on Fort Greene Place near Fulton Street and trying to rob a man walking past. Under a shed the light drops, the camera above the door is suddenly looking at plywood, and the corner of the shed becomes a place to wait. When a shed is coming, we add a temporary camera on the shed's own framing aimed along the covered walk, with power and cable that can come down with the shed, and we re-aim the permanent cameras for the months it is up.

Hoods, hat brims and the height of the lens

The Myrtle Grocery footage is the textbook case. A camera high in a corner sees a hood from above. A camera at about head height, close to where people stand and face, sees under it. For every door that matters we plan one camera whose only job is a face: beside the register for a shop, on the door frame or inside the vestibule for a house or walk-up, in the elevator lobby for a tower apartment's floor if the building allows it. The wide, high cameras stay too, because they show what happened and where people went, but they are not the cameras that identify anyone.

Glass, glare and the light at five in the morning

Most of the shop break-ins in the precinct reports came through glass, at the hour before sunrise when streetlights are still on and shop interiors are dark. A camera inside the shop window at night sees its own infrared light bouncing off the glass and very little else. A camera outside the glass, under the awning or the sign band, sees the person walking up. For storefronts we place the identifying camera outside, protected and out of easy reach, and keep the inside cameras pointed away from the glass at the register, the office and the back door. For houses we keep infrared cameras away from storm doors and vestibule glass, or use cameras that rely on a small amount of visible light instead.

Recorders that live where the thief walks

A recorder on a shelf beside the door or under a counter is the easiest thing in the building to carry out, and if it leaves, so does the footage. We put the recorder, the network switch, the modem and a battery unit in a locked spot away from the entry, usually a cellar closet in a rowhouse, a back office or upstairs closet in a shop building, and we set the most important cameras to send selected clips to a phone or an off-site copy so one theft does not erase the evidence.

Who Controls the Building

Brownstone Owner, Small Co-op, Tower Renter, Shop Tenant or NYCHA Resident: Who Says Yes to a Camera in Fort Greene

Many Fort Greene buildings hold more than one household or business behind one front door. A South Portland Avenue brownstone might be a five-unit co-op where every shareholder has a vote. A house on Clermont Avenue might be an owner's triplex over a garden rental. A Myrtle Avenue building might have a deli downstairs on a commercial lease and three apartments upstairs on residential leases, with a landlord who lives elsewhere. A tower near Ashland Place has a management company and a condo board or a corporate owner. The cameras themselves are the same in every case. What changes is who may mount them, where they may point and who may see the footage, and getting that wrong is the most common reason a camera comes down soon after it goes up.

Divided brownstones: two households, one stoop

The most common Fort Greene version of this question is a rowhouse where the owner lives on the top two or three floors and rents the garden level. The stoop, the street door and often the vestibule are shared. The garden apartment has its own door under the stoop. The rear yard might belong to either. We set up the recorder so the owner, as landlord, controls the cameras on the shared doors and the yard, and the tenant gets a live view of the doors they use, if the owner agrees, without access to recordings of the owner's own comings and goings. Cameras inside either home belong to whoever lives there. Writing that down before installation saves arguments later, and it is the first thing we ask about on a divided house.

Small co-op brownstones: one vote per shareholder

Several Fort Greene brownstones are co-ops of four to eight apartments, governed by a board drawn from the shareholders themselves. Common-area cameras there are a board decision, and in a building that small, every shareholder usually has a view about where the lobby camera points. We give the board a short written plan with a sketch of each camera's view, the cost per line and who would hold the recordings, which is usually what a small board needs to vote. Where a shareholder worries about being watched at their own door, we can show how a camera can be aimed to see the shared entrance without lingering on any one apartment.

Shops with apartments upstairs

On Myrtle, DeKalb and Fulton, the store and the building usually have different owners. The shop can install cameras inside its own space and at its own glass under the terms of its lease. The camera that watches the residents' door beside the shop belongs to the landlord, and so does the cable route through the shared cellar. We have no trouble running two separate systems, one for the shop and one for the building, side by side in the same cellar, each in its own locked box and on its own account, so neither side can see what the other records.

Building Specialists

Security Camera Installation for Fort Greene's Architecture

Fort Greene's building stock was laid down in a few distinct waves, and each one changes how a camera is mounted and how its cable gets to the recorder. These are the types we meet most.

Italianate and Greek Revival Rowhouses

Brick and brownstone houses of two to four stories with high stoops and deep cornices, mostly from the 1850s and 1860s, the core of the Fort Greene Historic District.

Second Empire and Neo-Grec Houses

Later rowhouses with mansard roofs and incised stone trim, where the front is busier and a camera has fewer plain surfaces to sit on.

Walk-Ups Over Shops

Three- to five-story brick buildings on Myrtle, DeKalb and Fulton with a store at grade, a separate residents' door and a shared cellar.

Public Housing Campuses

The Whitman and Ingersoll Houses north of Myrtle, six- to thirteen-story brick buildings from 1944 where NYCHA runs the cameras outside each apartment.

Prewar and Landmark Towers

The Williamsburgh Savings Bank Tower at One Hanson Place, a 1929 skyscraper converted to condominiums, an exterior landmark with designated interiors.

New Glass and Concrete Towers

Rental and condo high-rises on Ashland Place, Flatbush Avenue Extension, DeKalb Avenue and Willoughby Street, with managed lobbies and their own building systems.

How each building type changes the camera work

The short version is that the older the front, the more of the work happens inside. On a rowhouse we try to keep everything on the outside of the building small, close to a door and connected to the interior by a single hole through plain masonry right behind the mount. On a walk-up the cellar does the heavy lifting. In the towers, the building's own rules and systems set the limits. The sections below go through each in more detail.

Rowhouses: through the wall behind the mount, never along the front

The Landmarks Commission's guidebook says conduit for exterior equipment must be concealed by going through plain masonry or cladding behind the installation, and staff may approve only about ten inches of visible conduit along a wall. That fits how we prefer to work on any old front anyway. We choose a mount position on plain brick or a plain stone face, not on carved lintels, cornice brackets or the ironwork, and drill one hole through a mortar joint directly behind the camera base. Inside, the cable drops through the wall cavity or the vestibule ceiling to the cellar. On brownstone, which is a soft sandstone facing over brick that flakes if it is drilled carelessly, we drill into the joints where we can and seal every opening so water does not get behind the stone. Two cameras per 25 feet of frontage is the guidebook's limit for small buildings, and a typical Fort Greene house is narrower than that, so we usually plan the front as one identifying camera at the door plus, at most, one wider view, and put any extra coverage inside the vestibule glass or on the rear facade.

Inside these houses the surprises are in the walls. Many still have original plaster on lath, and the wood framing between floors is often more than a century old, with pipes and old gas lines in the same spaces a cable wants to use. We find our routes through closets, the space beside the chimney breast and the cellar ceiling before we open anything, and we patch every hole we make. The parlor floor's plaster moldings and ceiling medallions are never the route.

Walk-ups over shops: the cellar is the spine

In a three- or four-story building on Myrtle Avenue or Fulton Street, the cellar usually runs the full depth of the lot, and it is where the electric service, the gas meters, the boiler and the internet line all arrive. That makes it the natural spot for the building's recorder, as long as it is locked, dry and kept separate from the store's storage. Cable runs up from there in the stair hall's walls or a shared chase to the lobby, the mailboxes and each landing, and out under the sidewalk vault or along the cellar ceiling to the residents' door. Where the store uses part of the cellar, we put the building's equipment in its own locked enclosure and keep the shop's system completely separate.

Towers: inside your own walls, by the building's rules

In the newer high-rises around Ashland Place and the Downtown edge, the building already runs a camera system in the lobby, garage, elevators and amenity spaces, managed by a security desk or a management company. A resident's camera job there is almost always inside the apartment: a small camera that watches the inside of the front door, a balcony door if there is one, and the rooms you want to check on while away, all running on your own home network. We confirm the building's alteration rules before drilling into any concrete, because many towers forbid it without a sign-off, and we use adhesive or existing anchors where a building prefers. One Hanson Place, the old bank tower, has a designated interior lobby, so anything a condo board there wants in the common areas starts with the Landmarks Commission; a camera inside a unit does not.

Public housing: inside the apartment only

At the Whitman and Ingersoll Houses, NYCHA owns the hallways, lobbies, doors and exterior walls, and it runs its own camera program across the campus. Bklyner reported in 2016 that new lighting and cameras were coming to Ingersoll after years of residents pushing for them, and NYCHA told News12 in 2024 that Whitman has 149 cameras. If you live in either development, what we can do for you is a camera inside your own apartment, aimed at your own space, removable without damage when you move. We do not mount anything in the halls, on the door or outside, and we do not connect to NYCHA's system.

Field Notes

Field Notes: How We Walk a Fort Greene Rowhouse Before Choosing a Single Camera Spot

This is how we approach these houses in general, not a story about one particular job. We start across the street, not at the door. From the opposite sidewalk you can see what a camera on the front will actually face: the tree in the pit, the lamppost, the neighbor's stoop that juts out, any scaffold up the block. Then we walk up the stoop the way a stranger would and note where they would naturally stop and face the door, because that is where the face camera has to point. We go down to the areaway and stand under the stoop, where most people are surprised how dark and hidden it is. Inside, we ask who uses which door and who should see which camera, and we find the spot where the internet comes in, often a cable through the front wall into the cellar or up to the parlor. In the cellar we look at the joists, the old gas and water lines, any damp, and whether there is a closet we can lock. Out back, we stand in the garden and look up at the rear windows, the extension roof and the fences shared with three or four neighbors. Only then do we draw the camera positions, and usually there are fewer of them than the owner expected, each with a clear job.

The café break-ins near us ended with an arrest from surveillance footage. How can we tell whether our own cameras would produce footage that good?

Test them the way a detective would use them. Pick a night, have someone walk up to your door or window at the hour a break-in is likely, around five or six in the morning in Fort Greene's precinct reports, and then try to export that clip and look at it on a computer. If you cannot clearly see the person's face at the door, read the time stamp correctly, and follow them in the wide camera as they leave, the system would not have helped. The four businesses in that report, Hungry Ghost Coffee, Heavenly Market, SmashBurger and Ya Ya Tea, were broken into through glass in the dark, and the footage was good enough to identify a suspect. We do this test as part of every handover, and we will do it on an existing system as a paid setup visit, then tell you which cameras to move.

A Myrtle Avenue grocer once put up a "no hoodies" sign because his camera never showed a robber's face. Is a sign like that a substitute for moving the camera?

No. The sign asks people to help the camera; moving the camera fixes the camera. DNAinfo reported in 2014 that Myrtle Grocery's footage of a masked robbery did not show the robber's face, and critics said the signs drove customers away. A camera mounted at head height beside the register, close to where a customer stands to pay, sees much more of a face under a hood than a camera high in the corner, and it works whether or not anyone reads a sign. Even with a mask, a low camera records eyes, build, clothing and shoes clearly, and the outside camera picks up which way the person came from and went. We would put money into those two positions before any signage.

Someone broke the glass street door of a Cumberland Street building at five in the morning just to go through the packages, and the door cost about $1,000. Can cameras make that less likely, or only record it?

Both, if the camera is visible from the sidewalk and the packages are not. The Brooklyn Paper reported that the man at Cumberland Street rummaged through the packages and left with nothing, after doing about $1,000 of damage to the door. What draws someone to break a lobby door at that hour is usually being able to see boxes through the glass. We recommend a visible camera outside or just inside the door, where anyone approaching sees it, and a second camera inside that covers the package spot from a low angle. Then move deliveries out of the line of sight from the street, behind the inner door or into a closet, and a broken door becomes much less tempting.

One of the cameras at the Whitman Houses shooting site turned out to be partly behind a tree. How should our building check its own camera views over a whole year?

Look at every camera's picture twice a year, once in full leaf around June and once bare around December, and save a still from each so you can compare. News12 found the problem at 32 Auburn Place only after something terrible had happened. The things that creep into a view in Fort Greene are trees growing, a sidewalk shed going up, a new awning next door, a delivery bike parked in the same spot every day, or a camera nudged by a ladder during painting. We leave every client a camera map with a reference picture for each camera, so anyone can spot a view that has drifted, and a re-aim is quick once you know which camera to look at.

We run a small guesthouse near Park Avenue. Where do hallway cameras go so every room door is covered without recording into any room?

At the ends of each hallway, looking along it, mounted high enough to be out of reach but angled so a person at a door is seen from the side and front. The Casa Blanca hotel on Park Avenue was able to give police footage of two armed men who knocked on a guest's door at 2:30 a.m. and forced their way in, which is the kind of picture a hallway camera exists for. A camera across from a single door, on the other hand, looks straight into the room every time it opens, which is exactly what guests do not want. We also cover the front door, the desk, the stairs and the back entrance, and keep the recorder in a locked office.

Our rehearsal space near BAM has a room where performers leave cases and bags. Can a camera protect their gear without filming anyone changing?

Yes, by watching the doorway, not the room. When a cameraman's equipment vanished from a dressing room at the Brooklyn Paramount, police turned to the venue's cameras to see who had come and gone. A camera in the corridor, aimed at the door of the storage or dressing room, records every person who enters and leaves with what they are carrying, and it never sees inside. If the room has a second door to a stair or alley, that door gets the same treatment.

Checks were stolen from the blue mailbox at Ashland Place and Willoughby Street and no camera covered it. Can our building's camera point at a public mailbox on our sidewalk?

A camera on your own building may record what happens on a public sidewalk, including a mailbox, because there is no expectation of privacy on the street. The precinct report on the Ashland Place theft noted that no cameras were available, which is common on corners where buildings face the street with blank walls or with cameras pointed only at their own doors. Usually yes, and a window can be one of the best spots on a landmark block because nothing goes on the facade. A sidewalk shed is going up in front of our building for facade work. Does that change where the cameras go, and what happens when it comes down?

Yes, for as long as it stands. The burglar at a DeKalb Avenue restaurant seemed to know exactly where the keys and cash were kept. Should our cameras watch the office, and who should be able to see it?

Yes, watch the office and the safe or cash spot, and keep that camera and its recordings to the fewest people possible. Privacy and Permission

Stoops, Shared Halls and Neighbors' Windows: The Privacy Rules Behind a Fort Greene Camera Job

Fort Greene rowhouses share party walls, rear fences and sometimes stoops, and their back windows face a ring of other back windows across the gardens. Cameras have to work inside that closeness. These are the rules we follow, stated plainly. They are a practical summary, not legal advice.

Your own doors, your own yard. A camera on your house may record your stoop, your areaway, your garden and the public sidewalk in front. We aim rear cameras at your own door, windows and yard, and mask the part of the picture that would look into a neighbor's garden or windows, which most recorders can black out permanently.

Shared halls belong to the building. In a divided house or a walk-up, the vestibule, hall and stairs are the owner's or board's to cover, not any single tenant's. Brickunderground has pointed out that a landlord in New York is not obligated to let tenants view building footage, and must hand it over only to police or a court. If you are a landlord, telling tenants what the shared cameras see, and how to ask for a clip after a package goes missing, avoids a lot of friction.

Nothing inside anyone else's home. We do not point a camera into another household's apartment, through a neighbor's window or into a bathroom or bedroom, and we do not install hidden cameras in rental units.

Pictures, not conversations. We leave audio off on cameras that face shared or public spaces. New York's laws on recording conversations make sound a different question from pictures, and most Fort Greene jobs do not need it.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Fort Greene

"More cameras mean better security."

The Whitman Houses have 149 cameras, according to NYCHA, and residents still questioned the system after the 2024 shooting on Auburn Place, where one camera in view was partly blocked by a tree.

Count matters far less than whether one camera sees each face at each door. A two-camera rowhouse job with a face camera at the stoop and one at the garden door can outperform eight cameras on the cornice.

On a landmark front, the Landmarks guidebook caps the count anyway, at two per 25 feet of frontage for small buildings, which pushes the plan toward quality over quantity.

"Mount cameras as high as possible so nobody can reach them."

High cameras are hard to tamper with, and they are also the cameras that recorded the Myrtle Grocery robbery without a face.

High and low cameras do different jobs. The high one shows the scene and the getaway; the low one, at the door frame or register, identifies the person.

A low camera can be protected with a vandal-resistant housing and placed where a person must face it, such as beside a door they are trying to open.

"Wireless cameras are easy anywhere."

A Brownstoner forum reply about brownstone cameras put it simply: wireless in old brick buildings is often spotty, and the cameras still need power.

Fort Greene houses have thick brick party walls and deep lots, so a router on the parlor floor often cannot reach a garden camera reliably, and a dropped connection means a missing clip.

We use wired PoE cameras for anything that matters and keep wireless for the odd spot where a cable truly cannot go.

"The police and city cameras already cover the street."

The NYPD announced pole cameras for Myrtle Avenue between Carlton Avenue and St. Edwards Street and at a Fulton Street intersection in 2011, and in 2016 Operation Safe Shopper funded store cameras through the Myrtle Avenue Revitalization Project, with footage available to police.

Those cameras watch intersections and storefronts, from a distance, and keep footage for a set period. None of them sees your vestibule, your garden or your lobby.

The precinct reports show the same: the Ashland Place mailbox theft had no cameras available, even on a busy corner.

"Night vision works in any light."

Infrared works in darkness, but it reflects off glass and turns everything grey, and many Fort Greene break-ins happened through shop glass before dawn.

Outside the glass, a low-light color camera with a little visible light gives clothing colors and faces that help an investigation.

Under a stoop or a sidewalk shed, adding a small light within the door opening often does more than a pricier camera.

"Any handyman can mount a camera on a brownstone."

Mounting is easy; mounting on a landmark front without damaging soft stone, within the Landmarks conduit limit of about ten inches, is not.

Drill into a carved lintel or run cable across the front, and you may face both a repair bill and a Landmarks violation.

We drill through mortar joints behind the mount, seal every opening and keep cable inside.

"Fort Greene is a quiet brownstone neighborhood, so one doorbell camera is enough."

For many single-family houses a good doorbell plus a rear camera is a reasonable start. But the neighborhood also includes walk-ups over shops, small co-ops, hotels, venues and public housing.

The precinct record includes lobby package thefts, early-morning shop break-ins, car break-ins along Park Avenue and back-window burglaries off Fort Greene Place.

The right plan depends on the building and the doors people actually use, which is why we look before we quote.
