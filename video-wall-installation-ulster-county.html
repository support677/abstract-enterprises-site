The Neighborhood

Why Ulster County Properties Need Video Wall Installation

Ulster is a big, thinly settled county with an unusually old and unusually regulated building stock. The Census Bureau counts 183,330 residents in its July 2025 estimate, spread over 1,124 square miles of land at about 162 people per square mile in 2020, and 87,657 housing units. It has one city, Kingston, the county seat, and a run of towns and villages from the Hudson River to the high Catskills and the Shawangunk Ridge: New Paltz with its SUNY campus, Woodstock and Saugerties, Rosendale, Marbletown and Stone Ridge, Wawarsing and Ellenville. Its economy leans on visitors and on the rooms that serve them. In 2022 the county’s accommodation and food services businesses recorded $663.5 million in sales, and it counted 5,065 employer establishments in 2023. Those are the rooms where a video wall is asked for here: a theatre lobby, a gallery, a taproom, an inn’s event barn, a resort meeting room, a campus classroom, a training floor in a converted factory.

What sets Ulster apart from its neighbours is how much of that building stock is historic, and how firmly it is protected. Kingston’s Senate House, on Fair Street, began as a single-story Dutch stone house in 1676, and the city has four historic districts researched and sponsored by the Friends of Historic Kingston: the Stockade (1969), Rondout-West Strand (1979), Chestnut Street (1985) and Fair Street (1988), all on the National Register and all designated locally. Since 1969 a seven-member Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission has reviewed changes to the exterior of properties in those districts. Under the city’s code, any material change in the appearance of a landmark or a district property — including its light fixtures and signs, where visible from a public street or alley — needs a certificate of appropriateness first, and any type of sign in the Stockade District goes to the commission before a permit is issued. A few miles south, Historic Huguenot Street in New Paltz is a ten-acre National Historic Landmark District with seven stone houses from the early eighteenth century. A video wall in buildings like these is rarely a matter of finding studs.

The sign rules are the second Ulster particularity, and they bear directly on screens. Kingston’s form-based code, amended in November 2024, prohibits electronic signs in its historic districts and in several residential and neighbourhood zones, and where it allows them it limits them to one per building, at least 500 feet from the next, showing a static message for at least five minutes, changing in a second or less, no brighter than 0.3 foot-candle above ambient at the nearest property line, with an automatic dimmer and a default to a black screen if the sign fails. Window signs are capped at 25 percent of a shop window. Woodstock’s zoning goes further in its own way: it lists directly illuminated signs, unless specifically permitted, and all internally lighted signs among the signs it prohibits. A bright screen hung just inside a storefront window in either place can turn an interior decision into a sign question, and we check that before anyone buys a panel.

Then come power and licensing. Central Hudson serves 93,190 customers in Ulster County, according to its outage summary, and after severe thunderstorms pounded the region overnight into Sunday, July 5, 2026, the utility’s website showed 7,640 Ulster customers still without power at 5:51 p.m. the next day, among 36,709 across its territory. Ulster also has its own Electrical Licensing Law, adopted by the county legislature in May 2019 and last amended in October 2025. It defines electrical work as wiring and equipment for light, heat, cooling and power and also for “signaling, communication, alarm or data transmission,” and it says no one may carry on business as an electrical contractor in the county unless licensed under it or employed and supervised by someone who is. The Town of New Paltz tells contractors that an Ulster County Master Electrician license is required to obtain building permits for electrical work. A video wall touches both: its power circuits, and the data and control cabling that feed it. That is why an Ulster wall starts with an address check, an approvals list and a named electrician, and only then a screen size.

Building Specialists

Video Wall Installation for Ulster County’s Architecture

Ulster’s buildings span more than three centuries, and each era answers the mounting question differently. These are the types we meet most, with the approach each one usually needs.

Dutch and Huguenot stone houses

Rubble-stone and later stone walls, thick and irregular, often with plaster on the inside face, found in Kingston’s Stockade and along Huguenot Street in New Paltz. Fixing into the stone face is usually the wrong answer; mortar joints, a floor-supported frame or an independent stud wall in front of the stone are the usual ones.

Nineteenth-century brick commercial rows

Brick party walls with plaster, tin ceilings and storefront windows, typical of the Rondout waterfront and older main streets. Plaster-on-brick takes sleeve anchors into sound brick with care; window-adjacent screens trigger the sign questions above.

Early twentieth-century theatres and halls

Big volumes with ornamental plaster, balconies and proscenium openings, like Kingston’s 1927 Broadway Theatre, now the Ulster Performing Arts Center. Screens go where the decoration is not, and stage LED stands on its own frame.

Industrial sheds and factory floors

Steel kiln sheds on the riverfront, former manufacturing buildings and the large floor plates of the old IBM campus in the Town of Ulster. Strong structure, long distances, high ceilings and, in open-sided sheds, weather and dust at the screens.

Victorian resort buildings

Large wood-framed and masonry hotels with period interiors, where the guest experience depends on the interior looking as it always has. Displays tend to be temporary or concealed, and every penetration is agreed with the owner.

Barns, Catskill houses and modern builds

Post-and-beam barns turned into event spaces, wood-framed houses and newer steel-stud and block buildings on campuses and commercial strips. The newer the building, the more conventional the mount; the older the timber, the more it has to be inspected first.

Materials and methods by wall type

  • Rubble or coursed stone: no anchors into the stone face of a protected wall; where fixing is allowed, anchors go into sound mortar joints after the mortar is checked; otherwise a floor-supported frame with soft pads against the wall, or a separate framed wall standing just in front of the stone.
  • Old brick with plaster: the plaster is opened only at fixing points, anchors set into whole bricks rather than the joints when the mortar is soft, and cable kept in surface raceway rather than chased into the plaster.
  • Ornamental plaster and lath: no recessed boxes; fixings found from behind where an attic or back-of-house space allows; displays placed on plain surfaces rather than decorated ones.
  • Heavy timber: sound members identified, through-bolted plates or clamps where drilling is acceptable, and a freestanding frame where the timber is decorative or fragile.
  • Steel structure: beam clamps and unistrut rather than welding or drilling where possible, and a check that the structure is not already carrying more than it was meant to.
  • Block, concrete and steel studs: conventional rails, sleeve anchors in solid block cores, and backing across several studs, because one steel stud will not carry a tiled wall.

Load matters as much as material. Four commercial 55-inch LCD panels with their rails come to a few hundred pounds hanging on one plane, and an LED wall of similar area is often heavier still, carried by its own frame rather than by brackets. The survey ends with a fixing plan named for the actual wall, and that plan is part of every written proposal.

Compliance callouts for Ulster display work

  • Kingston historic districts and landmarks: a certificate of appropriateness is required for material changes visible from a public street or alley, including signs and light fixtures; any sign in the Stockade District goes to the commission before a permit; electronic signs are prohibited in historic districts.

Your Questions Answered

Video Wall Installation in Ulster County: Questions Answered

These are the questions Ulster owners, venue managers and tenants bring to a first call. Most of them are about the building and the rules around it rather than the screens. Each is answered once, here.

Kingston’s Historic Districts and Landmarks

Who has to sign off before a screen goes into a Stockade District storefront?

Usually the Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission, if the screen will be seen from the street, and it is safer to assume so until the city says otherwise. Kingston’s code requires a certificate of appropriateness from the Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission before anyone makes a material change in the appearance of a district property, and it names signs and light fixtures among the elements that count when they are visible from a public street or alley. Separately, any type of sign within the Stockade District goes to the commission for approval before a sign permit is issued. A bright, moving screen set just behind the glass is seen from the sidewalk exactly as a sign would be. The code also caps window signs at 25 percent of a shop window and two per window.

Our practice in the Stockade and the other districts is simple: if the screen will face the street, the owner asks the city before we order anything, using the drawings and photographs we prepare. If the owner would rather avoid the question, we move the wall to a side or back wall where it serves customers inside and is not seen from the street, which is where most Uptown displays end up working best anyway.

Kingston bans electronic signs in its historic districts — does that reach a video wall in our back room or lobby?

The prohibition is written about signs, and Kingston’s code deals with signs as things seen from outside: window signs, wall signs, projecting signs, freestanding signs. A wall of screens at the back of a restaurant, in an interior lobby or in a meeting room that is not visible from the street is an interior fit-out, not a sign, and the electronic-sign ban is not the rule that governs it. What can still apply inside a landmark is the commission’s interest in any interior the city has designated, and the ordinary building and electrical permits for new circuits.

The grey zone is the screen that is inside but faces glass: a display on the back wall of a narrow shop that is plainly visible from the sidewalk through the front window, or a lobby wall visible through a glass door. We treat those as possibly street-visible, show the owner how it will look from outside at night, and leave the final word to the city.

Outside the historic districts, what does Kingston allow for a digital sign facing the street?

More, but with tight limits. The current code permits electronic signs in some mixed-use and main-street zones, mounted to the building facade rather than freestanding, one per building and at least 500 feet from the next. A panelized electronic sign is limited to nine square feet, and on a larger sign no more than half the area may be electronic. The message must be static, with no movement or illusion of movement; each message or picture must stay up at least five minutes, except time-and-temperature; and a change must happen in one second or less, all at once.

The technical rules are the ones installers most often get wrong. The sign may not exceed 0.3 foot-candle above ambient light measured at the nearest property line; it must have an ambient light monitor that dims it automatically, with certification to the city that it has been preset to those levels; and it must revert to a black screen if it malfunctions. We build those behaviours into the player and the display settings, and we take a light-meter reading at the property line at dusk so the owner has a number to show if the city asks.

What does a landmarks commission application need to show when a display will be seen from the street?

Kingston’s code lists it. The application must give the location of the building and photographs of it, the exterior features to be changed, a perspective or rendering if the commission asks for one, and, where the change includes signs or lettering, all dimensions and colours, the materials and the method of illumination, with the location shown on the building. For a screen, the method of illumination is the screen itself, so we describe its brightness, its dimming and its content rules in plain terms.

We provide the owner with photographs, a measured drawing of the screen in place, a mock-up photo taken from the sidewalk, the panel’s specification and our proposed settings. A certificate of appropriateness is in addition to any building permit, not instead of it, so we also note what the electrician will file.

Woodstock and the Town Sign Laws

Woodstock bans internally lighted signs — where in a gallery can moving images go instead?

Inside the room, facing the visitors, not the street. The Town of Woodstock’s zoning law puts directly illuminated signs, unless specifically permitted, and all internally lighted signs on its list of prohibited signs, and it requires any illuminated sign or lighting device to use light of constant intensity, with nothing flashing, intermittent, rotating or moving. A screen in a window showing a looping promotion is very hard to square with that list.

What galleries and shops in Woodstock do instead is keep moving images for the inside of the room: a wall facing the visitors once they are through the door, set back far enough from the glass that it does not read from the street, or a screen showing an artist’s moving-image work as part of an exhibition rather than as advertising. If an owner wants something visible from outside, the right first step is a conversation with the town’s building and zoning office, not a purchase.

Our business is in New Paltz, Saugerties or another town — whose sign rules apply to a screen?

The town or village where the property sits, and sometimes both: villages in Ulster have their own governments and codes inside the towns that surround them, so a business on a village main street may answer to the village code rather than the town’s. Kingston’s and Woodstock’s rules described on this page do not carry over to New Paltz, Saugerties, Rosendale or Ellenville. Each writes its own sign chapter, and the definitions of a sign, a window sign and illumination differ from one code to the next.

Stone, Brick, Timber and Steel

Can a video wall be fixed to a seventeenth- or eighteenth-century stone wall at all?

Sometimes, but it is often not the best idea. Early stone walls in Kingston and New Paltz are thick, irregular and built with lime mortar; the face of the stone may be sound while the joints are soft, and an anchor in the wrong place can crack a stone that cannot be replaced. Where an owner and, if relevant, the landmarks commission agree to fixings, we drill only into sound mortar joints, use anchors suited to soft masonry, and keep the number of fixings to the minimum the load needs.

More often we avoid the stone. A freestanding or floor-supported frame can stand a few inches in front of the wall and carry the screens, touching the stone only through padded stand-offs for stability. In a room where the stone is the attraction, a slim framed wall built in front of one section gives a conventional mounting surface and can be removed without trace.

What carries the screens when the owner will not allow a single hole?

A frame that stands on the floor. For LCD walls this is a steel stand with a rear spine that holds the mounting rails, sized for the screens’ weight and weighted or braced so it cannot tip. For direct-view LED, it is a ground-stack frame on which the LED cabinets are bolted together, with outriggers or a rear brace. Both can be finished in black or painted, and both leave the room exactly as it was when they leave.

Floor-supported frames need a floor that can take the load at a few points; old timber floors are checked for span and condition, and we spread the load with a plate where needed. They also need a way to stop the frame being knocked over, which in public rooms usually means a light tie-back to a single agreed point.

Our venue is an open-sided shed or barn — can the screens stay up between events?

Only if they are chosen and protected for it. Indoor commercial LCD panels are specified for indoor conditions; LG’s 55VM5J-H video-wall panel, for example, lists an operating range of 0 to 40 degrees Celsius and 10 to 80 percent humidity. An unheated, open-sided structure on the Hudson or a barn in the hills will go beyond that in winter and on damp summer nights, and it lets in dust and insects that clog vents.

The choices are to build a sealed, climate-controlled enclosure for the screens and their controller, to use outdoor-rated LED designed for the weather, or to treat the screens as removable: mounted on rolling or quick-release frames and stored in a heated room between events. For seasonal venues, the last option is often the cheapest over the life of the wall, and we design the mounts for a two-person changeover.

How do cables reach a wall in a building with no ceiling void and walls that cannot be opened?

Along the surface, under the floor, or from behind. In a stone or plaster building with no drop ceiling, the routes are: surface raceway or conduit painted to match and run along a baseboard or a corner; a path up from a basement or crawl space through a single small hole in the floor beside the wall; or a path from an attic or a back-of-house room on the other side of a partition. Each one is agreed with the owner before we start.

To keep the number of runs small, we usually put the controller and media player in an equipment space and send the picture to the wall over one or two cables, using HDBaseT over category cable or fibre for long distances. One neat raceway carrying two cables is far easier to approve than a bundle.

Theatres, Galleries and Hospitality

Can an LED wall be rolled onto a historic stage instead of fixed to it?

Yes, and in a listed theatre it usually should be. Direct-view LED can be built on a ground-supported frame with locking casters, positioned for a show and rolled to the wings or a scene dock afterwards, with its power and signal brought in through the venue’s existing stage connections. Nothing is fixed to the proscenium or to decorative plaster, and the stage floor is protected with plates under the casters.

The practical limits are weight, width and the route off stage. We measure the doors, the wing space and the floor before choosing cabinet sizes, and we plan the wall so it can be split into sections that pass through the narrowest opening. For venues like Kingston’s Ulster Performing Arts Center, listed on the National Register since 1979, a mobile wall also keeps the room flexible for the many kinds of shows it hosts.

Can a theatre lobby wall run the season trailer and then show a delayed-start notice from the box office?

Yes. The wall plays a scheduled loop from its media player and holds a short list of ready-made messages, such as a delayed start, a sold-out show or a weather notice, that box-office staff can call up from a tablet or a simple button panel without touching the content system. When the message is cleared, the loop resumes where it left off.

Can screens show artwork or moving-image pieces without looking like a television?

Yes, and it is mostly a matter of calibration and framing. Out of the box, commercial displays are tuned to be bright and vivid for signage. For art, we calibrate the screens to a known colour standard, lower the brightness to suit the gallery’s light, match every tile so the seams do not show colour steps, and turn off on-screen menus and logos.

The frame matters as much. A flush, matte surround, a recessed shadow-line, hidden cables and a player that starts the work automatically at opening make the screen read as part of the installation. For artists’ works, we agree the file formats and playback settings with the artist or gallery before the opening, and test the piece on the actual screens.

What does the wall look like when it is switched off in a heritage interior?

Like a large black rectangle, which is why it deserves thought. In a period room, an off screen can be the most conspicuous thing in it. The options are to size the wall to the room rather than to the maximum the wall will hold, to set it within a painted surround or panelling so it reads as part of the joinery, to place it on a wall that is already dark or plain, or to show a still image in an idle state that suits the room.

For rooms where screens must disappear completely, a motorised cabinet, a screen that lowers into furniture or a removable frame stored between events are all possible. Each costs more than a fixed wall, and we price them separately so the owner can choose.

Can a resort meeting room wall be cleared away for a weekend event and back up on Monday?

Yes, if the system is designed as two parts: fixed infrastructure and movable screens. The cabling, power, controller and connection plate stay in the wall; the screens hang on a quick-release frame or stand on a rolling base that two people can move and re-dock. Re-docking uses labelled, keyed connectors, so the wall returns in the right order without a technician.

Historic resorts like Mohonk Mountain House, a Victorian castle hotel above New Paltz whose history begins with a ten-room inn bought in 1869, show why this matters: rooms are expected to look as they always have for weddings and gatherings, and the technology has to make way. We test the change-over with the staff who will do it.

Campuses, Training Floors and Big Buildings

A training room at iPark 87 is being fitted out on a bare factory floor — what should go in before the ceiling closes?

Everything that would be hard to add later. Before the ceiling and the partition walls close, we want the blocking or backing in the wall where the screens will go, a recessed box behind the wall for power and signal, a conduit or open path from that box up to the ceiling and back to the equipment room, power circuits from the electrician sized for the wall and the controller, and data runs to wherever lecterns, cameras and microphones will be.

The old IBM complex has more than a million square feet of buildings across about 200 acres, so distance is a real factor: an equipment room at the other end of a floor may be beyond the reach of a simple HDMI extender. We plan the signal path with HDBaseT or fibre and certify every run before the walls are painted.

How far can the controller be from the wall in a long converted factory or campus building?

It depends on the cable, not the building. Direct HDMI cable is reliable only for short distances, HDBaseT over good category cable reaches about 100 metres, and fibre or a video-over-IP system can go much further. In large former industrial buildings and campus blocks, the controller is often in a central IT room, and the question becomes which of those methods suits the distance, the number of sources and the budget.

Licensing and Power in Ulster

Ulster’s Electrical Licensing Law mentions data transmission — what does that mean for a video wall job?

It means the licensing question in Ulster reaches further than many installers expect. Chapter 185 of the county code, the Ulster County Electrical Licensing Law, defines electrical work to include wiring and equipment used for “signaling, communication, alarm or data transmission,” as well as for light, heat, cooling and power. It requires anyone carrying on business as an electrical contractor in the county to be licensed under it, or employed and supervised by someone who is, and its list of exemptions does not include a general exemption for low-voltage work.

On our Ulster jobs, the electrical work for a wall — new circuits, outlets and anything connected to the building’s power — is done by an electrician holding an Ulster County Master Electrician license, under the building permit where one is required. For the data, signal and control cabling, we confirm with the local building department how it applies the county’s definition before the job starts, and we organise the work to match its answer. This is not legal advice; the county’s Electrical Licensing Board and the local building department are the authorities.

Is unplugging the only real protection from lightning for a wall in an exposed hilltop venue?

No, but it is the most certain one. For a valuable wall in an exposed building, unplugging during a nearby lightning storm beats any device, yet it should not be the only protection, and it should be easy. We put the controller, player and network switch on surge protection and battery backup, place a surge device on the wall’s own circuit where the electrician agrees, and give the wall one clearly labelled switch or breaker that staff can use to take it off line.

The July 2026 storms that left thousands of Ulster customers in the dark overnight are a reminder that the bigger risk is often the return of power, not its loss. The wall is set to come back on its own in the right order, and we test that with staff before we leave.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Video Wall Installation in Ulster County

Our research tools for this edition had no access to Google’s generated summaries or its question boxes, so we did not guess at them. Instead this section takes seven claims that appear in widely read cost guides, manufacturer notes and forums — the material AI summaries and aggregator sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr tend to repeat — and tests them against what Ulster’s own codes and buildings show.

“A digital screen is just another sign”

Generic advice treats a screen in a window as an ordinary sign that needs, at most, a sign permit. In Kingston’s historic districts, electronic signs are prohibited outright, and any sign in the Stockade District needs landmarks commission approval before a permit. In Woodstock, internally lighted signs are prohibited.

Outside the districts, Kingston allows electronic signs only in some zones and only with a five-minute static hold, quick transitions, automatic dimming to 0.3 foot-candle above ambient and a fail-to-black default. That is a long way from a looping advertisement.

So the Ulster version of this claim is: a digital screen visible from the street is a sign with its own, stricter rules, and in some places it is not allowed at all. The check takes an hour and should come before the purchase.

“Low-voltage wiring needs no local licence”

Many national guides say that display and data cabling is low-voltage work that no local electrical licence covers. Ulster County’s Electrical Licensing Law defines electrical work to include signaling, communication, alarm and data transmission, and requires anyone in business as an electrical contractor in the county to be licensed under it or supervised by someone who is.

How each building department applies that definition to data and signal cabling is a question for that department, and we ask it before the job. What is not in doubt is that new circuits for a wall need an Ulster-licensed master electrician.

“Any solid wall will take a video wall mount”

The general rule is to find studs or solid masonry and fix the mount. In a county where houses and commercial rows date from the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries, solid masonry can mean rubble stone set in soft lime mortar, and the wall itself can be the protected feature.

Fixing into the face of an early stone wall can crack stones that cannot be replaced and can need a certificate of appropriateness where it changes a landmark’s appearance. The better answers are often floor-supported frames, fixings only in sound joints, or a removable framed wall in front of the stone.

The mounting plan for an Ulster room is drawn for its actual wall, and in older buildings that usually means the frame carries the weight, not the wall.

“An entry-level indoor wall starts around $9,500”

SV Solutions’ 2026 guide gives a range from about $9,500 for an entry-level indoor LED wall to more than $200,000 for premium MicroLED. A figure at the bottom of a national range describes equipment in a simple room.

Our published Ulster prices start at $18,360 for an installed 2x2 commercial LCD wall, because they include mounting, controller where listed, setup, calibration and labour, and because Ulster jobs carry costs a national figure leaves out: frames for walls that cannot be drilled, surface raceway, approvals drawings and long cable runs.

“A kit price is the price of the wall”

CrownTV’s cost guide is clear that online LED kit prices cover hardware only, with structure, power circuits, installation and calibration on top. AI summaries tend to keep the kit price and drop the caveat.

In Ulster the items left out are often the largest ones: a freestanding frame for a stone room, an enclosure for a barn, an electrician’s new circuit, or a box-office control panel. Our table shows mounting and fabrication as its own line for that reason.

“Pixel pitch times ten gives the viewing distance in feet”

This is Planar’s rule of thumb, and it is a useful one. Christie suggests about one metre of minimum viewing distance for each millimetre of pitch, and Barco a different ratio again, so the rules do not agree.

In Ulster’s theatres and halls, the seats run from the front row to a balcony; in galleries, visitors walk right up to the screen. A single number cannot cover both, and it says nothing about the content: a fine line of text needs a finer pitch than a moving landscape.

“Consumer TVs are fine if they are only on for a few hours”

Forum posts and quick guides often say a consumer television is fine for light use. Samsung itself says consumer TVs are designed for perhaps eight hours a day and that their warranty is invalidated by commercial use, and integrators on Reddit’s r/CommercialAV point out that consumer sets rely on convection cooling that works only in landscape.

An Ulster venue that is quiet midweek may be on for twelve hours on a festival weekend; a lobby wall may run from morning to close every day of the season. Commercial panels are built and warranted for those hours and for tiling.

Where a consumer set really is the right answer, for a small room used occasionally, we say so and install it as a single display rather than a wall.

Three Ulster Paths From First Call to Finished Wall

Most of the time on an Ulster job is spent before installation, and how much depends almost entirely on where the building is and which way the screens face. These are the three paths we see most often, written as the order of events rather than a promise of dates, because commission and board calendars are set by those bodies, not by us.

Path one: an inward-facing wall in an ordinary building. A meeting room, a training floor or the back wall of a restaurant outside any historic district, with nothing visible from the street. The order is photos and address, survey, written proposal, rough-in with the electrician (who files any electrical permit the work needs), then hanging and calibration. Approvals are limited to the owner, the landlord and the building department’s electrical permit. This is the shortest path, and the one we steer clients toward when the room allows it.

Path two: anything visible from the street in Kingston’s historic districts or on a landmark. Here the certificate of appropriateness comes before any order. The owner files with the Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission, using the photographs, drawings, mock-up photo and specification we prepare; the commission reviews it; and only once there is an answer do we confirm the equipment. Because electronic signs are not permitted in the districts, the realistic outcome for a street-facing screen is usually a redesign that turns it inward, and we often suggest doing that from the start to save a cycle of review.

Path three: a street-facing electronic sign outside the districts. In the Kingston zones that allow electronic signs, the work follows the sign permit process with the code’s design standards built in: facade mounting, size and spacing limits, a five-minute static hold, transitions under a second, an ambient light monitor with automatic dimming certified to the city, and a black-screen default. Elsewhere in the county the local sign chapter decides, and in Woodstock the prohibition on internally lighted signs usually settles the question early.

Whichever path applies, nothing is ordered until it is clear, and the proposal shows which path we expect and why. If the answer changes along the way — a commission asks for a smaller screen, a code officer reads a window display differently — the design changes before the money does.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Tape on the Wall, a Photo From the Sidewalk

Two habits shape every Ulster job we plan, whatever its size. The first is the full-size mock-up. Before a bracket or a frame is ordered, the outline of every screen goes on the wall in painter’s tape, with the seams marked, and we look at it from where people will actually be: the back of a theatre lobby, the door of a gallery, the far end of a resort meeting room. In old rooms, this is usually where the wall gets smaller, moves away from a window or a fireplace, or comes off the wall altogether onto a frame.

The second is the photo from the sidewalk. If there is any chance a screen will be seen from outside, we stand in the street at dusk and photograph the mock-up, or a temporary screen at the proposed brightness, through the window. In Kingston’s districts and in Woodstock, that one picture tells the owner more about the sign question than any description, and it is the picture a commission or a code officer will want to see.

A third habit follows from the county’s licensing law. On the first visit we ask who the electrician will be, and if the owner does not have one, we bring in an electrician who holds an Ulster County Master Electrician license. The electrician sees the mock-up, the circuit is agreed at the wall rather than on a drawing, and the permit, where one is needed, is filed by the person whose license it rests on. It saves the most common late surprise on an Ulster job: a finished frame with no power where the screens need it.

Neither step takes long. Both go into the written proposal, so the owner can see what was checked before money is committed.

FAQ

Ulster County Video Wall Installation FAQ

Can a wall be taken down later without leaving marks on old plaster, brick or stone?
+

Mostly, yes, if removal is planned on day one. We favour fixings that go into mortar joints rather than the face of old brick or stone, freestanding or floor-supported frames where the owner wants no holes at all, and surface raceway that can be unscrewed. Before we start we photograph the wall and record every fixing point on the drawing, so that whoever removes the wall years from now knows exactly what was done and what needs patching. A frame that touches the wall only at a few protected points leaves almost nothing behind.

Do you prepare drawings a landmarks commission or planning board can review?
+

Yes. For a display that will be visible from the street, or any work inside a designated landmark, we prepare a simple package for the owner to file: photographs of the building and the wall, the screen dimensions, the mounting method, where cables run, how the screen is lit and dimmed, and how it would be removed. Kingston’s code asks for dimensions, colours, materials and the method of illumination when a change includes signs or lettering, so we write to that list. We do not file on the owner’s behalf unless asked to attend.

Our stone building eats Wi-Fi. How does new content reach the wall?
+

By cable, wherever we can. Thick masonry walls weaken wireless signals, so we run a wired network connection to the media player, or put the player beside the building’s router and send the picture to the wall over HDBaseT or fibre. Content is stored on the player, so the wall keeps playing even if the connection drops; the cable only matters for sending new content. Where a cable cannot be run without damaging the fabric, a wireless bridge between two rooms with a clear line can work, and we test it before relying on it.

Can a screen go into a wedding or event room without showing up in every photograph?
+

Yes, with placement and settings. Event photographers shoot toward the couple, the stage or the head table, so a screen on that wall will be in many frames, and a bright one will pull the camera’s exposure. We place screens on side walls or behind the guests where the room allows, set a dim, neutral idle image for the ceremony and speeches, and give the venue a one-touch blackout so the wall can go fully dark when the photographer asks. A screen that is off or showing a still in the room’s colours largely disappears in pictures.

Will you work alongside the venue’s own lighting and sound company?
+

Yes, and on show-related work we prefer to meet them before we design anything. Their lighting rig decides where glare will land on the screens, their console may need a feed from the wall’s controller or a feed into it, and their power plan may already use the circuit we were going to ask for. We agree on who owns each cable, connector and setting, and we label the hand-off points so that on a show day there is no argument about whose box a fault is in.

Can a wall be fixed to the posts and beams of a converted barn?
+

Often, but the timber has to be judged first. Old hand-hewn posts and beams can be strong yet split, twisted or notched, and some owners do not want them drilled at all. We look for sound members, use through-bolted plates or clamps where drilling is acceptable, and use a freestanding frame tied lightly to the structure where it is not. We also check what the wall would sit in front of, because a barn’s gaps and doors let in dust, insects and cold that matter to screens.

Can the screen frames be finished to sit with old woodwork?
+

Yes. The screens themselves are black glass and metal, but the frame, trim and any enclosure around them can be built or painted to suit the room: a painted wood surround in a parlour, a dark metal frame in an industrial space, or a recessed shadow-line so the screens read as a panel rather than a box on the wall. We show a sample finish against the real woodwork before ordering, because the same black looks different under candle-coloured bulbs and under daylight.

Can the lobby screens show a live picture from the stage camera?
+

Yes. A camera at the back of the house, or a feed from the venue’s own production switcher, can be sent to the lobby wall through an encoder or an HDBaseT link, so late arrivals and bar staff can see the stage. Expect a short delay compared with the room, and agree with the performers or their management whether a live picture is allowed, since some touring contracts restrict it. We add a switch so staff can turn the feed off, and the wall falls back to its normal loop when they do.

Can you reuse the wiring from our old projector?
+

Sometimes. A projector’s ceiling run usually carries one video cable, power and perhaps a network cable to a point the wall will not use, so the cable rarely ends up in the right place. What it often gives us is a proven path: an existing sleeve through a wall or a chase between floors that saves opening an old surface again. We test the old cables before deciding; if they certify for the signal the wall needs, they are reused, and if not, they become the pull string for new ones.

Can the wall pull in posts from our website or social media automatically?
+

Usually, yes. Most digital-signage software can show a web page, a calendar feed or a social-media feed in one zone of the wall and refresh it on a schedule, so the events listed on your website appear on the lobby screens without anyone retyping them. The limits are the platforms’ own rules and what your feeds actually publish. We test the feed on the real screens for legibility, set the player to keep the last good version if the feed fails, and give staff a way to hide it on nights it would be out of place.
