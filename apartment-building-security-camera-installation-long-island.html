The Neighborhood

Why Long Island Apartment Properties Need Security Cameras

On an island where roughly four in five homes are owner-occupied, the apartments that do exist carry a lot of weight. Census QuickFacts puts the owner-occupied rate at 81.9% in Nassau and 82.2% in Suffolk for 2020–2024, which leaves somewhere near one household in five renting. Those renters live in a handful of building types: low garden complexes from the post-war decades, co-op and condo buildings clustered near village centres and train stations, newer mid-rises built on former parking fields beside the Long Island Rail Road, apartments above storefronts on main streets, and legal accessory apartments carved out of single-family houses. Each of those buildings has a board, an owner, a managing agent or a landlord who is answerable when something goes wrong in a common area.

What goes wrong is usually outside. A garden complex spreads its residents across several buildings and a shared parking field, and most of the trouble we are asked about happens in the lot, at the dumpster enclosure, at the mailbox bank or at a side door that never quite latches. Long Island saw that pattern sharply with catalytic converter thefts: CBS New York reported Nassau County going from 472 thefts in 2021 to 2,292 in 2022, and Suffolk from 282 to 1,581. On the r/longisland thread about those figures, one resident wrote that a converter can come off in about thirty seconds and is hard to catch, which is exactly the problem a building lot camera has to solve: not a wide pretty picture, but a clear view of a vehicle and a person at the moment it happens, in the dark.

Regulation shapes the job too, and it is different from New York City. Outside the five boroughs, rent stabilization only applies where a city, town or village has opted into the Emergency Tenant Protection Act. New York State Homes and Community Renewal lists the Nassau localities, among them the Cities of Glen Cove and Long Beach, the Town of North Hempstead and villages including Cedarhurst, Floral Park, Freeport, Great Neck and Great Neck Plaza. Buildings there sit inside a state-administered system, with tenants who know their rights and a paper trail for every change in services. Co-ops and condos answer to their own governing documents and their shareholders or unit owners. Accessory apartments are permitted town by town, and the Herald has reported that unincorporated parts of the Town of Hempstead have “more than their fair share of illegal rentals.” A camera system on Long Island has to fit whichever of those worlds the property lives in.

Then there is the building itself. Garden apartments put the camera problem horizontally: two or three storeys tall, but spread across an acre or more, with buildings that may each have their own basement and their own electrical service. Cable has to travel between buildings, not up a riser. Lots are big and lit unevenly. Trees that were planted as saplings in the 1960s now block the sightlines a camera needs. Newer station-area buildings are the opposite: one structure, a garage, a package room, amenity spaces and access control already built in, where the camera system has to talk to what the developer installed.

That is the work we do across Nassau and Suffolk. We plan coverage around how residents and outsiders actually move through the property, run cable between buildings without leaving the grounds torn up, put the recorder somewhere a super can reach and a stranger cannot, set retention so footage exists when the police ask for it, and write it all up so a board or an owner can approve it in one meeting.

  • About one household in five rents: owner-occupied rates of 81.9% (Nassau) and 82.2% (Suffolk), Census QuickFacts 2020–2024. Apartments are a minority of homes, which is why the ones that exist tend to be full and closely watched by owners and boards.
  • 458,166 and 512,565 households: Nassau and Suffolk, 2020–2024, averaging 2.99 and 2.93 people each (Census QuickFacts). Bigger households mean more cars per unit in a garden-complex lot and more packages at the mailroom.
  • 2,292 catalytic converter thefts in Nassau in 2022, up from 472 the year before; Suffolk went from 282 to 1,581 (CBS New York, January 2023). Parking fields are where apartment residents are most exposed.
  • 489 apartments delivered in 2020 in the first phase of Station Yards at the Ronkonkoma LIRR station, with 388 more homes in the second phase, according to the Governor’s office (October 2024). Station-area rentals are where most new Long Island apartments are going up.
  • Buildings of six or more units built between 1947 and 1974 are the ones ETPA rent stabilization can reach in an opted-in municipality — which describes a large share of Long Island’s garden apartment stock.
  • Two counties, two sets of contractor rules: Suffolk’s Office of Consumer Affairs licenses more than 15,000 vendors, over half of them home improvement contractors, and Nassau runs its own county licensing — on top of the state alarm-installer licence that covers camera systems.

Building Specialists

Apartment Camera Systems for Long Island’s Architecture

The building type decides almost everything: where the recorder lives, how cable travels, what the cameras are bolted to and who signs off. These are the multifamily types we work in most across Nassau and Suffolk, and what each one asks of a camera system.

Garden Apartment Complexes

Two- and three-storey brick buildings set around lawns and a shared lot, typically with separate entrances per stairwell rather than one lobby. The camera challenge is distance: many doors, many paths, and buildings far enough apart that cable needs planning. Each stairwell door is a point of entry, so coverage favours the paths that lead to them and the lot that feeds those paths.

Co-op Buildings

Elevator buildings and walk-ups near village centres in places like Great Neck, Long Beach and Garden City, governed by a board under the proprietary lease and bylaws. Lobby, elevator cab, package area, garage and service entrance are the usual list. The board decides access rights, and our proposal is written for its meeting.

Condominium Associations

Townhouse-style condos and mid-rise condo buildings, with a board and a managing agent and, often, a declaration that says who can alter common elements. Cameras typically cover the clubhouse, pool gate, mail kiosk, entrances and guest parking.

Station-Area Mid-Rises

New four- to six-storey rental buildings near LIRR stations, from Ronkonkoma’s Station Yards to downtown Patchogue’s New Village. One structure with a garage, package room, gym and roof deck, where the developer’s access control and network already exist and the cameras must slot into them.

Apartments Over Storefronts

Two or three floors of rentals above shops on main streets, with a separate street door to the stairs and an alley or rear lot shared with the stores. The residential entry needs its own camera that the retail tenants’ systems don’t control.

Two-Family Houses & Accessory Apartments

Houses with a legal second unit — over a garage, in a basement, or in an extension — under town accessory-apartment rules. Small systems, but privacy matters most here, because the owner and the tenant live a wall apart.

Senior & 55-Plus Communities

Single-storey or low-rise clusters where residents value a quiet, visible deterrent and staff want to see the front entrance, the community room and the paths at night. Cameras are placed for wellbeing checks at common spaces, never inside homes.

Older Walk-Ups in Village Centres

Pre-war and post-war walk-ups of six to twenty units in the older village downtowns, with a single stair, a basement boiler room and a buzzer panel that has seen better days. Cable runs up the stairwell in raceway or through the cellar ceiling.

How each building type changes the install

Garden complexes: we start by finding the electrical and network “anchor” in each building — usually the boiler room or a basement storage room with a dedicated circuit — and decide whether each building gets its own small recorder or a PoE switch that feeds back to one central recorder. Between buildings, fiber is the reliable choice for anything beyond a standard copper run, and it carries no lightning path between separate electrical services. Cameras go on building corners at second-storey height, where they cover two faces at once and are out of reach, and on light poles in the lot where the building faces can’t see. Brick is drilled with masonry bits and anchors sized for the bracket, and every exterior penetration is sealed.

Co-ops and condos: the wiring is usually easier than the approvals. We work from the building’s riser or a hallway ceiling, use surface raceway painted to match where walls can’t be opened, and keep work in common elements unless the board authorises otherwise. Elevator cab cameras are coordinated with the elevator maintenance company, which controls the traveling cable. The recorder goes in a locked room, and we document every login we create so the board, not an individual, owns the system.

Station-area mid-rises: the building already has a network closet, a managed switch stack and an access control platform. We confirm camera brand and recorder compatibility with what is there, request VLANs and switch ports from whoever manages the network, and place cameras so the package room, garage gates and amenity doors line up with the access logs. Fire-rated walls get firestopped penetrations.

Mixed-use and walk-ups: the residential door, stair and rear lot are the priority, wired separately from the shop below so the landlord controls them. In older walk-ups we often run cable through the cellar and up the stair in raceway, and we check that old intercom or alarm wiring we might reuse actually tests clean before relying on it.

Accessory apartments and two-families: fewer cameras, more masking. We set privacy zones on any view that touches the other household’s windows or private yard, and agree in writing who holds the recorder and the app.

Most Long Island co-op and condo boards pair cameras with access control in the same budget year, because a fob system tells you which credential opened the door and the camera shows who was holding it. If the building’s buzzer is failing as well, our intercom repair crews on Long Island can often keep the existing panel going until the board is ready to replace it.

Compliance callouts for Long Island apartment cameras

  • State alarm-installer licence. New York’s Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers, and the licence covers closed-circuit TV used for detecting or monitoring intrusion, theft or movement. AESS holds NYS licence #12000287431.
  • County home-improvement licensing. Suffolk’s Consumer Affairs Code makes it unlawful to do home improvement contracting on residential property without a county licence, and Nassau runs its own licensing; towns and villages may add permits. We confirm what applies to the address before work starts.
  • No cameras in restrooms or changing rooms. New York Labor Law § 203-c bars employers from video recording employees in a restroom, locker room or room designated for changing, and Penal Law § 250.45 makes recording a person where they expect privacy a crime. We never place cameras inside units, bathrooms or staff changing areas.
  • Audio is a separate legal question. Recording a conversation without the consent of at least one party, by someone not present, is eavesdropping under Penal Law § 250.05. Building cameras are installed video-only unless the owner’s counsel has approved audio for a specific location.
  • Fire and building code. Penetrations through rated walls and floors are firestopped, low-voltage cable in plenum spaces is plenum-rated, and nothing is fastened to sprinkler pipe or fire alarm devices.
  • Accessibility. Equipment at entries is kept clear of the accessible route and door hardware, so a camera or enclosure never narrows a path or blocks a push plate.

This is general information about how we design systems, not legal advice. Boards and owners with questions about tenant notice, leases or governing documents should ask their attorney.

Your Questions Answered

Apartment Building Cameras on Long Island: Questions Answered

These are the questions Long Island boards, owners, managing agents and small landlords ask once they get past “how much.” They are grouped by the decision behind them; shorter questions about scheduling, insurance and policies are in the FAQ further down.

Spread-out properties and many buildings

Our garden apartment complex has eight separate two-storey buildings — does every building need its own recorder?

Not necessarily. What every building needs is a protected place where its cameras get power and a network connection — usually a PoE switch in the boiler or storage room. From there, the footage can go to a recorder in the same room or travel to one central recorder in the management office or the building with the best security.

We choose by three things. Distance and cable route: if the buildings can be linked reliably, a central recorder is simpler to manage and cheaper in drives. Power: a recorder per building keeps recording even if the link between buildings is cut. And access: if the super lives on site in one building and the managing agent works remotely, one central system with remote access is easier to secure. On larger complexes we often do a hybrid — small recorders per cluster, all visible in one app.

How do you get camera cable from building to building across a Long Island complex without tearing up the lawns?

First we look for what’s already there. Many older complexes have empty conduit or retired telephone and cable TV pipes between buildings, and if they test clear we pull through them. Next option is aerial: buildings that are close can be linked with a messenger-supported outdoor cable at a safe height, or along the faces of connecting structures like carports. Where neither works, we trench — a narrow cut for conduit, laid along walkways or bed edges and restored, not a backhoe across the lawn.

The cable itself is usually outdoor-rated single-mode or multimode fiber between buildings, because it ignores distance limits within a property and doesn’t carry a lightning surge from one building’s electrical system to another’s. Where a wireless point-to-point bridge has clear line of sight between rooftops, that is a valid option too, and we tell you honestly when trees make it a bad one.

What should the cameras in a garden-complex parking lot actually be able to show after a catalytic converter theft?

Three things: the vehicle that came in, the people who got out, and what they did under the car. One wide camera on a building corner rarely gives you any of those at night; it gives you movement. So we design lot coverage in layers. A tight camera at each lot entrance records every vehicle and, where it’s set up for it, the plate as it slows for the turn. Overview cameras on poles or building corners cover the rows so you can follow a car to where it parks. And the carport bays or dark corners where residents’ cars sit get cameras close enough to show a person crouched beside a wheel.

Lighting matters as much as the camera. We note where the lot lighting is weak during the walkthrough and recommend cameras with strong low-light sensors or infrared for those rows, and we tell the owner when a light fixture, not a camera, is the cheaper fix.

Boards, owners and who controls the footage

Can a co-op board in Nassau put cameras in the hallways without a shareholder vote?

That depends on the building’s proprietary lease, bylaws and house rules, not on anything we decide. Many boards treat common-area cameras as a security and maintenance decision within their authority; others have bylaws that require shareholder approval for capital projects over a set amount. Your managing agent or the co-op’s attorney can tell you which applies.

What we do is make the decision easy to explain. Our proposal shows every camera on a plan with what it sees, confirms none look into an apartment, states the retention period and lists who will have access. Boards tell us that a clear plan, circulated before the meeting, is what turns a contested agenda item into a quick approval.

Who should hold the passwords in a condo where the managing agent changes every few years?

The association, always. The most common rescue job we see on Long Island is a system nobody can log into because the administrator account belonged to a former agent, a former board president or a previous installer. The fix is set up correctly at the start: the master administrator credentials are recorded in a sealed document given to the board, and day-to-day logins are created separately for the agent, the super and any board member who needs them.

When the agent changes, the board deletes one user and creates another without touching the system. We also register the recorder and any cloud service in the association’s name and email, not ours and not the agent’s, so the equipment the association paid for stays the association’s.

Does a rent-stabilized building in an ETPA village have to tell DHCR or tenants before adding cameras?

Adding cameras to common areas is generally an addition, not a reduction, of building services, and it does not on its own change a tenant’s regulated rent. But stabilized buildings have a formal process for services and rent adjustments through New York State Homes and Community Renewal, and owners who want to seek any increase tied to an improvement should talk to their attorney or HCR before the work, not after.

Separately from the rules, we recommend telling residents in writing before installation: which areas will be covered, that no camera looks into an apartment, and who to contact with questions. In buildings across Glen Cove, Long Beach, Freeport and the Great Neck villages, where tenants know the ETPA system well, a short notice does more to prevent complaints than anything else.

Can a homeowner with a legal accessory apartment put a camera on the shared side entrance?

Usually yes, as long as the camera covers the shared approach and door rather than the inside of the tenant’s unit or a space the tenant uses privately. Town rules on accessory apartments vary — the Herald has reported that Huntington allows them in owner-occupied homes with an annual permit while Hempstead’s policy is limited to family members and seniors — and the lease is the best place to state that a camera covers the entry.

Technically, we set privacy masks so the tenant’s windows and any private patio are blacked out in the recording, and we can give the tenant their own view of the entry camera so the system protects both households. That last step tends to turn a camera from a point of friction into something the tenant asked for.

Privacy, placement and local rules

Is it legal to point a camera at the laundry room or the super's workshop in a New York building?

A camera covering the laundry room door and machines is a normal common-area placement. What New York forbids is recording in places where people expect privacy: Labor Law § 203-c bars employers from video recording employees in restrooms, locker rooms or rooms designated for changing clothes, and Penal Law § 250.45 covers secretly recording people where they have a reasonable expectation of privacy.

So in practice: the laundry room yes, a restroom off the laundry room no. The super’s workshop or tool room can be covered at its door for theft; if the super changes clothes there, that room stays camera-free. We mark these decisions on the plan so the board has a record of why each camera is where it is.

Our property straddles a village line — whose permit rules apply to the camera work?

Each part of the work follows the rules of the place where it is done. Long Island is divided into towns, two cities in Nassau and dozens of incorporated villages, and a garden complex or a commercial strip with apartments can sit across a boundary. Low-voltage camera work typically doesn’t need a building permit, but trenching, pole foundations and anything electrical can trigger village or town requirements, and county home-improvement licensing applies by county.

During the walkthrough we note which tax lot each building and pole location sits on, check the municipality for each, and flag any part of the scope that needs a permit or an electrician before it’s priced. That way a pole foundation in one village isn’t held up by paperwork you found out about on install day.

Does a mid-rise near an LIRR station need different cameras than a garden complex?

Yes, in emphasis. A station-area mid-rise is one building with controlled entries, a garage with gates, a package room and shared amenities, and often a busy public sidewalk on one side. Its cameras concentrate on the points where access control already makes decisions — the garage gate, the lobby door, the package room — and on the garage drive aisles. A garden complex has many entrances and a big open lot, so its cameras concentrate on paths and parking.

Mid-rises also usually come with a network built by the developer. We ask early for the network documentation and the access control platform name, because matching those saves weeks. Where the developer installed cameras already, we often expand that platform rather than start a second one beside it.

Recording, police requests and keeping it running

How long should a Long Island apartment property keep footage before it records over?

Most of our residential properties settle on 30 days. It is long enough for a resident who was away on a long weekend to report a hit-and-run in the lot, for a managing agent to look into a complaint, and for a detective to request footage. Shorter than two weeks and incidents get recorded over before anyone knows to look. Much longer than 30 days and the drive budget climbs with little practical gain.

Retention is a setting, not a guess. We calculate drive size from the number of cameras, resolution, frame rate and how much motion each camera sees, then confirm the recorder is actually reaching the target a week after install. The board should write its chosen retention period into its house rules so it is applied consistently.

How do we hand footage to Nassau or Suffolk police without giving them our whole system?

Export the clip, not the login. We show the super and the managing agent how to find an event by camera and time, export it to a USB drive or a secure download link in a format the police can play, and record what was exported and to whom. No officer, resident or insurer needs a password to your recorder to get evidence from it.

We also set the recorder’s clock to sync automatically, because a clip with the wrong timestamp is the first thing that weakens it, and we leave a one-page export guide in the equipment room. For a serious incident, preserve the footage the same day: lock or export it before it reaches the end of the retention period.

Can the old analog cameras on a 1960s complex be kept while we upgrade?

Often, for a while. Many older Long Island complexes have coax cameras from one or two upgrades ago. A hybrid recorder can take the existing analog cameras and new IP cameras side by side, so the owner can replace the most important positions first — entrances and the lot — and keep the rest until they fail or the budget allows.

We test each coax run before reusing it. Corroded connectors and water-damaged cable are common on exterior runs that have sat through decades of salt air near the South Shore, and a camera on a failing cable will drop out exactly when you need it. Where a run tests well, it can even carry a new camera using coax adapters, avoiding a new pull. Our DVR upgrade service on Long Island covers recorder swaps on their own.

What happens to the cameras when a nor'easter knocks out power to the complex?

Without backup power, they stop, and so does the recording. For the recorder and the network switch that powers the cameras, we fit a battery backup sized to carry them through short outages and to shut the recorder down cleanly when the battery runs low, so the drive isn’t corrupted. Properties with a standby generator can have the camera equipment put on a generator-backed circuit by their electrician.

Storms also test the exterior work. We use weather-rated cameras and housings, drip loops at every cable entry, sealed penetrations, and mounts fixed into masonry rather than vinyl or trim, so a wind-driven rain off the Great South Bay or the Sound doesn’t find its way into the cable or the camera.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Apartment Building Cameras on Long Island

Search for apartment building cameras and the AI summaries, along with cost guides on sites like Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, draw on national, mostly single-family data. Here is where that picture breaks down for a Long Island multifamily property.

“Price it per camera”

The claim you will see most is a per-camera installed price, as if a building system were a stack of identical units. It sounds tidy and it is the number boards ask about first.

On a Long Island garden complex the camera is rarely the expensive part. The cost lives in getting signal between buildings, in pole mounts for the lot, in the head-end in each building, and in the hours spent aiming and tuning each view at night. Two properties with the same camera count can differ by a wide margin because one has conduit between buildings and the other needs a trench.

That is why our pricing section starts from published package ranges and then quotes larger properties per building after a walkthrough. A per-camera figure from a national guide is a starting conversation, not a budget.

“Wireless cameras are fine for apartments”

Summaries often suggest Wi-Fi cameras to save on wiring, and for a single unit or a small house that can be true.

Across a complex, Wi-Fi cameras depend on a router that reaches each corner of each building and the far end of the lot, and on batteries or power at every location. Brick walls, distance and dozens of neighbouring networks make that unreliable, and an unreliable camera is the one that is offline on the night of the incident.

We use wired PoE cameras for anything that matters, fiber or a dedicated point-to-point bridge between buildings, and keep Wi-Fi for the network the residents use. The wiring costs more once and fails far less afterwards.

“Cloud storage is the modern choice”

Many summaries present cloud recording as the obvious default, with local recorders as outdated.

Cloud makes sense for a few cameras with a good upload connection. A property recording thirty or forty cameras continuously would need a large and steady upload from a building whose internet service was bought for the office and the laundry card reader, and a per-camera subscription that never ends. Many Long Island properties also have only one internet circuit for the whole site.

We record locally on a recorder the property owns, with secure remote viewing, and add cloud backup only for the few cameras where off-site copies matter. The board gets reliability without a permanent fee per camera.

“Any camera installer can do this”

The AI answer to who can install cameras is usually any handyman or electrician.

In New York, installing CCTV used to detect or monitor intrusion, theft or movement falls under the Department of State’s security and fire alarm installer licence. Suffolk and Nassau each add county home-improvement licensing for residential work, and a co-op or condo will want a certificate of insurance naming it before anyone sets a ladder.

Ask any installer for their state licence number and their county licence where it applies. Ours is NYS #12000287431, and our certificates of insurance are issued to the building’s exact requirements.

“More megapixels means better evidence”

Summaries tend to rank cameras by resolution, suggesting 4K everywhere.

Higher resolution helps when it puts more pixels on a face or a plate at the distance that matters. It doesn’t help a camera facing into headlights, mounted too high, or pointed at a lot with no light. It also multiplies storage, which shortens retention on the same drives.

We pick resolution and lens per position: higher for a lot entrance or a long walkway, standard for a vestibule where the subject is close. Placement, angle and light decide whether footage identifies anyone.

“Tenants have no say over common-area cameras”

Some answers flatly say landlords can put cameras anywhere in common areas without telling anyone.

On Long Island the governing documents matter: co-op proprietary leases, condo declarations, ETPA rules in opted-in municipalities and the lease itself. Even where a board or owner has authority, residents who learn about cameras by noticing them are the ones who complain, and audio recording brings New York’s eavesdropping law into play.

Our approach is video only in common areas, never inside units, with a written notice to residents before install and a plan the board can show anyone who asks.

“Once installed, a camera system runs itself”

Summaries rarely mention upkeep, implying cameras are fit-and-forget.

Drives fail, spiders build webs across infrared domes, trees grow into views, firmware needs security updates, and clocks drift. On older systems we are called to, it is common to find recorders that stopped writing to the drive months before anyone checked.

We set the recorder to alert when a camera goes offline or recording stops, and we offer scheduled health checks so the board hears about a problem before the police ask for footage that doesn’t exist.

Nassau and Suffolk share an island and a lot of housing types, but the apartment camera work we do in each feels different. The differences come from density, from the age of the buildings and from how each county and its municipalities are organised.

Fixing Earlier Installs

Common Problems in Building Camera Systems Someone Else Installed

  • The recorder stopped recording months ago. A failed drive or a full drive set to stop rather than overwrite. Nobody noticed because live view still worked. We replace the drive and turn on recording alerts.
  • Nobody has the password. The admin account belonged to a former agent or installer. Depending on the make, we recover or reset the recorder and set up credentials the board owns.
  • Lot cameras show only headlights at night. Cameras aimed straight down a drive lane into oncoming lights, or too high to see faces. We re-aim, change lenses or move them.
  • Wi-Fi cameras dropping out. Consumer Wi-Fi cameras on the far side of a brick building from the router. We replace the critical ones with wired PoE cameras.
  • Cables stapled across brick and vinyl. Unprotected exterior runs that have cracked in the sun or been chewed. We re-run them in conduit or raceway with proper entries.
  • A camera that looks into a window. An exterior camera that catches a resident’s apartment. We re-aim it or add a privacy mask so it records only the common area.
  • The clock is wrong. Footage stamped hours or days off, which weakens it as evidence. We set automatic time synchronisation.
  • Trees grew into the view. A camera that covered the lot when installed now sees leaves. We re-position it or advise on trimming.
Through the Year

A Year of Camera Problems on a Long Island Apartment Property

Most building camera trouble is seasonal, and knowing the season helps a board decide what to fix first. This is the pattern our technicians see on residential properties across both counties.

  • Late autumn into winter: the dark comes early. Once the clocks change, residents come home from the train and the parkways after dark, and the lot is busiest exactly when it is least lit. Cameras that looked fine on a summer afternoon walkthrough show their weaknesses now. This is the time to check night images at the lot entrance and the paths to each stairwell door.
  • The holiday stretch: packages everywhere. Mailrooms, vestibules and the steps of garden-complex buildings fill with deliveries. A camera that sees the package shelf and the door in one frame, and a resident notice about how to report a missing box, cut down on arguments with the management office.
  • Midwinter: storms and power. Nor’easters bring wind-driven rain, ice on brackets and power cuts. Recorders without battery backup lose footage and sometimes their drives. A pre-winter check of the backup unit and the sealing at each exterior entry is cheap insurance.
  • Spring: trees leaf out. Views planned when the branches were bare disappear behind leaves in May. A walk round each camera after leaf-out shows which ones need repositioning or which branches the landscaper should take back.
  • Summer: pools, courtyards and move-in season. Complexes with pools and courtyards see more people on the grounds in the evening. Lease turnover brings moving trucks, propped doors and bulk items left by the dumpster. The pool gate, the courtyard and the trash enclosure earn their cameras now.
  • Late summer and early autumn: hurricane season. Coastal buildings from Long Beach to the bays and along the Sound get the same attention to backup power and exterior sealing as in winter, plus a check that nothing can be torn loose by high wind.

A board that books one health check a year usually chooses early autumn: it catches summer damage, confirms the battery backup before storm season and checks the night images before the dark evenings arrive.

Camera Policy

What a Long Island Board’s Camera Policy Usually Covers

The system is only half the job; the other half is the rules the board or owner sets for using it. We don’t write legal policies, but we are often asked what other properties decide, so this is the outline boards and owners typically work through with their managing agent and attorney.

  • Where cameras go and where they never go. Common areas and exteriors only; no cameras inside units, restrooms or staff changing areas; privacy masks wherever an exterior camera catches a window.
  • Video only, or not. Whether any location records audio at all, given New York’s eavesdropping law. Most properties choose video only everywhere.
  • Retention. How many days footage is kept before it records over, written down so it is applied the same way every time someone asks.
  • Who can watch live and who can play back. Usually the super, the managing agent and named board members, each with their own login. Live views for residents, if any, limited to an entry camera.
  • How footage is released. To police on request, to insurers through the managing agent, to residents only for incidents involving them and with the board’s approval, and always as an exported clip with a record of who received it.
  • Notice to residents. A short written notice when cameras are installed or added, and a line in new leases or house rules that common areas are recorded.
  • Who maintains the system. Who checks that it is recording, who calls for service, and how passwords are handed over when the agent or the board changes.

When we deliver a system we leave a one-page summary of how it is actually configured — retention, user roles, masked areas and the export procedure — so the board’s written policy and the real settings match. That match is what makes footage easy to use when it matters.

Comparing Proposals

Checking Camera Proposals Side by Side Before the Board Votes

Boards and owners often collect two or three proposals, and they rarely describe the same system. One quotes sixteen cameras, another twenty-four, a third quotes a cloud plan. These are the points worth lining up before anyone compares the totals.

  • The coverage plan itself. A proposal without a drawing or a camera-by-camera list of what each one sees can’t be compared. Ask every bidder to mark the lot entrance, each building door and the mailroom, and say what a camera there will identify.
  • How buildings are connected. Fiber, copper, wireless bridge or a recorder in every building — and who restores the grounds if there is trenching. A low price that assumes Wi-Fi across a complex is not the same system as one with a wired backbone.
  • Retention in days, at the stated camera count. Drive size alone tells you little. Ask for the number of days the system will hold at the resolution quoted.
  • Ownership and fees. Whether the property owns the recorder outright, and whether there are monthly charges for cloud storage, remote access or monitoring.
  • Licences and insurance. The bidder’s New York State alarm-installer licence number, the county home-improvement licence where the work requires it, and a certificate of insurance matching the building’s requirements.
  • Who holds the passwords. A written commitment that administrator credentials go to the board or owner at hand-over.
  • Backup power and alerts. Whether battery backup and offline notifications are included or extra.
  • Warranty and service terms. What is covered, for how long, and the hourly rate for service afterwards.

If our proposal is one of the ones on the table, we are happy to walk the board through the differences line by line. A system that costs a little more but holds a month of footage at night-usable quality, on a recorder the board owns, is usually the better value over its life.

Field Notes

Field Notes From Long Island Apartment Walkthroughs

How our technicians work through a garden-complex walkthrough in Nassau or Suffolk (general practice, not a single customer’s job)

We start in the parking lot, not the lobby, because that is where most of the incidents a board describes actually happened. We walk the lot entrance first and stand where a car slows to turn in, because that is the one place every vehicle passes at a readable angle. Then we walk each row, noting which light poles work, which are dark, and where trees have grown over since the poles went in.

Next comes the hunt for what is underground. In older complexes we look for handholes, old telephone pedestals and pipes entering each building’s basement, because an empty conduit between two buildings can save a trench. Inside each building we find the room with a dedicated circuit and space on the wall for a small enclosure, and we check the temperature there in summer if we can.

Last, we sit with the super and ask where the side doors don’t latch, where packages go missing and where outsiders cut through. Those answers usually move two or three cameras from where a drawing would have put them.
