The Neighborhood

Why Vinegar Hill Properties Need Door Buzzer Repair

Vinegar Hill is one of the smallest neighborhoods in Brooklyn, and almost every buzzer in it belongs to a small building. Wikipedia puts the whole neighborhood at about a tenth of a square mile, roughly six blocks between DUMBO and the Brooklyn Navy Yard, running from Front Street near the water down to the Brooklyn-Queens Expressway and from Bridge Street east to the Navy Yard. Inside that pocket, the Landmarks Preservation Commission's 1997 designation report describes three small groups of brick Greek Revival row houses, a mid-nineteenth-century firehouse, part of a 1908 factory and a handful of wood-frame houses, most of them along Front Street, Gold Street, Water Street and Hudson Avenue. Wrapped around them are a few converted industrial buildings, the Con Edison site on the north side and, to the south, the Farragut Houses.

That mix decides what a buzzer repair looks like here. A typical Vinegar Hill entry system is not a lobby panel with forty buttons. It is a doorbell transformer screwed to a joist, two or three push buttons beside a street door, a thin pair of wires running up an inside wall, and, where there is more than one household, a strike in the door frame and a release button upstairs. Brownstoner's Suzanne Spellen described 50 Hudson Avenue in 2015 as typical of the older buildings here: three stories of brick, two floors of apartments over a storefront, simple Greek Revival. A house like that has two apartments to buzz in, a door that has been opened and slammed for close to two hundred years, and a bell circuit that has been patched by whoever owned it last.

When something in that chain fails, the people who notice are an owner who lives upstairs, a tenant on the top floor, a delivery driver standing on the Belgian block, or a family whose rear house cannot hear the street at all. There is rarely a super. There is often no managing agent. And because the buildings are so small, the city's own records barely register the problem, which we explain in the next section. What the owner needs is someone who will find the actual failed part, fix it without making a mess of a historic doorway, and leave the circuit labeled so the next repair is easier.

The neighborhood's setting adds two practical wrinkles. Vinegar Hill sits beside large neighbors: the Navy Yard behind its iron fence on one side, the Con Edison property on the other, where New York YIMBY reported in January 2024 that demolition had begun for the Brooklyn Clean Energy Hub, with completion aimed for 2029. And many of its houses sit inside the historic district, so the street button and the door around it are things the public sees. Neither changes how a bell transformer works, but both shape how we plan a visit, as later sections show.

Vinegar Hill facts that shape a buzzer repair

  • A very small, low-rise core: Wikipedia gives Vinegar Hill about 0.101 square miles and a 2016 population of 2,671, a figure that includes the Farragut Houses towers on its southern edge.
  • A landmarked district since 1997: the LPC designated the Vinegar Hill Historic District on January 14, 1997, in three parts: Front Street rows, the Gold Street and Water Street group, and the Hudson Avenue houses.
  • Shops turned into homes: the designation report says newcomers in the 1970s converted most of the old storefronts to residential use, especially along Hudson Avenue, so many front doors here sit in former shop entrances.
  • Houses behind houses: the report records rear houses on some lots, including 204 Front Street, where a rear house of about 1860 to 1867 stands behind an 1877 front house, and 51 Hudson Avenue, whose 1906 rear house is "not visible from the street."
  • Almost no city buzzer record: on November 11, 2026, HPD's violation file showed one buzzer violation on the historic blocks, at 204 Front Street in 2008, and the 311 file showed no buzzer complaints on Hudson Avenue, Front, Plymouth, Water, John, Evans or Little Streets since 2020.
  • NYCHA on the edge: the Farragut Houses, which NYCHA lists at 1,390 apartments in ten 13- and 14-story buildings, run their own entry systems through NYCHA.

The City Record

An Almost Empty File: What the City's Buzzer Records Say, and Don't Say, About Vinegar Hill

On many of our Brooklyn neighborhood pages, the city's data tells a loud story: dozens of buzzer complaints to 311, repeat addresses, HPD violations piling up on the same walk-ups. Vinegar Hill's story is the opposite, and it is worth understanding because it changes who has to notice a broken buzzer and who has to fix it. On November 11, 2026 we queried two NYC Open Data files for the streets that make up and border the neighborhood: Hudson Avenue, Front, Plymouth, Water, Gold, John, Evans, Little, York, Sands, Bridge, Navy, High, Nassau, Concord and Prospect Streets, in ZIP codes 11201 and 11251.

HPD's Housing Maintenance Code violation file returned four buzzer violations in its whole history for those streets. Three were at addresses south of the expressway, outside Vinegar Hill: two at a large building on Nassau Street in 2018 and 2019, and one at a Gold Street address in 2009 cited "at front areaway." Only one sits on the historic blocks. On May 28, 2008, HPD wrote a class B violation at 204 Front Street, apartment 2, ordering the owner to "properly repair the broken or defective inoperative bell & return buzzer system 1 sty entrance to 2 sty apt 2." The record shows it as closed. That is the entire HPD buzzer history of the landmarked core.

The 311 file tells the same story. Since 2020 it holds eight bell, buzzer and intercom complaints across all of those streets, at four addresses. Three of the four addresses are outside the neighborhood: a large building on Sands Street west of Bridge Street, and two on Gold Street well south of the expressway, one of which produced four complaints in a single June 2022 week. The fourth, a Gold Street address, filed one complaint on August 14, 2026, closed because HPD "was unable to complete the inspection." There are no buzzer complaints at all since 2020 on Hudson Avenue, Front Street, Plymouth Street, Water Street, John Street, Evans Street or Little Street.

Why the file is so thin

It would be easy to read those numbers as proof that Vinegar Hill's buzzers never break. They do. The file is thin for three structural reasons, and each one matters to an owner. First, HPD's buzzer enforcement mostly reaches rental multiple dwellings, buildings with three or more apartments, and it starts with a tenant complaint. A large share of Vinegar Hill's houses are one- and two-family homes, many lived in by their owners, and when the bell fails in a house like that there is nobody to complain to but yourself. Second, even in small rentals, tenants who know their landlord personally tend to text the owner rather than call 311. Third, the neighborhood's largest residential complex, the Farragut Houses, belongs to NYCHA, and NYCHA residents' door and intercom problems go to NYCHA's own repair system, not to an HPD inspection, so they do not appear in this violation file the way a private building's would.

The practical result is that in Vinegar Hill, the person who has to find a buzzer fault is usually the owner. Nobody else is going to write it up. An owner who lives upstairs may not notice for weeks that the garden tenant's bell has stopped; an owner whose rear house is empty for the winter may only discover in spring that its bell never worked after a storm. That is why we suggest that owners of small houses here test their own system a couple of times a year: press each button at the street while someone listens inside, try the release from every unit, and watch whether the door actually lets go and then closes and latches on its own.

What the one violation on Front Street shows

The 2008 violation at 204 Front Street is a useful example precisely because it is ordinary. HPD described the fault as a bell and "return buzzer" system from the first-story entrance to a second-story apartment. In plain terms: a button at the street door that should ring upstairs, and a button upstairs that should buzz the door open. That is the whole system in most Vinegar Hill houses with more than one household, and the violation names both halves because they can fail separately. A bell that rings but a door that won't release is usually a strike, a release button or the wire between them. A door that releases but a bell that never rings is usually the street button, the bell or its pair. We test the two halves on their own for that reason.

The same address is also one of the lots the LPC report describes as having a rear house behind a front house. We have no information about the 2008 repair itself and draw no conclusion about that building; we mention it only because it shows, in the city's own words, the kind of small two-part system that fills these blocks. If you own a building here and want to see its record, both files are public. Search HPD's online building lookup by address, or search 311 service requests for "bell/buzzer/intercom," and send us what you find; dates and apartment designations help a technician plan.

Small Houses

Two Buttons, One Transformer and a Strike: How a Vinegar Hill House Buzzer Works and Fails

Most of the entry systems we see in Vinegar Hill would fit in a shoebox. The heart of each is a bell transformer, a small device that steps household power down to somewhere between about 10 and 24 volts. In older houses it is often screwed to a joist or a beam in the cellar, sometimes near the electrical panel and sometimes nowhere near it, wherever the electrician who installed it decades ago found a handy spot. From there, thin bell wire runs to the push buttons at the street door, to a bell, buzzer or chime in each household, and, where the door can be released from upstairs, to an electric strike in the door frame and a release button in each unit.

That simplicity is both the good news and the trap. Good news, because almost any fault in a system like this can be found with a meter in an hour and fixed with ordinary parts. A trap, because simple systems are the ones that get patched by everyone: the previous owner added a chime, a renovation contractor moved a button, a tenant swapped the bell for a wireless doorbell and left the old wires taped in the wall. By the time we arrive, a two-button system can have five splices, two transformers and a mystery wire that goes nowhere. The repair is still usually small. Finding it is the work.

The failures we see most in houses this old

The transformer. Bell transformers fail quietly. They hum, run warm and then stop, or they keep going at a voltage too weak to pull a strike open. In a house that has added a video doorbell, a louder chime or a second strike without changing the transformer, the original unit may simply be too small for the load. We read its label, measure it with nothing running and again while a button is held and the release is pressed, and replace it if it sags. A new transformer is mounted where it can be found again, on its own labeled box, not hidden behind insulation.

The street button. Push buttons beside a Vinegar Hill door live outdoors on brick, brownstone trim, wood or the pier of a former shop. Water gets behind them, screws loosen in soft mortar or old wood, and the contacts inside corrode. A button that works only when pressed hard, or works in dry weather and fails in wet, is usually the button or its two wires. Replacing a button is one of the cheapest repairs on our list; the care goes into mounting it so it stays put in old material and sealing the hole behind it.

The wire. Bell wire in an old house runs through cellars, up chases beside chimneys, under floorboards and through walls that have been plastered over. A pair can be cut by a plumber, chewed, crushed under a new floor or corroded at a splice in a damp corner. When one household's bell is dead but the others work, and the button and bell both test fine, the pair between them is the suspect. We find it with a tone generator rather than by opening walls, and where an old cable has a spare conductor we use that first.

The strike and the door. In a house where the street door can be released from upstairs, the strike in the frame is the part that takes the most punishment. It hums every time someone buzzes, and it has to let go of a latch that is pressed against it by a door that may have swelled, sagged or been forced at some point in its life. A strike that hums but won't release is often fine electrically and simply jammed. We test it with the door held off the latch before we condemn it.

Front house, rear house, one bell circuit

A handful of Vinegar Hill lots carry two houses: one on the sidewalk and a second behind it, across a yard or reached through the front house. The LPC report describes rear houses at 204 Front Street and at 51 Hudson Avenue, where the 1906 brick rear house built by the Parfitt Brothers is "not visible from the street." On a lot like that, the street door's bell wire, and sometimes a release, has to travel through the front house's cellar or across the yard to reach the back. It is the longest run in any small Vinegar Hill system, and the most exposed: through a wall, under a yard that has been dug up for drainage or gardening, or along a fence.

When the front house's bell works and the rear house's doesn't, we start where the rear run leaves the shared wiring, usually near the transformer, and measure there before going out to the back. If the voltage is good at the junction and gone at the rear house, the fault is in the run between them, and we trace it with a tone probe. A run that has to cross the yard again is quoted on its own line, because the route (through a sleeve under the yard, along a wall, or overhead) is a choice the owner should make with the price in front of them. If the rear house has no release and the owner wants one, that is an addition, not a repair, and we price it separately.

Old Shop Doorways

When the Front Door Used to Be a Store Entrance: Buzzer Repair in Hudson Avenue's Converted Shopfronts

Hudson Avenue was a busy shopping street for most of the nineteenth century. The LPC report counts 666 retail outlets in the area in 1885, and describes the Hudson Avenue houses as having been built with shops on the ground story. When the stores closed, many were turned into living space; the report says the newcomers of the 1970s "converted most of the storefronts to residential use, especially along Hudson Avenue." Forgotten NY's Kevin Walsh, walking the street in 2009, called it a "Brigadoonish enclave of frame homes, long-disused storefronts." For a buzzer technician, that history leaves a particular kind of front door.

In a converted shopfront house, the entrance may be the old shop door itself, set back between piers where display windows once were, or a newer door cut in where a window used to be. The bell button is often mounted on whatever was handy: a wooden pilaster, a painted metal pier, a strip of trim added during the conversion. The wire to it may run through the space behind the old display window, which is now part of a living room, or up through what was once the shop's ceiling. And the door frame may never have been designed for a strike at all, because a store door of the 1880s was locked with a key, not released from upstairs.

What that means on a repair

The button needs a solid mount. Buttons screwed into old trim or a hollow pier loosen with every press, and once they move, the wires behind them fatigue and break. We remount on sound material, use a backing plate where the trim is soft, and seal the opening so rain driven against the storefront doesn't reach the contacts. Where the button sits on a cast-iron or decorative surface, we don't drill new holes in it; the LPC's Permit Guidebook asks that equipment not be fastened through cast iron or decorative surfaces, and an old shop pier is usually the most visible part of these facades.

The strike needs an honest frame. Where a strike was added during a conversion, it was often cut into a frame that was never meant for one, or into a door stop added later. Those strikes tend to sit slightly out of line with the latch, and they work only when the door is pulled or pushed just so. Before we replace any strike in a converted shop doorway, we check how the door hangs, whether the latch meets the keeper squarely, and whether the frame around the strike is solid. If the frame itself is split or loose, that is a carpenter's or door contractor's job, and we say so in writing before replacing a part that would only fail again.

The visitor may be inside your living room the moment the door opens. A shop entrance often has no vestibule. The street door opens straight into what used to be the sales floor and is now a front room. That is a good reason to know who you are letting in before you press release, and it is the most common reason owners in these houses ask about adding a small video door station to a repair. We can quote that as an option, sized to the transformer and wire already in the house, but it is never folded into a repair without the owner's say-so.

The Historic District

Repairing a Buzzer Inside the Vinegar Hill Historic District Without Changing the Doorway

The Vinegar Hill Historic District is small but specific. The LPC designated it on January 14, 1997, after public hearings in September and November 1996, and drew it in three parts: the north side of Front Street from 225 to 249 plus 202 and 204 Front; the east side of Gold Street from 69 to 77 together with 280 to 286 Water Street; and the Hudson Avenue houses from 49 to 79 on one side and 50 to 54 and 70 to 74 on the other, along with 312 Water Street. If your house is on one of those lists, the doorway and everything mounted on the front of it is part of a landmark.

For most buzzer repairs that changes nothing, because most buzzer repairs happen inside. A transformer in the cellar, a pair in the wall, a bell upstairs, a release button in an apartment and even a strike inside the door frame are not facade changes. The LPC's guidebook treats ordinary maintenance and repair as work that does not need a permit. Where the guidebook does speak to intercoms, it is about where a panel goes and how it is attached: within or beside the door opening, one panel per opening, with a design compatible with the building, and not fastened through cast iron or decorative surfaces.

How we keep a repair on the right side of that line

  • Repair in place first. An old button plate with a failed button can usually take a new button from behind, in the same hole and a matching finish, which changes nothing the public sees.
  • Same footprint if a plate must change. Where a plate is too worn to hold a button, we measure and photograph it and look for a replacement that fits the same footprint and holes before anything is ordered.
  • No new surface wiring on the front. New or replacement wire is routed inside the house, through the frame or the wall behind the button, rather than run across the brick or the trim.
  • Anything bigger is the owner's decision. If a repair would mean moving a button, adding a panel or changing its size or look, we put that in writing and leave the permit question to the owner and the Commission before we buy a part.

Owners outside the three listed groups, on the blocks between them and on the streets toward Bridge Street, are not inside the district, but many of their houses are just as old and their doorways deserve the same care. We treat every Vinegar Hill front door as if someone will want it to look the same in another hundred years, because someone usually does.

Farragut Houses

The Farragut Houses Doors: Who Fixes the Entry System at Vinegar Hill's NYCHA Towers

The largest residential buildings in Vinegar Hill are the ten towers of the Farragut Houses, south of the historic blocks. NYCHA lists the development at 1,390 apartments and about 3,440 residents, more people than live in the rest of the neighborhood. Their front doors and intercoms are a different world from a Front Street row house, and residents sometimes find our page while looking for help, so it is worth being clear about how those doors are repaired.

The entrance doors, locks and intercoms at Farragut belong to NYCHA, and their repair runs through NYCHA's own work-order system, not through a private contractor hired by a tenant. That has been a sore point for years. In August 2013, DNAinfo's Janet Upadhye reported that Farragut residents had 4,034 unanswered repair requests as of February 15 of that year, and quoted a resident whose front door had "a large crack" that made it impossible to close properly. In October 2018, the NYC Comptroller's office published observations of NYCHA building entrances and reported that at Farragut, "on August 1, 2018, auditors observed that 8 out of the 10 front entrance doors (80 percent) were open." Across Brooklyn, the same letter counted 241 of 1,432 NYCHA front entrance doors, 17 percent, open.

There has been work since. CityLand reported that on November 27, 2023, NYCHA announced the completion of more than $24 million in safety and security upgrades at 23 campuses, Farragut among them, including "layered access control installations, new intercoms, and steel-reinforced aluminum doors." The Comptroller's letter also quotes the rule those systems answer to: an older multiple dwelling with eight or more apartments "shall be equipped with automatic self-closing doors and self-locking doors and shall also be equipped with an intercommunication system."

So, plainly: if you live at Farragut and your building door or intercom is broken, the route is a NYCHA work order, and keeping a written record of the dates you reported it helps. We are not the right company for that door, and we will tell you so if you call. Where we do help is next door: the privately owned houses, lofts and small apartment buildings on the blocks around the development, whose owners are responsible for their own doors.

Lofts and Big Neighbors

Lofts, the Old Firehouse and a Power Station Next Door: The Larger Doors at Vinegar Hill's Edges

Not every Vinegar Hill buzzer is a two-button plate. The neighborhood's industrial past left a few larger buildings that now hold apartments, studios and offices. The LPC report describes the six-story Benjamin Moore factory at 231 to 233 Front Street, designed by William B. Tubby in 1908, and the former Constitution Engine Company No. 7 firehouse at 225 to 227 Front Street, which the city decommissioned and sold and which was converted to residential use in 1976. Toward Bridge Street and the Navy Yard fence, other former warehouses and factories have been turned into multi-unit buildings over the decades.

These buildings bring the same problems we see in converted industrial buildings across north Brooklyn. A firehouse or a factory was never designed with a residents' entrance, so the street door, the intercom and the wire to each unit were added later, often more than once. Doors are large and heavy, frames are steel or very old wood, and runs from the street panel to the farthest unit are long. Some of the newer conversions use a networked entry panel that calls residents' phones instead of a wired handset, which adds a network switch, a power-over-Ethernet supply, an internet connection and an administrator account to the list of things that can go wrong.

How we approach a loft entry system

We start at the door the public uses and work back. On an audio system, that means the panel, the release, the strike or magnetic lock, and then the supply and the wiring to each unit, with voltage measured at the far end of the longest run as well as at the supply, because a long run can deliver too little to the last apartment even when the supply is fine. On a networked panel, it means confirming that the panel powers up, that it can reach the building network and its own cloud service, and that the directory still lists each unit correctly. Where the building's administrator login is lost, we document exactly what we could and couldn't check without it, so the owner knows what the manufacturer or the original installer has to unlock.

Construction and utility work on the north side

The Con Edison property that dominates Vinegar Hill's north edge has been a construction site since demolition of the old Hudson Avenue Generating Station began. New York YIMBY reported in January 2024 that the site would become the Brooklyn Clean Energy Hub, a transmission substation, with barges used to limit truck traffic and completion aimed for 2029. We make no claim that this work has damaged anyone's buzzer. What we can say from experience elsewhere in the borough is that long construction projects bring utility shutoffs, street openings and contractors into nearby cellars, and each of those is a moment when a bell transformer gets unplugged or a splice gets disturbed. If your buzzer stopped working on a day something else happened on your block, tell us the date; it often points straight at the fault.

How the building type changes the repair

The Greek Revival row house is a wiring-path problem. The walls are brick with plaster on the inside, and there is usually one way up for the bell wire, beside the stair or a chimney. When an upstairs bell fails, we test the button, the bell and the transformer first, then trace the pair through that one path. Where the original pair has failed somewhere in a plastered wall, a spare conductor or a route through the stair hall's ceiling and a closet is usually possible without cutting decorative plaster.

The converted shop house is a mounting and alignment problem. The button is on a pier or trim that was never meant to hold one, and the strike, if there is one, was added to a frame built for a key lock. We remount the button on sound backing, check the latch against the keeper, and name any frame repair as a carpenter's job before replacing the strike.

The wood-frame house is a moisture and movement problem. Wood frames swell and shrink with the seasons, so a strike that worked in January may bind in August. Wire in timber walls is also more likely to have been stapled, nailed through or disturbed by later carpentry. We check the door's fit at the strike in its current state, set the keeper so the latch has a little room, and look for staples and nails along any run that fails.

The lot with a rear house is a distance problem. A small transformer that is fine for a front-house bell may deliver too little at the end of a long run to a rear house, especially if the rear house has a strike or a buzzer that draws more current. We measure at both ends and size the supply for the far end.

The 1870s apartment house is a small multiple dwelling, with the city's rules to match. Several apartments share a plate, a supply and the wiring up the stair hall, so a fault in one apartment's station can affect its neighbors. We test every apartment on the line, not only the one that complained, and leave the owner a record of each.

The converted industrial building is a systems problem. There may be several entrances, a freight door, a long corridor and a networked panel. We identify every door on the system first, then trace power, release and voice or network to each, and keep the elevator company's, the internet provider's and the manufacturer's parts separate from ours in the written report.

Rules and practical limits at business and apartment doors

  • Multiple dwellings: the NY Attorney General's Tenants' Rights Guide says buildings with three or more apartments built or converted after January 1, 1968 must have self-closing, self-locking doors at every entrance, and those with eight or more apartments must also have a two-way voice intercom that buzzes the door open; older buildings must have self-locking doors and a two-way intercom if a majority of the apartments ask for one, and the landlord may recover the cost.
  • Self-closing doors: HPD treats a defective self-closing door in a multiple dwelling as an immediately hazardous class C violation, to be corrected within 14 days of the notice, so a buzzer repair always includes the closer and the latch.
  • Landmarked fronts: inside the Vinegar Hill Historic District, the LPC's guidebook treats ordinary repair as maintenance, and new intercom panels go within or beside the door opening, one per opening, never fastened through cast iron or decorative surfaces.
  • Egress first: on any door people leave through, a strike or magnetic lock must never stop someone inside from getting out; fail-safe and fail-secure choices are explained before one is replaced.
  • Line voltage: a new circuit, or work inside an electrical panel, is for a licensed electrician under a Department of Buildings permit; we name it in the quote and coordinate the low-voltage side.
  • Reach: a call button or keypad the public uses at a business door should sit within the 2010 ADA Standards' reach range, with 48 inches as the high forward reach.
Field Notes

Field Notes: Working a Two-Unit Greek Revival House From the Button Down

We don't tell stories about particular Vinegar Hill addresses, because we only publish what we can document. What we can describe is the order a technician follows in the house type that fills these blocks, a brick row house split into two or three units with a bell circuit and a strike, because the order is the same every time and it is what keeps a small repair small.

The visit starts at the street door, with someone inside. We press each button and listen for each bell, then ask the person upstairs to press release while we watch the strike and the latch. A strike that clicks and lets go, but a door that then won't close and latch by itself, is a closer problem, not a buzzer problem, and we note it. Then we go down. In the cellar we look for the transformer, which in a house this old can be anywhere: on a joist near the front wall, on the side of the electrical panel, behind a boiler. We read its label and measure it at rest, then with a button held, then with the release held, because an old transformer can read fine until the strike asks for current. If it sags, it is the likely culprit, and nothing upstairs needs touching. If it holds, we look at every splice we can reach, especially any taped twist from an earlier repair, then climb to the bell that failed and measure there. Voltage at the cellar and none at the second floor puts the fault in the wire between them. That sequence usually takes well under an hour, and it means the part we replace is the part that failed.

Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Repair in Vinegar Hill: Questions Answered

Lots with a front house and a rear house

The bell from our Front Street door to the small house at the back of our lot went dead, while the front house's bell still rings. Which part of that long run usually fails?

Usually the stretch that leaves the front house. Because the front bell works, the street button's common wire, the transformer and the street door are all doing their job, so the fault is on the branch that serves only the rear house: its own button contact at the street, its own pair, or the bell or buzzer in the rear house. The most exposed part of that branch is where it leaves the front house, through a rear wall, under a yard, along a fence or across a passage, and that is where we find most breaks. We measure at the junction where the rear pair joins the shared wiring, then at the rear house. If there is voltage at the junction and none at the rear house, a tone probe follows the run until it stops. A repair on an existing run is priced as a wire repair; if the run has to be replaced, the route is a decision we put in front of the owner with its price, because a sleeve under the yard and a run along a wall cost very different amounts and look very different.

Visitors for our rear-house tenant have to ring the front house and wait for someone there to let them in. Can the rear house get its own button and release on the shared street door?

In most cases, yes, and it is one of the more useful changes we make on these lots, but it is an addition, not a repair, so it is quoted separately. The rear house needs three things: a button at the street door wired to a bell in the rear house, a release button in the rear house wired to the strike in the street door, and enough supply to run both houses' bells and the strike at the end of a longer wire. The street door may already have a strike for the front house; if so, the rear house's release simply joins it. If the existing plate has no room for another button, inside the historic district we look first for a matching plate in the same footprint rather than a second plate beside the door. And if the rear house is reached through the front house's hall rather than its own gate, the owner should think about which doors a visitor will pass through once buzzed in, because releasing the street door may give them the front house's hallway too.

Converted shop houses on Hudson Avenue

Our Hudson Avenue door is the old store entrance, recessed between two former display windows, and the bell button is fixed to the wooden pier beside it. When it is replaced, can it go back in exactly the same spot?

Almost always, and in a landmarked house it is the spot we prefer, because it changes nothing at the doorway that the public sees. The usual reason a button on a shop pier fails is that the wood behind it has softened or the screws have stripped, so the button rocks with every press and the wires behind it break. We take the button out, check the wood, and if it is still sound we fit a new button in the same opening with a small backing plate or longer fasteners into firmer material. If the wood has rotted, a carpenter's repair to the pier comes first, and we say so in writing. We seal the opening behind the button so water driven against the old storefront does not run down the wires, and we match the finish of the old button wherever a matching part exists. If the pier is cast iron or carries decorative work, we work through the existing holes only.

Our street door opens straight into the front room that used to be the shop floor, with no vestibule in between. Is there a safer way to let visitors in than pressing release and hoping?

Yes, and it is a fair concern in a house where the street door is also the door to your living room. The simplest step is to stop releasing blind: a small video door station at the street, with a screen or phone app inside, lets you see who is there before you press anything. Many converted shop houses here can take one on the existing two-wire bell circuit or a short new run, provided the transformer is upgraded to suit it. A second option is to keep the release only on a button you have to walk to, near the door, so nobody buzzes from upstairs without looking. A third, where the house has room, is an inner door or gate that creates a small entry. We quote those as options alongside the repair; the repair itself, getting the bell and the release you have working again, comes first and is priced on its own.

Wood-frame houses and deep cellars

Our wood-frame house still has an old mechanical twist bell set in the front door, and an electric bell was added beside the door decades later. Can the electric bell be repaired without taking out the twist bell?

Yes. The two have nothing to do with each other: a twist or crank bell is purely mechanical, a key on the outside that rings a bell mounted on the inside of the door, while the electric bell is a button, a pair of wires, a transformer and a bell or chime. We repair the electric circuit and leave the twist bell exactly as it is. The only place they meet is the door itself, so if the electric button has to be remounted, we keep its fasteners clear of the twist bell's mechanism and of the door's old hardware. Plenty of owners of old houses like to keep both; the twist bell for its sound and history, the electric one so someone on the top floor can actually hear a visitor.

The transformer for our bell is in the sub-cellar, below the regular cellar, and the only way down is a ladder through a hatch. Will a technician go down there, and does it change the visit?

Yes, we will go down, and the main thing it changes is planning. The LPC report notes that at least one Hudson Avenue house has a sub-cellar, and lower levels like that turn up in several of the oldest houses here. Before the visit, we ask the owner to make sure the hatch can be opened, that there is a light or that we know there is none, and that whatever is stored on the ladder or at its foot is moved. On the day, a technician reaches the transformer, reads its label and measures it, and checks the splices nearby, which in a damp lower level are a common failure point. If the transformer has to be replaced, we usually recommend mounting the new one somewhere reachable, in the regular cellar or near the electrical panel, as long as the wiring allows, so the next person doesn't need a ladder. Moving it a long way, or onto a new circuit, can involve an electrician, and the quote says so.

Apartment houses, lofts and the street itself

Our street door rattles every time a heavy vehicle rolls over the Belgian block outside, and lately the door sometimes pops open by itself a moment after closing. Is the street to blame, or the buzzer?

Usually neither on its own; it is the latch and the strike not holding firmly enough, and the vibration is what exposes it. A door that pops open after closing has a latch that is only just catching the strike's keeper, because the door has shifted, the keeper has worn, or the strike's screws have loosened. Repeated vibration from the cobbled street can work screws loose over time, and it can make a marginal latch let go. We check whether the latch reaches fully into the keeper when the door is closed by its closer, tighten or reset the strike, shim the keeper if the door has moved, and adjust the closer so the door closes with enough force to latch every time. If the strike's own keeper is worn, we replace the strike. A door that unlatches on its own is a security problem in a house and, in a multiple dwelling, a self-closing-door problem the city takes seriously, so it is worth fixing promptly.

Our four-story 1870s apartment house has a cast-iron-faced button panel set into the doorway that looks original, though the buttons behind it were replaced decades ago. Can it be rewired while the iron face stays?

Usually, yes. Panels like that are typically a decorative iron or bronze face with the working parts, buttons, a speaker and their terminals, mounted behind it. We take the face off carefully, photograph and label every wire before disconnecting anything, test each button and its pair, and replace only the buttons, terminals or speaker that have failed, fitting parts that sit in the existing openings. The face goes back on its original fasteners. If a button opening is worn so badly that a new button won't hold, we look for a sleeve or bezel that fits inside it before suggesting any change to the face. Because these panels are in the most visible part of the doorway, and in a landmarked building in the part the LPC guidebook cares about most, we do not drill or alter the face itself, and if a repair truly can't be done behind it, we say so in writing and leave the next decision to the owner.

The button plate at our Water Street door takes direct sun off the river all afternoon, and its plastic buttons have yellowed, cracked and started to stick. Is that a repair, or does the whole plate have to go?

It is normally a repair. Sun and weather break down the plastic in cheaper buttons and name windows long before the metal plate or the wiring wears out, and a cracked button lets water reach the contacts behind it, which is why sun-damaged buttons start to stick or fail in wet weather. We replace the buttons with metal or UV-resistant ones that fit the plate's existing openings, replace any name-window covers, and check the contacts and wires behind each button while the plate is off. If the plate itself has corroded through or its openings no longer hold a button firmly, then a replacement plate in the same footprint is the next step, priced as a faceplate repair from our list. A small hood or a different finish can help on a door that faces the sun, but on a landmarked house that would be a change to the doorway, so it is the owner's choice and may need the Commission's input.

When the utility cuts power on our block for scheduled work, should our buzzer come back by itself once the power returns?
+

A simple bell circuit should, because a transformer has nothing to reset. It is fairly common in old houses, because spare telephone pairs were handy. We test those pairs, seal the splices properly and label them, or move the bell onto its own wire if the shared cable is unreliable.

Our 1830s house has a cellar that floods in heavy storms. What can be done so the bell transformer and splices survive the next one?

We move the transformer and any splices up out of the wet zone, onto the upper part of a wall or a joist, in a closed box, and use gel-filled connectors that resist moisture.
