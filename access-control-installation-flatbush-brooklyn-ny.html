The Neighborhood

Why Flatbush Properties Need Access Control

Flatbush has never had official borders. Wikipedia notes that the Flatbush Development Corporation once drew them at Parkside Avenue, Bedford Avenue, Avenue H and Coney Island Avenue, while the New York Times later used New York Avenue and Ocean Avenue as the edges. Either way the district sits in Brooklyn Community District 14, spans the ZIP codes 11203, 11210, 11225 and 11226, and is policed mostly by the 70th Precinct from 154 Lawrence Avenue. For an access control installer, though, the map that matters is a different one: who owns each door, and who has a key to it.

The answer, in Flatbush, is usually a landlord. The NYU Furman Center counted a homeownership rate of 24.4 percent for Flatbush and Midwood in 2024, so roughly three households in four rent. The same profile lists a population of 147,911 and a rental vacancy rate of only 3.1 percent. The Furman Center’s older 2010 profile put the share of rental units that were rent-regulated at 82.5 percent, a figure that is now dated but still describes the kind of building we are called to most: an apartment house where the street door, the lobby, the cellar and the package room all belong to the owner, and where every change to how tenants get in is a change to a regulated service.

Much of that housing went up in one burst. Wikipedia describes how a post-World War I tax exemption for new residential building, which ran until 1932, produced the six-story Art Deco and Colonial Revival elevator apartment houses that line Ocean Avenue and the edges of the old village. Walk-ups with stores beneath them fill the blocks along Flatbush, Church, Nostrand and Rogers Avenues. And then there are the complexes. Flatbush Gardens, built in the late 1940s as Vanderveer Estates on the site of the old Flatbush Water Works, is 59 six-story buildings with about 2,500 largely rent-stabilized apartments on roughly 21 acres, according to the Brooklyn Eagle and THE CITY. A building like that has dozens of street doors that all have to lock, every day, for thousands of people.

The reporting from these buildings has a pattern, and it is not subtle. In July 2025, News 12 described an unnamed Flatbush apartment building where a man who did not live there was recorded inside on two nights, covering a hallway camera with his hand. Residents blamed broken entrance locks. When the station’s crew visited, the front entrance that had just been repaired opened with keys from the News 12 van. In September 2024, after a man entered a Flatbush Gardens building and kicked in a woman’s apartment door, one tenant told News 12, “if the basic front door isn’t working, yeah, I feel unsafe.” Neither story is about high technology. Both are about a front door that does not keep strangers out.

The same thing shows up away from the apartment houses. In February 2024, News 12 reported that three men forced their way through the back door of a house of worship at 26 Rugby Road in the middle of a Sunday afternoon, while four monks were inside. In 2025, BoroPark24 reported one building on Church Avenue broken into three times in less than four months, once through a side window, once through a forced side door and once over a fence and through a wall. The city’s Avenue NYC study of Flatbush’s commercial corridors counted 1,662 storefronts along Church, Flatbush, Nostrand and Rogers Avenues and found that 53 percent of the merchants surveyed wanted safety changes. A good access plan in Flatbush looks at the back of the building as hard as the front.

None of this is a reason to panic, and we do not sell systems that way. The Furman Center’s serious crime rate for the district was 8.1 per 1,000 residents in 2024, about half the 15.6 it recorded in 2006. What the stories describe are ordinary failures that ordinary hardware fixes: a lock that any key can turn, a door that does not close behind the last person, a back door nobody watches, and a list of key holders nobody can name. Access control is the tool that replaces all of those with a door that relocks every time, a credential for each person that can be cancelled on its own, and a record of which door was used. In a rent-stabilized building, it also has to be done inside rules that the state housing agency and the city have already written down, which is where most of this page spends its time.

The building decides the hardware. Below are the six kinds of property we are called to most in Flatbush, with the door each one usually has and what it usually needs.

Six-story elevator apartment houses

The interwar buildings of Ocean Avenue and the blocks around it, often with a pair of street doors, a vestibule and a lobby. A strike on the outer or inner door, a video panel in place of the buzzer board, and readers on the cellar and package room.

Walk-ups over stores

Three- and four-story buildings on the avenues with a residential door squeezed beside a shop. The tenants’ door on its own credentials and schedule, kept apart from the store’s, and a back door to the yard that the super can control.

Garden complexes

Groups of six-story buildings around courtyards and lawns, like Flatbush Gardens. Many identical entrances, high tenant turnover, and the need for each door to keep working on its own when anything upstream fails.

Two- and three-family houses

Attached and semi-detached houses on the side streets, often with an owner downstairs and tenants above. A shared front door with one call button per household, and separate codes the owner can cancel without changing anyone else’s.

Houses of worship

Churches, temples and community halls, some with clergy or caretakers living on site. Front doors that open for services, back and side doors that should almost never open from outside, and alerts that reach someone who can act.

Newer mixed-use buildings

Buildings with ground-floor retail and apartments above, including the redevelopment planned for the landmarked Sears building at Beverley Road and Bedford Avenue. Networked readers, package rooms and amenity doors planned from the drawings.

What the materials mean for the install

The interwar apartment houses were built in brick, often with stone or terra-cotta trim around the entrance. Readers and panels go into the mortar joints, not the face of the brick or the ornament, so a later change leaves a joint to repoint rather than a broken piece of trim. Their street doors are often paired metal-and-glass doors with narrow stiles and a center mullion; the lock usually goes into the mullion, the frame header or a panic bar rather than the edge of the door. Where a building still has an original door with ornament worth keeping, we plan the hardware so that nothing visible from the street is cut into it.

Walk-ups over stores tend to have a steel residential door that has been kicked, welded and rehung more than once, in a frame that may be filled with grout. We cut a clean pocket for the strike, correct the hang of the door first, and fit a closer sized for a door that faces the wind off a wide avenue. Two-family houses on the side streets usually have wood doors in wood frames; there the work is finding solid wood in a jamb that has been bored for three or four generations of locks, and replacing that section if we cannot.

The Conversion a Housing Examiner Would Sign: Fobs in Rent-Stabilized Flatbush Buildings

In a rent-stabilized building, the way tenants get through the street door is a building-wide service, and the state’s housing agency, the Division of Housing and Community Renewal, treats a switch from keys to fobs as a change to that service. Owners apply; tenants can object; DHCR decides, and it often approves with conditions. Two rulings reported by the landlord-tenant publication LVT show what those conditions look like in practice, and we use them as a checklist on every Flatbush conversion, whether or not the owner has filed anything yet.

The first, an administrative review decided on July 29, 2016, upheld an approval that came with a long list of terms. Metal keys still had to open the building in an emergency. The system could record entrances but not departures. A camera watched the entry system. Management helped tenants with access during office hours, and the super after hours. Tenants who keep the Sabbath and cannot use electronic devices had to be accommodated. The system needed a 48-hour battery backup. Every occupant, children included, got a free fob, and each tenant could have up to four more for guests or employees; a replacement could cost no more than $25. Tenants had to show identification when collecting fobs, but the landlord could not record it, could not ask for or keep more than one Social Security number per apartment, and could not put a tenant’s name, address or photograph on the fob. No equipment was to be installed inside the apartments.

The second, decided on May 18, 2017, involved an owner that wanted to swap a wired intercom for a telephone-based system and keys for fobs. DHCR held the owner to its standard terms: up to four additional free fobs per apartment for a tenant’s employees, on cards that expire and can be renewed, or for family and friends who visit regularly. The conversion also had to keep intercom service reaching every apartment, with a rent reduction to cover the landline it needed. Brick Underground, reporting on DHCR’s approach in 2018, summed up the same pattern: fobs free to lawful occupants, a cap on replacement charges, and no new identity information collected from tenants.

Two things follow for an installer. The first is that a good part of the DHCR checklist is a hardware and configuration question, not a legal one. Whether the system logs exits, how big the battery is, whether a keyed cylinder remains on the door, whether anything has to go into the apartments, what is printed on the fob: those are decisions we make on the drawing, and we make them so the owner never has to argue for a setting the agency has already rejected. The second is that the rest of the checklist belongs to the owner and the owner’s lawyer: the application, the notices, the rent questions. We do not file with DHCR, we do not give legal advice, and we tell owners so at the first visit.

The city adds its own layer. HPD’s summary of the Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, says that owners of multiple dwellings who use smart access, which it defines to include key fobs, RFID cards, phone apps and biometrics, must give tenants a written data retention and privacy policy, get express consent in writing or through an app before collecting reference data, collect only a short list of minimum information, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, never sell it, never use it to track a tenant outside the building, and never use it to harass or evict anyone. Owners also may not require a smart access system to get into a tenant’s own apartment. Every Flatbush conversion we design starts from those limits.

How we set up a Flatbush conversion so nothing has to be undone later

  • A key still opens the door. A keyed override stays in the street door, on a restricted keyway, with the keys held by the owner, the super and the fire department’s lockbox arrangement where one exists.
  • Entries only, kept short. The controller records the door and the credential for each entry, not exits, and keeps raw entry events for a period the owner chooses within the law’s limits, then deletes or anonymizes them.
  • Nothing personal on the fob. Fobs are numbered, not named; the number links to a household only inside the administrator account.
  • Every occupant counted. The fob order is sized from the owner’s occupancy records, children included, plus spares for the extra fobs tenants are entitled to ask for.
  • Battery sized for two days. The power supply and battery are chosen to carry the strike, readers and controller for 48 hours, and we test them before we leave.
  • No boxes in the apartments. Wherever possible the system calls tenants’ existing phones, so no equipment has to be fixed inside an apartment; where indoor screens are wanted, they are offered, not imposed.
  • A non-electronic path for those who need one. For tenants who will not use electronic devices on the Sabbath, the plan includes a mechanical route the owner and the tenants agree on, decided by them, not by us.

A Lock Any Key Can Turn: Why We Start at the Cylinder

The most telling detail in the July 2025 News 12 report is not the man in the hallway. It is that the building’s front entrance, freshly repaired, opened with keys from the reporters’ van. A lock that opens with somebody else’s keys is either worn so badly that it barely holds, or it is a common, unrestricted cylinder of the kind sold everywhere, and the key ring in a passing van happens to fit it. Either way, every tenant who ever had a copy cut, every former super and every contractor who borrowed one has the same power as the person who lives there.

Fobs fix that only if the cylinder is fixed too. A fob reader on a street door almost always leaves a mechanical override in the door, and it should, because DHCR has expected one and because fire and power failures happen. If that override is the same weak cylinder, the building has added a reader and kept the hole. So the first thing we do at a Flatbush street door is try it: we look at the cylinder’s make and keyway, we check how far the latch actually throws into the strike, and we close the door slowly to see whether it latches at all. Then we write down how many keys the super thinks exist. The number is never small.

The fix comes in a fixed order. The door has to swing freely and close by itself, which means hinges, a closer that is adjusted and working, and a frame that is square enough for the latch to land. The latch has to engage a strike that is fixed into solid metal or wood, with a guard where a pry bar could reach it. The override cylinder goes onto a restricted keyway, so copies can be cut only with the owner’s authorization, and the old keys stop working the day it changes. Only then does the reader matter, and once it is in, the strike relocks the door every time it closes, whoever forgot to pull it shut.

The Flatbush Gardens tenant who said that “the basic front door isn’t working” was describing the same problem at a bigger scale. When THE CITY reported on the complex’s tax exemption in 2023, it counted nearly 3,000 open housing code violations, and listed missing doors among the basic maintenance failures. No card reader works on a door that is not there. We will happily install readers across a large property, but we price the door work first, and we will tell a managing agent which doors need a carpenter or a new frame before they need us.

The four questions we answer at every Flatbush street door

  • Does it close and latch by itself, every time? If not, the closer, hinges or frame come before any electronics.
  • Who has a key? If nobody can say, the cylinder is changed to a restricted keyway when the strike goes in.
  • What does it do when the power goes? A street door is normally fail-secure, staying locked from outside while the inside handle always opens it.
  • Who finds out when it fails? A door contact reports a door held or forced to the super and the managing agent, not only to an app nobody opens.

Fifty-Nine Buildings, One Management Office: Access Control for Flatbush’s Garden Complexes

The Brooklyn Eagle’s history of Flatbush Gardens describes 59 buildings and about 2,500 apartments, most of them rent-stabilized, bought by Clipper Equity in 2005 for $140 million and renamed from Vanderveer Estates. THE CITY’s 2023 report on the complex’s 40-year tax exemption, worth an estimated $191 million, added that the deal reserved 250 apartments for homeless households as vacancies arise. Whatever one thinks of the owner, the numbers describe an access control problem of a particular shape: dozens of identical street doors spread across one property, thousands of residents, steady turnover, a management office that issues every credential, and a population that includes people for whom a working front door matters more than usual.

A system for a complex like that has to be designed so that a failure stays small. Each building’s doors run from a controller inside that building, with its own battery and its own copy of the credential list, so that if the network link to the office drops, or a server needs to be restarted, the doors in Building 12 keep opening for Building 12’s tenants and nobody else. The office sees all of the buildings at once, issues and cancels fobs, and gets alerts, but no single box anywhere can lock out the whole property. That is the difference between a door system and a door system that becomes the next news story.

The second design rule is that the people who run the system get only the rights their job needs. A leasing agent can issue a fob to a new household and cancel one at move-out. A super can see which doors in his buildings are reporting faults and release a door for a contractor. Security staff can see live door alerts. Very few people should be able to search the entry history, and every search should leave its own record. The Tenant Data Privacy Law limits what can be done with that history; a sensible role structure keeps the building from drifting into uses the law forbids, such as treating entry records as evidence in a dispute with a tenant.

The third rule is that the doors behind the lobby count. In a complex, the package rooms, laundry rooms, compactor rooms, roof bulkheads and cellar doors outnumber the street doors, and they are where strangers who slip in behind a resident spend their time. Readers on those doors, with the same credentials as the street door, let each household reach the laundry and the package room for its own building, while the super and the porters get the service spaces. Doors that should never be propped, such as roof and compactor doors, get contacts that report when they are.

Smaller Flatbush owners face the same questions on a smaller scale. A 40-unit apartment house on Ocean Avenue or a six-family walk-up near the Junction will never issue 2,500 fobs, but it still turns over apartments, still has a super who needs the cellar and the roof, and still has a street door that must close and lock by itself under the Multiple Dwelling Law. The usual first phase there is the street door and the package room, wired into the existing buzzer or replacing it with a video panel, with the cellar and yard doors added once the building is used to the system. The privacy paperwork applies at six units as surely as at thousands.

The Door Behind the Building: Temples, Churches and Side Entrances

Three men forced the back door of the house of worship at 26 Rugby Road at about 2:30 on a Sunday afternoon in February 2024, News 12 reported, while four monks were inside; they took phones and cash and ransacked the space before running off. The detail worth noticing is the time. Most people picture a forced door as something that happens at 3 a.m. at an empty building. This one happened in daylight, at a building where people live, through the door at the back that nobody was watching.

Flatbush has a great many buildings like that: churches with a rectory or a caretaker’s apartment, temples where clergy live upstairs, storefront congregations with an apartment overhead, community centers with an office that stays open late. Their front doors are designed to welcome people at set times. Their back and side doors are usually plain steel doors onto a yard, an alley or a parking area, with a deadbolt somebody is supposed to remember. A good plan treats the two kinds of door completely differently. The front door opens on a schedule for services and closes behind the last person. The back door stays locked from outside all the time, opens from inside with one push for anyone leaving, and reports at once if it is ever forced or held.

The Church Avenue building that BoroPark24 described being broken into three times in 2025 makes the other half of the point: burglars took a side window, a side door and a fence and wall, never the front. Access control is the right tool for doors, and only for doors. On a building like that we look at the side door first, with a heavy-duty strike, a guard over the latch and a contact that sounds an alert if it is forced, and then we say plainly that the window and the fence line need bars, lighting, cameras or an alarm, which we can quote separately. A reader on the front door would not have stopped any of those three entries.

How we set up a place of worship that people also live in

  • Two systems in one building. The residence has its own door and its own credentials, kept apart from the congregation’s schedules and visitor codes.
  • Back doors locked by default. No schedule ever unlocks a back or side door from outside; staff use a fob, deliveries are let in by someone who can see them.
  • Alerts that reach a person. A forced-door or held-door alert goes to the clergy or caretaker on site and to a second phone off site, so it is never one phone in a pocket on silent.
  • A camera that sees the back door. Where there is a camera, it is aimed at the door the system controls, so the alert and the picture describe the same event.
  • Exits stay free. Every door used as a way out opens from inside without a key, code or card, during services and in a power cut.

The city’s Avenue NYC study of Flatbush, carried out by Small Business Services with the Church Avenue BID, the Flatbush Nostrand Junction BID and local merchant groups between October 2021 and June 2022, walked four corridors: Church Avenue from Coney Island Avenue to East 56th Street, Flatbush Avenue from Parkside Avenue to Avenue H, Nostrand Avenue from Parkside Avenue to Avenue J, and Rogers Avenue from Clarkson Avenue to Church Avenue. It found 1,662 storefronts, 79 percent of the merchants it surveyed renting their space, and more than half asking for safety changes. Each corridor has its own kind of door.

Church Avenue

The spine of Little Caribbean, designated in 2017, with Church Avenue co-named Bob Marley Boulevard. Bakeries, groceries, restaurants, money transfer and phone stores, most with apartments above. The usual job is a back or cellar door with staff codes and a residential door kept apart from the shop’s.

Flatbush Avenue

The old main street, from Parkside past Erasmus Hall and the Kings Theatre to the Junction, with its business improvement district dating to 1990. Storefront chains and independents, clinics and offices upstairs. Staff-only stockrooms, upstairs suites with buzz-in entry and roll-down-gate shops with a back door to the yard.

Nostrand Avenue

A long shopping street running south to the Junction and Brooklyn College, with restaurants, salons, pharmacies and medical offices. Separate staff and patient doors, cellar storage reached from the shop floor, and suites shared by several practices.

Rogers Avenue

A shorter corridor between Clarkson and Church Avenues, with storefronts under walk-up apartments. Small shops where the owner works alone and needs to see who is at the back door before opening it.

Cortelyou Road

The restaurant and cafe strip on Flatbush’s Ditmas Park edge, covered by its own Avenue NYC study. Early-morning staff, late closings and kitchens with a door to the yard, where codes that follow the shift matter more than anything else.

The Junction

Where Flatbush and Nostrand Avenues meet beside Brooklyn College and the Flatbush Avenue–Brooklyn College subway terminal, home to some of the district’s largest stores. Delivery doors, staff entrances and offices above that need readers and a record of every opening.

Inside a Flatbush storefront

Most stores on the four avenues are narrow and deep. A glass front behind a roll-down gate, a long selling floor, a stockroom or kitchen at the back, a door to a yard or an alley, and very often a stair or a pair of sidewalk doors down to a cellar. The owner usually rents, the apartments above usually belong to the same landlord, and the cellar is often shared with the building. That layout produces the same three requests again and again: a back door that is locked from outside unless someone with a code opens it, a cellar door that only staff can use, and a way to tell, the next morning, who opened up and when.

What it rarely needs is a reader on the front door. Shops on these avenues live on walk-in trade, and a buzz-in front door is the right answer only for stores that hold high-value stock or cash and can afford to turn away a browser. For everyone else the front stays a normal storefront door with a good lock behind the gate, and the money goes into the back of the store, where break-ins and staff key problems actually happen. Because nearly four in five merchants rent, we also plan for the end of the lease: hardware that can be removed cleanly, and a written note for the landlord of exactly what was fixed to the building.

Rules that apply to Flatbush business doors

  • Egress first. Under the NYC Building Code, an electrically locked door on a path of exit has to release on power failure and when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate, and a magnetic lock needs a release built into the door hardware that works with one hand. Exit doors from stores, offices and halls always open from inside without a key, card or code.
  • Listed equipment. Controllers and electric locks on exit doors should be listed to UL 294 for access control; we fit listed equipment and keep the listings in the job file.
  • Apartments above the store. Under Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a, the street entrances of class A multiple dwellings erected or converted after January 1, 1968 must be self-closing and self-locking, kept locked unless an attendant is on duty, and buildings with eight or more apartments need an intercom with voice and remote release. Older buildings must install them when a majority of tenants request it in writing. A shop cannot put the building’s residential door on a daytime unlock schedule.
  • Roof doors. The same section requires roof doors to be self-closing but not self-locking, openable from inside without a key. A roof door can carry a contact and an alarm, never a lock that traps someone on the stair.
  • Tenant data. Where a building with apartments uses fobs, apps or other smart access for its residential doors, the Tenant Data Privacy Law’s policy, consent and 90-day limits apply. A store’s own staff door is a separate matter, but a door shared with the apartments is not.
  • Licensing. New York State requires a license to install, service or maintain security systems for others. AESS works under license #12000287431.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Flatbush: Questions Answered

Rent-stabilized buildings moving from keys to fobs

One DHCR approval let a building log people coming in but not going out. Can the reader on our Flatbush street door be set up that way?

Yes. Most controllers record an event when a credential is presented at a reader, and a street door with a reader only on the outside and a plain push or lever on the inside already records entries and nothing else. The exit side is a request-to-exit sensor or a button that tells the controller the door is being opened from inside so it does not raise a forced-door alarm; we configure that sensor so it shapes the door alarm without writing a named exit record. DHCR’s July 2016 ruling, as LVT reported it, upheld an approval on exactly that basis: entrances could be recorded, departures could not.

We put the setting in writing in the door plan, so the owner can show it to tenants and, if it comes to that, to the agency. If an owner wants exit readers, for example on a door into a staff area, we explain why that is a different decision on a residential door and leave it to the owner and the owner’s counsel.

Our landlord says the new entry system needs a box fixed inside every apartment. Is that how these systems have to work?

No. A modern video entry panel can call tenants’ own phones, through an app or as an ordinary call, so nothing needs to go inside an apartment for the street door to work. Indoor screens are an option some tenants like, especially older residents who do not want to answer the door through an app, but they are an option. In the 2016 ruling LVT reported, one of the conditions DHCR left in place was that no additional equipment would be installed in tenants’ apartments.

Where a building already has wired handsets in each apartment, the existing handsets can sometimes stay connected to the new panel, and where they cannot, the owner can offer replacements to those who want them. In the 2017 case, where an owner moved to a phone-based intercom, DHCR required intercom service to keep reaching every apartment and ordered a rent reduction to cover the landline it needed. The cheaper path is usually to plan the system around phones tenants already have.

Management wants every tenant to come in and re-register their fobs each year. Is that a reasonable way to keep the list clean?

The conditions LVT reported from the 2016 DHCR ruling had fobs issued from the owner’s file records, with those records verified no more than once a year. A yearly check against the lease files is therefore a pattern the agency has accepted; asking tenants to queue at the office with identification more often than that, or cutting off fobs for anyone who misses a date, is the kind of burden an examiner would question.

The system can do most of the cleaning without the tenants. Each fob belongs to a household and an apartment in the administrator account. When a lease ends, the household’s fobs end with it; when a fob has not been used for months, the office gets a list to check against its records. The once-a-year review then takes an afternoon in the office, not a morning in line for every tenant.

Our building’s fob door keeps locking tenants out, and management won’t give us any backup. What can tenants do?

This is a tenant question rather than an installation one, but we hear it often enough to answer it honestly. Brick Underground put it to a panel in 2018 after a reader described a fob system that failed when the internet went down or it rained, with a landlord who refused a backup. Attorney William Gribben said a backup was needed when the fob fails, such as a staffed door or a conventional lock. Tenant lawyer Sam Himmelstein suggested tenants organize and, if necessary, bring an HP action in Housing Court asking for an order guaranteeing 24-hour access, noting that a hearing typically comes within seven to ten days. A broker on the panel suggested a complaint to HPD through 311.

From our side, a fob door that locks people out in the rain is a door that was installed with the wrong reader, the wrong power supply or no battery, and none of that is hard to fix. We work for owners and managing agents, not for tenants against them, but if your landlord calls us, the first thing we will recommend is the mechanical override that should have been there from the start.

Front doors that open for the wrong person

A TV crew opened our building’s newly repaired front door with keys from their own van. How is that possible, and what fixes it?

It means the cylinder in the door is either badly worn or a common, unrestricted model, so that ordinary keys of the same family will turn it, and that is what News 12 found at a Flatbush building in July 2025. A worn cylinder or a loose latch can also let a door open to a key that only roughly fits, or to a hard pull. Repairing the door without changing the cylinder leaves the same weakness in place.

The fix is a high-quality cylinder on a restricted keyway, so new keys can be cut only with the owner’s authorization, fitted at the same time as an electric strike and a reader so that day-to-day entry moves to fobs. The few remaining keys become the override, held by the owner and the super. We also check the latch throw and add a guard over the latch, because a door that can be slipped or pried does not need a key at all.

A man who doesn’t live here covered our hallway camera with his hand two nights in a row. Can the door system catch what the camera missed?

It can tell you how he got in, which a covered camera cannot. If the street door is on a controlled lock with a door contact, every opening is either a credential, a release from an apartment, an exit from inside, or a forced or held door, and the controller logs which it was. A stranger who walks in behind a resident shows up as one entry with the door held open longer than usual; a stranger with a copied key shows up as an opening with no credential at all, which raises an alert. Paired with a camera aimed at the entrance from a height that a hand cannot reach, that is usually enough to tell management which door and which minute to look at.

Many camera systems can also send an alert when a lens is covered or the picture goes dark, and we turn that on for hallway cameras we install. The point of the door system is to make the camera’s job smaller: if the street door closes and locks every time, far fewer people are standing in the hallway to begin with.

Our building has more than eight apartments. Can a fob reader take the place of the intercom the law requires?

No. Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a requires class A multiple dwellings with eight or more apartments, erected or converted after January 1, 1968, to have an intercom that lets a tenant speak to a visitor at the entrance and release the door remotely, and older buildings must install one when a majority of tenants ask in writing. A fob reader lets residents in; it does nothing for a visitor, a delivery or a home attendant who needs someone upstairs to answer.

That is why most Flatbush apartment houses get both in one device: a video entry panel with a call button or directory for each apartment, a speaker and camera for the visitor, and a fob reader for the residents. It replaces the old buzzer board rather than sitting beside it, and on our Brooklyn list it falls in the $1,500 to $3,000 line for a single entrance.

Complexes, places of worship and stores

Apartments in our complex are being set aside for households coming out of shelters as units open up. Should their credentials be handled any differently?

Not differently from any other tenant, and that is the point. THE CITY reported that the Flatbush Gardens tax deal reserved 250 apartments for homeless households as vacancies arise, and similar set-asides exist across the city. A household that signs a lease is a tenant like every other, with the same fobs, the same number of free extras and the same privacy protections under the Tenant Data Privacy Law. A system that marks those households, or logs their entries more closely, would be both unfair and a misuse of the data the law protects.

What helps is speed. Some new households arrive with a caseworker, a move-in date set by an agency and very little notice. A management office that can issue fobs on the day the lease is signed, rather than at a weekly fob session, means nobody spends their first week buzzing a neighbor to get home.

Men forced the back door of our temple in the middle of a Sunday afternoon while our monks were inside. What should that door be doing in daylight?

Staying locked from outside, all day, every day, and telling someone at once if it is forced. That is what happened at 26 Rugby Road in February 2024, according to News 12, and it is the pattern we plan against at every place of worship that is also a home. The back door gets an electric strike or a heavy-duty lock that relocks whenever it closes, a latch guard, a door contact and a forced-door alert that goes to whoever is in the building and to a second person off site. It always opens from inside with one push.

If people need to come in that way, such as a caretaker, a cook or a delivery, they get their own fob or code with the hours they need, or they ring a station with a camera so someone inside sees them before releasing the door. What the door should never do is rely on someone remembering to turn a deadbolt after the last person goes out to the yard.

Our store’s side door was pried open overnight. Will an electric strike hold better than the deadbolt we had, or worse?

It depends entirely on the strike and how it is fitted. A light-duty strike in a thin frame is weaker than a good deadbolt, because a pry bar can spread the frame and pop the latch. A heavy-duty strike rated for exterior doors, set into a frame we reinforce, with a latch guard plate over the gap and a latch that throws fully into it, holds far better than a deadbolt somebody forgot to turn, and it relocks every time the door shuts. On a side door that has already been attacked, we often keep a deadbolt as well, thrown by the last person out at night, so the door has the strike for daytime traffic and two locks after closing.

The bigger gain is knowing. A door contact on that side door, tied to the alarm or to a phone alert, means the next attempt is reported while it happens. The Church Avenue building BoroPark24 reported in 2025 was entered through a side door once and through a window and a fence twice, so we would look at the whole side of the building, not just the lock.

Guides talk about three types of access control. Which kind goes on a Flatbush walk-up’s street door?

The three types most guides list, discretionary, mandatory and role-based, are ways of deciding who may open what, borrowed from computer security. In a walk-up, the practical answer is a simple form of role-based control: residents can open the street door and their own building’s laundry and package room, the super can open those plus the cellar and the roof, contractors get a code for the days they work, and the owner or managing agent decides. Nobody in the building sets their own rules for a door, which is what “discretionary” would mean.

The choice that matters more is the hardware type. A standalone keypad or reader with its user list inside the device suits a single door. A wired controller in the cellar with several doors on it suits most walk-ups and apartment houses. A cloud-managed system suits owners who run several buildings from one office. All three can follow the same rules; they differ in how many doors they serve and how they are managed.

Types of Access Control We Install in Flatbush

Electric strikes and electrified exit hardware

The part that releases the latch. Heavy-duty fail-secure strikes for street and back doors, strikes and panic bars made for the narrow metal-and-glass doors of interwar lobbies, and electrified exit devices where a door must always open with one push.

Restricted-keyway override cylinders

The mechanical key that stays in the door for emergencies and power failures, on a keyway whose copies can be cut only with the owner’s authorization, so the override is not the weak point.

Encrypted fob and card readers

Weatherproof readers for street doors, cellar doors and yard gates, using encrypted credentials. The old 125 kHz fobs still common in older buildings can be copied with an inexpensive device; encrypted fobs cannot be copied that way.

Video entry panels

One-button stations for houses, keypad stations for walk-ups and touchscreen directories for large lobbies, each with a camera, a speaker and a reader, calling tenants’ phones or indoor screens.

Door contacts and request-to-exit devices

Contacts that report a door forced or held open, and exit sensors or buttons that tell the controller someone is leaving, set so the system records entries without logging tenants as they go out.

Controllers, batteries and management

Controllers that keep each building’s doors working with no network at all, batteries sized to carry a door for two days, and management from a browser or app with separate roles for owners, managers and supers.

National alarm companies, DIY kits and a local installer

Plenty of Flatbush households already have a doorbell camera from a big-box store, and plenty of small businesses pay a national alarm company every month. Those products do one job each, and for a house that may be enough. Where they fall short is a building’s shared door: a doorbell camera shows who is outside but does not decide who gets in, a battery smart lock bought online usually works only from one brand’s app, and neither one gives a managing agent a way to issue fobs to forty households, cancel one at move-out, or prove to a housing examiner what the system records.

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Flatbush

These are generalizations we hear repeated in quick online answers, consumer articles and national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each is set against what a Flatbush door actually needs. We do not attribute any specific claim or figure to any one of those sources.

“Putting fobs on the front door makes a building secure.”

A fob reader controls who can open a door with a fob. It does nothing about the cylinder beside it, the closer above it or the latch that has to drop into the strike.

The Flatbush building News 12 visited in July 2025 had just had its front entrance repaired, and it still opened with keys from the station’s van. A reader fitted to that door would have left the same weakness sitting a few inches away.

We price and fix the door, the cylinder and the closer before the reader, and we tell owners when the door itself has to be replaced.

“A landlord can switch the building from keys to fobs whenever it likes.”

General answers describe fob conversions as an owner’s decision. In a rent-stabilized building, which describes a large share of Flatbush’s rental housing, the state housing agency treats it as a change in a building-wide service.

DHCR rulings reported by LVT have approved conversions on conditions: metal keys still opening the building in an emergency, free fobs for every occupant, a cap on replacement fees, no recording of tenants’ identification, a long battery backup and accommodation for Sabbath observers.

We design the system to meet those conditions from the start, and we tell owners that the application itself is a matter for them and their lawyers.

“A fob system keeps a full record of everyone who comes and goes.”

Many guides sell access control on its records. In a New York City apartment building, the Tenant Data Privacy Law limits what a smart-access system may collect, requires authentication data to be destroyed within 90 days unless anonymized, and bars using the data to harass or evict tenants.

DHCR’s 2016 ruling, as reported, went further on one point: the system could record entrances but not departures.

On residential doors we set systems to record entries only and to keep them briefly, and we hand the owner the settings in writing.

“Hallway cameras do the same job.”

Cameras are often presented as the alternative to door control. They record; they do not decide who gets in.

In the July 2025 News 12 report, a man who did not live in the building walked up to a hallway camera and covered it with his hand, two nights running. By then he was already inside.

The useful order is a door that locks every time, a door contact that reports when it is held or forced, and then a camera aimed at that door from somewhere a hand cannot reach.

“Access control costs a few hundred dollars a door.”

National guides often quote the shelf price of a keypad lock and call it a door. That is the price of one part.

On our Brooklyn list, a controlled door with a reader or keypad, an electric strike, the cable, a programmed controller and ten fobs is $1,500 to $2,500. A video panel replacing a buzzer board is $1,500 to $3,000, and several doors on one cloud-managed system is $4,000 to $15,000.

Flatbush pays the Brooklyn base list, with no surcharge for an old building, a rent-stabilized building or a large complex.

“Access control is about the front door.”

Most quick answers picture one entrance. Flatbush’s reporting points elsewhere.

The 2024 break-in at the house of worship on Rugby Road came through the back door on a Sunday afternoon, and the Church Avenue building BoroPark24 described in 2025 was entered through a side window, a side door and over a fence, never the front.

We walk the back and sides of every building first, put the doors we can control on the system, and say plainly which windows and fences need something other than access control.

“A smart lock stops working when the internet goes down.”

This one is true of badly designed systems, and the complaint is real: Brick Underground’s 2018 reader described a fob system that failed whenever the internet dropped or it rained.

A properly designed door does not depend on the internet at all. The controller holds its own credential list and opens the door for valid fobs whether or not it can reach the cloud, and the battery carries it through a power cut.

We test every door with the network unplugged and the power off before we leave the building, and the mechanical override is there for anything else.

How It Works

Our Installation Process

The same eight steps cover a two-family house off Rogers Avenue and a complex of six-story buildings. What changes in Flatbush is the weight given to steps four and six: the doors and cylinders are fixed before anything electronic goes on, and the record settings are agreed with the owner in writing before the first fob is handed out.

1. Photos and a short call

You send a photo of each door from outside and inside, tell us how many apartments or staff use it, who holds keys today, and whether the building is rent-stabilized.

2. Walk the doors with the super

A technician checks every entrance with whoever holds the keys: street door, vestibule, cellar, yard, roof bulkhead, package and laundry rooms, plus the cable route, the power source and the old buzzer wiring.

3. Written door plan and Brooklyn price

A door-by-door plan naming the hardware, what each door does in a power cut and a fire alarm, which credentials each household gets and what the system records, priced from the Brooklyn list.

4. Make every door close and latch

Before electronics, each door is adjusted or repaired to close and latch on its own, and the cylinder is tested and, where needed, changed so a common key cannot open it.

5. Cable, controller and power

Controller and battery in a locked cellar spot or closet, cable along the cellar ceiling or up an existing chase to each door, power from the owner’s circuit, labeled at the panel.

6. Credentials and records set up

Fobs, codes and phone keys issued household by household from the owner’s records, numbered not named, with entry-only logging and retention settings the owner has signed off.

7. Test on battery and with power off

Every door tested on battery, with the network unplugged and with all power removed, to prove the inside handle or exit device always opens it and the override key works.

8. Hand over and follow up

Administrator accounts for the owner or managing agent, a one-page guide for the super and the tenants, and a follow-up after the first weeks to adjust closers, schedules and alerts.

Twice a year: a five-minute door check for the super

When the clocks change, open the street door and let it go: does it close and latch on its own, without a push? Do the same at the cellar, package room and yard doors. Look at the battery status in the management app before the first cold week. Ask the office for the list of fobs that have not been used in months and check it against the rent roll. If anything sticks, drags or hums without releasing, a call now costs less than a lockout on a January night.

What moves a Flatbush price up or down

  • The door itself. A sound door that closes on its own takes a strike quickly; a sagging steel door, a bent frame or a missing closer has to be corrected first and adds labor.
  • The cylinder. Changing an unrestricted override to a restricted keyway adds a modest parts cost and saves the building from paying for the same weakness twice.
  • The cable route. An open cellar under the lobby makes the run short; a lobby over a slab, a finished ceiling or a long trip to a rear yard door adds time.
  • The old buzzer. A panel that replaces the buzzer board and calls tenants’ phones costs less than one that has to keep feeding handsets in every apartment.
  • How many buildings. In a complex, each building needs its own controller and battery, but the design, programming and training are shared, which brings the cost per door down.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Try a Stranger’s Key Before Choosing a Reader

This describes how we survey a Flatbush street door, not a particular customer. Before we talk about fobs or panels, we ask the super for the building’s key and then try the door with keys that should not open it: a spare blank of the same common family, the key to our own shop door. If either one turns the cylinder, or if a firm pull opens the door without any key at all, the building’s first problem is not technology. We write that down at the top of the door plan, ahead of any reader.

Then we stand inside the vestibule and let the door close by itself three times. We watch whether it latches on the first try, whether the latch actually drops into the strike or rides on its edge, and how long it hangs open. We ask the super how many street door keys exist, and we ask the managing agent the same question separately. The two answers almost never match. Only after that do we look at the buzzer board, the cable in the cellar and the power, because a reader on a door that does not latch, or beside a cylinder anyone can open, protects nobody.

FAQ

Flatbush Access Control FAQ

What does it cost to swap a Flatbush walk-up’s old buzzer board for a video panel with a fob reader?
+

On our Brooklyn list a video intercom panel with fob access that replaces a buzzer runs $1,500 to $3,000 for one entrance. The lower end covers a small walk-up where the panel calls tenants’ phones and the cable route from the cellar is short. A large directory panel, a second door in a vestibule or keeping wired handsets working in every apartment moves it up. Sales tax is added, and the written figure is fixed after the visit.

Should a tenant’s name or apartment number be printed on their fob?
+

No. A fob that carries a name, an apartment number or an address tells anyone who finds it on the sidewalk which door it opens and whose home is behind it. In the 2016 conversion ruling LVT reported, DHCR left in place a condition that a tenant’s name, address and photograph could not appear on the fob. We issue numbered fobs, and the link between a number and a household exists only inside the administrator account.

Can the entry panel list apartment numbers instead of tenants’ names?
+

Yes, and in most Flatbush apartment houses we recommend it. Visitors dial an apartment number, or choose it from a directory that shows numbers or initials only, and the panel calls whoever the household has set up to answer. Names can be added for households that want them. A directory of numbers keeps tenants’ names off a public sidewalk and saves the office from updating the panel every time someone moves.

Do the fobs have batteries that wear out?
+

No. Ordinary access fobs and cards are passive: they draw a tiny amount of power from the reader’s field when they are held close to it, and they have no battery to replace. They do wear out physically, usually from a cracked case or years on a key ring, and a fob that has been through the wash may stop working. A replacement is programmed in the administrator account in a minute.

How close does a fob have to be to the reader, and why not farther?
+

Usually within an inch or two, a deliberate short range. A reader that opened the door for any fob within a few feet would unlock the street door for a resident simply walking past it, or for someone standing behind them. Long-range readers exist for gates and garages, where a driver stays in the car, but on a pedestrian door a short, deliberate tap is what keeps the door locked until someone means to open it.

Our entrance faces the afternoon sun. Will the panel’s camera show visitors as dark shapes?
+

It can, with a cheap camera. When a bright street sits behind a visitor, a basic camera exposes for the sky and turns the face into a silhouette. We choose panels with wide dynamic range for entrances like that, mount the camera at face height rather than above the door, and check the picture on the site visit at the time of day the sun is worst. A small hood or a change of position sometimes helps as much as the camera.

What information about each tenant actually goes into a fob system?
+

Very little, if it is set up properly. HPD’s summary of the Tenant Data Privacy Law describes a short list of minimum data, such as the tenant’s name, apartment and the doors they can use, a preferred way to be contacted, and lease move-in and move-out dates, with access times recorded only for security. We set up the system to hold that and no more. Identification documents, Social Security numbers and photographs do not need to be in it.

If a tenant reports a fob lost and then finds it, can it be switched back on?
+

Yes. On the systems we install, a lost fob is suspended rather than deleted, so it stops opening every door at once but stays in the account. If it turns up in a coat pocket a week later, the office can switch it back on instead of issuing and paying for a new one. A fob that has been suspended for a long time, or that was reported stolen rather than lost, is usually better replaced.

Can the reader show a tenant why their fob was refused, so they know to call the office?
+

Partly. Most readers give one signal for an accepted fob and another for a refused one, and many panels can show a short message on screen. We set the refused signal clearly and post the office’s number near the door, so a tenant whose fob has expired or been suspended by mistake knows to call rather than wait for a neighbor. The reader does not explain the reason on the sidewalk, which would tell a stranger too much.

Does every controlled door need its own controller?
+

No. A single controller in the cellar commonly runs two to four doors, and it can be expanded, so a walk-up’s street door, package room, cellar and yard door often share one. Separate controllers make sense when doors are in different buildings, when a store and the apartments above it should not share equipment, or when a door is so far away that a second controller near it is simpler than a long cable run.

Who in a Flatbush building should be able to cancel a fob late at night?
+

At least two people, and not only the office. A fob lost on the way home at eleven at night should be switched off then, not the next morning, so we give the super or an on-call manager the right to suspend credentials from a phone, alongside the managing agent. Suspending a fob is a low-risk action; issuing new ones and reading entry records can stay with fewer people in the office.
