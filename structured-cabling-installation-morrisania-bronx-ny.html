The Neighborhood

Why Morrisania Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Structured cabling installation in Morrisania comes down to two problems that most cost guides never mention: getting a clean, tested path through buildings from five or six different eras, and putting the network equipment somewhere that only the building can reach. The neighborhood's housing runs, in one study guide's words, from older row houses to modern apartment complexes. A real-estate firm that works here describes pre-war and newer multifamily buildings, many rent-stabilized or income-restricted, side by side with mixed-use buildings that put stores or community spaces under apartments. Wikipedia counts twenty NYCHA developments within Morrisania and notes the subsidized attached townhouses and new apartment buildings that have been going up on vacant lots across the neighborhood for decades. Each of those types was wired, if it was wired at all, by a different generation of telephone and cable installers, and very few of them have a single documented path from where service enters to every apartment, office or store that needs it.

The layers start early. The Gotham Center calls the Village of Morrisania, founded in 1848, the first area west of the Bronx River to be densely settled in what is now the Bronx. Kiddle's history traces the village to land Gouverneur Morris Jr. sold beside the railroad he had allowed across the family's estate, and records the area joining New York City in 1874 and the Third Avenue Elevated reaching it in 1887. UrbanAreas.net notes that by the time the subway arrived in 1904, tenements had replaced houses as the main form of housing, and CityNeighborhoods.nyc describes Morrisania by the early 1900s as fully urbanized: brick tenements, schoolhouses, synagogues and corner stores. The Morris High School Historic District, with its brick row houses built mostly between 1900 and 1904, survives from that period. So does much of the walk-up stock along streets like Boston Road and Franklin Avenue. These are buildings of brick bearing walls, plaster, wooden floors and cellars where every utility has entered for over a century, and every one of them has had telephone, then cable television, then internet threaded through after the fact.

Then came the losses. The Neighborhoods newsletter reports that arson and disinvestment in the late 1960s and 1970s destroyed 40 percent of Morrisania's housing stock and that more than half the population left. CityNeighborhoods.nyc calls Morrisania one of the epicenters of the South Bronx's devastation, and credits groups such as Banana Kelly, Phipps Houses and the Mid-Bronx Desperadoes with leading the reclaiming of abandoned buildings and the rebuilding of affordable housing. The city's cultural inventory for Morrisania puts the result in numbers: since 1990, about 8,500 new housing units have been built here and another 9,000 preserved. For a cabling contractor, that history explains what we expect to find behind the walls. A building gut-rehabbed in the 1980s was wired for one telephone line and a cable TV drop per apartment. A townhouse built on a vacant lot in the 1990s or 2000s may have had a phone jack in the kitchen and coax in the living room. A building finished in the last few years may have a real telecom room and conduit to every floor. And the buildings preserved rather than rebuilt still carry their pre-war walls with decades of surface cable on top.

The public housing that defines much of Morrisania's skyline came in a separate wave. Wikipedia dates the completion of Forest Houses, fifteen buildings of 9, 10 and 14 stories, to November 12, 1956, and Butler Houses, six 21-story buildings, to December 1964, part of the four contiguous developments known as Claremont Village along with the Morris, Morrisania and Webster houses. Morrisania Air Rights, completed in 1980, was built on the air rights above Metro-North's tracks. Many of these developments already have a building-wide internet program: the Mayor's office describes Big Apple Connect as a free bundle of in-home internet with a modem and router, basic cable and common-area Wi-Fi hotspots, and Optimum lists Morrisania, Forest and Morrisania Air Rights among its participating Bronx properties. NYCHA's own capital work runs through NYCHA's processes and contractors, so this page is written for the privately owned, nonprofit-sponsored and newly built buildings around those campuses, and for the stores, programs and businesses that sit among them. Those owners do not get a citywide program. If their tenants or staff are to have a wired connection, the building has to provide the path.

The need is real. The New York State Comptroller's 2025 report says the Bronx, where more than one in three households has no broadband access, has the highest share of any borough. BroadbandNow observes that Verizon Fios has less coverage in the West and South Bronx and that a household in Morrisania probably has access to Optimum. We do not take a side between providers, and coverage changes building by building. But whichever company serves an address, its line stops at the building's edge or in the cellar. From there to apartment 5C, to the back office of a Third Avenue store or to the second-floor classroom of a community program, the connection travels on whatever cable the building has, or does not have. That inside path is what structured cabling is.

There is a second reason Morrisania owners call us, and it is less about speed than about control. News 12 Bronx reported in May 2026 on a building at 1208 Franklin Avenue taken over by squatters, with 311 reports of trespassing dating back to June 2025 and neighbors saying it had been going on far longer. Tenants at 530 East 169th Street told News 12 about broken elevators, missing heat and hot water and the building's lack of security, and the city confirmed multiple violations there. Tenants in other Franklin Avenue buildings described people smoking and using drugs in stairwells and common areas. We are not drawing conclusions about any of those buildings' networks. But stories like these describe exactly the conditions in which a router on a cellar shelf, a switch in an unlocked stair closet or a bundle of cable taped along a hallway ceiling gets unplugged, cut or carried away. In Morrisania, the question of where the network equipment lives and who can reach it is part of the cabling design, not an afterthought.

Where the Network Lives

Locked Closets in Buildings Where the Doors Do Not Always Hold: Placing Network Equipment in Morrisania

Most structured cabling guides assume the building has a telecom room that only staff can enter. Many Morrisania buildings do not. In a pre-war walk-up, the provider's line usually comes in at the cellar, and the router ends up wherever the installer could find an outlet: on a shelf beside the boiler, on the wall of the stair hall under the first flight, or on top of the meter bank. In a 1980s rehab, there may be a small closet off the lobby that also holds the intercom power supply and a mop bucket. In a newer building, there is usually a proper room, but its key may have been copied for every contractor who has passed through. When the street door does not always latch or the cellar door is propped open, every one of those locations is reachable by people who should not be there. Our first deliverable on a Morrisania building is therefore not a cable count. It is an agreement about where the equipment will live, how it will be locked and who will hold the key.

Two locks, one log

Where a building cannot guarantee that the room itself stays locked, we put the equipment in a lockable steel cabinet inside that room. That gives two barriers instead of one, and the inner key can be held by fewer people than the room key. We do not install locks on building doors ourselves when that is the owner's locksmith's work, but the plan names the door, the lock that is needed and who is responsible for it. We also write down, in the handover file, every person the owner has told us holds a key to the cabinet. That list is the first thing the owner will want if a cable is ever found unplugged. It sounds like paperwork, and it is, but in a building where contractors, providers, supers and managing agents have come and gone, knowing who can open the box is half the battle.

Keep the riser out of reach, not just out of sight

The cable that carries the network from the cellar cabinet to the upper floors is the building's backbone. If it runs along the stair-hall ceiling, taped or stapled, it can be pulled down by anyone with a long enough reach, and a single cut takes several floors offline at once. We route risers where they are hardest to reach: inside stacked closets, inside a chase where one exists, or inside steel conduit or a metal raceway fixed to the wall high above the stair, where a painted metal channel is far harder to damage than a bare cable. Every place the riser crosses a floor gets a listed firestop seal. And where the building's layout allows it, we give the top floors a second path, so one damaged section does not cut off everyone above it.

Power, heat and water in the cellar

Cellars in pre-war buildings are often warm near the boiler and damp along the foundation walls. Network equipment tolerates neither well. We mount cabinets well off the floor, on an interior wall rather than against damp masonry where possible, and away from the boiler's heat and any overhead pipes that could drip. The battery backup is sized to the actual load in the cabinet, so the router, switch and any camera recorder ride through a short outage. If the cabinet needs a new receptacle or circuit, that work goes to a licensed electrician and our quote says so in writing.

Building equipment and tenant equipment are not the same thing

A building's own systems, such as cameras, entry panels, a super's computer or a security office, belong on the building's network, on the building's internet account and in the building's locked cabinet. A tenant's router belongs in the tenant's apartment. A store's register network belongs to the store. When those get mixed together on a single shelf, a billing change or a provider swap for one party can knock out the others, and nobody knows whose cable is whose. We plan separate cables, separate panel ports and separate network segments for each, and we label them so a provider's technician can tell at a glance which ones are theirs to touch.

Amenity rooms are where the drop count grows

An apartment needs one or two jacks. A building with a community room, a library lounge, a fitness room and a shared kitchen can need as many cables in those rooms as in a whole floor of apartments. A community room wants a drop behind the wall where a screen will hang, a drop for a computer or a sign-in station, and a ceiling access point. A library lounge wants an access point and a couple of desk drops for shared computers or a printer. A fitness room wants an access point and a drop behind any screen or equipment console the operator plans to connect. A shared kitchen may need nothing but Wi-Fi, or a drop for a reservation tablet. The building's cameras and any card reader on these rooms' doors need their own runs. Nobody lists all of this on the first set of drawings, so on a new building our first conversation is with the operator about how each room will actually be used, then with the architect about where each cable can go.

Mixed-use means two networks in one building

When apartments sit over stores, as they will at 3565 Third Avenue and 1119 Boston Road, the building ends up with at least two groups of users who should never share a cable plant. The residential side needs a riser to every floor and a building-controlled closet. The commercial side needs its own path from where service enters to each store's back room, so a store can bring in its own provider and its own equipment without entering the residential closet. We draw both paths on the same sleeve and pathway plan, with spare sleeves at each crossing for systems nobody has chosen yet, and we keep the commercial tenants' racks physically apart from the building's.

Airtight buildings and the holes cable makes

StreetEasy describes 3365 Third Avenue as the Bronx's first completed passive house. Passive House buildings are built with airtight envelopes, and it follows, though the source does not say so, that every cable entry through that envelope has to be planned rather than drilled on the day. Our approach on any high-performance building is to agree with the architect where low-voltage service enters the building, to use a sleeve or entry fitting the design team has approved for sealing, and to keep penetrations through the envelope to the minimum by bringing all cable in at one point and distributing it inside. Holes through interior rated walls and floors still get listed firestop seals in the usual way.

The security office is the building's hub

Several of the new buildings list a security office or security as an amenity. That room tends to collect everything: the camera monitors, the door station, the computer staff use to log visitors, sometimes a phone. Each needs a wired connection, and behind them, cable has to reach every camera, the entry panel and every reader, all landing in a closet the operator controls. We plan that office and its closet together, on the operator's own network segment and battery backup, so none of it depends on a resident's account. The camera, entry and access hardware itself is licensed security work; we design the cabling so those systems arrive at a clean, tested pathway.

Closets and pathways in buildings with access problems

Trespassers have been reported in our walk-up's stairwells and cellar. Where should the building's network cabinet go so nobody can unplug it or carry it off?

Into a space the building controls, inside a locked steel cabinet, mounted high on a wall. The best location is usually a cellar room with its own lockable door, close to where the provider's line enters so the run is short. If no such room exists, the next choice is a closet off the lobby or stair hall that can be locked, with the cabinet locked again inside it. A super's office can work, but only with the owner's written agreement and a documented second key holder, so the network does not depend on one person. What we avoid is any open shelf, any spot within reach of a passerby and any place below a pipe or near the boiler. The cabinet's battery backup lives inside it, and the cable enters from above or behind so nothing hangs loose to grab. The handover file lists every key holder the owner names, which is the first thing anyone needs if equipment ever goes missing or a cable turns up unplugged.

Can the cable between the cellar cabinet and the upper floors be protected so a damaged run in a stairwell does not cut off the whole building?

Yes, in two ways. First, by routing: we look for a vertical path that keeps the riser out of reach, such as stacked closets, a chase or a disused shaft that can be used safely, before accepting the stair hall. When the stair hall is the only route, the cable goes inside steel conduit or a metal raceway fixed high on the wall above the handrail, not taped to the ceiling, and every floor crossing is sealed with a listed firestop product. Second, by design: in a larger building, we can split the riser so upper and lower floors are fed by separate cables, or add a second path to the top floors, so damage to one section affects fewer apartments. Both options are priced as separate lines so the owner can weigh the cost against how often the stairwell has been a problem. Either way, every riser cable is tested end to end and labeled, so a damaged one can be found quickly.

Our building just added a security guard post. Should the guard's station and the network equipment sit in the same room?

Usually not. The guard needs a monitor, a door station and perhaps a computer at the post, and each of those needs a wired connection. But the cabinet holding the switch, router and camera recorder is better kept in a separate locked room that the guard can reach but that visitors walking past the post cannot. If the post is the only staffed point in the building, putting the cabinet within the guard's line of sight can make sense, provided it is still locked and still out of reach of anyone at the counter. We run the cables for the post from the cabinet on the building's own network segment, with battery backup sized for the cameras, so the post keeps working through a short power dip. If the guard service changes, the cabinet keys stay with the owner, not the vendor.

Our new building is being built to Passive House standards. How does cable enter without breaking the airtight envelope?

By planning the entry with the design team rather than drilling it on site. The developer's low-voltage contractor finished our building and moved on. How do we confirm that what they left is tested and documented?

If any of those are missing, we can audit the building: test a sample or every drop, compare labels to the actual runs, open the closet and note what is installed, and write up what is complete and what is not. That gives the owner or managing agent something concrete to take back to the developer or to file with the building's records. FAQ

Morrisania Structured Cabling Installation FAQ

Will drilling or coring set off the building's smoke detectors?
+

Dust from drilling and coring can reach nearby detectors, so we plan for it. We use dust control at the drill, work one penetration at a time, and ask the building, and its fire alarm company where there is a building system, how detectors near the work should be handled. We never disable or tamper with fire alarm devices ourselves; that decision belongs to the building and its fire alarm contractor.

How do you carry cable and equipment up when the elevator is out of service?
+

By the stairs, and we plan for it. If you tell us when you book that the elevator is down or unreliable, we stage materials on the ground floor, carry up only what each floor needs, and allow extra time in the schedule. A long elevator outage can change the order of work, so we confirm the elevator's status the day before the visit.

Can you give the security desk a list of who will arrive each day?
+

Yes. Before the job starts we send the owner or managing agent the names of the technicians expected, the days and hours of work, and a contact number. That can be passed to the guard or front desk so the crew is checked in like any approved contractor, and residents can be told who to expect.

Can the network cabinet itself be watched or alarmed so staff know if it is opened?
+

Yes. A camera aimed at the cabinet door can record to the building's own recorder, and a door contact on the cabinet can be tied into an alarm or access system so opening it is logged. The cabling for both is simple to include in the plan. The camera and alarm devices themselves are licensed security work, which we quote alongside the cable.

If a tenant damages the jack in their apartment, is the repair covered?
+

Physical damage is excluded from our product warranty, along with the other exclusions in our written warranty terms. A damaged jack is usually a quick repair: the cable is tested, the jack is replaced and re-terminated, and the drop is tested again. We quote that repair in writing before doing it.

Our property backs onto the Metro-North tracks. Does any of your work happen on railroad land?
+

No. Our work stays on and inside the owner's property. We do not attach anything to railroad structures or fences, and if a route would require access to land that is not the owner's, we find another route. Any exterior run stays on the building's own walls, in outdoor-rated cable and protective conduit.

Do you take away the cable boxes, scraps and packaging at the end of each day?
+

Yes. Empty cable boxes, offcuts, packaging and drilling debris leave with the crew rather than going into the building's trash area. Materials that stay on site overnight for a multi-day job are stored in a space the owner agrees to, ideally locked, so nothing is left in a hallway or open cellar.

Can cable reach a room by running under a carpeted floor in a row house?
+

Sometimes along the edge, rarely across the middle. Flat cable under carpet wears and gets damaged where people walk. The better routes are usually along the baseboard behind the carpet edge, up from the cellar ceiling into the wall, or through a closet. We look at the floor and the rooms below on the walk-through and suggest the route that will last.

Will new data cable interfere with the TV service already in each apartment?
+

No. Network cable and the coaxial cable most TV services use are separate systems, and running new data cable does not affect a tenant's TV signal. We identify existing coax and phone lines before working near them so nothing live is disturbed, and we leave any provider's equipment exactly as we found it.

Do you photograph walls and ceilings before starting, in case of a later damage question?
+

Yes. We photograph the areas where we will work before we start, including existing cracks, stains and old holes, and photograph the finished work and every sealed penetration afterward. Those photos go into the handover file, which protects the owner, the tenants and us if a question comes up later.

How long should we keep the test results and port map after the job?
+

For as long as the cable is in the building. The test file is the baseline every future troubleshooting visit compares against, and the port map is what lets a new super or provider find the right cable. Keep the digital copy with the building's permanent records, not just with the current super or agent, and keep the printed map inside the cabinet.

Do the new jacks need a separate power outlet beside them?
+

A data jack does not need power. Access points and cameras usually draw power over the data cable from the switch, so they need no outlet either. The cabinet or rack does need a receptacle for its switch, router and battery backup; if one is not already there, that is a job for a licensed electrician and our quote says so.
