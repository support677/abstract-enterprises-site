The Neighborhood

Why Putnam County Homes Need Sonos Installation

A Putnam Sonos job is measured in feet of lawn as much as square feet of living room. In Manhattan the hard part is the ceiling. In Putnam it is usually the distance: from the router in the basement to the deck, from the deck down the slope to the dock, from the house across the driveway to a garage that has become a gym or a studio. The county's own hazard mitigation plan describes nine municipalities spread over more than 240 square miles, and notes that Philipstown, Putnam Valley and the western part of Kent have the most rugged terrain, with many peaks above 1,000 feet. Houses on that ground sit back from the road, down long drives, on lots where the best part of the property is a long way from the electronics.

Then there are the lakes. Lake Mahopac, which the hamlet of Mahopac encircles, covers 587 acres and has three privately owned islands. It was a summer resort reached by rail from New York City in the nineteenth century, and became a year-round community after the last Putnam Division passenger train to Mahopac ran in 1959. Lake Oscawana, which the Town of Putnam Valley calls the largest of its lakes and ponds, is about 364 acres, roughly two miles long and half a mile wide, according to its lake association, with about seven miles of shoreline. And from 1928 the Smadbeck brothers, working with McGolrick Realty, laid out three planned summer communities around man-made lakes: Lake Carmel in Kent, Lake Peekskill in Putnam Valley and Putnam Lake in Patterson. The Patterson historical society's account of Putnam Lake records 20-by-100-foot lots sold for $50, with room for little more than a bungalow and an outhouse, and a community that was largely empty after Labor Day.

Those two kinds of lake house make two very different Sonos jobs. A lakefront house on Mahopac or Oscawana usually wants a soundbar in a living room with a wall of windows, a pair of speakers on the deck and, more and more often, music on the dock itself, a hundred feet or more from the nearest Amp. A winterized bungalow on a narrow Putnam Lake or Lake Carmel lot wants the opposite: small rooms, low ceilings, rooms added one at a time over decades, and speakers that fit a house never built to hide wire. A poster on a weekend-house thread in r/upstate_new_york described Lake Peekskill as having been “mostly converted summer bungalows on very small lots”, and that matches what we expect to find when we walk in.

Weather is the third part of the plan. Putnam's lines run through woods and along ridge roads, and storms take them down. On April 14, 2020, the day after a wind storm, NYSEG reported 3,800 customers still without power in its Brewster division, 2,700 of them in Putnam County. On Christmas Day that year, NYSEG said winds gusting to 71 mph had cut power to 27,375 customers across the same division, which covers Dutchess, Putnam and Westchester. On the Hudson side, The Journal News reported more than 700 Central Hudson customers in Cold Spring and Garrison without power on August 3, 2026, with Route 9D closed by flooding in Garrison. A Sonos system that lives at the end of those lines has to be built to ride through outages, not just to sound good on a calm evening.

Finally there are the people who own the houses. The Census Bureau counts 38,713 housing units in Putnam and puts the owner-occupied rate at 83.8 percent, so the person who calls us is almost always the person who decides where a cable may go. The American Community Survey counts 1,757 homes in the county as held for seasonal, recreational or occasional use, which is the census's term for second homes. And while 94.8 percent of Putnam households have a broadband subscription, New York's ConnectALL program announced in September 2025 an award of about $11.5 million to Comcast to bring fiber to 390 Putnam County locations that still lacked it. The internet is usually there; whether it is fast, and whether it reaches the far end of the property, is the question we answer on every visit.

Cold Spring and Garrison, on the Hudson, are the contrast. The Cold Spring Historic District is described as one of the best-preserved nineteenth-century townscapes in the Hudson River region, with most of its houses built before the Civil War, when the nearby West Point Foundry was at its height. Village houses there sit close together on small lots, the Village's Historic District Review Board reviews exterior changes that can be seen from the street, and a Sonos plan is much more about thick old walls and quiet neighbours than about a dock two hundred feet away.

Building by building: what each Putnam house type does to the wiring

Lake bungalows. Most have no attic worth the name and no basement to run cable through, so ceiling speakers depend on what the crawlspace allows. Where an addition meets the original cottage, there is often an old outside wall still inside the house, with whatever siding or sheathing it had when it was the exterior. Wi-Fi that reaches the kitchen can fade past that wall. We test signal in each room before recommending anything, and lean on wireless speakers on brackets, with a wired access point placed to cover the newer part of the house.

Lakefront houses. The walk-out lower level facing the water is usually the best home for an Amp that drives the deck and dock speakers: it is closer to the outdoor runs, it is heated, and the wall facing the lake is often already penetrated for outdoor lights and outlets. We keep the Amp on a ventilated shelf rather than the floor, because Sonos rates it to operate between 32 and 104 degrees Fahrenheit, and run outdoor cable through a single sealed entry.

Commuter houses. These are the most predictable. Drywall on wood studs, accessible attics over the bedrooms, unfinished areas over the garage. The challenge is usually that the router is at one end of a long house and the family room is at the other, a half-flight down. One wired Sonos product or a wired access point near the family room is often the whole fix.

Houses on long drives. Here the house is the hub for several buildings. We plan the indoor system first, then the links outward: the garage, the shed, the guest space. Each link is chosen by distance and by whether the outbuilding has its own electrical service. Where the internet itself is slow, we explain which music sources depend on it and which, such as a turntable on a line-in, do not.

Village houses. Thick walls stop Wi-Fi in its tracks, and Sonos's own interference guide rates plaster high and brick medium for signal loss. A wired access point on each floor, reached through a closet or a stair wall, usually does more than any speaker upgrade. Outdoor speakers go on the garden side of the house, if anywhere, and levels are set with you, because the next house is close.

Finishing a lower level or adding on to a lake cottage? Run the speaker and network cable while the studs are open. Our structured cabling installers in Putnam County pre-wire for Sonos and leave each run labelled and tested.

From the House to the Water

Planning Sonos Across a Putnam Lake Property

Every lake-house plan we write starts with a tape measure, not a product list. We walk from the place the Amp will live to each spot that wants music — the deck rail, the bottom of the lawn, the end of the dock, the boathouse door — and write down the distance. Those numbers decide the cable, where the Amps go and whether a second one is needed closer to the water.

Sonos publishes its own chart for this. For the distance between a Sonos Amp and its speakers, it recommends 16-gauge wire for runs under 80 feet, 14-gauge from 80 to 200 feet and 12-gauge from 200 to 300 feet, and says runs over 300 feet are not recommended. Thinner wire over a long run wastes power as heat and takes the punch out of the bass; on a lake property the long runs are exactly the ones that matter.

Getting the network to the bottom of the property

A second Amp in a boathouse or garage only helps if the network reaches it. Ordinary copper network cable is rated for 100 meters, or 328 feet, end to end under the usual 10/100/1000BASE-T standard. Inside that distance, a buried Ethernet run in conduit to a small switch in the outbuilding is the steady option. Beyond it, or where the outbuilding has its own electrical service, we use fiber with a media converter at each end, which carries no electricity between buildings at all. Where trenching is not possible, an outdoor-rated access point on the house, aimed at the dock or the garage, can carry Sonos as long as it is wired back to the router: Sonos lists plug-in Wi-Fi extenders as unsupported, and tells owners not to plug Sonos products into the Ethernet port of an access point or extender unless that device is itself cabled back to the network.

A Sonos community answer to an owner wanting music on a dock put it simply: plug-in extenders cause problems, mesh systems and hard-wired access points are supported, and every Sonos device has to be on the same network. That is the rule we design to. Two separate networks — one for the house, one for the dock — leave the dock speaker invisible to the app on the porch.

Water, ice and power at the shoreline

Sonos's outdoor architectural speakers are designed for humidity, water, heat, UV and freezing temperatures, are rated IP66 and are specified for −13°F to 149°F, so a Putnam winter will not hurt them on a post or under an eave. What they cannot do is sit where the lake reaches them, where ice heaves a dock in March or where a wake soaks them every weekend. On a fixed dock we mount them high on the landward end; on a floating or seasonal dock we usually recommend a portable that comes back to the house.

Electricity on the dock is a separate trade. The National Electrical Code's Article 555 covers wiring for marinas, boatyards and docking facilities, and its ground-fault rules for receptacles near the water are strict, because shock hazards around docks are real. New dock outlets, lighting and boat-lift circuits belong to a licensed electrician. Our speakers on a dock are passive and run on low-voltage speaker wire from an Amp on dry land, which is one of the reasons we prefer them to plugging a powered speaker into a dock outlet.

Sound across the water

Owners are often surprised by how far music carries on a lake. On calm evenings, cooler air sits on the water under warmer air above, and acoustics researchers such as Penn State's Graduate Program in Acoustics describe how that kind of temperature inversion bends sound back down toward the surface instead of letting it rise away. A deck pair that sounds polite on the deck can be clearly heard across a narrow cove.

Town codes reflect that. The Town of Carmel's noise chapter limits noise in residential districts to 65 dB(A) between 8 a.m. and 6 p.m. and 55 dB(A) between 6 p.m. and 8 a.m., and its sound-amplification permits do not allow loudspeakers before 10 a.m. or after 8 p.m. Sunday through Thursday. The Town of Putnam Valley's noise law prohibits radio and recording sounds audible more than 100 feet from the source between 10 p.m. and 7 a.m. on weekdays and 11 p.m. and 9 a.m. on weekends. We aim outdoor speakers down at the seating and away from the open water, keep them close to where people sit, and set a maximum volume for each outdoor room in the app with you. This is general information, not legal advice; check your own town's code for events.

Before anyone digs

A conduit to the dock or the garage means a trench across a lawn that may hold a septic field, a well line, buried power to a boat lift or an invisible dog fence. The utility one-call system, reached through 811, asks for notice at least two and no more than ten days before digging, and marks utility-owned lines for free; private lines such as a well feed or a lamp post are the owner's to locate. We walk the route with you, ask what is buried, and route around septic fields and tree roots before a spade goes in.

Storm Season

Building a Putnam Sonos System That Rides Through Outages

The utilities' own updates show why we plan for the power going out. NYSEG's Brewster division covers Putnam along with Dutchess and Westchester, and Central Hudson serves customers on the river side around Cold Spring and Garrison. Wind, wet snow and summer thunderstorms bring trees down onto lines along the ridge roads, and a house at the end of a long private drive may be among the last to be reconnected.

In our experience the Sonos products themselves usually rejoin the network once power and the router are back. The trouble comes from the pieces around them. A router that takes several minutes to boot, a modem that the provider replaced after a surge, a mesh satellite plugged into an outlet that tripped, or an Amp on a power strip that switched itself off — each can leave rooms missing from the app after the lights are back on.

  • Battery backup where it counts. A small uninterruptible power supply under the router, the switch and the Amp that drives the outdoor zones bridges the short flickers that come with every thunderstorm and gives equipment a clean shutdown in a long outage. It will not run the house; it keeps the network from rebooting every time the lights blink.
  • Surge protection on cables that leave the house. A run to the dock or a garage is a long conductor across open ground. We bring it into the house through a sealed entry, add surge protection rated for the cable type, and keep a fiber link wherever a building has its own electrical service.
  • Equipment you can reach. The router and Amps go somewhere a caretaker or family member can find and restart them, labelled, not behind a sealed cabinet panel.
  • A written restart order. Modem first, then router, then switches and access points, then the Sonos products if any are still missing. We tape it inside the closet door.

Generators are a decision for you and your electrician. If the house has one, we put the network equipment on a circuit it supports, so the Wi-Fi comes back with the generator.

The lawn, the deck and the dock

How far down the lawn can a wired speaker go before the Amp has to move closer to the water?

Sonos's own guidance answers most of this. Its wire-gauge chart calls for 16-gauge cable under 80 feet, 14-gauge between 80 and 200 feet and 12-gauge between 200 and 300 feet from the Amp, and says runs longer than 300 feet are not recommended. So on a typical Mahopac or Oscawana lot, a deck pair and a lawn pair can usually share an Amp in the lower level of the house, and a dock pair can too if the dock is within about 300 feet of it.

In practice we start thinking about a second Amp well before the limit. If the dock is 220 feet away down a slope, the 12-gauge cable, the conduit and the trench all add cost, and a second Amp in a boathouse or shed near the water, fed by a network link, often comes out simpler. It also keeps the dock zone independent of the deck zone, which matters more than people expect on a busy summer weekend.

Can speakers on the dock run off the dock's existing outlet?

Not the way we install them, and we would be cautious about anyone who suggests it. Outlets on a dock fall under the National Electrical Code's Article 555, which covers wiring at marinas, boatyards and docking facilities and sets tight ground-fault rules because of shock hazards near water. Whether an existing dock outlet meets today's rules is a question for a licensed electrician, not for us.

What we fit on a dock are passive outdoor speakers. They need no outlet at all: they take low-voltage speaker wire from an Amp on dry land, inside the house or a boathouse. That keeps the powered electronics away from the water, and it means the only thing on the dock is a weather-rated speaker on a bracket and a cable in conduit.

Why does the music sound louder from across the lake at night than it does on our own deck?

Because of the air over the water. On calm evenings the lake cools the air right above it, while warmer air sits higher up. Acoustics researchers, including Penn State's Graduate Program in Acoustics, describe how that kind of temperature inversion bends sound back toward the surface instead of letting it spread upward and fade. Across open water there is nothing to absorb it, so a level that feels gentle on your deck can carry clearly to the houses on the far shore.

We plan around it in three ways: more speakers placed close to the seating rather than one pair turned up, speakers angled down toward the deck instead of out over the water, and a volume limit saved for each outdoor room in the app. Town codes in Carmel and Putnam Valley also put limits on evening sound in residential areas, so a quieter design is a practical one as well as a neighbourly one.

Can outdoor speakers be fixed to dock posts or the boathouse instead of the house wall?

Often, yes, and on a lakefront lot it usually sounds better. Sonos's outdoor architectural speakers are rated IP66 and specified for temperatures from −13°F to 149°F, so a solid post or the wall of a boathouse is fine for them. What matters is the structure: a fixed timber post or a framed boathouse wall can carry a bracket for years, while a floating section or a seasonal dock that comes out each autumn cannot.

We mount them high enough to stay clear of waves, ice and wet gear, on the landward end where possible, with the cable run in conduit along the underside or the frame rather than across the walking surface. If the dock is the kind that is pulled in for the winter, we put the speakers on the shore end or on the boathouse and leave the moving part free.

Garages, boathouses and the far end of the drive

When does the run to the boathouse or garage need fiber instead of network cable?

Two things decide it: distance and electricity. Copper network cable is rated for 100 meters, or 328 feet, end to end under the usual Ethernet standard, so a garage 400 feet up the drive is beyond what copper should be asked to do. Fiber carries the signal far further than any Putnam property needs, with a small media converter at each end.

The second reason is the electrical service. If the garage or boathouse has its own panel or meter, copper between the buildings connects two separate grounding systems, and a nearby lightning strike or a fault can travel along it. Fiber is glass and carries no current, so it isolates the buildings completely. Under 328 feet, between buildings on the same service, buried Ethernet in conduit is usually fine; beyond that, or where the services are separate, we quote fiber.

Our detached garage has its own electrical panel — does that change how the music gets out there?

It changes the network link, not the speakers. A garage with its own panel is usually fed from the house by a buried power cable, and the panel in the garage may be bonded to its own ground rod. Running copper Ethernet between two buildings like that is exactly the case where we prefer fiber, so the network doesn't become a path between the two electrical systems.

Inside the garage, the plan is the same as a room in the house: a small switch or access point fed by the link, then either wireless Sonos speakers, a pair of ceiling or wall speakers on a local Amp, or both. Because a garage gym or workshop is often unheated, the Amp goes in a heated corner or a closet, or stays in the house with speaker wire running out if the garage is close enough on Sonos's wire chart.

Lightning struck a tree near the house last summer — can a surge travel in on the outdoor speaker wire?

It can, which is why we treat every cable that leaves the house as something that needs protecting. A long speaker or network run across a lawn or up a hillside is a conductor stretched across open ground, and a strike nearby can induce a surge in it even without a direct hit. The damage typically shows up in whatever the cable plugs into: an Amp, a switch port or the router.

Our approach is to bring outdoor cable into the house through one sealed entry, fit surge protection suited to the cable at that point, keep runs in conduit, and use fiber for links to other buildings so there is no copper path at all. None of that makes equipment strike-proof, and nothing does, but it gives a surge far fewer ways in. Damage that does occur is a matter for your homeowner's insurer and the product's maker.

Lake bungalows and cottages

Our winterized Putnam Lake bungalow has low ceilings and no attic — where can speakers go?

Usually on the walls rather than in the ceiling. Without an attic or a floor above, there is no way to see what sits behind a ceiling, run wire across it or reach a speaker later without cutting more holes, and many of these cottages were built and extended without much space in the roof at all. Sonos's in-ceiling manual tells installers to survey for joists, pipes, ducts and wiring before cutting, and in a shallow roof that survey often rules a ceiling speaker out.

The good news is that small rooms need little speaker. An Era 100 on a wall bracket or a shelf, a Ray or Beam on the TV wall, and a portable for the porch will fill most bungalows. Where there is a crawlspace under the floor, wall speakers low on an interior wall or a cable to a soundbar can be fed from below.

The cottage has been added onto three times — why does Wi-Fi die past the old part of the house?

Usually because the original outside wall is still there. When a lake cottage grows, each addition is built against the last exterior wall, and that wall often keeps whatever was on it — siding, sheathing, sometimes foil-faced insulation or an old window filled in. To a Wi-Fi signal, that's an outside wall in the middle of the house. Add a fireplace or a chimney stack, and one end of a long cottage can be very weak.

We test signal in each room before recommending anything, then fix the network rather than the speakers: one wired access point placed in the newer half, fed by a cable through the crawlspace or along a baseboard, or a supported mesh system with its nodes placed on each side of the old wall. Plug-in extenders are out, because Sonos doesn't support them.

There's only a crawlspace under the cottage — can speaker wire run beneath the floor?

Generally yes, and it is often the only hidden route in a bungalow. From the crawlspace we can drill up into an interior wall cavity, drop a cable to a soundbar or a wall speaker, and run speaker or network cable from one end of the cottage to the other without opening finished walls. Cable is fixed to the joists, not left on the ground, and kept away from the heating ducts and the plumbing.

Two cautions. Crawlspaces near a lake can be damp, so we use cable suited to the conditions and keep any connection points inside the house, not under it. And some are simply too tight, wet or blocked by old insulation to work in safely; if we see anything that belongs to a plumber, an electrician or an abatement specialist, we stop and tell you before going further.

The seasonal house and storm season

We close the lake house from Labor Day to Memorial Day — what should happen to the Sonos system over the winter?

It depends on whether the heat and the internet stay on. If the house is kept heated and connected, the simplest choice is to leave everything running: the system keeps receiving updates, and Sonos lets owners schedule automatic updates for a chosen two-hour period, so nothing has to be updated on the first warm Saturday. If the house is shut down cold, unplug the Amps, soundbars and portables, bring any portable speakers back to the city with you, and leave the outdoor architectural speakers in place, since they are rated to −13°F.

On reopening, power the network first, then the Sonos products, and give them a few minutes before opening the app. We leave a one-page open-and-close sheet with every lake-house system covering exactly that, and we can book an opening visit each spring for owners who would rather not deal with it.

Is a battery backup worth adding for the router and the Sonos Amp?

For most Putnam houses on overhead lines, yes. A modest uninterruptible power supply under the modem, router, switch and the Amp that drives the outdoor speakers rides through the flickers that come with summer thunderstorms, so the network isn't rebooting every time the lights blink and the speakers don't fall out of their groups. In a longer outage it gives the equipment a clean shutdown instead of a hard one.

It is not a generator. A battery sized for network gear will not run a soundbar and a TV for an evening, and it shouldn't try. We size it for the equipment that keeps the network alive, put it where it can breathe and be reached, and label it so the next person knows what it is for.

Commuter houses on the ridge roads

Our split-level has half-flights between every room — can one Sonos zone cover the stair hall and the rooms off it?

Usually as two or three zones that are grouped together, rather than one. A split-level's open stair hall connects four short floors, and a single pair of speakers in the hall will be too loud on one landing and too quiet in the rooms off the next. Separate rooms in the app for the kitchen level, the family-room level and the bedroom level, grouped when you want the same music everywhere, give more even sound.

Wiring is easier than it looks. The levels overlap, so a cable can often drop from the attic over the bedrooms straight into the wall behind the family room, a half-flight down, without crossing a finished ceiling. And one wired Sonos product or a wired access point on the lower level usually fixes the weak signal that half-floors and a garage between them tend to cause.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Sonos Installation in Putnam County

A note on method: our research tools could not load Google's AI-generated answers for Putnam searches, so we do not quote them. Each heading below is an assumption that turns up again and again in quick answers, cost guides and forum advice about outdoor and whole-home audio, written in our own words. Under each one is what changes on a Putnam property.

“A mesh Wi-Fi kit will cover the dock”

Quick answers about outdoor music tend to assume the house Wi-Fi, perhaps with one more mesh unit, will reach wherever the speakers go. On a quarter-acre suburban lot that is often true.

On a lakefront lot in Putnam the dock can be two hundred feet from the house and thirty feet below it, through trees and past a boathouse. Sonos supports mesh systems and hard-wired access points but not plug-in extenders, and every Sonos product has to be on the same network as the app.

We measure signal at the water, then choose between an outdoor access point cabled back to the router, a buried link to a boathouse, or a portable that doesn't need a permanent connection at all.

“Any speaker wire will do for outdoor speakers”

Generic installation guides often treat speaker wire as one product and leave the gauge as an afterthought, or suggest whatever came in the box.

Sonos's own chart says otherwise: 16-gauge under 80 feet, 14-gauge from 80 to 200 feet, 12-gauge from 200 to 300 feet, and no runs beyond 300 feet. Down a sloping lake lawn, the difference between the right gauge and the wrong one is audible.

We measure each run, size the cable to Sonos's chart, put it in conduit where it crosses the lawn and keep every connection above ground.

“Just run a network cable to the garage”

Forum advice about getting internet to an outbuilding often starts and ends with “bury a Cat6 cable”, and for a short run between buildings on one electrical service that is reasonable.

Copper Ethernet is rated for 328 feet end to end, and on Putnam properties where the garage sits at the top of a long drive, or the boathouse has its own panel, copper is either too short or the wrong material. Fiber carries no current between buildings.

We choose copper or fiber per link and say why in the written quote, so you are not paying for fiber where copper will do or risking copper where it won't.

“Outdoor speakers plug into the nearest outlet”

Many retail listings for outdoor speakers show powered models plugged into an outdoor outlet, and quick answers carry that picture over to every outdoor zone.

Sonos's outdoor architectural speakers are passive and need an Amp; and outlets on a dock come under the National Electrical Code's Article 555, with strict ground-fault rules near water. That is electrician's work, not a speaker installer's.

We keep powered electronics on dry land and run low-voltage speaker wire to the water, and we hand any new outlet or dock circuit to your licensed electrician.

“Outdoors, louder is better”

Party audio is sold on volume, and quick answers repeat the idea that outside you simply need bigger speakers turned up higher.

Over a lake, sound travels further than people expect, especially on still evenings when the air near the water is cooler than the air above. The Town of Carmel limits residential noise to 55 dB(A) after 6 p.m., and Putnam Valley's code restricts music audible beyond 100 feet late at night.

More speakers near the seating, angled down and kept at modest levels, carry music around a deck without carrying it across the cove. We set and save those levels with you on the deck.

“Multi-room audio runs $500 to $1,000 per room”

That range comes from a national cost guide, Angi, for adding zones or multi-room audio, and for an indoor room with easy wiring it is a fair rough figure.

An outdoor zone at the bottom of a Putnam lawn is not an indoor room. A trench, conduit, heavier cable, a sealed wall entry and possibly a second Amp in a boathouse change the cost entirely, and none of that shows up in a per-room average.

So our indoor setup and soundbar lines are fixed and published, while each outdoor zone gets a written figure after we have walked and measured it. AESS's Putnam AV page publishes its own package ranges for bigger systems.

“A Hudson Valley house means an old farmhouse or a new build”

General advice about the region tends to picture either a big new house with pre-wiring or a stone farmhouse with thick walls.

Putnam has plenty of both, but it also has hundreds of lake cottages laid out on 20-by-100-foot lots from 1928 onward and winterized since, and nineteenth-century village houses packed close together in Cold Spring. Neither has room for a rack or an equipment closet.

We design for the house that is actually there: wireless-first for bungalows and village houses, wired backbones where the property is large, and nothing that needs more space than the house can give.

Field Notes

Field Notes From Putnam Sonos Walkthroughs

What a lakefront walkthrough normally involves for our crews; it describes the routine, and no real client or address

We start outside, not in the living room. With the owner, we walk from the back wall of the house to every place that wants music, counting paces or running a wheel: the deck, the lawn, the fire pit, the dock, the boathouse door. At the water we stand where people actually sit and check Wi-Fi on a phone. Those few minutes usually settle whether the outdoor zones share one Amp or need two.

Then we ask what is in the ground. Septic field, well line, buried power to the dock or a lamp post, an irrigation loop, a pet fence. The answers decide the conduit route before anyone draws it on paper, and anything uncertain waits for the utility mark-out and the owner's own records.

Last comes the inside: where the internet enters, where a ventilated shelf for the Amps can go near the lake-side wall, whether any outbuilding has its own panel, and how the house is used in winter. The sketch we make on the deck table, with distances written along each line, becomes the written quote.

FAQ

Putnam County Sonos Installation FAQ

Is there a travel charge for Sonos work at a Putnam lake house?
+

No. The AESS Putnam County price list carries no travel or parking charge, so a visit to a lake house in Patterson or a village house in Cold Spring is priced on the same lines: $125 for Sonos setup, $129 to wall-mount a soundbar, and $250 for the first hour of a setup-only visit, then 30-minute blocks. Outdoor zones and outbuilding links are quoted in writing after a walkthrough.

Can the deck pair and the dock pair on one Amp play at different volumes?
+

No. Every speaker connected to one Sonos Amp plays as a single room, with one source and one volume. Sonos says an Amp can drive up to three pairs of its architectural speakers in parallel, but they all play together. If you want the dock quiet while the deck is busier, or different music in each place, each zone needs its own Amp.

Who digs the trench for the cable to the dock or garage?
+

We plan the route, lay the conduit and pull the cable, and the written quote says who digs. Short, shallow runs across a lawn we can usually handle; long runs, rocky ground or work near a septic field go to your landscaper or excavator, with our conduit going in before the trench is backfilled. Either way, the utility one-call service through 811 is contacted first so buried utility lines are marked.

Is a screened porch indoors or outdoors as far as a Sonos speaker is concerned?
+

Treat it as outdoors. Rain blows through screens, morning dew settles on everything, and a porch can drop below freezing in winter. Sonos warns that speakers which are not waterproof can be damaged if left outside for extended periods, so on a screened porch we fit the outdoor architectural pair under the roof, wired to an indoor Amp, or leave a portable that comes inside when the season ends.

Our property has a main house and a guest cottage with separate internet. Can they share one Sonos system?
+

Only if both buildings are on the same network. Sonos requires the app and every speaker to be on one network, so two separate internet services mean two separate Sonos systems. If you want one system, we link the cottage to the main house's network with copper or fiber and retire the cottage's own router, or keep two systems and show you how to switch between them in the app.

Can you do outdoor work in winter when the ground is frozen?
+

Indoor work, network links through existing paths and anything mounted on the house can go ahead in any month. Trenching across a frozen lawn waits for the thaw, so in winter we often install the indoor side, the Amps and the wall entries first, cap the cable ends, and come back in spring to run the conduit and fit the dock or lawn speakers.

Can we take a Sonos speaker out on the boat?
+

A portable, yes. Sonos rates the Roam 2 IP67, saying it can be submerged in up to about three feet of water for several minutes, and it plays over Bluetooth from a phone when it is away from the house Wi-Fi. The larger Move 2 is rated IP56. Back at the house, either one plays on the home system again over Wi-Fi.

What kind of cable goes to the dock? Will ordinary speaker wire do?
+

Not for a buried run. Indoor speaker wire is not made to sit in damp ground or sunlight, so outdoors we use cable rated for the conditions, run inside conduit where it crosses the lawn, in the gauge Sonos's chart calls for at that distance. Every connection is made indoors or inside a sealed box, never left in the ground or on the dock deck.
