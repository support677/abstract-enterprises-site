The Neighborhood

Why New York City Condominiums Need a Security Plan Written for Owners

Condominium living is no longer a niche in this city. The NYC Comptroller's March 2024 housing spotlight, built on the 2023 Housing and Vacancy Survey, counts 318,000 occupied apartments in condominiums, and puts co-ops and condos together at 22 percent of the city's occupied housing. That is a lot of front doors governed by a board of managers rather than a landlord, and a lot of buildings where the people paying for the intercom are the same people who vote on it.

A condo is owned differently from every other kind of building, and condominium security has to be planned that way. Each owner holds a deed to a unit. Everything outside the units is shared. New York's Condominium Act spells out what that shared part usually includes: the act's definition of common elements lists the "halls, corridors, lobbies, stairs, stairways, fire escapes, and entrances and exits of the building," the "basements, cellars, yards, gardens, recreational or community facilities, parking areas and storage spaces," and "all apparatus and installations existing for common use" (Real Property Law §339-e). Read that list next to a security plan and it describes almost every place a camera, reader or entrance panel goes. Unless the building's declaration says otherwise, those spaces belong to all the owners together, and the board runs them on everyone's behalf.

The owners themselves are a more varied group than most people expect. The same Comptroller analysis found that 18 percent of condo units citywide are owned by LLCs, rising to 21 percent in Manhattan. Many condo apartments are rented out by their owners, some sit empty for part of the year, and some change hands often. For the front door that means a constantly moving list of people who need a fob, an intercom listing or an app login, and a list of people who should no longer have one. In our experience the single most common weakness in a New York condo is not a broken camera. It is a credential database nobody has cleaned since the building opened.

The condominium form also explains why condo boards hire differently. A condo board of managers is elected by the unit owners under bylaws that the Condominium Act requires to cover the board's powers and duties, whether it may hire a managing agent, how meetings of unit owners are called, and the method of adopting rules for the use of the common elements (§339-v). So a security upgrade is rarely one person's decision. The board weighs it, the managing agent collects bids and paperwork, the treasurer looks at the common charges and the reserves, and the owners hear about it in a notice and at the annual meeting. A contractor who can only talk about camera megapixels is answering the wrong question.

Facts that frame condo building security in New York City

  • 318,000 occupied condo apartments in the city, alongside 450,000 in co-ops; together 22% of occupied housing (NYC Comptroller, March 12, 2024, from NYCHVS 2023).
  • 18% of condo units are owned by LLCs citywide and 21% in Manhattan (same report, from Department of Finance tax records), one reason owner and occupant lists drift apart.
  • Common elements by default include lobbies, corridors, entrances, cellars, recreational facilities, parking areas, storage spaces and installations for common use, unless the declaration provides otherwise (RPL §339-e(3)).
  • Bylaws must set out the board's powers, whether it may hire a managing agent, how unit owners' meetings work, and how rules for the common elements are adopted; they may also govern reserves for repairs, replacements and improvements (RPL §339-v).
  • The intercom mandate: state law makes a working two-way voice intercom, with a release button in each apartment and a self-locking street door, standard equipment for post-1967 class A multiple dwellings of eight or more apartments in New York City; older buildings owe one when most tenants ask in writing (Multiple Dwelling Law §§3, 50-a).
  • Fob and app data has rules: Local Law 63 of 2021, the Tenant Data Privacy Law, makes buildings using keyless entry publish a privacy policy, collect consent, and purge authentication records after 90 days unless they are anonymized (HPD summary).

Who does what in a condo security project

The board of managers decides what the common elements get and approves the spending. A condo board wants two or three clearly different options, a cost for each that will survive the bidding process, and the ongoing costs spelled out, because those land in the common charges every owner pays. Board members are volunteers; they want to explain the choice to their neighbors without having to become intercom experts.

The managing agent turns the vote into a contract: vendor paperwork, the certificate of insurance once the job is awarded, the work schedule, the owner notices and the coordination with the resident manager or super. For a managing agent running a portfolio of condominiums, the value is one contractor who handles the door entry, the fobs and the cameras the same way in every building.

Unit owners want the panel to show their name, the call to reach their phone or their monitor, the gym door to open for them and not for the people who moved out, and their visitors and deliveries handled without fuss. Owners who rent out their units also want a simple way to get their tenants credentials and take them back at lease end.

Residents who rent from owners use the system every day but have no vote. They are the people most likely to be missing from the directory and the most likely to hold an old fob after they leave, so the credential process has to include them.

The resident manager or super keeps it all running. If the super can add a renter to the directory, disable a lost fob or pull an hour of video for the managing agent without calling anyone, the board saves money every month.

Condo intercom systems: repair, replacement and modernization

The condo intercom system is the one piece of building technology every owner, renter, guest and courier touches. Our condo intercom work covers the entrance panel and any secondary door stations, the door release and its power supply, the wiring up the building, the in-unit monitors or handsets, the smartphone app if there is one, and the resident directory that ties them together. We service audio intercoms, video intercoms, telephone-entry systems that dial owners' phones, and IP intercoms that run over the building network, including systems another company installed.

When a condo board talks about condo intercom replacement, the real question is how far to go. A modernization can keep the existing two-wire or multi-wire cable and change only the entrance panel and the in-unit stations; it can move an audio building to video intercom; it can go fully IP over new network cable; or it can replace in-unit hardware with a smartphone system so most apartments need no new wiring. Each condo intercom upgrade path changes the price, the amount of work inside units, the monthly cost and how long the system will last. A condominium intercom replacement proposal from us puts those paths side by side, with what each does for visitor calls, deliveries, the directory and remote door release, so the board can compare them against other bids and against its reserve budget. Condo boards preparing an intercom RFP will find the scope checklist, bid comparison steps and owner-notice timeline in our condo intercom replacement guide, which is being prepared now.

When the system has just stopped, we begin with a $250 service call and find the fault: a blank panel, silence on certain floors, a release that clicks but never frees the door, calls landing in the wrong unit, or an app that went quiet after a phone update. Our intercom repair NYC page describes fault-tracing in lived-in buildings; for a full replacement, the intercom installation in New York City page covers equipment and installation.

Condo access control and key fobs

Condo access control swaps metal keys on the lobby, side, garage, package room, gym, pool, roof and storage doors for credentials the building can manage: key fobs, cards, PIN codes or mobile credentials. For a condominium the payoff is control over a population that changes all the time. When an owner sells, a renter's lease ends, a dog walker stops coming or a contractor finishes a renovation, the managing agent turns off that credential in a minute instead of rekeying a door. Logs show which credential opened which door and when, which settles a lot of arguments about the package room and the gym.

Many condo buildings opened ten or fifteen years ago with 125 kHz proximity fobs. Our access control installation NYC page explains why that matters: those fobs broadcast a fixed number that inexpensive handheld devices can read and copy. Moving a building to encrypted smart cards or mobile credentials replaces the readers and reissues credentials to every resident; for a 30-unit building that page puts the typical cost at $3,000–$8,000 installed. For condos we pair every access control job with a written credential policy (how many fobs per unit, what renters get, what happens at closing or lease end) and a privacy policy that fits the city's Tenant Data Privacy Law, covered further down this page.

Condo elevator access control

In a high-rise or luxury condo the elevator is a second lobby. Condo elevator access control limits each credential to the floors its holder belongs on, opens the amenity floor during posted hours, gives a penthouse or a private-landing unit its own protected stop, and lets a delivery or moving credential reach only one floor for one day. The reader connects to the car's control system, so the work is coordinated with the building's elevator contractor and must leave firefighter and fire service operation intact. Our elevator access control installation in NYC page explains floor restriction, the elevator company's role and what the system does during a fire alarm.

Door buzzers in boutique and walk-up condos

Not every condominium is a glass tower. Small conversion condos, brownstone condos and walk-ups with a handful of units often still run a simple condo door buzzer: a button in each unit, a strike on the street door and a transformer in the cellar. When the buzzer goes silent, the door buzzes but stays locked, or one unit stops working, the cause is usually the transformer, the strike, a relay, a corroded splice or a single unit station. Our door buzzer repair in NYC page walks through how we diagnose those faults. A buzzer that keeps failing is often the natural moment for a small condo association to move to a video entry panel with app calling.

Condo security cameras, briefly

On a condo, cameras belong where the association answers for what happens: front and garage doors, the lobby, mail and parcel areas, cars and stairs, the pool deck and gym, trash and storage rooms. Hanging the camera is the easy part. The board has to settle who watches, who may pull a clip for an owner or a detective, how long footage lives, and which views stay clear of unit entries and private terraces. Condo-specific placement and pricing will sit on our condo security camera installation in NYC page, now being written, and our apartment building security cameras in NYC page covers camera systems for multi-unit buildings generally.

Amenity spaces: gyms, pools, roof decks, storage and garages

Amenities are where condo security gets personal. Owners pay for the gym and the roof deck through their common charges and expect them to be for residents only; the board needs to know who was in the pool area when the furniture went missing. We install readers on each amenity door with schedules the board sets (the gym open early to late, the roof deck closed overnight, the children's playroom limited to daytime), cameras covering entrances and equipment, and intercom stations at gates or pool entries where guests need to call up. For storage cages and bike rooms, a reader with a log is often enough. For a garage, the gate or overhead door gets a reader or long-range tag reader, a camera on the ramp and an intercom station for visitors and service vehicles.

Package rooms, lockers and deliveries

Deliveries are the daily stress test of any condo building security plan. A workable setup usually combines a few pieces: a delivery code or one-time credential at the entrance so couriers reach the vestibule or package room without someone buzzing in strangers, a reader on the package room so only residents and staff enter, and a camera on the shelves and the door. Where the board has chosen smart package lockers, we handle the integration side: the door entry, power and network connection, and the camera coverage, while the locker company supplies and supports the lockers themselves. The goal is the same whether the building has a full-time concierge, a part-time porter or nobody at the desk.

Guest, renter and short-term access

Condos with many investor-owned units ask us how to handle guests and short stays. What we provide is the technical side: temporary credentials that expire on a set date, PIN codes limited to certain doors and hours, app invitations an owner can send and revoke, and logs that show when a guest credential was used. Whether a particular building's bylaws or New York's short-term rental rules allow a given arrangement is a question for the board and its counsel, not for us; we configure the system to match the policy the board adopts.

Alarms for staff rooms, storage and commercial units

Inside the units, alarms are each owner's own business. The association's needs are narrower: the management office, the super's workshop and supply room, a roof bulkhead, sometimes a commercial unit the condominium itself owns. For those spaces we put in a panel with door contacts, motion and glass-break sensors and a panic button, and can connect it to central-station monitoring through our provider on either of our two plans, $45 a month including the phone app or $30 a month using the keypad alone. Details sit on our alarm installation NYC page.

Fire alarm work, within our scope

A condo fire alarm is not a security add-on; in this city it is a separately regulated system that starts with drawings sealed by an engineer, goes through FDNY filing and review, and ends with an FDNY acceptance test. We accept that work only inside that process and inside our licensing scope, and because the approved drawings set the price, no fire alarm figure appears on this page. Where fire alarm and security always meet is the door: any reader-controlled door or mag lock has to drop when the fire alarm or sprinklers trip, and we wire and coordinate that release. The fire alarm installation NYC page lays out how those jobs proceed.

Cabling, network and backup power behind it all

Behind every entrance panel and reader is plumbing most owners never see: cable to each floor, a switch that powers cameras and stations over the network cable, a rack, and a battery unit that carries the front door through a brief power cut. We install and label all of it, segment the security gear away from the residents' amenity Wi-Fi, and leave a drawing showing each run. City rules spare this class of work from electrical permits when it stays under 50 volts and outside the life-safety exceptions (Admin. Code §28-105.4.7). Bigger backbone jobs are on our structured cabling installation NYC page. Line-voltage work is not ours: the condominium's electrician brings a 120-volt circuit where one is needed, and we take it from the outlet.

Concierge desks and virtual doorman providers

Buildings with a staffed desk need a master station or desk software that calls units, releases secondary doors, shows the cameras and logs visitors and packages. Buildings without staff sometimes look at a virtual doorman or remote concierge service. We do not operate one. What we do is install the entrance panels, cameras, readers and network that such a provider works through, and connect new equipment to a provider the board already uses, so the board can change providers later without ripping out hardware.

Integration: one user list, one picture

Integrated condo security means the systems share information instead of sitting side by side. A visitor's call can show the entrance camera on the owner's monitor; a fob tap at the garage can be matched to the clip from the ramp camera; the package room reader and its camera tell the same story. Above all, integration means one list of people: when an owner closes on a sale or a renter leaves, removing them once removes their directory listing, their fobs and their app access. Before anything is ordered we confirm the equipment the board picks can actually work together, and where the building already has working cameras or readers worth keeping, we connect to them instead of replacing them.

Accessible entry for every owner

Some owners are in their eighties; some are visually impaired; some simply refuse to put building software on their phones. A condominium entrance should still work for all of them: large type and strong contrast on the directory, unit stations with oversized buttons and volume control, a way to ring a landline instead of an app, and a release long enough for a walker or a double stroller to clear the door. If the lobby already has a power door operator, the intercom and readers trigger it too.

Maintenance plans and support between visits

A new system degrades the same way the old one did unless someone looks after it. Under a condo security maintenance agreement we come back on a schedule to exercise each lock and reader, wipe weathered stations and camera domes, read the health of recorder drives and UPS batteries, install firmware, and reconcile the credential list with the managing agent's current roster of owners and tenants. In between, phone and text support costs nothing: a photo of the screen is usually enough for us to talk a super through enrolling a new tenant or killing a lost fob. Out-of-warranty repairs begin with the $250 service call, and agreements are priced to each building's equipment, so no contract figure is posted here.

Building Specialists

Condo Security for New York City's Building Types

"Condominium" describes ownership, not architecture. A six-unit brownstone conversion and a sixty-story tower with a sky lounge are both condos, both answer to a board of managers, and need almost nothing in common from a security contractor. These are the condo building types we work in across the five boroughs and what each one usually asks for.

Luxury new-development towers

Glass and steel, a staffed lobby, a package room, a gym, a pool or spa, a roof terrace and a garage. These get the full set: video entry with app calling, encrypted credentials, elevator floor control, amenity schedules and a camera system sized for dozens of doors.

Conversion condos

Former rental buildings, warehouses, lofts or offices turned into condominiums. The shell is old and the finishes are new, so cable paths are tight, landmarked facades may limit exterior hardware, and the building often inherits part of an older intercom.

Boutique condos (5–20 units)

Small buildings with no staff and a self-managed or lightly managed board. One entrance panel, a few readers, simple administration a board member can handle, and no subscription the association does not need.

Townhouse and HOA-style communities

Rows of attached homes or low buildings sharing drives, lots and gates, run by a homeowners' association or a condo board. Gate entry, perimeter cameras on lots and paths, and weatherproof hardware matter more than lobby panels.

Mixed-use condos

Residential units above retail, medical or office units that are separate condo units with their own owners. The residential entrance, the commercial doors and shared spaces like the cellar or loading area each need their own credentials and camera permissions.

Large multi-building complexes

Several towers or many buildings on one site, often with a clubhouse, parking structures and walking paths. The design priority is one directory, one credential database and one management login across every entrance.

How the work changes with construction and age

Metal studs and drywall. Newer condos are mostly framed with metal studs and finished in drywall, which sounds easy until you are working in a finished corridor owners paid a premium for. We plan runs through ceiling spaces, chases and the trash or utility rooms on each floor, patch nothing we do not have to open, and set entrance panels in back boxes sized for the hardware. Where a sponsor installed conduit for future work, we find it and use it; where they did not, we show the board the route before anyone drills.

Old shells, new insides. Conversion condos combine century-old masonry with modern units. The old shell means thick walls, few chases and sometimes landmarked entrances, while the units may already have network wiring. In these buildings we often run a new IP backbone through the common elements and use smartphone calling or in-unit stations on existing network drops, which avoids opening finished apartment walls.

Sponsor-installed systems. Many New York condos still run the intercom, access control and cameras the developer chose. Those systems are often perfectly good hardware with a management problem: the software account, the cloud subscription or the admin password was set up in the developer's or an installer's name and never transferred to the condo association. Part of our survey in a newer building is finding out who actually controls each system, and the first recommendation is often simply to move every account and license into the association's name.

Amenity floors and roofs. Gyms, pools, lounges and roof decks bring doors that stay locked most of the day, glass that limits where readers can go, and outdoor equipment exposed to wind and sun. We use mullion-width readers on narrow frames, weather-rated stations outdoors, and door-position contacts so the managing agent knows when the pool gate is left open.

Garages and loading areas. A condo garage adds overhead doors, gate operators and vehicle traffic. We connect readers or long-range tag readers to the gate operator rather than replacing it, place cameras for the ramp and the pedestrian door, and give service vehicles a call station so the desk or an owner can let them in. Wiring for the gate operator's high-voltage side stays with the building's electrician or the gate company.

Doorman and non-doorman condos. In a staffed building the entrance panel mainly serves overnight visitors and secondary doors; the desk needs a master station or software that calls units, opens doors, sees cameras and logs packages. In a non-doorman condo the entrance panel does the doorman's job: clear video of visitors, door release from an owner's phone, a controlled path for couriers, and a record of every entry. Part-time desks need both modes and a clean switch between them each evening.

Prewar and postwar condo conversions. Prewar buildings converted to condo ownership tend to keep their original intercom risers longer than they should; postwar conversions usually have more space in the ceilings and simpler cable paths. In either case we test the existing cable pair by pair before telling a board it can be reused, because a modernization that depends on bad wire turns into a callback.

Commercial Condo Security

Managing Agents, Sponsors and Mixed-Use Condos in NYC

Much of our condo work arrives through managing agents and property management companies. A firm managing many condominiums wants the same things on every job: a contractor who can take the intercom, the credentials and the cameras off its plate, equipment and programming conventions consistent enough that a resident manager covering another building can find their way around, and paperwork that satisfies each board's insurance and vendor requirements. We run portfolios that way: one point of contact, a written scope per building and the same labeling and naming everywhere.

Mixed-use condominiums add layers. A ground-floor restaurant, a medical office or a retail unit is usually a separate condo unit with its own owner and its own share of the common charges. That owner should see the cameras on their storefront and not the residential lobby; the residential fob should not open the commercial back door; and couriers for the commercial unit should not be calling residents. We draw those lines into the system design and help the managing agent put them in writing for both sides.

What every managing agent can count on

  • A window that means something. Every appointment gets a two-hour arrival window, and the crew shows up within it rather than early, so the super is not caught off guard.
  • Paperwork at the right moment. Our general liability certificate, listing the condominium and the agent the way the building asks, goes out when the contract is awarded; it is not attached to a bid.
  • Quick to book, straight about timing. A visit lands on the schedule within 2 hours of the request on weekdays and weekends alike. Morning slots are assigned as jobs are paid, so we do not commit to same-day work.
  • Extras disclosed before the vote. Ordered equipment cannot go back, so anything that might add cost, such as a worn strike or a cable that could fail testing, is spelled out in the scope before the board signs off.
  • A building that can run without us. At the end you get a labeled rack, a plan of every door and camera, admin access registered to the association, and an hour with the resident manager on daily tasks.

Taking over from the sponsor

The first few years of a new condominium are a transition. The sponsor or developer installs and often pays for the original systems, then control passes to an owner-elected board. Security systems are easy to lose track of in that handover. Before the sponsor steps back, a board should make sure it receives: administrator logins for the intercom, access control and camera software; any cloud or license subscriptions transferred into the association's name with renewal dates; the equipment list with models and serial numbers; as-built drawings showing every door, reader, camera and cable; the installer's warranty terms and contact; and the current credential list. We can audit an existing sponsor installation for a new board, list what is missing and fix the gaps, whether or not we installed it.

Condo security RFPs and fair bid comparisons

Larger condominiums and professionally managed associations usually send security work out to bid. The RFPs that go wrong describe the goal and not the building: "replace the intercom with a video system" leaves each contractor to guess at the number of entrances, whether the cable can stay, whether units get monitors or an app, and what happens to the elevator readers. The board ends up comparing a panel swap against a full rebuild and cannot tell which bid is cheap and which is incomplete.

A good condo security request for proposal settles those facts first: unit count and entrances, the existing systems and their age, whether reusing sound cable is acceptable, in-unit monitors versus app versus both, which doors get readers, elevator scope, amenity schedules, the building's allowed work hours, and who owns the software accounts at the end. Then it asks every bidder for the same things: an itemized price, the equipment list with model numbers, all recurring subscription or license fees, labor and equipment warranties, the schedule and how many days units need access, and the training and documentation at handover. Industry voices have noticed the same split we see in bids; Joe Cioffi III, vice president of Bergen Protective Systems, told CooperatorNews in August 2016 "There's a company out there that doesn't offer inside stations and is only web-based," adding, "I find that to be a downside." Whether a board agrees or not, the RFP should make every bidder state plainly what goes inside the units.

Send us an RFP and we will answer it in whatever template the managing agent uses, once we have walked the building. No RFP yet? Our survey write-up describes the building closely enough that the board can hand it to competing contractors as the common scope. Insurance and vendor forms come once the contract is awarded.

From a 10-unit condo to a 400-unit condo

Under 20 units. A boutique or conversion condo, usually one entrance and no staff. The whole system may be one entrance panel, door hardware and a few readers. What matters most is that a board member can add a renter to the directory or kill a lost fob from a laptop, and that the association is not locked into fees it does not need.

20 to 100 units. The typical managed condo: one or two entrances, a garage or storage area, a small gym, a part-time super. This is where the choice between reusing the existing cable and pulling new network cable usually decides the price, and where a video entrance panel with app calling and encrypted fobs fits most budgets.

100 to 300 units. Now there is usually a desk, a parcel room, several amenity doors and probably parking. Condo Security Systems in NYC: Questions Answered

Budget, reserves and common charges

What will a building-wide condo security upgrade cost, and why can't anyone price it over the phone?

Because the price lives in details nobody can see from a phone call: how many entrances, whether the existing cable tests sound, whether units get monitors or an app, how many amenity and garage doors need readers, and whether elevators are included. That is why repairs start with the $250 service call and new systems start with a free survey and a written scope. For orientation, our intercom installation page lists $1,200–$5,000+ for a multi-unit intercom in a 5–30 unit building, and our access control page puts an encrypted-credential upgrade for a 30-unit building at $3,000–$8,000 installed. Towers, garages and elevator control are priced only after the survey.

How do condo boards usually fund an intercom or access control replacement?

The Condominium Act lets bylaws provide for reserves for "major and minor maintenance, repairs, additions, improvements, replacements" (RPL §339-v), and many associations pay for a security replacement from that reserve. Others fold it into the operating budget over two years, phase it, or use a special assessment. Which route fits is the board's decision with its accountant and counsel; we do not give financial advice. What we supply is the numbers in each shape: a one-time project total, a phased schedule where each phase works on its own, and a list of recurring costs that belong in the operating budget.

Is financing available for a condominium security project?

Financing is available through Wisetack, subject to approval. Mention it when you request the proposal and it is included with the written scope.

Board approval and owner communication

Does a condo board need a unit owner vote to replace the building intercom?

Usually not, but the answer is in your own documents. The Condominium Act requires the bylaws to set out the board's powers and duties and the percentage of unit owners needed for decisions binding on everyone (RPL §339-v), and many declarations and bylaws give the board authority over common-element repairs and replacements while requiring an owner vote above a spending threshold or for certain improvements. Read the declaration and bylaws with counsel before the board commits. Whatever the route, keep the survey, the competing bids and the minutes that show why the board chose what it did.

What should a condo board send unit owners before the work starts?

A short written notice once dates are firm, sent far enough ahead that owners who rent out their units can pass it to their tenants. It should say what is being replaced, which days the entrance or intercom will be affected, how people will get in during the cutover, whether anyone must provide unit access and on what dates, and how residents will be set up on the new system. We give the managing agent a plain-language draft and an after-cutover instruction sheet, including a version owners can forward to renters.

The intercom

Who pays to fix the intercom handset inside a condo unit: the owner or the association?

It depends on how your declaration and bylaws divide the unit from the common elements, and buildings differ. Some treat the in-unit station as part of the unit; others treat the whole intercom as a common installation. Neither we nor this page can decide that for a specific building, and it is a question for the managing agent and counsel. Practically, we diagnose first: if a whole floor is dead the fault is usually in shared wiring or equipment, and if one unit is dead the station or its connection is the likely cause. The $250 service call tells everyone which it is.

Can a condo replace its intercom without wiring work inside every unit?

In many buildings, yes. Two families of systems leave unit wiring alone: entry panels that call residents on a phone app, and telephone-entry panels that ring a number the resident already has. Newer condos can also put in-unit stations on network jacks installed during construction. Where the plan calls for new video monitors in each unit, or the shared cable itself is failing, someone has to get into those units. The cable test at the survey tells us which camp the building is in, and unit visits are then booked floor by floor in short blocks.

How do we know our condo intercom is due for replacement rather than another repair?

Count the warning signs. Is the entrance panel or its controller out of production, so parts come from salvage? Do faults hop from one unit to another month after month, which points at the shared cable or head-end rather than any one station? Has the annual repair spend crept past what one phase of a replacement would cost? One failed handset in a sound system calls for a repair. A panel nobody makes anymore plus cable that tests poorly on several floors calls for replacement, and our proposal prices both so the board sees the crossover.

Credentials, privacy and cameras

What happens to fobs and app access when a condo owner sells or a renter moves out?

The aim is that access ends the day occupancy ends. On a well-run system the managing agent or super removes the seller's or departing tenant's fobs, phone credentials and directory entry at closing or lease end, then enrolls the newcomer at move-in, without a service call. The weak link in condos is the renter, because the association deals with the owner and may never be told a lease ended. We tie the move-out step to the paperwork the building already processes for sales and lease approvals, so it happens every time.

Does New York City's smart-access privacy law apply to condominium buildings?

Read literally, it reaches keyless-entry systems in class A multiple dwellings, and nothing in its text sets condominiums aside. Its core duties are a published retention and privacy policy, written or in-app consent, a way into one's own unit that does not depend on the smart system, and a 90-day limit on keeping authentication data that is not anonymized. Whether and how it binds a particular condo, especially units lived in by their owners, is for the building's lawyer. Our part is configuring retention, consent and logs to whatever the board decides.

Can an owner add a video doorbell or camera at their own unit door?

Check with the board first, in writing. The hallway and the outer face of a unit door are common elements in most condominiums, and a doorbell camera there captures neighbors and their visitors whether it means to or not. Plenty of boards permit them under an alteration agreement or a house rule that limits models, aim and recording. We will mount one for an owner once the managing agent sends us that written approval.

Who should be allowed to view and export condo camera footage?

Write the answer into a house rule before the first camera is mounted. Many condos let the desk watch live, limit playback and downloads to the managing agent plus one or two officers, keep recordings for a period that covers incidents reported days afterward, and require a short written request when an owner or police detective wants footage. Steer every lens away from unit entries and private terraces and keep microphones off, which sidesteps eavesdropping-law issues. Counsel should review the rule; this is not legal advice.

Living with the project

How do you keep residents getting in while the entrance system is being replaced?

Nobody gets locked out, because the existing entrance keeps working until its replacement has passed testing. We install the new panel, cable and readers alongside the old ones, then switch over in a brief, pre-announced slot early in the morning, with a staff member or one of our technicians at the door whenever the lock is involved. In-unit work moves floor by floor, crews arrive within their window, and drilling stays inside the building's permitted hours.

What records should a condo association keep once the new system is in?

File everything a future board or agent would need to keep the system alive: the signed contract and final scope; an equipment register with model and serial numbers; as-built plans of doors, readers, cameras and cable paths; administrator logins kept by the managing agent under the association's name; every subscription and license with its renewal date; warranty terms; and the adopted credential, camera and smart-access privacy policies. Boards turn over and agents change. These files are what let the next people pick up without starting from zero, and they document the board's reasoning if it is ever questioned.

What paperwork does a managing agent typically ask a condo security contractor for?

Expect requests for an insurance certificate listing the condo and its agent as additional insureds, an executed contract or work order, the contractor's license number (NYS #12000287431 in our case), a schedule that flags unit-access days, and occasionally the building's own vendor or access agreement. We send the certificate once we are awarded the job and build the access days into the schedule early enough for owner notices.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Condo Security Systems in NYC

AI assistants and cost-guide sites tend to answer condo security questions with suburban single-family assumptions or with generic "apartment building" advice that ignores how condominiums are owned and governed. These seven claims show up again and again in directory sites like Angi and HomeAdvisor, vendor blogs and chatbot answers. Here is what we see in New York City condo buildings.

1. "In a condo, the intercom is each owner's responsibility"

Because condo owners own their units, it is easy to assume they own everything that serves them. Some generic answers tell owners to call their own electrician when the intercom stops working.

In most buildings the entrance panel, the shared wiring, the power supply and the door release sit in or serve the common elements, which the Condominium Act defines by default to include entrances, lobbies and "all apparatus and installations existing for common use." The in-unit station may be treated differently depending on the declaration.

The practical answer is to diagnose before assigning blame. A fault that hits a whole floor is almost never one owner's problem; a single silent unit might be. The $250 service call tells the managing agent which situation the building is in.

2. "A new building's system is covered by the developer"

Owners in newer condos often believe the sponsor or the original installer will look after the intercom and cameras for years. Chatbots repeat the idea that new construction "comes with" support.

Installer and manufacturer warranties expire, usually well before the building's first major capital plan. Worse, cloud accounts and admin passwords are frequently left in the developer's or installer's name, so when something breaks after the transition the board cannot even log in.

A sponsor handover audit fixes that: confirm every account, license and warranty, move them into the association's name, and get the as-built drawings before the developer's team disappears.

3. "Key fobs are secure enough"

Generic advice treats any fob system as a big step up from metal keys, and it is, for revoking access. It says less about whether the fobs themselves can be copied.

Many condos built in the last fifteen years use 125 kHz proximity fobs that broadcast a fixed number, which our access control page explains can be read and cloned with inexpensive handheld devices. In a building with years of renter turnover, nobody really knows how many working copies exist.

Moving to encrypted credentials replaces the readers and reissues fobs or phone credentials to everyone; for a 30-unit building our access control page puts that at $3,000–$8,000 installed. Even before that, a full audit of the credential list against the owner and renter list closes most of the gap.

4. "A cloud intercom has no real ongoing cost"

App-based and cloud-managed entry systems are often sold on a low installation price. AI summaries echo the pitch and skip the monthly or annual fees.

Many cloud platforms charge per door, per unit or per month, and those fees become a permanent line in the common charges. Over ten years a cheaper install can cost an association more than a system with a higher upfront price and no subscription; for a small boutique condo the math can go the other way.

We list every recurring fee in the proposal so the treasurer can compare ten-year totals, not just installation prices. Some boards choose the subscription for its remote management, and that is a sound choice when it is made with the numbers in view.

5. "A condo board can't change anything without an owner vote"

Some answers assume every common-area project needs a vote of all owners, which can stall a broken intercom for months while a meeting is organized.

The Condominium Act leaves this to the building's bylaws, which must set out the board's powers and duties and the percentage of owners needed for binding decisions (RPL §339-v). Many bylaws let the board handle repairs and replacements of common elements, with an owner vote reserved for spending above a threshold or for certain improvements.

The board should check its own documents with counsel. A board that can act should act on a failed entrance quickly, and document its choice with the survey, bids and minutes.

6. "A virtual doorman replaces the equipment and the staff"

Remote concierge services are often presented as a complete security solution for non-doorman condos, as if signing a service contract were the whole project.

A virtual doorman provider still works through physical equipment: a video entrance panel, a door release, cameras, readers, a network connection and backup power. If that hardware is old or badly installed, the remote service inherits every weakness. And when a board ends the service, it needs equipment it can keep using.

We do not run a virtual doorman service. We install and maintain the equipment such a provider uses and connect it to the provider the board picks, which keeps the hardware the association's own.

7. "Just give guests and renters the door code"

Chatbot advice for investor owners often amounts to sharing a code or a spare fob with guests and renters, because it is easy.

Shared codes and handed-down fobs are exactly how condo buildings lose track of who can get in. A code that has been passed around is effectively public, and a fob given to a renter three leases ago may still open the gym.

Modern systems issue each guest or renter a credential that expires on a date and works only on chosen doors, and logs its use. Whether the building's bylaws or New York's short-term rental rules permit a given use is for the board and counsel; the technology is there to enforce whatever policy they set.

Line voltage is outside our scope. We do everything low-voltage, meaning cable, locks and strikes, panels, readers, cameras and setup, and when a new 120-volt outlet or circuit is required the condominium's electrician provides it before we connect.

Within limits.
