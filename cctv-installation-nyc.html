The Neighborhood

Why New York City Properties Need CCTV Installation

New York is a city of shared walls, shared stairs and shared sidewalks, and that changes how a CCTV system has to be designed. A camera on a suburban house watches one driveway. A camera on a Washington Heights corner store watches two sidewalks, a subway entrance, a cellar hatch and the door of the apartment building next door. A camera in a Bushwick walk-up hallway sees every tenant, every delivery and every stranger who slipped in behind a resident. Density means more people pass each lens, more angles need covering, and more owners, boards and neighbors have a say in where a camera can go.

It also means older buildings. According to the Citizens Housing & Planning Council's analysis of the 2023 New York City Housing and Vacancy Survey, about 54 percent of the city's housing, roughly two million homes, was built before 1947, and 62 percent of units sit in buildings with six or more apartments. In Manhattan the figure is 97 percent. Those numbers are the daily reality of CCTV work here: brick and plaster instead of drywall, cellar boiler rooms instead of a utility closet, stair halls with no ceiling cavity, and storefronts carved into the ground floor of a 1920s apartment house.

The theft numbers matter too, and they are more nuanced than most sales pages admit. The NYPD's CompStat citywide report for the week of November 2–8, 2026 shows 39,978 retail theft complaints so far this year, down 12.2 percent from 45,555 at the same point in 2025, and 9,537 burglaries, down 14.1 percent. Crime is falling, but nearly forty thousand retail thefts in ten months is still a lot of stolen merchandise, and every one of those complaints is stronger when the store can hand over a clear clip with a correct time stamp. When we install CCTV here, the goal is evidence that holds up, not a blinking box that makes a store look protected.

So the right CCTV system for New York City is rarely the box on a big-box store shelf. It is a wired system planned around the building: where cable can actually run, where the recorder will be safe from a smash-and-grab, which sidewalk the board or landlord will allow a lens to see, and how the owner will pull footage on a phone at 2 a.m. when the alarm company calls. That planning is what a CCTV installer is really paid for.

New York City facts that shape every CCTV job

  • 39,978 retail theft complaints citywide in 2026 through November 8, down 12.2% year over year (NYPD CompStat, preliminary).
  • 9,537 burglaries citywide in the same period, down 14.1% from 11,099 (NYPD CompStat).
  • About 54% of homes were built before 1947, which means masonry, plaster and cellar equipment rooms on most jobs (CHPC from the 2023 NYCHVS).
  • 62% of units are in buildings of six or more apartments, so most residential CCTV is a common-area, board or landlord decision (CHPC).
  • 35 businesses in Flushing's 109th Precinct joined the first NYPD camera registration and integration pilot in June 2023, later expanded to nine more precincts (NYC Mayor's Office).
  • Two cameras per 25 feet of street frontage is the staff-level limit on small landmarked buildings, under the Landmarks Preservation Commission's permit guidance.

How CCTV jobs differ across the five boroughs

Manhattan is storefronts under apartment houses, elevator co-ops, office suites and a lot of landmarked frontage. Jobs here are about tight access hours set by managing agents, certificates of insurance before the crew is allowed upstairs, and cameras that have to pass a board's or the Landmarks Preservation Commission's eye. Upper Manhattan, from Harlem to Inwood, adds dense rows of corner stores and five- and six-story walk-ups where the recorder almost always ends up in the cellar.

Brooklyn mixes brownstone and row-house blocks with walk-ups, newer condo buildings and industrial lofts. Homeowners ask for the stoop, the areaway, the garden and the back fence; landlords of small walk-ups ask for the front door, the mailboxes and the roof hatch; warehouse and studio tenants in the industrial pockets ask for loading doors and long fence lines.

Queens covers everything from the busy retail of Main Street in Flushing and Jamaica Avenue to attached two-family houses with driveways and detached homes with yards. Driveway and garage coverage comes up far more often here than in Manhattan, and so do multi-camera systems for small restaurants and markets.

The Bronx has large pre-war elevator buildings along the Grand Concourse, walk-ups in every neighborhood, the shopping strip on Fordham Road and industrial blocks in Hunts Point and Port Morris. Big-building jobs often start with an old analog system: we test what is there, keep the good coax, and move the building onto a new recorder.

Staten Island is mostly one- and two-family homes, so residential CCTV looks more like a suburban job: driveways, side yards, garages and porches. Commercial work centers on strip-center stores, auto shops and parking lots, where plate-readable views of the entrance are the priority.

What is CCTV installation, in plain terms?

CCTV stands for closed circuit television: cameras that send video over a private cable to a recorder you control, rather than broadcasting it. Closed circuit television installation used to mean analog cameras, coax and a VCR. Today the same idea covers HD analog cameras on coax to a digital video recorder (DVR) and IP cameras on network cable to a network video recorder (NVR). The video still lives on your recorder, on your premises. The internet is only used when you choose to view it remotely.

A CCTV installation job has five parts: design (which camera, which lens, where, and why), cabling (the path each cable takes from the camera to the recorder), mounting (the right box, bracket or arm for the surface), commissioning (recording schedules, motion and smart detection zones, night settings, time sync, user accounts and remote access) and handover (showing you how to find and export footage). Skipping any one of them is how a city building ends up with eight cameras that record nothing useful.

Residential CCTV for houses, brownstones and two-families

Home CCTV installation in the city is usually four to eight cameras. A typical brownstone gets one camera over the stoop looking down at visitors' faces, one at the areaway gate under the stoop, one on the rear facade over the garden, and one inside the parlor-floor hall. A Queens or Staten Island two-family adds the driveway, the side alley and the garage door. We put the recorder in the basement or a closet, give each household its own login if it is a two-family, and set up motion alerts that ignore the sidewalk so the phone only buzzes when someone comes onto the property.

Package theft from vestibules and stoops is one of the most common reasons homeowners call. A camera at the right height, aimed at the door rather than the street, records the face of whoever picks up the box, which is what a police report or a building manager needs. A wide camera across the whole street usually does not.

Offices, medical and dental suites

Office CCTV installation in a Manhattan or downtown Brooklyn tower is a tenant job inside the landlord's rules. The building's own cameras cover the lobby and elevators; the tenant's system covers the suite entrance, reception, the server or IT closet and sometimes a storage room. Medical and dental offices keep cameras out of treatment and exam rooms and away from screens with patient information, and point them at the entrance, the waiting room and the front desk instead. Cable usually runs above the hung ceiling to a small recorder in the IT closet, on the tenant's own network rather than the building's, and the landlord's engineer often wants to see the plan before we open the ceiling.

When people call us

Most CCTV calls in New York City come at one of a few moments. After a break-in or a burglary, when the owner discovers the old DVR overwrote the footage or never recorded at all. Before the holiday shopping season, when stores want the aisles and register covered before Black Friday traffic. At a new business opening or grand opening, when the build-out is the cheapest time to run cable. When a building changes hands or a new board takes over and inherits a mix of dead cameras. And when a property sits vacant during a renovation, where copper theft and squatters are the concern. Each one points to a slightly different system, which is why we ask what happened before we talk about cameras.

Building Specialists

CCTV Installation for New York City's Architecture

Most of the time on a city CCTV job is not spent mounting cameras. It is spent getting cable from where the camera has to be to where the recorder can safely live, through walls that were built before anyone imagined a network cable. These are the building types we see most, and how each one changes the plan.

🧱

Pre-war walk-ups

Four to six floors, a single stair, brick bearing walls, plaster on lath and often no hung ceiling anywhere. Cable usually rides an existing riser chase, the dumbwaiter shaft, or surface raceway painted to match the stair hall, and the recorder goes in the cellar or a locked closet off the first-floor hall.

🏛️

Co-ops and condos

The board or managing agent approves every camera in a common area, and often wants a written camera list before anyone drills. We prepare a placement plan the board can review, keep lenses off apartment doors where the house rules require it, and set up separate viewer logins for the managing agent and the super.

🏠

Brownstones and row houses

Stoop, areaway, garden door and the back yard are the four views owners ask for. Brownstone and limestone fronts are often inside historic districts, so front-facade cameras follow Landmarks Preservation Commission rules, while the rear facade is usually simpler.

🏬

Mixed-use storefront buildings

A store on the ground floor and apartments above. The store's CCTV belongs to the tenant, the building's lobby cameras belong to the landlord, and the cable paths and recorder locations have to stay separate so each party controls its own footage.

🏙️

Elevator rentals and towers

Telecom closets on each floor, a shared riser, a lobby desk and sometimes an existing analog system from the 2000s. These jobs are mostly about reusing good risers, adding PoE switches per floor and putting the whole building on one NVR with clean labeling.

🏚️

Vacant and under-renovation buildings

Copper theft, squatters and dumping are the worries. We install a compact recorder with a battery backup in a locked enclosure and cover entrances, the cellar door and the yard, then extend the system when the building is occupied.

Cable through masonry, plaster and pressed tin

CCTV wiring in a pre-war building is a set of choices, not a single technique. Through solid brick we core small holes at mortar joints so the patch disappears and the wall face is not cracked. Inside plaster-on-lath walls we fish where cavities exist, often from the cellar up inside a closet partition, and stop where fire blocking or a masonry return makes fishing impossible. Pressed-tin and plaster ceilings in older stores are a no-drill zone unless the owner wants it, so cable travels behind crown moldings, inside soffits or in slim raceway. Every opening we make into a rated wall or floor is closed with firestop material, because a CCTV cable through a fire separation is still a penetration.

Cellar recorder rooms

The cellar is where most New York City recorders end up: it is central to the cable paths and out of a burglar's reach. It is also damp, dusty and shared with boilers and meters. We mount the NVR or DVR on a shelf or a small wall rack off the floor, away from the boiler's heat, on a battery backup, and in a locked cabinet when the cellar is shared with other tenants. The live view goes upstairs to the monitor over a single cable, so the box that actually holds the evidence never sits behind the counter where it can be carried out.

Shared risers and conduit paths

Many elevator buildings already have a telephone or cable-TV riser running floor to floor. Where the landlord or board allows it, we pull Cat6 up that path to a small PoE switch on each floor or every few floors, so no single camera cable has to travel the full height of the building. Cat6 carries power and video about 328 feet (100 meters) per run; a twelve-story building with the recorder in the cellar quickly passes that, so floor switches or fiber between floors are part of the design rather than an afterthought. Exterior runs, cellar runs exposed to damage and runs in commercial kitchens go in conduit.

Landmarked buildings and historic districts

If the building is an individual landmark or sits in a historic district, cameras on the street facades need a Landmarks Preservation Commission permit. The LPC Permit Guidebook lets staff approve security cameras when the installation meets its criteria: cameras well spaced, no more than two per 25 feet of street frontage on small buildings and three on large ones, attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, never through cast iron or decorative surfaces, and conduit concealed, with only about ten inches of visible conduit allowed and painted to match. The application needs the camera's cut sheet. We design landmarked jobs to those rules from the start, provide the cut sheets and photos, and wait for the permit before mounting anything on the primary facade. Anything outside the staff criteria can go to a public hearing, which is slower, so we try hard to stay inside them.

Vestibules, stair halls, mailrooms and roofs

Apartment building CCTV in the city comes down to a short list of choke points. The outer door and the vestibule between the two doors, where a camera inside the inner door records faces as people buzz in. The mailboxes and package area, where most theft complaints start. The stair at the first floor and the roof landing, because the roof door is how people get in from the next building. The cellar door, the laundry room and the trash or recycling area, which is where tenants' complaints about strangers and illegal dumping usually point. In an elevator building, a camera in the elevator lobby on the ground floor and one in each car are common requests. Cameras that look into individual apartments are not part of any plan we draw.

Stair halls are the hardest spot to light and the easiest to get wrong. A camera at the top of a flight looking down sees the top of every head; one at the landing looking toward the door sees faces. Hallway lighting on timers or motion sensors also matters, because a camera that switches between infrared and daylight every few minutes gives grainy, flickering footage. We note those fixtures on the survey and set the cameras to match.

Outdoor mounting on brick, stone and awnings

Outdoor CCTV installation on city buildings means fastening to a surface that is a hundred years old. On brick, we anchor into solid brick or mortar joints with the right anchors and a junction box so the cable connection is not exposed to rain. On stone, we avoid carved bands, cornices and lintels. Under awnings, we mount to the awning frame or the sign band only when it is solid enough, and run the cable inside the frame. Every exterior camera gets a weatherproof junction box or back box and a drip loop, because water in a connector is the single most common reason outdoor cameras die after a winter.

Taking over an old analog system in a large building

Many large Bronx, Upper Manhattan and Brooklyn buildings already have a CCTV system from the 2000s: a dozen or more analog cameras, coax up the risers and a DVR in the super's office that may or may not still record. Before quoting a new system we test each cable run, note which cameras still work, and check whether the old cable paths reach the places the board or owner now cares about. Good coax gets reused with HD analog cameras or Ethernet-over-coax adapters; damaged or water-soaked runs get replaced with Cat6. The old recorder comes out, the new one goes in somewhere secure, and every camera gets a new label that matches the camera map. Owners usually end up with a 4K-capable system on much of the cable they already paid for.

Who owns the footage, and who can see it

In a multi-unit building, the question of who can view CCTV footage matters as much as where the cameras go. In a rental, the landlord owns the system and decides who views it. In a co-op or condo, the board usually sets a written policy: who has live view (often the super or the doorman), who can review recordings (often the managing agent), and how a resident asks for footage of an incident. We set up the recorder to match that policy, with separate accounts for each role, so live view at the front desk does not come with permission to export or delete recordings. In a mixed-use building, the store's recorder and the building's recorder stay separate, so each party controls its own footage.

Co-op board and landlord approval

In a co-op or condo, hallways, lobbies and facades are common elements, so cameras there are a board decision. As attorney Ricardo M. Vera of Newman Ferrara LLP explained in a Cooperator News Q&A, condo documents typically bar owners from changing common elements without board approval, and cameras that record audio raise bigger concerns under New York Penal Law. Renters face the same thing with the landlord: a store tenant may install CCTV inside the leased space but usually needs written consent before drilling the facade or the shared hallway. We ask who has signed off before we schedule, and we do not install on common elements without it. This is general information, not legal advice; your board's documents and your lease control.

Commercial CCTV

Business CCTV Installation in NYC

Commercial CCTV installation in New York City is mostly small business work: delis, bodegas, pharmacies, restaurants, salons, laundromats, phone repair shops and the offices upstairs from them. Commercial CCTV systems for stores, restaurants, offices and multi-unit buildings run $1,500–$5,000+, depending on the number of cameras, the recorder, how much of the cable has to be concealed and how many locations share one app.

The corridors we work on most

  • Broadway in Washington Heights and Inwood: corner delis, bodegas and phone stores beside the 1-train entrances, many in 1910s–1920s apartment houses with stone fronts.
  • 125th Street in Harlem: national chains next to family stores; the 125th Street BID is one of the areas in the NYPD's camera integration pilot.
  • Fordham Road in the Bronx: apparel, sneaker and discount stores with deep sales floors and high theft pressure around the holidays.
  • Fulton Street and Flatbush Avenue in Brooklyn: busy retail strips with rolling gates, narrow frontages and stockrooms in the cellar.
  • Main Street in Flushing and Jamaica Avenue in Queens: dense mixed retail, restaurants and upstairs offices; the 109th Precinct in Flushing hosted the first NYPD camera registration pilot.
  • Staten Island's Richmond Avenue and Forest Avenue: strip-center stores with parking lots, where plate-readable views of the lot matter as much as the register.

Bodegas, delis and corner stores

Corner stores are the most common commercial CCTV job we do in the city, and they follow a pattern. Two or three cameras look down both sidewalks from under the awning so the entry approach is recorded from each street. One camera sits over the door at face height on the way in. One covers the register and the cash drawer from behind and above the clerk. Domes over each aisle and the cooler doors, one over the deli counter, one in the stockroom or cellar, and one at the back door or the cellar hatch. That is usually eight to twelve cameras for a typical corner store, which is why a 16-channel recorder is the sensible size even if you start with eight.

Field Notes

A Manhattan corner deli near the 157 Street station. This store sits on the corner of Broadway, in the ground floor of a stone-fronted apartment house, a few steps from the 157 Street subway entrance. The photos on this page are from that job. Outside, a white turret dome is tucked under the corner of the yellow awning, right below the sign band, where it can look down both frontages and is out of easy reach. A second camera rides a black steel wall arm off the stone facade at the far end of the storefront, and smaller cameras sit under the awning edge along both frontages. Inside, small domes sit on the drop-tile ceiling over the aisles, the snack racks and the sandwich counter, placed between the light panels so the fixtures do not wash out the image at night.

The owner wanted to see the whole store from the counter, so a wall-mounted TV over the shelving shows the multi-camera grid: aisles, the register, the cooler wall and the entrance in one view. That monitor does two jobs. Staff can watch the floor while they make sandwiches, and every customer who walks in sees that the store is recorded. On a corner store, that visible monitor often does as much as the cameras themselves.

Pharmacies, phone stores, laundromats and liquor stores

Each small-store type has its own weak spot. Pharmacies need the counter, the front-end shelves of health and beauty products and the delivery door. Phone and electronics stores need tight views of the display cases and the repair bench, where the most expensive items sit. Laundromats run long hours with one attendant, so owners want the change machines, the folding tables and the door, plus a phone view to check on the store from home. Liquor stores need the register and the top-shelf wall, where bottles worth a hundred dollars sit within arm's reach. We plan each of these around the items that actually go missing, which the owner usually knows better than anyone.

Why a recorder in the back beats a box under the counter

The most common mistake we see in city stores is a DVR on the shelf under the register, beside the monitor. A burglar who knows anything about cameras takes the recorder first, and the footage leaves with it. Our corner-store installs put the recorder in the cellar, a locked office or a hidden enclosure, run one display cable to the monitor at the counter, and set up the phone app so the owner can pull clips even if the store is closed. It costs little extra at install time and it is the difference between a police report with video and one without.

Holiday season, Black Friday and after a break-in

Retail CCTV calls peak twice a year: in the weeks before the holiday shopping season, and in the days after a store has been hit. Before the holidays, the priority is the register, the high-value shelves near the door and the stockroom where seasonal inventory piles up. Owners who book in October or early November get the system in and tested before Black Friday traffic; owners who call the week of Thanksgiving are competing for the same install slots as everyone else. After a break-in, the first job is a look at what the old system actually recorded. Usually the answer is that the recorder overwrote the night within days, the clock was hours off, or the camera over the door was pointed at the ceiling after someone bumped it. We fix those first, then add what was missing.

New stores and build-outs

A new business opening is the cheapest time to install CCTV, because the ceiling is open and the walls are not painted yet. If you are building out a storefront, a restaurant or an office, bring us in when the electrician is roughing in. We pull camera cable to each location, leave it coiled and labeled, and come back after the ceiling and finishes are done to mount cameras and set up the recorder. That avoids opening a finished ceiling later and keeps the cable completely hidden.

Restaurants, salons and offices

Restaurants want the host stand, the bar, the register, the back door and the delivery pickup shelf; kitchens get conduit and cameras rated for grease and steam. Salons and medical or dental offices usually need the entrance, reception and the cash area only, with treatment rooms left alone. Offices want the suite door, reception and the server closet. In every case we design the views around a question: if something happens here, which camera gives you the face, and which one gives you the time and direction of travel?

Warehouses, garages and lots

Industrial blocks in the South Bronx, Sunset Park, Long Island City and Maspeth bring loading docks, high ceilings and long fence lines. These jobs use bullet and varifocal cameras with longer infrared range, plate-readable views at the gate, and fiber or PoE extenders where runs pass 300 feet. See our warehouse camera page for that work in more depth.

Parking garages, lots and construction sites

Garage and lot CCTV is built around the entry lane. One camera framed tight on the lane at plate height and an angle the plate can be read from, one wider camera showing the car and the driver, and overview cameras covering the rows where break-ins happen. Garage ramps under buildings are often dark and wet, so we use cameras rated for the conditions and mount them out of the way of tall vans. Construction sites need something different: temporary power, cameras on poles or the hoist, a recorder in a locked job box, and a plan to move cameras as the building rises. Our parking lot camera page covers lot layouts in more depth.

Owners with several stores or buildings

Many New York owners run more than one location: two delis in different neighborhoods, a few walk-ups across two boroughs, or a restaurant group. For multi-site CCTV we keep each location on its own recorder, so a broken internet line at one site does not affect the others, and put all the sites in one app on the owner's phone. Every site uses the same camera naming, the same retention target and the same password rules, so the manager who covers a shift at the second store already knows where to look. When the owner opens a new location, the system matches the others from the first day.

Compliance callouts for NYC businesses

  • No cameras in restrooms, locker rooms or changing rooms. New York Labor Law §203-c bars employers from video recording in those rooms. We will not install there.
  • Audio is a separate decision. New York's eavesdropping law (Penal Law §250.05) makes it a crime to record a conversation unless at least one party consents. Cameras with microphones can be ordered with audio off, and we leave it off unless the owner decides otherwise after taking advice.
  • Biometric signs. Under NYC Administrative Code §22-1202, in force since July 2021, retail stores, food and drink establishments and entertainment venues that collect customers' biometric identifiers, such as face geometry, must post a clear sign at customer entrances and may not sell or share that data.
  • Unlawful surveillance. Penal Law §250.45 covers recording people where they have a reasonable expectation of privacy, such as dressing or undressing. Camera views stop at your property's private spaces.
  • Police access is voluntary. The NYPD's camera program lets a business register or integrate its CCTV with the local precinct for free, and the owner chooses how and when police can see it.

None of this is legal advice. It is the short list we walk through with every store owner so the system we install is one they can defend.

Storage math, H.264 vs H.265 and the PoE budget

Three numbers decide whether a CCTV system keeps the footage you need. The first is bitrate: a 4MP camera might send 2–4 Mbps, an 8MP camera 4–8 Mbps, and a busy aisle sends more than an empty cellar. The second is compression. H.265 typically stores the same picture in roughly half the space of H.264, which is why we set every compatible camera to H.265. The third is the retention target. Multiply bitrate by seconds in a day and days to keep, and the drive size falls out; we do that math for your camera list rather than guessing.

On IP systems the PoE power budget matters as much. A recorder or switch has a total wattage it can deliver; a turret camera may draw 5–7 watts, an infrared bullet more, and an active-deterrence or PTZ camera much more. We add up the cameras and leave headroom, so the night the infrared turns on is not the night the switch drops a port.

What cables are used for CCTV installation

The cable is the part of a CCTV system you will least want to replace, so we choose it for the next camera, not just this one. Cat6 is our standard for IP cameras: it carries video and PoE power up to about 100 meters, and it is the cable most building owners already recognize. Cat6A is worth the extra cost in new buildings and long runs near heavy electrical equipment, because it is better shielded from interference and leaves room for future high-bandwidth cameras. RG59 coax, often bundled with a power pair as "siamese" cable, is the HD analog standard and what most older city systems already have in the walls. Fiber goes between buildings, between a store and a detached garage, or up a tall riser where copper would pass its distance limit. Outdoor and cellar runs use jacket ratings suited to the location, and cable in plenum ceilings is plenum-rated.

PTZ, fisheye, thermal and license plate cameras

Specialty cameras have their place, but they are not where most New York systems should start. A PTZ camera can pan and zoom across a large lot or a building plaza, but it only records where it is pointed at that moment, so it should be added to fixed cameras, not replace them. A fisheye or panoramic camera covers a whole small room from the center of the ceiling and works well over a register area or a small lobby, though faces at the edges are distorted. Thermal cameras detect people in total darkness and through light fog on long fence lines; in a city they mostly make sense for rail yards, large lots and industrial perimeters. License plate recognition cameras need a narrow, well-lit lane, the right angle and the right shutter speed; we quote them after seeing the driveway or garage ramp, because a plate camera placed at the wrong angle reads nothing.

Camera placement: height, angle and field of view

Where CCTV cameras should be installed is the question behind every other question. Our rules of thumb in city buildings: entry cameras at roughly 7 to 9 feet, aimed slightly down at the door so they catch a face under a hat brim, not the top of a head. Overview cameras high in a corner, covering the room and the direction people walk. Register cameras above and behind the clerk, framing the counter, the drawer and the customer's hands. Outdoor cameras under an eave, awning or sign band, out of reach and out of the rain. And every camera aimed so that the brightest part of the scene, usually a glass door or a window, is not filling the frame.

Field of view is a trade. A wide lens of around 2.8mm sees a whole small store but makes a face at the far wall tiny. A 4mm or 6mm lens, or a varifocal camera, narrows the view and gives more pixels on the subject. Good design uses a few wide cameras for the overview and a few tighter ones on the door, the register and the gate. That is how a system with eight cameras gives better evidence than one with sixteen.

Night footage on a city block

New York is rarely dark, and that causes its own problems. Streetlights, illuminated signs and storefront LED strips throw uneven light across a sidewalk, and a camera's infrared can bounce off a nearby awning, sign or wall and wash out the image. Under an awning, a camera's own infrared reflecting off the awning fabric is a common cause of foggy night video. We test views after dark when the job allows it, turn infrared down or off where ambient light is enough, use color night cameras where there is steady light, and angle cameras so signs and headlights are not in the center of the frame.

Every recorder we install gets a strong unique admin password, current firmware, and its own user accounts for each person who views it. Where the store or building has a network, cameras live on the recorder's own PoE ports or on a separate VLAN rather than on the same network as the register or tenants' Wi-Fi. Remote access goes through the maker's app, not an open port on the router.

What AI Gets Wrong About CCTV Installation in New York City

"A CCTV system needs Wi-Fi or internet to record"

AI summaries often blur CCTV together with consumer cloud cameras, and conclude that no internet means no recording. That is true of many battery and Wi-Fi cameras that upload clips to a server. It is not true of a wired CCTV system.

A DVR or NVR records from its cameras over its own cables to its own hard drive. If the store's internet goes down, or a walk-up has no internet in the cellar at all, the recorder keeps recording. The internet is only needed to watch remotely or to receive phone alerts.

In New York City this matters more than people think, because the recorder is often in a cellar with no Wi-Fi signal. We either run one network cable from the recorder to the router or leave it offline and show the owner how to view on the monitor and export to a USB drive.

"Wireless cameras are the simple choice for apartments"

The common AI answer is that wireless means no wiring and an easy install. Wireless cameras still need power at every camera, and an outdoor camera on a facade needs an outlet or a cable either way.

The bigger issue is the building. Brick, stone and plaster on metal lath eat Wi-Fi signal. A camera two floors and three masonry walls from the router drops frames or disconnects, and the gap usually shows up exactly when something happens.

For a single room in a modern apartment, Wi-Fi can be fine. For a stair hall, a cellar, a yard or a storefront, a wired camera is the reliable option, and in a pre-war building it is rarely much harder to install than people fear.

"You can put a camera anywhere on your own building"

AI answers tend to treat placement as a technical question. In New York it is just as often a permission question. A store tenant does not own the facade, a condo owner does not own the hallway, and a landmarked building's facade needs a permit.

The Landmarks Preservation Commission's permit guidance sets limits on spacing, attachment and visible conduit, and co-op and condo documents usually require board approval for anything on common elements. Pointing a camera into a neighbor's window or a private space is a separate problem altogether.

We build the approval step into the schedule. Most boards and landlords approve a clear, written camera plan quickly; what they reject is a camera that appeared on the facade without asking.

"More megapixels always means better footage"

An AI overview will usually tell you to buy the highest resolution you can afford. Resolution helps, but only when the lens frames the right area and the light works. A 4K camera with a wide lens across a 40-foot sales floor still gives you a small face.

Under a New York awning, backlight is the real enemy: a bright sidewalk behind a dark doorway turns faces into silhouettes. Wide dynamic range, the right mounting height and a camera aimed inward at the door often beat a higher-megapixel camera aimed at the street.

We choose lens and placement first, then resolution. That is why a mix of 4MP turrets and one or two 8MP or varifocal cameras for the door and register is often the better design than eight 4K cameras.

"Cloud storage is the modern standard for security cameras"

Cloud plans are common for consumer cameras, so AI tools often present them as standard. They come with a monthly fee per camera and depend on upload speed, and a city store with eight to twelve cameras would need a strong upstream connection to send all of it.

Our systems record locally with $0 monthly fees, and the recorder is the evidence vault. The protection against a stolen recorder is placement: a locked cellar cabinet or a hidden enclosure, not the cloud.

If an owner wants an off-site copy of key clips, the apps can export to a phone or email in minutes. For most New York businesses, that plus a well-hidden recorder covers the risk without a subscription.

"CCTV installation takes a couple of hours"

Generic cost guides quote install times from homes with attics and vinyl siding. A four-camera job on a drop ceiling in an open store can be quick. A twelve-camera walk-up where every cable has to be fished through plaster or routed through a cellar is a full day or more.

Building rules add time too: many co-ops and managed buildings allow work only on weekdays during set hours, need a certificate of insurance on file, and want the super present when we open the cellar.

So we quote the time after the survey, not before. We book the visit within 2 hours of your request, but we never promise same-day installation, because the honest answer depends on the walls.

"Theft is out of control, so cameras alone will stop it"

Some AI answers still repeat the retail theft surge headlines of a few years ago. The current numbers are better: the NYPD's CompStat report through November 8, 2026 shows retail theft down 12.2 percent and burglary down 14.1 percent from the same point last year.

Better is not zero. Nearly 40,000 retail theft complaints in ten months is still a heavy load on small stores, and cameras are what turn a complaint into a case. Police can only use what the recorder caught clearly, with the right time stamp.

Cameras deter some theft, especially with a visible monitor, but their main job is evidence. As Barbara Askins of the 125th Street BID put it when the NYPD camera program launched, "if you shoplift on 125th Street, you will be seen, and it can lead to an arrest." (NYC Mayor's Office, May 16, 2024)

Will my CCTV system keep recording during a power outage?
+

Only if it has backup power. We recommend a battery backup (UPS) on the recorder and the PoE switch, which keeps the system recording through short outages and protects the hard drive from sudden shutdowns. Run time depends on the number of cameras and the battery size.

Can a renter install a CCTV camera outside an apartment door in NYC?
+

Usually not without permission. The hallway belongs to the landlord, or to the co-op or condo as a common element, so a camera there needs written approval. A camera inside your own apartment, or a peephole-style door camera the building allows, is a simpler route.

Will a CCTV recorder in a cellar with no internet still record?
+

Yes. A wired CCTV system records to the DVR or NVR hard drive over its own cables, with or without internet. Internet is only needed to view the cameras on a phone away from the property or to get motion alerts.

Can I turn on facial recognition on my store cameras in NYC?
+

Some cameras offer face detection features, but New York City Administrative Code section 22-1202 requires retail stores, food and drink businesses and entertainment venues that collect customers’ biometric identifiers to post a clear sign at customer entrances, and it bars selling or sharing that data. Get legal advice before enabling it; we set cameras up with those features off by default.
