The Neighborhood

Why Cobble Hill Properties Need Alarm Installation

Cobble Hill is about forty blocks of northwest Brooklyn. Atlantic Avenue bounds it on the north and Court Street on the east, Degraw Street closes it on the south, and the trench of the Brooklyn–Queens Expressway marks the west, with some definitions adding a rectangle running east to Hoyt Street between Wyckoff and Degraw. Brooklyn Heights sits across Atlantic, Boerum Hill across Court, Carroll Gardens below Degraw and the Columbia Street Waterfront beyond the highway. The neighbourhood is in Community District 6, carries the 11201 and 11231 ZIP codes and is policed from the 76th Precinct station at 191 Union Street. Its name was only adopted in 1959, borrowed from a hill that once stood near the corner of Atlantic Avenue and Court Street and was levelled long ago.

The houses came quickly once the City of Brooklyn grew south of Atlantic Avenue in 1834 and the South Ferry began running to Whitehall Street in 1836. Rowhouses went up from about 1835, and the transformation was nearly complete by 1860. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Cobble Hill Historic District on December 20, 1969 (its designation report carries the date December 30), covering about twenty-two blocks between Atlantic, Court, Degraw and Hicks, and extended it on June 7, 1988. What makes the neighbourhood unusual for an alarm installer is not the age of the brick, though; plenty of Brooklyn is as old. It is how much of Cobble Hill was built around shared outdoor space.

Alfred Tredway White, a merchant and philanthropist, built three experiments in housing for working families here in the late 1870s. The Home Buildings at Hicks and Baltic wrapped an L-shaped courtyard that took more than half the lot; the Tower Buildings on Hicks between Warren and Baltic wrapped a U-shaped one; and both reached their apartments by exterior staircases and open balconies, chosen for light, air and fire safety. Behind them, the Workingmen's Cottages on Warren Place form a mews of 34 small Gothic houses, averaging about twelve and a half feet wide and 32 feet deep, facing each other across an English garden and closed off from the rest of the neighbourhood by a gate. A few blocks south, Verandah Place, a lane only twenty feet wide, began as a service alley; some of its small brick houses were first built as stables. And every ordinary rowhouse block has its own interior of gardens, one touching the next. That is a lot of doors and windows that open onto ground other people walk across.

Local police reporting points at the same thing everywhere in the city, which is the opening nobody was watching. In August 2015 DNAinfo listed a week of break-ins in the 76th Precinct: an unlocked rear door on the 300 block of Smith Street, broken door locks on Baltic Street near the Boerum Hill border and on the 400 block of Hicks Street, a building basement on Huntington Street, and an apartment on Union Street entered by removing an air conditioner from a rear window. The precinct had logged 76 burglaries from January 1 to August 2 that year against 47 the year before. A few months earlier, the precinct's commanding officer passed on to the Cobble Hill Association what one arrested burglar told officers: Carroll Gardens and Cobble Hill were easy targets because many residents were known to leave their doors unlocked. In 2013 Brooklyn Paper reported a man who jimmied a building's front door on Baltic Street between Smith and Hoyt with a screwdriver and took a package from the hallway.

Read together, those reports describe an ordinary pattern rather than an exotic one. Doors were forced or simply found unlocked, a rear window became a door once the air conditioner came out, a basement was entered from below and a building's street door gave way to a screwdriver. None of that needed a sophisticated burglar, and none of it would have been stopped by a camera pointed at a garden. All of it would have been reported, within seconds, by a contact on the opening that was used and an interior detector behind it, which is why the designs on this page start at the walls.

Two newer facts change the picture again. The Long Island College Hospital, founded in 1857 and closed in 2014, has been replaced by River Park, a group of condominium buildings by Fortis: 5 River Park at 347 Henry Street, fifteen storeys and twenty-five homes, was finished by late 2022, with taller towers planned at 350 Hicks Street and on Pacific Street and an NYU Langone ambulatory building at 339 Hicks. So the same few blocks now hold 1840s rowhouses, 1870s courtyard flats, a mews of cottages and brand-new glass condominiums, often within sight of a construction crane. And the commercial edge is busy all day and empty at night: Court Street and Smith Street shops and restaurants, and the Middle Eastern grocers and shops that have made Atlantic Avenue a destination since the 1940s.

Alarm Acting Up in Cobble Hill: Same-Day Repair

Repair calls from Cobble Hill follow the shape of the homes. A contact on a balcony door in a courtyard building stops reporting after a wet winter because the door has dropped on its hinges. A wireless sensor on a cottage's rear window has a flat battery and the keypad has been chirping for a week. A motion detector someone added in a garden-floor kitchen trips every afternoon when the sun comes off the neighbouring garden wall. A shop panel on Court Street reads a communication fault because its old radio has lost its network. Most single faults like these are traced and fixed within one to two hours of the technician arriving.

Out-of-warranty service is billed at $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum ($585), which covers diagnosis, realigning or replacing contacts, batteries and failed devices, re-terminating cable, reprogramming and a signal test to the monitoring station. Parts are quoted once we know what failed. If another company installed the system, a photo of the panel label and the keypad before the visit lets us bring parts that fit.

  • Cottages and lane houses: flat batteries in rear-window sensors, a front-door contact knocked out of line by a new weather strip, a keypad left in a sunny spot whose display has faded.
  • Courtyard and gallery flats: exterior doors that have sagged on their hinges, contacts corroded by condensation, detectors re-aimed by a painter.
  • Rowhouses: swollen sash that no longer closes far enough for the magnet, cellar contacts damaged during boiler work, a glass-break detector covered by new curtains.
  • New condos: devices registered to the developer's account, communicators never activated, a keypad wired but never programmed.
  • Shops: panels on a lost cellular network, codes belonging to staff who left long ago, a rear-door contact broken by deliveries.

If the keypad is beeping at night, most panels let you silence the trouble tone with your code while the fault waits for morning. Call and we will tell you whether the home is still protected in the meantime and book the next free arrival window.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Cobble Hill's Architecture

Cobble Hill packs an unusual variety of housing into a few blocks. These are the types we plan around, and what each one asks of an alarm.

Warren Place cottages

Thirty-four narrow two-storey brick houses facing a gated garden. One front door, a handful of windows, a small rear yard, and neighbours passing the front all day.

Tower and Home Buildings

Romanesque Revival courtyard flats from 1876–1879, reached by exterior stairs and open balconies. Front doors are outdoors; windows face either the street or the courtyard.

Verandah Place and lane houses

Small brick houses on a twenty-foot lane, some converted from stables, facing Cobble Hill Park. Wide original openings and very little setback from the lane.

Historic district rowhouses

Greek Revival, Italianate and Queen Anne houses built mostly from the 1830s, with stoops, garden floors, cellars and rear walls that face the block-interior gardens.

River Park condominiums

New buildings on the former hospital site, starting with fifteen-storey 5 River Park on Henry Street: large glass, terraces, a building front desk and a developer's pre-wire.

Mixed-use buildings on the avenues

Shops with flats above along Court Street, Smith Street and Atlantic Avenue, where the business and the apartments share a facade but keep different hours.

Cottages: small homes with a public front

A house twelve and a half feet wide has very few ways in, which makes it easy to cover completely and tempting to over-equip. The front door gets a recessed or slim surface contact, each ground-floor window gets its own contact, and the rear door and any reachable rear window are treated exactly like the front, because a small yard is still a way in. Inside, one well-placed detector on the stair or in the main room covers most of the house. What we do not do in a mews is put a motion sensor, a light trigger or an alarm-linked camera facing the shared garden. Neighbours walk past at all hours; anything that reacts to them will either be switched off or start an argument. The keypad goes where it cannot be seen through the glass of the front door, and the siren stays indoors, sized for a small house rather than a warehouse.

Courtyard flats: doors that open outdoors

In a gallery-access building the apartment door is an exterior door. It sees rain, cold and condensation, it moves with the seasons, and it is passed by neighbours on the balcony. We use contacts rated for exterior conditions, set the gap tolerance for a door that shifts between summer and winter, and put the detector inside the apartment where only someone who has already come in can trip it. Windows that face the courtyard get contacts if they can be reached from a balcony or stair, and glass-break if they are large. Because the building is shared, nothing goes on the common balconies or stairs without the owners' or association's agreement, and in most cases nothing needs to.

Lane houses and converted stables

Converted stables often keep wide openings, sometimes a pair of carriage doors or a large window where a door once was, set right on the lane. Wide doors are protected with a heavy-duty contact at the meeting point and, where the doors flex, a second contact at the top so that someone prising one leaf does not slip past. Large glass that replaced an old opening gets glass-break detection. Because the lane faces a public park, we keep outdoor sensors out of the design entirely and let cameras, if the owner wants them, record rather than trigger.

Rowhouses that back onto gardens

The street front of a Cobble Hill rowhouse is in full view and usually inside the historic district; the rear faces a garden that touches four or five other gardens. That rear wall is where most of the design effort goes. The garden-level door and windows always get contacts. Glass doors and big rear windows get glass-break. Upper rear windows that can be reached from an extension roof, a fence or a fire escape get contacts too. Indoors, a detector on the stair between the garden floor and the parlor floor means anyone who gets in from the garden has to pass it. The siren stays inside, because an exterior box on a landmarked front is new equipment on a primary facade.

Historic district rules for anything outside

Most of the old housing stock between Atlantic, Court, Degraw and Hicks is inside the Cobble Hill Historic District, so any equipment added to the outside of a building there is a Landmarks Preservation Commission matter. In practice an intrusion alarm rarely needs anything outside at all: contacts, detectors, keypads, the siren, the panel and the communicator all live indoors. We keep it that way by default. Where an owner wants a camera outside, we discuss the rear wall first, keep cable concealed and leave the street front alone, and we point owners to the Commission's own guidance rather than guessing at approvals.

New condominiums on the hospital site

A new condominium unit is the opposite of a cottage: drywall, a developer's pre-wire, building security in the lobby and a lot of glass. The work is mostly connecting and finishing. We test whatever low-voltage cable the developer left at the entry and at each window, mount the panel and keypad, add a terrace-door contact and glass-break for the window walls, connect a cellular communicator and register the system with the monitoring station. A high floor does not make glass irrelevant; terraces and connected balconies can be reached, and the front door to the corridor is still the most used way in.

Many owners who protect the openings also want to see the gate, the lane or the garden. Our Cobble Hill security camera installation page explains how cameras can record shared space without triggering the alarm, and both systems can live in one app.

Shared Ground

Alarms for Homes That Face Courtyards, Gardens and Lanes

Most alarm advice assumes a house with a private yard and a front door on the street. Much of Cobble Hill does not fit that picture, and the difference decides what works. When a window faces a shared garden, a balcony or a lane, the people outside it are mostly neighbours with every right to be there. A sensor that reacts to presence outside the walls cannot tell them from an intruder, and an alarm that cries wolf in front of thirty households is switched off by spring.

So the rule we follow is envelope first. Every door and every window that can be reached gets its own contact, which only reports when that opening actually moves. Large panes get acoustic glass-break detection, which listens for breaking glass inside the room and ignores people talking or walking outside. Interior motion detectors are aimed into rooms and along stairs, away from glass that looks onto shared space, so that only someone already inside can trip them. Cameras covering a garden or gate record and notify you on your phone if you want them to, but they do not set off the siren or call the monitoring station by themselves.

The second rule is about who hears what. In a courtyard or a mews a siren is a public event, so we tune the sequence: a short entry delay at the main door, an instant alarm on openings nobody normally uses, a push notification to the household the moment a sensor trips, and the central station call after that. The siren itself is inside the home, loud enough to drive an intruder out and to be heard next door, without an exterior box sounding across a garden. We also keep the owners' or association's rules in view. A shared gate, a common balcony or a courtyard wall belongs to more than one household, and we do not fit anything to common property without the agreement of whoever controls it.

The third rule is habit. The burglar who told the 76th Precinct that Cobble Hill was easy because doors were left unlocked was describing behaviour, not hardware. An alarm that arms with one touch when you leave, a lock that bolts itself when the system arms, and a phone alert when a door has been left open are worth more in a neighbourhood like this than an extra sensor on a window nobody can reach.

Cameras that record without setting anything off

Owners on a courtyard or lane often want a camera, and there is no conflict with the approach above as long as the camera's role is clear. We configure garden, gate and lane cameras to record continuously or on motion and, if you like, to send a phone notification you can mute while you are home. What they do not do is arm the siren or call the monitoring station by themselves. When an actual alarm comes from a contact or an interior detector, the recording from the same minute is there to show what happened, which is the moment a camera is most useful. Where a camera would look into a neighbour's windows or garden, we aim it away or use privacy masking so it only covers your own door and path.

Living beside a construction site

The River Park work on the old hospital blocks has put cranes, hoists, deliveries and site fencing next to rowhouses and courtyard buildings for years, and more towers are planned on Hicks and Pacific Streets. A long construction project changes three things for an alarm. Vibration from piling, demolition and heavy trucks can upset shock sensors and loose window contacts. Dust can trip smoke detectors. And the site itself changes what can be reached from outside: a sidewalk shed, a hoist or a fence line can bring a window that was safely out of reach within a step or two. We plan for all three by leaning on contacts and acoustic glass-break rather than shock sensors, by reviewing smoke detector types near windows that are opened during dusty work, and by walking the outside of the home again whenever the site's footprint changes. Owners who live beside a site can also ask for a short review after each major phase, so the system is tuned to the next stage of the work rather than the last.

Working with owners' groups, boards and managers

Shared ground usually comes with shared decision-making. A mews may have an owners' arrangement for the gate and garden; a courtyard building converted to condominiums has a board and a managing agent; a new condominium has house rules for contractors, insurance certificates and working hours. We ask about these at the first call, because the answer changes the schedule rather than the design. Work inside your own home rarely needs anyone else's sign-off, but access through a shared gate or lobby, parking for our van, noise during drilling and any cable that has to pass through a common area all do.

For buildings that want it, we provide a short scope of work naming every device, where it goes and confirming that nothing will be fixed to common property, along with our license number and an insurance certificate. When an owners' group wants something for everyone, such as an entry station on a shared gate, we quote it to the group as a separate job, so no single household carries the cost or the decision.

A room-by-room checklist for a home on shared ground

When we survey a cottage, a courtyard flat or a garden-floor apartment, we go through the same list. At the main door: a contact, a keypad out of sight of the glass and of anyone standing outside, and a short entry delay. At every other door, including the one to the yard or balcony that nobody uses in winter: a contact set to alarm instantly, because a door that is never used for coming home should never need a delay. At each window: a contact if it can be reached from the ground, a stair, a balcony, a fence or a neighbouring roof, and nothing if it truly cannot, because a sensor on an unreachable window adds cost without adding protection.

In each main room, we ask what the glass looks onto. If it faces shared ground, motion detectors are aimed away from it and an acoustic glass-break detector covers the panes. If it faces only the street from an upper floor, the room may need nothing beyond the hallway detector. On the stair, a single detector catches anyone moving between floors. At the panel location, we look for a closet or cupboard away from the doors, so the control box cannot be reached and smashed during the entry delay. Finally, at the edge of the property, we list what belongs to the household and what belongs to everyone, so nothing is fixed to common property without the right permission.

The result is usually a smaller device count than people expect, and a system that never reacts to the neighbours. That second point is what keeps it switched on.

Commercial Alarm Installation

Business Alarm Installation on Court Street, Smith Street and Atlantic Avenue

Cobble Hill's commerce runs along its edges. Court Street, on the east, is the main shopping street, with grocers, bookshops, cafés, boutiques and the 1922 South Brooklyn Savings Institution building that Trader Joe's moved into in 2008. Northern Smith Street, a block further east, is known as Restaurant Row. Atlantic Avenue, on the north, holds one of the city's best-known clusters of Middle Eastern grocers and shops; Sahadi's, a family business that moved to the avenue in 1948, is the most famous. Staubitz Market, a butcher, has traded on Court Street since 1917.

These are small, deep shops with glass at the front, a stockroom or kitchen behind and often a cellar below. In December 2012 DNAinfo reported a burglary at a Court Street jewellery boutique around 6 a.m., just before the morning rush: the front gate was damaged and the glass door shattered. That is the front-of-shop pattern. The back-of-shop pattern shows up in the 2015 precinct list, where a Smith Street home was entered through an unlocked rear door, and in the basement break-in on Huntington Street. A business alarm here has to cover both.

Our commercial designs on the avenues start with the obvious openings and then add what the neighbourhood's buildings require. Front glass gets glass-break detection sized to the room. The front door and any gate get contacts. The rear door gets a heavy-duty contact and the stockroom a motion detector. Cellars, where imported stock or a prep kitchen often lives, get contacts on every door and, where the cellar shares a wall with the next building, a shock or vibration sensor on that wall. Early-morning deliveries are handled with time-limited codes that work only in the delivery window, and the owner receives an alert if nobody has armed the system by closing time.

Grocers and importers on Atlantic Avenue

The avenue's grocers and importers hold more stock than their storefronts suggest, much of it in cellars and back rooms reached by stairs, lifts or sidewalk doors. That stock is the reason a cellar gets its own area on the panel, armed whenever the cellar is closed even if the shop floor is open, with a motion detector at the foot of the stairs and contacts on every way in. Owners who run long hours appreciate a system that lets the cellar stay armed during trading, so a door left open at the back is noticed immediately rather than at closing time.

Offices, studios and clinics above the shops

The upper floors of buildings on Court Street and Atlantic Avenue hold small offices, therapy and medical practices, design studios and classrooms as well as apartments. These tenants share a street door and stair with everyone else in the building and often keep files, computers and equipment worth protecting. Their alarms work like a small apartment system with business features: a contact on the suite door and any window onto a fire escape or rear roof, a detector inside, codes for each member of staff, and a report of who opened and closed each day. Where the practice keeps records that must be secured, we place a contact on the records room door and set it to report at any hour, separate from the main arming schedule.

Restaurants on Restaurant Row

A Smith Street restaurant is busy late and empty by early morning, which is the opposite rhythm to a shop. Staff leave in ones and twos after midnight, deliveries arrive before the first cook, and kitchens run hot. We set the closing routine so the last person out can arm the dining room and kitchen together from one keypad by the exit they actually use, and we put the owner's phone on the list for a closing reminder if the system is still disarmed at a set hour. Kitchen detectors are placed away from heat, steam and grease, and smoke detection for the kitchen is planned with the fire alarm side rather than the burglar side. Every employee gets an individual code that can be deleted the day they leave, which is what makes the event log worth reading when something goes missing.

Mixed-use buildings add one more rule. The shop and the apartments upstairs keep different hours and must never share an arming sequence, so the business gets its own panel or partition, its own codes and its own monitoring account, and the residential door is never part of the shop's system.

Compliance notes for Cobble Hill businesses

  • Installer licensing: New York State's General Business Law Article 6-D requires anyone in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems to hold a Department of State license; ours is #12000287431.
  • Historic district storefronts: shops inside the Cobble Hill Historic District, which runs to Court Street and Atlantic Avenue, follow Landmarks Preservation Commission rules for anything added to the exterior; we keep sounders and conduit inside.
  • Fire alarm work: smoke and heat detection in restaurants and assembly spaces falls under the NYC Fire Code and Building Code and is designed separately; see our fire alarm installation in Cobble Hill page.
  • Egress: alarm contacts never lock a door. Electric locking on an exit is an access control matter under the Building Code's egress rules.

Alarm Installation in Cobble Hill: Questions Answered

These are the questions Cobble Hill households and shopkeepers ask once they see how much of their home faces shared space, new construction or a busy avenue.

Courtyards, mews and shared gardens

Our cottage faces a gated garden that thirty-odd neighbours walk through. Is an outdoor motion sensor ever usable there?

Almost never as an alarm trigger. An outdoor detector cannot tell a neighbour carrying shopping from someone trying your window, and in a shared garden it would trip many times a day. Either the household learns to ignore it or it gets switched off, and both outcomes make the system weaker.

Instead we put contacts on the openings that face the garden and a detector inside the cottage. If you want to know who is in the garden, a camera can record and send a notification to your phone without arming anything, and you can mute those notifications while you are home.

Should a house on a shared garden have a siren at all, or only a silent alert to our phones?

Keep a siren, but put it inside. A silent-only system relies on someone reacting in time, while a loud interior siren usually ends a break-in within seconds because the intruder cannot work in the noise. Indoors, it is heard next door but does not blast across the whole garden.

What we change is the order of events. Your phones get a notification the instant a sensor trips, then the siren sounds after the entry delay at the main door or immediately on an opening you never use, and the monitoring station calls after that. That order gives you a chance to cancel a mistake before the whole courtyard knows about it.

Can our alarm watch the mews gate, or does that need every other owner's agreement?

A gate that serves the whole mews belongs to more than one household, so fitting anything to it is a decision for whoever controls the shared property, not for one owner. We will not drill a common gate or wall on one household's say-so.

What one household can do is protect its own front door, windows and rear yard completely, which covers every way into the home. If the owners want the gate itself monitored or fitted with an entry system, we can quote it for the group, with alerts going to whoever the owners choose.

Our apartment door opens onto an open-air balcony instead of an indoor hallway. Does the door contact need to be different?

Yes. A door that faces the weather moves more through the year and collects condensation, so we use a contact rated for exterior conditions and set the alignment for winter and summer positions. A contact chosen for an indoor hallway door tends to fault the first cold month.

We also place the keypad so it cannot be read from the balcony through any glass in or beside the door, and set a short entry delay, because a neighbour passing on the gallery should never be able to watch you type your code.

Our flat has windows on both the street and the courtyard. Which side needs more protection?

Usually the courtyard side. Street windows above the ground floor are often out of reach, while courtyard windows may sit beside an open stair or a balcony that any resident or visitor can use. Windows that can be reached from a stair, balcony or roof get contacts; large panes get glass-break; windows that cannot be reached are covered by the interior detector.

We check each window from outside during the survey rather than assuming, because two windows on the same wall can be very different once a stair landing or a ledge is involved.

Lanes, stables and garden floors

Our former stable still has a pair of wide carriage doors onto the lane. How is an opening like that alarmed?

With a heavy-duty contact at the point where the two leaves meet and, if the doors flex, a second contact near the top, so prising one leaf back cannot open a gap without reporting. Where only one leaf is ever used, the fixed leaf gets its own contact as well.

If the carriage doors have been replaced with glass, glass-break detection covers the panes. Inside, a detector aimed away from the doors' glass watches the room behind them, so a passer-by on the lane is never in its field.

Our garden apartment has a wall of glass doors onto a garden that touches four others. What protects glass like that best?

Two layers. Each door gets a contact so the alarm knows when one is opened, and an acoustic glass-break detector in the room listens for the particular sound of a pane breaking, so the alarm still reacts if someone breaks the glass without opening the door.

We test the glass-break detector with a simulator at the far end of the glass during installation, adjust its sensitivity for the size of the room, and keep motion detectors pointed away from the glass so that sunlight and movement in the garden do not trigger them.

We run a window air conditioner in a rear window all summer. How is that opening protected?

The 2015 precinct report included an apartment on Union Street entered by removing an air conditioner from a rear window, so it is a fair worry. A contact on the sash only helps if the sash moves, and many units are wedged in with the sash fixed.

We fit brackets or blocks so the sash cannot be raised further, place the contact so it reports if the unit is pushed in and the sash lifts, and make sure the room is covered by the interior detector or a glass-break detector. In autumn, when the unit comes out, the contact goes back to normal duty.

New condominiums and living beside construction

The developer of our new River Park unit left wiring in the walls. Can it become a working alarm?

Often, yes. Developers commonly leave low-voltage cable at the entry door, at a keypad location and sometimes at windows. We test each run, label it, and connect it to a new panel; where a run is missing or damaged we add a wireless device instead of opening finished walls.

Bring us whatever documentation came with the unit. If there is a building management system or a separate fire alarm in the building, we keep your alarm entirely separate from it and coordinate with the building's management before any work in common areas.

Our building has a front desk and cameras in the lobby. Does a condo unit still need its own alarm?

Building security watches the lobby and common areas; it does not watch your door at 2 a.m. on a residents-only floor, your terrace or the inside of your home while you travel. A unit alarm covers exactly those gaps, and it reports to you rather than to the front desk.

Many owners in full-service buildings choose a modest system: a contact on the entry door and terrace door, glass-break on the window walls and one interior detector, with app alerts and optional monitoring.

Our unit is high up with floor-to-ceiling glass and a terrace. What is worth protecting so far above the street?

The entry door first, because it is the most used way in on any floor. Then the terrace door, because terraces can connect to neighbouring terraces or be reached from the roof, and then the glass, with acoustic detectors sized for large rooms.

Smoke and carbon monoxide monitoring and a water sensor under the kitchen sink and beside the washer are worth considering in a new building too, since a leak on a high floor affects every unit below it.

Construction next door shakes our rowhouse. Will the vibration trip shock or glass-break sensors?

It can if the system relies on shock sensors fixed to windows or walls, which react to vibration by design. During heavy work nearby we lower their sensitivity or switch the affected zones to rely on contacts and acoustic glass-break, which listen for the sound signature of breaking glass rather than for shaking.

If you live beside a major site, tell us before a demolition or piling phase starts. We can review the event log afterwards to see which zones the work disturbed and tune them, rather than leaving a system that false-alarms every weekday morning.

Dust from the work next door keeps setting off our monitored smoke detector. What can be changed?

Fine construction dust is a common cause of false smoke alarms. Ask us to inspect and clean the detector, and consider whether a heat detector, which reacts to temperature rather than particles, suits that particular spot better, for example near a window that is open while dust is in the air.

Do not simply unplug or cover a smoke detector. If the trouble continues we can replace the detector with a type suited to the location, and confirm with you who the monitoring station calls when a fire signal arrives.

Shops on Court Street, Smith Street and Atlantic Avenue

Our Atlantic Avenue grocery takes deliveries before dawn. How does the delivery crew get in without the master code?

Give the crew its own user code that works only during the delivery window and only for the area that covers the loading door and stockroom. Outside those hours the code does nothing, and every use appears in the event log with a time.

The owner can also get an alert when that code is used and another if the stockroom has not been re-armed within a set time, so a door left open after a delivery does not stay open all morning.

Our cellar stockroom shares a wall with the shop next door. Can the alarm notice someone breaking through the wall instead of a door?

Yes. A shock or vibration sensor mounted on the shared wall reacts to hammering, drilling or cutting through masonry, which a door contact would never see. It is set to ignore normal building vibration and tested on the wall itself.

A motion detector in the cellar adds a second check, so that anyone who does get through the wall is detected as soon as they move into the room.

Locks and habits

Can a smart lock and the alarm work together so the door locks itself when we arm?

Yes, on most current alarm platforms. When you arm from the keypad or the app, a compatible smart lock bolts the door; when you disarm with your code, it can unlock. The app also shows whether each lock is locked, and can warn you if a door has been left unlocked after a set time.

We pick a lock that fits the door and its existing hardware, including older doors in the historic district, and make sure the door still opens by hand from inside at all times.

Monitoring, notifications and the call list

Every system we install can notify the household directly through the app, and most owners add central station monitoring on top. The two do different jobs. App notifications are fast and personal: the garden door opened at 3 p.m. while you were at work, the cleaner disarmed at 10:02, the stockroom was not re-armed after the delivery. Central station monitoring is the backstop when nobody is looking at a phone: an operator receives the alarm, works through your call list and dispatches help according to the instructions you set.

In shared settings the call list deserves thought. A cottage owner who travels may want a trusted neighbour on the mews as a second contact, because that neighbour can look at the front door within a minute. A shop owner may want the manager first and the owner second. A condo owner may want the building's front desk told about an alarm in the unit, or may not; it is a choice, and we set it up the way you decide rather than by default.

Wired or wireless for a cottage, a flat or a condo

Small homes in Cobble Hill are usually best served by wireless devices. A twelve-foot cottage or a courtyard flat has short distances to the panel, finished walls nobody wants opened and often a board or association that prefers no drilling through shared structure. Wireless contacts and detectors fix with small screws or adhesive, can be moved if the layout changes and report their own battery level. Wired devices still make sense where cable is easy to run, such as a rowhouse with an open cellar ceiling below the garden floor, a shop with a drop ceiling, or a new condo where the developer left cable in the walls. Many systems end up mixed, and the plan we give you marks each device as wired or wireless with the reason.

Power cuts and connection backups

Every panel we install carries a backup battery, and wireless sensors run on their own batteries, so a short power cut does not leave the home unprotected. For reporting, we prefer a cellular communicator, often paired with the home's internet connection, so the system can still reach the monitoring station and your phone if one path fails. In older Cobble Hill houses we still find panels wired to copper phone lines that were cancelled years ago, which is why every installation and every takeover ends with a test signal that the monitoring station confirms.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Cobble Hill

We could not open Google's AI Overview answers from the environment this page was researched in, so we are not quoting them. The claims below are ones people bring to us after asking chatbots or reading national cost guides from Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each is checked against the homes of Cobble Hill.

"Outdoor motion detection is the best early warning."

For a detached house with a private yard, an outdoor detector can warn of someone before they reach a window. That advice assumes the only people outside are trespassers.

On Warren Place, in the Tower Buildings' courtyards or along a garden that touches four others, most people outside your window are neighbours. An outdoor trigger there produces false alarms many times a day.

The early warning that works in shared space is a contact on each opening and glass-break on large panes. They report the moment someone tries to get in, and never because someone walked past.

"Small homes don't need professional alarms."

A small home needs fewer devices, which is why a cottage system costs less. It does not need less care. With only one door and a handful of windows, every one of them matters.

In a twelve-foot-wide house the keypad is often visible through the front door, and the rear yard is a second way in that is easy to forget. Those are placement decisions, not equipment decisions.

A short visit gets them right. The equipment list for a cottage might be five contacts, one detector and a keypad; the value is in where each one goes.

"A building with a doorman doesn't need unit alarms."

Front-desk staff and lobby cameras protect the entrance and common areas. They do not see a residents-only corridor at night or the inside of your home.

The new condominium buildings on the former hospital site have exactly that layout: building security downstairs, private terraces and large glass upstairs.

A modest unit system on the entry door, terrace door and glass reports directly to you, which is the gap a lobby desk cannot fill.

"Glass-break sensors false-alarm constantly."

Older or badly placed detectors could react to a dropped pan or a slammed door. Current acoustic detectors listen for the combination of sounds a breaking pane makes and are far more selective.

The difference is in testing. We use a glass-break simulator at the far end of each protected window during installation and set the sensitivity for the room.

For garden-floor glass doors and condo window walls, a properly tested detector is the only sensor that reacts to a broken pane without the door being opened.

"Any alarm works the same next to a construction site."

Contacts and acoustic glass-break do. Shock sensors fixed to walls and windows do not, because they react to vibration, which is what piling, demolition and heavy deliveries produce.

Parts of Cobble Hill have lived beside the River Park construction on the old hospital site for years, with towers planned on Hicks and Pacific Streets.

Near a site like that we rely on contacts and acoustic detection and tune or bypass shock sensors during heavy phases, then review the event log to confirm.

"Professional alarms always lock you into a long contract."

Some dealer plans do bundle equipment into multi-year monitoring agreements, and the national guides are right to warn about that.

It is not universal. Our monitoring starts from $19.99 a month with no long-term contract, and the system still sends app alerts if you decide not to pay for monitoring.

Ask any installer, us included, to show the cancellation terms in writing before you sign.

"Anyone handy can install an alarm for you."

An owner may install equipment in their own home. But in New York State, anyone in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems for others must be licensed by the Department of State under General Business Law Article 6-D.

That matters for insurance paperwork and for monitored systems, and it is easy to check by asking for the license number.

Our Installation Process

A five-device cottage system and a shop with a cellar follow the same seven steps. In Cobble Hill the second step, mapping what each opening faces, does most of the design work.

1. Opening list and photos

You send a short list of doors and windows and what each one faces, with photos. We use it to sketch the envelope and spot openings onto shared ground before anyone visits.

2. Survey inside and out

A technician checks every opening from inside and, where allowed, from the garden, balcony or lane, notes which can be reached, and confirms any building, association or historic district rules.

3. Written plan and Brooklyn price

Each device is listed with its purpose and location, triggers are separated from recording-only cameras, and a fixed written price is set from our published Brooklyn figures before any deposit.

4. Fit the envelope

Contacts go on every reachable door and window, glass-break detectors on large panes, and the right hardware on gallery doors, carriage doors and shop back doors.

5. Add interior detection and the siren

Motion detectors are aimed into rooms and stairs and away from glass that faces shared space, and the siren is mounted indoors where it fills the home.

6. Program codes, alerts and order of events

Every user gets a code, delivery or helper codes get time limits, phone alerts are set to arrive before the siren and the station call, and smart locks are linked to arming.

7. Walk-test and hand over

Every device is tripped and glass-break is tested with a simulator, the monitoring station confirms a signal, and you receive the device list, codes guide and app login.

After handover: tell us when the neighbourhood changes

Cobble Hill changes around you: a crane goes up on the next block, a neighbour adds an extension with a roof under your window, the courtyard gets new lighting. When that happens, a short review of which zones trip and why keeps the system reliable. Owners in the new buildings should also tell us when the building's own systems change, for example a new lobby entry system or a change in the managing agent, so that the call list and any building contacts stay current. Twice a year, use test mode to open each protected door and window, check wireless battery levels before winter, and delete codes nobody needs.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Walk the Shared Side Last

This is a note on our method, not a story about any customer. On homes that face shared ground, the final walk-test is done in a particular order. First, every contact and indoor detector is tripped from inside with the system in test mode, so we know the envelope reports. Then one technician goes outside into the garden, balcony or lane, where we are allowed to, and simply behaves like a neighbour: walks past the windows, stands at the glass, talks, carries a bag, opens and closes the gate. The other watches the panel and the app.

Nothing should report during that second walk. If anything does, a detector is seeing through glass or a camera is wired as a trigger when it should only record, and it gets re-aimed or reconfigured before we leave. Only then do we test glass-break with the simulator and send the signal to the monitoring station.

The reason is simple: in a courtyard or mews, an alarm's reputation with the neighbours is decided in its first fortnight.

How do we test the alarm ourselves without the police or the monitoring station reacting?
+

Call the monitoring station or use the app to put the account in test mode first, then open each protected door and window and walk past each detector while watching the keypad or app. When you finish, take the account out of test mode and confirm the station received your test signals. We show every household how to do this at handover.

Can a dog walker or cleaner get a code that only works at certain hours?
+

Yes. Most current panels let you create user codes limited to particular days and times, and some can be limited to one area of the home. Each use is recorded with the user name, and you can get a phone notification whenever that code arms or disarms the system.

Do wireless sensor batteries need replacing, and will the system warn us before one dies?
+

Yes on both counts. Wireless contacts and detectors run on small batteries that typically last several years depending on the model and how often the device reports, and the panel shows a low-battery warning on the keypad and in the app well before a sensor stops working. The panel's own backup battery is checked by the system and replaced during a service visit when it weakens.

Can the app show which door or window was opened by name?
+

Yes. During programming we name every zone in plain words, such as garden glass door, rear bedroom window or cellar stockroom, so alerts and the event history tell you exactly which opening reported rather than a zone number.

Is a pet door in our garden door a weak point for the alarm?
+

It can be, because a door contact only reports when the whole door opens. We look at the size of the flap and whether an arm could reach a lock through it, cover the room behind it with a pet-tolerant detector or glass-break as appropriate, and suggest a lockable flap that is closed when the system is armed away.

Does an alarm panel need its own electrical outlet?
+

The panel needs a constant power source, usually a small plug-in transformer on an outlet that is not controlled by a wall switch, or a hard-wired connection in larger systems. We secure the transformer so it cannot be unplugged by accident and the backup battery covers short outages.

Can we keep our existing Ring cameras when you install a monitored alarm?
+

Often, yes. Ring cameras can keep running in their own app alongside a separately monitored alarm, or Ring alarm equipment can be installed and tested as customer-owned equipment for $200 to $400. Where you want one app for everything, we explain which cameras are compatible with the alarm platform before you buy anything new.

How soon after we accept the quote can installation start?

Do you give us an alarm certificate our insurer can file?

Yes. For monitored systems we provide the documentation insurers usually ask for, including the installer's license number, the monitoring arrangement and a list of the protection installed, so you can send it to your carrier and ask about any discount it offers.
