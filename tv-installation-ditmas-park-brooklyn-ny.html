The Neighborhood

Why Ditmas Park Properties Need TV Installation

A flat-screen TV is a modern object, and most of Ditmas Park was built for a century that had never imagined one. When the Landmarks Preservation Commission voted on November 25, 2025 to create the Ditmas Park West and Beverley Square West historic districts, it described houses put up for families who wanted space outside the crowded city: tree-lined streets, large lots, and what were then modern conveniences, including gas lighting, hot and cold running water, indoor plumbing and coal-burning furnaces. Ditmas Park West's 127 houses were nearly all built between 1902 and 1910, and nearly half of them were designed by one architect, Arlington D. Isham. Next door, the original Ditmas Park Historic District was designated in 1981, joining Prospect Park South from 1979 and Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park from 2008. Brick Underground, quoting the borough historian Ron Schweiger, put the whole Victorian Flatbush area at roughly 2,000 to 2,500 homes over about 100 square blocks, "probably the largest collection of this type of architecture anywhere in the United States, in one area."

For a TV installer, that history is not background color. It is a description of the wall. A house designed to be lit by gas has, or once had, gas pipes running up its walls to where the fixtures were. A house heated by a coal furnace has chimneys, and the chimney breast in the parlor is usually the wall the furniture faces. A Queen Anne or Shingle-style house of 1905 was framed in wood and finished inside with plaster on wood lath, which is hard on the surface, crumbly in the middle and only as strong as the framing behind it. And because the developers of Victorian Flatbush built freestanding houses rather than rows, with Dean Alvord famously choosing "not to do rowhouses" in Prospect Park South according to the Flatbush Development Corporation, most Ditmas Park houses have windows on four sides. That means more light and fewer long, solid walls, which makes the one good TV wall in a room very valuable and very easy to spoil with a badly placed bracket.

The houses have also lived a long time. A 2007 Brownstoner forum thread on renovating Ditmas Victorians has owners agreeing that "a total upgrade on wiring and plumbing is the most important" job, and estimates that include "plaster repair etc." A Ditmas Park homeowner told 6sqft in 2018 that when she bought her nearly century-old house, "most of the wiring was still cloth-wrapped." Other houses have been brought fully up to date: a listing for an 1899 house on East 18th Street in 2025 advertised new electrical, plumbing and CAT-6 wiring and a finished basement suited to a media room. On a single block you can find both, and the wall behind your TV may be original 1905 plaster, a patch from the 1970s, or fresh drywall from a 2020 gut renovation. We do not guess which. We look, we scan, and we drill a single small pilot hole where the bracket will hide it before we choose a fixing.

Not everyone in Ditmas Park lives in a Victorian house, and the other half of the neighborhood has its own walls. Along Ocean Avenue and on the side streets near Newkirk and Cortelyou there are six-story elevator buildings from the 1920s and 1930s, such as 770 Ocean Avenue from 1923 and the 185-unit Art Deco building at 570 Westminster Road, completed in 1936 to designs by Seelig & Finkelstein. Brownstoner's 2026 listing for an apartment there describes shouldered arches, a coved living-room ceiling, parquet floors and picture rails. There are post-war buildings too, like the 106-unit building at 1250 Ocean Avenue from 1953, and small pre-war walk-ups such as the four-story, 16-unit building at 377 Argyle Road from 1925. And many of the big houses have been divided: Brownstoner described 551 Rugby Road as one of three units in what was originally a two-family Edwardian home with two tiers of columned porches. Each of those changes who owns the wall, who must approve the work and what the drill will meet.

Then there is the question of who is watching and how. Brooklyn Community Board 14, which covers Ditmas Park, says in its FY2027 statement of needs that more than 48 percent of the district's area is one- and two-family homes, while Prospect Park South, Ditmas Park and Midwood Park–Fiske Terrace together make up about 6 percent of its lots. So a big share of TV calls come from houses where the owner lives, often with a parent or a tenant on another floor, and a big share from renters in elevator buildings who need a landlord's or a board's blessing. A family in a Victorian house might want the TV in the parlor for the evening, a second one in a third-floor room under the eaves for the children and an antenna for local channels. A renter on Ocean Avenue might want the screen on the one wall that does not have a radiator or a window, without losing the deposit. A bar on Cortelyou Road wants every seat to see the game. Those are three different jobs with three different plans, and this page walks through each.

Same-Day Repair in Ditmas Park

Need Repair in Ditmas Park? A Screen Pulling Off Old Plaster, a Mount That Was Never in the Framing, or a Cord Pushed Through the Wall

The urgent TV calls from Victorian houses have a pattern, and almost all of it comes back to the plaster. The most common is a screen that has begun to lean forward or sink at one corner. When we look behind it, the bracket has usually been fixed with anchors made for drywall: plastic plugs or small hollow-wall toggles that grip the half inch of hard plaster on the surface and nothing behind it. Plaster on lath is a sandwich of a hard skin, a softer brown coat and the strips of wood, with gaps between the strips where the plaster squeezed through when it was wet. An anchor that lands in one of those gaps feels tight on the day and then slowly crushes its way loose, especially on a full-motion arm that someone pulls out every evening. The second pattern is a bracket on a chimney breast drilled into the mortar between the bricks rather than the bricks themselves. The third is what an earlier owner left behind: a bracket with no TV, a hole with a cord coming out of it, or a power strip hidden behind the screen with the TV's own cord fed down inside the wall.

If the screen is tipping, do not try to push it back up. If two people can lift it safely, take it off the bracket, unplug it and lay it face up on a blanket, then call (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list. If the same TV goes back up on a properly fixed bracket in the same visit, it is priced as a new mount on that wall, $179.99 plus the plaster or brick line, with the take-down added. Where the old holes sit exactly where the new fixings need to go, we move the bracket an inch or two along the framing rather than reusing torn plaster. If what we find is a problem in the house itself, such as a crack running across the plaster or a bulge where the plaster has come away from the lath, we say so plainly, because that is a job for a plasterer before it is a job for a TV installer, and hanging a screen on it would only hide the problem for a while. A cord found inside the wall is pulled out and replaced with an in-wall power and cable kit or a cord cover, for the reasons set out further down this page.

The freestanding Victorian: plaster on lath over a wood frame, and a parlor full of openings

The classic Ditmas Park house is a wood-framed building of two and a half or three stories with plaster on wood lath on almost every interior wall. That is good news for a TV, as long as the fixings go into the frame. The Brownstoner forum thread on mounting a TV above a fireplace has an experienced Brooklyn owner putting it simply: "It's much easier to mount things on a non-party wall, with plaster over wood lath." The studs behind the plaster are real lumber, often a little larger than modern studs, and a lag bolt through the plaster and the lath into the middle of a stud holds a heavy screen for decades. What it takes is finding the studs, which in a house of this age are not always where a modern tape measure says they should be. Framing was laid out by carpenters around windows, doors and chimneys, and the spacing can change from one bay of a wall to the next. We find each stud we use individually, with a scanner set to deep mode and then with a fine pilot bit, rather than measuring sixteen inches from the corner.

The bigger challenge in a Victorian parlor is that there is so little plain wall. Freestanding houses have windows on all four sides, and the main rooms of a Queen Anne or Colonial Revival house usually also have a fireplace, wide openings to the hall and the dining room, and sometimes sliding doors that disappear into the wall when they are open. The wall that looks perfect for a TV may be the one hiding a door pocket, a radiator riser, a chimney flue or the old gas-light pipe that once fed a wall fixture. Every one of those is a reason not to drill in a particular place, and every one can be found before the drill goes in. We start each job by walking the room and asking questions that sound odd until you see why: does that door slide, where is the radiator on the floor above, was there ever a light fixture on this wall, which way does the chimney go. The answers decide the bracket position more than the scanner does.

Then there is the porch. Ditmas Park houses are known for deep front porches, and the 6sqft listing for 320 East 18th Street from 1899 leads with its wraparound one. A porch changes the light in the front parlor: the room is shaded at midday and then flooded with low sun in the late afternoon, depending on which way the house faces. A screen on the wall opposite the porch windows catches that reflection. We check glare with painter's tape at the time of day you usually watch, or ask you to send a photo of the wall at that hour, and often the answer is a side wall, a slight tilt or a different room. We do not mount TVs out on an open porch; it is an outdoor space, the houses in the historic districts have exteriors the city regulates, and the price list on this page covers indoor work.

The house divided into flats: whose wall is it?

A large share of Ditmas Park's big houses are no longer single-family. Some became two-family houses decades ago; others have been divided into condominiums, like 551 Rugby Road, which Brownstoner described as one of three units in what was originally a two-family Edwardian home with two tiers of columned porches. Community Board 14 has asked the city for more action on illegal conversions in what would be single-family homes, which tells you how often the subject comes up. For a TV, the practical question is ownership. In a condominium the walls between units, around the shared stair and the outside of the house are normally common elements, and the condo's rules decide who may drill them. In a rented flat the owner of the house decides. In a house shared by relatives there may be no rules at all, but there is still somebody on the other side of the wall.

Divided houses also produce some unusual walls. A partition added in the 1950s to make a separate apartment may be drywall on thin framing, or even wood paneling over nothing much, right next to a 1905 plaster wall that is twice as strong. A former bedroom may now be a living room whose only solid wall backs onto a shared stair. A kitchen added upstairs may have plumbing in the wall you were looking at. We ask how the house was divided and when, if you know, and we read each wall separately. If the wall you want is shared with a stair or another unit, we can send a short description of the work for you to pass to the other owners or the board: which wall, the size of the holes, whether anything goes inside the wall, and our license and insurance. That message usually takes care of the question before it becomes an argument.

The Art Deco elevator co-op: arches, coved ceilings, picture rails and a board

The six-story buildings of the 1930s, like 570 Westminster Road, make the carry easy and the paperwork more formal. Brownstoner's 2026 listing for a top-floor apartment there describes the features that make these apartments lovely and also limit where a TV can go: broad doorways with shouldered arches, a coved ceiling in the living room, parquet floors with inlaid borders, and picture rails. The building has a live-in super and a full-time doorman, and like most co-ops it will want to know who is coming, when, and with what insurance. We send a certificate of insurance naming the co-op and the managing agent before the visit. If the board asks for the elevator to be padded or booked, we need a day's notice.

Inside, the walls of a building of this age are usually plaster applied directly over masonry or over hollow clay tile partitions, not over wood lath. Hollow clay tile is the wall that catches out handymen most often. It sounds solid when you knock, and it looks like masonry in the dust, but the tile has thin faces and empty cells behind them. A standard masonry anchor can burst the face of the tile and spin in the void, and a plastic plug just turns. The right fixing is an anchor designed for hollow masonry, set with a slow drill so the face of the tile does not shatter, with more fixing points spreading the load. Where the plaster sits on solid brick, which is common on the outside walls, the brick line applies. Either way, we decide from the pilot hole, not from the age of the building, and you hear the add-on before the next hole.

The picture rail deserves its own word, because people ask about it. That narrow molding near the ceiling was made for hanging framed pictures on cords or hooks, and some owners wonder whether a TV can hang from it too. It cannot. A picture rail is nailed into the plaster and the edge of the framing with small nails meant to carry a frame, not a 40-pound screen that someone will lean on while plugging in a game console. A TV goes on a bracket fixed into the wall itself, below the rail, and the rail stays exactly as it was.

The 1920s Ocean Avenue building: radiators, long rooms and plaster on masonry

Ocean Avenue's six-story buildings from the 1920s, such as 770 Ocean Avenue from 1923 with 41 units, are similar to the Westminster Road co-ops in construction but often run as rentals, which means a landlord's consent rather than a board's. The living rooms are often long and narrow, with windows at one end and a radiator under them, and the longest unbroken wall is usually an inside wall shared with the next apartment or the corridor. That wall is commonly masonry or clay tile under plaster, which holds a TV well with the right anchor. It also carries sound, so if your neighbor is on the other side, the soundbar placement matters as much as the bracket.

The thing to watch for in these buildings is old surface wiring and gas. Electrical upgrades over the decades often added outlets in metal boxes on the surface of the wall, fed by conduit that runs along the baseboard or up a corner. Those runs are easy to miss when a TV is in front of them and easy to drill into if nobody is looking. Kitchens sometimes have gas lines that pass through a living-room wall on their way to the range. We look before we scan, and we scan before we drill. In a building with open housing-code violations, and Community Board 14 reports that the district's violation rate per 1,000 units rose from 506 in 2010 to 879 in 2024, we also look for cracked or bulging plaster near the chosen wall and tell you if it should be repaired before anything is hung on it.

The post-war building: block, concrete and a renovation or two

The post-war buildings in and around Ditmas Park, like 1250 Ocean Avenue from 1953, were built with concrete floors and block or brick walls, and many apartments have since been renovated with drywall partitions. In one living room we can find a block wall along the corridor, a drywall wall between the living room and the bedroom, and plaster on brick on the outside wall. Each behaves differently. Drywall on wood studs is the base price; plaster or brick carries its own line on the list; concrete block needs a hammer drill, fixings placed in the solid webs rather than the hollow cores, and the layout of the bracket matched to the block's courses. A wall like that cannot hide cables inside it, so the cable plan matters from the start.

The good news is that most post-war apartments have a framed partition somewhere in the main room, and that is often the best TV wall precisely because an in-wall power and cable kit fits inside it. If the wall you prefer is block and the one beside it is a framed partition, we show you both and the price difference before deciding. If the building does not allow any opening in its walls, the cables go in a paintable cord cover or straight down into the console below the screen, which is often the neatest answer anyway.

The small walk-up and the finished basement

Ditmas Park's small pre-war walk-ups, like the four-story, 16-unit building at 377 Argyle Road from 1925, put the job at the top of narrow stairs. A large TV in its box has to go up on its edge around tight landings, which is fine when the box is the original one with its foam inside and a problem when the screen has been unboxed and wrapped in a blanket. If you are buying, have it delivered in its box and leave the box sealed until we arrive. Inside, walk-ups of that age usually have plaster on lath partitions and plaster on brick outside walls, and the wall sharing a building line with the next property is often solid brick. A party wall like that holds a screen very well with masonry anchors set into the brick rather than the mortar.

Finished basements are the other place Ditmas Park households put a big screen. A listing for an 1899 house on East 18th Street described a fully finished lower level that "can accommodate a media or playroom and home office," and a homeowner told 6sqft about turning a storage-and-laundry basement into a rec room. Basement walls are usually furring and drywall over the masonry foundation, or the foundation itself, and the basement is where the house's electrical panel, network equipment and cable entry usually live. That makes it the easiest room to wire, and the room where a TV most needs to be kept clear of anything that could flood. We mount basement screens high enough that the outlet and the devices are off the floor, and we keep the cable box and streaming device on a shelf rather than on the slab.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The First Fifteen Minutes in a Ditmas Park Parlor

These notes describe how our technicians work in houses like Ditmas Park's. They are our standing practice, written for this kind of house, not a story about any one address.

Look up before looking at the wall. The technician starts at the ceiling: is there a medallion or a capped fitting where a gas fixture once hung, and on the walls, any small round patches at sconce height where wall fixtures used to be? Each of those marks a pipe that may still run inside the wall below it. Then the floor: which way do the boards run, and is there a radiator above this room on the next floor, whose riser may come down the corner we were about to use?

Find out what slides and what burns. If the room has sliding doors, the technician opens and closes them to see exactly where the pocket ends, because a lag bolt through a pocket wall scrapes the door every time it moves. If there is a fireplace, the next question is whether it has ever been used in your time, and where the flue runs up through the breast. A decorative fireplace still has a flue; it just does not have a fire.

Knock, scan, then one hidden hole. A knock along the wall at screen height tells a lot, and a deep-mode scan shows where the framing and metal are, but on old plaster a scanner is easily fooled. So the technician drills one small pilot hole where the bracket plate will later cover it and reads the dust and the feel of the bit. White powder, then wood, means plaster on lath over a stud. White powder, then red grit, means plaster on brick. A sudden drop into nothing means a void between lath strips or a hollow tile. That one hole decides the fixing, and if it changes the add-on from the quote, you hear it before any more drilling.

Tape, then sit. Painter's tape marks the outline of the screen. You sit where you usually watch, and the technician checks the height, the glare from the porch windows and the swing of every nearby door. Moving tape costs nothing; moving a bracket on 1905 plaster costs a patch.

Level to true, not to the house. After a century, Victorian floors and ceilings are rarely level. The bracket is leveled with a long level to true horizontal, then checked by eye from the couch against the window frames, because a screen that matches a sloping ceiling line can look straighter than one that is technically perfect. We talk you through that choice before tightening anything.

Wall by Wall

Plaster, Lath, Brick and Tile: What Holds a TV in a Ditmas Park Wall and What It Adds to the Price

The base price on our Brooklyn list, $179.99, covers a TV up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs, labor only, with the bracket sold separately. Very little of Ditmas Park's housing was built with drywall, which is why we explain the wall lines before anything else. Each add-on exists because that wall needs different bits, different fixings and more time, not because an old house is an excuse to charge more.

Plaster on wood lath (+$45). This is the wall of almost every Ditmas Park house built before the First World War: thin wooden strips nailed across the studs, covered with a rough base coat that pushed through the gaps to form "keys," a brown coat, and a hard finish coat. All together it can be close to an inch thick. It is strong when pressed and weak when pulled, and the keys that hold it to the lath can break with age, vibration or a careless drill. A fixing that lasts goes through the plaster and the lath into the middle of a stud, with the pilot hole drilled slowly at low speed so the plaster does not crack outward around it. Where a bracket's slots cannot reach a stud, a toggle rated for the load and long enough to span the plaster and lath is used, and on heavier screens we add fixings rather than trusting fewer. Plaster in these houses is often patched, and a patch from the 1970s may be gypsum board glued into a hole, which is why each fixing point is checked on its own.

Plaster on brick, including chimney breasts (+$65). The Brownstoner thread on mounting TVs over fireplaces gets this right: the fireplaces in many Brooklyn houses have "no studs, just plaster over brick," and long masonry anchors through the plaster into the brick hold heavy loads. We use a hammer drill only after the first pilot hole confirms brick, set the anchor into the brick itself rather than the softer mortar joint, and keep each hole a safe distance from the edges of the brick. On a chimney breast the depth matters too, because the flue is somewhere inside it. In a Ditmas Park house the breast is often a projecting box of brick with the flue running up its center or to one side, and the face of the breast can be one brick thick in front of the flue. We drill only as deep as the anchor needs, and where we can tell which side the flue runs, we keep the fixings to the other side or to the solid corners of the breast.

Plaster on hollow clay tile (priced as masonry, +$65). Common in the 1920s and 1930s apartment buildings on Ocean Avenue and Westminster Road. Tile sounds solid and is not. We use anchors made for hollow masonry and set them gently so the tile face stays whole.

Drywall on wood studs (base price). Found in renovated rooms, finished basements, attic rooms and the partitions of divided houses. It is the simplest wall, as long as the drywall is actually on studs and not glued over old plaster, which also happens. If it is glued over plaster, it is priced and fixed as plaster.

Hiding the wires without breaking the electrical code

The most common request after "put it on the wall" is "make the wires disappear," and there is a right way and a wrong way to do it. The wrong way is to cut a hole behind the screen, another near the floor, and feed the TV's own power cord down inside the wall. The National Electrical Code does not allow it. Gizmodo's widely shared explanation of the rule quotes the code's Article 400 ban on flexible cords "where run through holes in walls" and "where concealed by walls, floors, or ceilings," and points to an outlet behind the TV as the proper fix. In a wood-framed Victorian house the reasons are especially plain: a cord inside a wall can be nicked by a nail or a fixing, it cannot be inspected, and it sits in a hollow wall cavity of old dry lumber.

The right way, on a hollow framed wall, is an in-wall power and cable kit. It puts a recessed power inlet low on the wall that plugs into your existing outlet with a short cord, carries power up inside the wall in the kit's own rated cable, and gives a recessed outlet behind the TV, with a separate pass-through for HDMI, antenna and network cables. On our list it is $169 including the kit. It does not touch the house's wiring at all, which matters in a house where some circuits may still be the original cloth-wrapped kind. We do not do electrical work, and we will not open, extend or alter the house's wiring. If the outlet the kit plugs into is an old two-prong outlet, or one that sparks or feels warm, we will tell you, and that is a call for a licensed electrician before the TV goes up.

The kit needs a hollow cavity between its two openings, which plaster on lath over a wood frame usually provides. Inside that cavity we check for the things that stop a cable: a horizontal fire block, a header over a nearby door, a gas-light pipe or a run of old knob-and-tube wiring. If the cavity is blocked, the kit goes into the next stud bay. For walls that cannot be opened, such as a chimney breast, a party wall of brick or a co-op wall the board does not want cut, the choice is a paintable cord cover ($59), which runs neatly up the surface and can be painted to match the wall, or a layout with the console directly below the screen so the cables drop behind the furniture. On a chimney breast with a mantel, the cords often run down the side of the breast in a cover and along the back of the mantel shelf, where they are barely visible.

In a rental, the kit means two neat openings that will need patching when you leave, and a cord cover means a few small screw holes. Either is easy to restore, but it is exactly the kind of change a landlord or board may want to agree to first, which is the subject of the next sections.

Antennas and the Historic Districts

Local Channels, Antennas and Landmark Houses: What Can Go Outside and What Stays Indoors

More Ditmas Park households are dropping cable for streaming apps and a free antenna for local channels, and in a house in a historic district that raises a question most TV pages never touch: where can the antenna go? We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. What follows is what the public guidance says and how we work with it.

The federal rule protects most antennas. The Federal Communications Commission's rule on over-the-air reception devices covers TV antennas and satellite dishes one meter (39.37 inches) or less in diameter. Under it, an owner or a tenant can generally install one on property they own or control, and in rentals and condominiums that means "exclusive use" areas such as a terrace, balcony or patio. The FCC's own guide lists two important exceptions. Restrictions are allowed for safety, and they are allowed "where a historic site is involved." And the rule does not apply to common areas, which "may include the roof or exterior walls of a multiple dwelling unit."

The historic districts care about what the street sees. Ditmas Park, Ditmas Park West, Beverley Square West, Prospect Park South and Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park are all designated historic districts, and the Landmarks Preservation Commission regulates changes to the outside of the houses in them. Its Permit Guidebook chapter on rooftop and mechanical equipment shows the general approach: equipment should be non-visible or minimally visible from the street, and equipment in yards "should be placed in a non-visible location." The guidebook does not set out a specific antenna rule in that chapter, so if you want anything on the roof, the porch or the front of a landmarked house, the right first step is to ask the LPC. We do not install antennas or dishes on the exterior of houses.

Indoors is usually the answer anyway. In much of Ditmas Park an indoor antenna does a reasonable job, because the houses are wood-framed and their walls let more signal through than a brick row or a masonry tower. The best spot is often high up and near a window, ideally one facing toward the broadcast towers, or in the attic, where a larger indoor antenna can sit out of sight and feed the TVs below. During a setup-only visit ($195 for the first hour, then 30-minute blocks) we connect the antenna, run a channel scan, try a few positions, and set the TV to switch cleanly between the antenna and your streaming apps. If the antenna cable has to come down from the attic to the parlor, the in-wall kit's pass-through or a cord cover carries it.

Apartment buildings are different. The roof and outside walls of a co-op or rental building are common areas, so the FCC rule does not give a resident the right to put anything there. If you have a terrace or balcony that is yours alone, the rule may apply to it, subject to the building's reasonable safety rules. Most residents in the Ocean Avenue and Westminster Road buildings use a window-mounted indoor antenna or skip the antenna and stream local channels instead. We set up either.

What is inside a 1905 wall

Our house on Argyle Road was built for gas lighting. Could an old gas pipe still be inside the wall where we want the TV?

It is possible, and it is the main reason we look before we drill. The Landmarks Preservation Commission lists gas lighting among the original conveniences of the houses in these districts, and gas fixtures were often fed by small iron pipes running up inside the walls to wall brackets and ceiling fixtures. Many were capped decades ago, some were reused for electrical wiring, and a few may still be connected. Signs include a small round patch or a capped fitting at sconce height, a medallion with no fixture, or a fixture that was converted to electric. A deep scan shows metal in the wall, and the pilot hole is drilled slowly where the bracket will cover it. If we find a pipe, we move the bracket to miss it, and if anything suggests a live gas line has been disturbed, we stop and you call the utility.

The parlor has sliding doors that disappear into the wall we wanted for the TV. Can a bracket go on that wall at all?

Usually yes, but not over the pocket. A sliding door that slides into the wall needs a hollow pocket roughly as wide as the door itself, and any screw that reaches into that space will scrape or jam the door the next time it is opened. Before deciding, we open and close the door to see exactly where the pocket ends, and we scan for the framing on the far side of it. If the solid part of the wall beyond the pocket is wide enough for the screen, the bracket goes there, fixed into the studs. If the pocket runs almost the full width of the wall, the better choice is a different wall, or a TV on its stand in front of it, and we show you both with painter's tape before any hole is made.

Our fireplace has not been lit in years. Could the flue inside the chimney breast still get in the way of the anchors?

Yes, which is why we treat a decorative fireplace as a chimney, not a wall. A breast built for a coal-era fireplace has a flue inside it whether or not anyone lights a fire, and in many houses the brick in front of the flue is only one brick thick. Anchors only need a few inches of depth, so we set them into the solid brick at the sides and corners of the breast where possible, keep them shallow, and never drill blind into the middle of the breast. If the fireplace is still used, heat rising from it is bad for a TV, a point the Brownstoner fireplace thread raised years ago, and we will usually suggest another wall. On our list the above-fireplace line is $130, plus $65 for fixing into brick.

My scanner shows framing at uneven gaps across the living-room wall of our 1908 house. Can the bracket still catch two studs?

Almost always. Uneven gaps are normal in a house framed by hand around windows, doors and chimneys, and the studs are often slightly larger than modern ones. Most TV brackets have long horizontal slots or a wide wall plate precisely so they can catch two studs that are not at standard spacing. We find each stud individually, confirm it with a fine pilot hole, and position the bracket so both lag bolts land near the center of their studs. If the screen has to sit somewhere the studs do not reach, we use toggles rated for the load through the plaster and lath, and on heavier TVs we add fixings rather than relying on two.

Rooms under the eaves, rooms off the porch

Our third-floor room is under a sloped ceiling and the only straight wall is a short knee wall. Can a TV go there?

Often it can, and it is a common spot for a children's or guest room screen in a Ditmas Park house. A knee wall is the low vertical wall where the sloped ceiling meets the floor, usually two to four feet high, and it is often framed lightly, sometimes with storage space or insulation behind it. We check what is behind it first, because a knee wall that is just a thin partition in front of an attic void needs its studs found and used, and a screen low on a knee wall should be watched from a low seat or a bed. A tilting bracket helps here, tilting up rather than down. If the knee wall is too short or too weak, the gable wall at the end of the room, which is usually a full-height framed wall, is the better choice.

We use the enclosed sunporch at the front of our house as a family room. Is it a sensible place for a TV?

It can be, with two cautions. Enclosed porches in old houses are usually mostly windows, so the solid wall is often the original front wall of the house, which faces the room and is good for a bracket, or a short end wall. They also get much colder in winter and hotter in summer than the house, and TV makers publish an operating temperature range in the manual, so check it if the porch is not heated. Glare is the bigger everyday problem, with sun coming in from three sides. We check the screen position with tape at the time of day you watch, and a tilting bracket or a screen on the house wall facing away from the brightest windows usually solves it. Porches open to the weather are outdoors, and we do not mount there.

Our house was rewired with network jacks in every room. Can the new TV use the wired jack instead of Wi-Fi?

Yes, and in a house with three floors of plaster it is usually worth it. A wired network connection does not slow down when the microwave runs or when the signal has to pass through several walls. Most smart TVs and streaming devices have an Ethernet port, and if the jack is near the TV position we connect it during setup. If the jack is on another wall, the cable can go through the in-wall kit's pass-through or in a cord cover. If the jacks were installed by a previous owner and nobody knows which one goes where, we trace the one you need with a toner and test it before the screen covers it.

The original woodwork in our dining room runs halfway up the wall. Can a TV go above it without drilling into the wood?

Yes. Many old houses have wainscoting, paneling or a plate rail on the lower part of the dining-room wall with plaster above it. We keep the bracket entirely on the plaster above the woodwork, fixed into the studs behind, so no fixing touches the wood. The limit is height: if the woodwork is tall, a screen above it can end up higher than is comfortable from a dining chair, so we check with tape from a seated position. Cables can run down behind the screen in a cover painted to match the plaster and then along the top edge of the woodwork, or the screen can sit on a sideboard in front of the paneling if you would rather not have a bracket there at all.

We confirm each fixing point with a small pilot hole where the bracket plate will hide it, and choose the fixing for what is really there.

"Over the fireplace is the natural place for a TV"

Many AI answers present the mantel wall as the default, because the room is arranged around it.

In a Ditmas Park parlor the breast hides a flue, the face of the brick in front of it can be thin, and a working fireplace sends heat straight up at the screen. Even the Brooklyn owners on that Brownstoner thread warned that a TV over a mantel "is awfully high and invites a sore neck."

It can be done well, with shallow anchors in the solid parts of the breast and a tilting or pull-down bracket, but we always show you the alternative wall too.

"Hide the cords by running them inside the wall"

A common suggestion is to cut two holes and feed the TV's power cord down through the wall cavity.

The National Electrical Code does not allow flexible cords run through walls or concealed inside them. In a wood-framed house with old, dry lumber and sometimes original cloth-wrapped wiring, that rule is not a technicality.

Hidden power needs an in-wall kit with its own rated cable and a recessed outlet behind the screen. On brick, the honest options are a cord cover or a console below.

"You can put an antenna or dish anywhere on your own house"

Answers about cutting the cord often say the federal rule lets anyone put up an antenna or small dish.

The FCC rule does protect most small antennas, but its own guide allows restrictions "where a historic site is involved" and excludes common areas, including the roofs and outside walls of apartment buildings. "Renters cannot mount TVs" or "it never matters"

AI answers swing between treating any drilling in a rental as forbidden and treating it as harmless.

Smart-TV setup guides tend to assume a single router covers the home.

Does the Landmarks Preservation Commission have to approve a TV on an inside wall of a landmarked Ditmas Park house?
+

Not for a TV bracket on an interior wall. The commission regulates changes to the outside of houses in the historic districts, so anything on the porch, the roof or the front of the house is a question for the LPC first. Hanging a TV inside, with the cables in an in-wall kit or a cord cover, changes nothing the street can see and does not need a building permit.

I already bought a bracket online. Will you hang it, and does that change the price?
+

Yes, as long as it is rated for your TV's weight and size and suits the wall. Send us the bracket's model number with the TV's, and we check both before the visit. The labor is priced from the same list either way, starting at $179.99 plus the wall line.

How do you get a big TV up our porch steps and through a narrow Victorian front door?
+

Carefully and in its box. Keep the TV in its original carton with the foam until we arrive, because that is the safest way to carry it up steps and around tight hall turns. Measure the front door and the narrowest point of the stair if the TV is going upstairs, and tell us when you book.

Can one remote switch between the antenna channels and our streaming apps?

On most current smart TVs, yes. During a setup-only visit, $195 for the first hour on our list, we connect the antenna, run the channel scan, sign in to your apps with you and set the TV so live channels and apps sit on the same home screen.

What does a setup-only visit cover if the TV is already on the wall?

Anything that is not mounting: connecting a cable box, a game console, a streaming device or an antenna, joining the TV to Wi-Fi or a wired jack, picture and sound settings, and showing you how it all works. It is $195 for the first hour, then billed in 30-minute blocks.

Can the soundbar sit on the mantel shelf under the TV instead of on a bracket?

Do you repair or repaint the plaster around the bracket afterwards?

Our living-room outlets are the old two-prong kind. Can the in-wall kit still be used?

Our walls are open for a renovation. What should our contractor do now so the TV is easy to mount later?

Ask the contractor to add solid wood blocking between the studs at TV height on the chosen wall, and to have an electrician put an outlet behind where the screen will hang and run a conduit or open path down to the media console.
