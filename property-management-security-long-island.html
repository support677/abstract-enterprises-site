The Neighborhood

Why Long Island Properties Need One Security Contractor Across Nassau and Suffolk

Managing rentals east of the city line is a different job from managing them inside it. A New York City portfolio is mostly vertical: one front door, one riser, one boiler room per address. A Long Island portfolio tends to be horizontal. A single property might be a 1950s garden-apartment complex with fourteen two-story buildings around a lawn, each with its own front door, its own laundry room and its own cluster of mailboxes, plus a long parking lot and a maintenance garage at the far end. The next property on the same list might be a five-story building with stores on the ground floor two minutes from a Long Island Rail Road platform, and the one after that a handful of two-family houses in three different hamlets. Security work for that kind of list is mostly about distance, weather and jurisdiction, and far less about any single box on a wall.

Rentals are also a smaller share of the housing here, which shapes who does this work. Census figures put the owner-occupied rate in Nassau County at 81.9 percent for 2020–2024, and Census Reporter shows about 82 percent of occupied homes across the Nassau–Suffolk division as owner-occupied. The apartments that do exist are concentrated: in older garden complexes, in village downtowns and, increasingly, in new buildings near train stations. Plenty of security companies on Long Island are built around single-family homes and alarm monitoring contracts. Fewer are set up to walk a 200-unit complex with a managing agent, test the cable between buildings, and hand back a written scope per property that an owner can approve.

The newer stock is growing fast in a few places. Newsday reported in September 2026 that more than 1,100 rental units have opened in the Village of Mineola since 2010, with roughly 1,000 more planned, and that construction started on a 172-unit building a short walk from the village's LIRR station. In November 2026 the paper reported construction underway on a 215-unit apartment building near the Baldwin station, and Patchogue has a 262-unit development on West Main Street about half a mile from its station. One developer told Newsday, "The model of Mineola will eventually grow in other villages and towns." For managers, that means more buildings arriving with cloud intercoms, phone-based entry and dozens of cameras, all of which need someone to run them after the developer's installer leaves.

Jurisdiction is the other half of the problem. Nassau County alone contains two cities, three towns and 64 incorporated villages, and Suffolk adds ten towns and its own villages on top. Each can have its own building department, its own rental permit rules and, in Suffolk, its own answer to whether the county's alarm registration law reaches it at all. A manager with properties in Hempstead, Islip and Brookhaven is dealing with three different rental-permit regimes before a single camera goes up. One contractor who knows where those lines fall, and who tells you before a job which office will care about it, saves the manager from learning each town's habits the expensive way.

Finally, the New York City rules that most security marketing assumes do not apply here. The Multiple Dwelling Law, which contains the well-known intercom and self-locking door requirement for buildings of eight or more apartments, covers cities of 325,000 people or more, which on Long Island means none. Towns and villages fall under the Multiple Residence Law and the state Uniform Code, enforced locally. HPD violations, the city's 24-hour and one-week tenant access notices, and the city's smart-access privacy law are not the framework for a building in Freeport or Ronkonkoma. That changes how a Long Island property manager should read most of what turns up in a search, and it is part of why this page exists separately from our New York City property management page.

  • 81.9% owner-occupied in Nassau (U.S. Census QuickFacts, 2020–2024); rentals are the minority, clustered in complexes, downtowns and station areas.
  • About 8,100 rent-stabilized units in Nassau County, according to The Real Deal's June 2026 report on the Nassau rent guidelines board.   • More than 1,100 rental units opened in Mineola since 2010, with about 1,000 more planned (Newsday, September 2026).

  • Two years or 15 months: the term of a Town of Islip rental occupancy permit (two years) and a Town of Brookhaven rental registration (15 months), both issued after an inspection.

Entry panels and intercoms: one building, or fourteen front doors

On a mid-rise near a train station, the intercom question looks like it does in the city: one lobby panel, a list of apartments, a door release. On a garden complex it is a different animal. Each two-story building usually has its own small vestibule with its own panel, sometimes a row of buttons with apartment numbers, sometimes nothing but a lock and a buzzer wired decades ago. When a manager tells us “the intercom at the complex is broken,” the first job is finding out which of the panels are failing, whether they share a power supply in one basement, and whether the cable between them still carries a signal after years of wet soil and frost.

Repairs start with the $250 service call. The component prices on our door buzzer repair page for Long Island run $250–$525 to replace an apartment station, $325–$650 for a 24-volt transformer, $450–$950 for an electric strike with alignment, $650–$1,800 for lobby panel work and $850–$2,400 to repair or re-terminate a riser pair. Diagnosis on a multi-panel complex takes longer than on a single lobby, and we tell the office before leaving which buildings are healthy and which need parts.

When the panels are past saving, the choice is between new panels on the existing wire, a system that calls tenants' phones over the internet, or new network cable to each building. On scattered garden buildings the phone-based route often wins, because it avoids trenching. On a newer station-area building with clean risers, a wired IP system may be the better buy. The installation side, including multi-unit systems that run $1,200–$4,500+ on our published Long Island pricing, is on our page for intercom installation on Long Island; for faults on an existing system, see intercom repair on Long Island. Key fobs and readers: who gets in, and how fast the office can change it

Turnover drives most access control decisions in a rental portfolio. Every move-out on a keyed complex means rekeying an apartment and often a building door, and the master keys that porters and contractors carry drift out of control within a year. Fob and card readers on building doors, laundry rooms, storage areas and maintenance garages let the office cancel one tenant's credential and issue another without calling a locksmith. Our access control installation page for Long Island publishes $1,500–$2,500 installed for a single-door reader with an electric strike, and $3,500–$12,000 installed for a gate with a vehicle reader and barrier arm.

The choice that matters most on a spread-out property is where the system is managed. A cloud-managed controller lets a manager in a Melville office deactivate a fob at a Riverhead complex from a laptop. An on-site controller with no remote access means someone drives out every time a tenant leaves. We set up systems so the management office can add and remove credentials itself, train the staff who will do it, and come back only for hardware changes. New York City's Tenant Data Privacy Act, which governs smart-access systems in the city, is a city law and does not follow a fob across the county line; we still recommend that owners run any face or fingerprint reader past their own counsel before installing it in a residential building, and we do not give legal advice on it.

Elevator floor control in station-area buildings

Elevators are rare in garden complexes and common in the new five- and six-story buildings going up around LIRR stations. In those buildings, floor control keeps a delivery driver or a visitor from riding to every floor, and it lets the office limit the garage level, the roof deck or a fitness room to tenants who have signed a waiver. Our elevator access control page for Long Island lists a card, fob, code or intercom reader from $1,725 per elevator car in Nassau and $1,800 per car in Suffolk, and biometric readers from $2,875 and $3,000 per car. Floor-by-floor restriction is quoted per building, and the elevator company's licensed mechanic makes the connection inside the controller; we coordinate the date with them.

Door hardware, strikes and mag locks

A reader is only as good as the door it is mounted beside. On garden buildings, the vestibule doors are often original, the closers are worn, and the frames have shifted. A new reader on a door that does not latch by itself buys nothing. Before we quote a reader, we check the closer, the latch, the frame and the hinge side, and we put any door repair on the scope as its own line so the owner can see it. Magnetic locks are useful on glass storefront-style doors common in newer buildings, but they need a request-to-exit device and a fire-alarm release where a fire alarm exists, and the building's code office has the final word on egress hardware. We install the low-voltage and door hardware side; any new high-voltage circuit for a lock power supply is the building's electrician, because we do not do electrical work.

Cameras across grounds, lots and laundry rooms

Camera work on Long Island rentals is mostly outdoor work. The expensive questions are where the recorder lives, how the signal reaches cameras at the far end of a lot, and whether the property has any light at night. A complex with a single management office often records every building in one place; a scattered portfolio usually gets a recorder per property, with the office viewing all of them from one app. For the camera side specific to rental buildings, including placement, retention and published prices, see our page on apartment building security camera installation on Long Island. Four-camera systems start at $999 in Nassau and $1,099 in Suffolk on our Long Island camera price list; most properties need more than four, and those are quoted after the walk.

Managers who also look after board-run buildings will find the co-op and condo versions of this at co-op security camera installation on Long Island and condo security camera installation on Long Island. Commercial properties in the same portfolio, such as a strip of stores or a small office building, are covered by our commercial security camera installation page for Long Island. We do not sell a monitored-video service on this page; the cameras record locally and the office or the owner views them.

Parking lots, vehicle gates and garages

Parking is a bigger share of a Long Island property than it is of a city building, and it is where many of the incidents a manager hears about actually happen: car break-ins, dumped furniture, cars that belong to nobody on the lease, and damage that no one admits to. Cameras on parking rows work best when they are mounted high, aimed along the rows rather than across them, and placed where existing lights reach. A camera aimed at the lot entrance from a short distance gives far better detail on each vehicle than one wide view trying to cover the whole field.

Where an owner wants to control who parks at all, a vehicle gate with a reader for tenants and a call box for visitors is the usual answer; our Long Island access control pricing for a gate with a vehicle reader and barrier arm is listed earlier on this page. Garages under station-area buildings get a reader at the vehicle door and at the stair or elevator from the garage, so the garage cannot be used as a back door into the residential floors.

Mailboxes, packages and delivery doors

Garden complexes were built for the mail, not for daily parcel deliveries. Cluster mailboxes sit in open vestibules or outdoors, and boxes pile up on stairs and stoops. What works depends on the layout. Where each building has a vestibule, a small camera over the mailboxes and a reader-controlled inner door keep packages inside a locked space. Where the complex has a management office, a single package room with a reader and a camera near the office can replace a dozen exposed drop points. In station-area buildings, a separate delivery code or a courier credential that only opens the package room keeps drivers out of the residential lobby. We install the doors, readers and cameras; we do not run a package service.

Alarms for offices, maintenance garages and vacant units, and the permits that come with them

Management offices, leasing offices, maintenance shops and empty apartments between tenants are where Long Island managers most often want an intrusion alarm. Central-station monitoring through our provider is $30 a month keypad-only or $45 a month with the phone app, the two tiers on our alarm installation page for Long Island. Alarm installation for a small business starts at $1,500 on that page, and larger layouts are quoted after the walk.

Every monitored alarm on Long Island also comes with paperwork. In Nassau, the county police issue burglar alarm permits at $100 for a residence and $200 for a business, valid for two years. In Suffolk, the county's Alarm Management Program, under chapter 290 of the county code, requires alarm owners in the Suffolk County Police District to register, at $50 for residences and $100 for commercial locations, and gives registered locations two false alarms before a service fee. Some Suffolk towns and villages with their own police departments are outside that program. We tell the office which permit applies to each property and make sure the panel is programmed to keep false dispatches down; the permit itself is in the alarm owner's name.

Fire alarm work: Suffolk, yes; Nassau, not at this time

Fire alarm systems are a separate trade from cameras and access control, and they never share equipment with a camera system. In Suffolk County we take on commercial and multifamily fire alarm work that follows the local fire marshal's review, starting from sealed drawings and ending with an acceptance test, as described on our page for fire alarm installation on Long Island. In Nassau County, fire alarm permits require a company number from the County Fire Marshal that we do not hold at this time, so we do not take Nassau fire alarm work; we can still handle the access control release that ties a door to an existing fire alarm, coordinated with the fire alarm company of record. Fire alarm work is never priced on this page, because every system is quoted per building from its drawings.

Cable between buildings and the 100-meter problem

Ethernet cable, which carries most modern cameras, readers and IP intercoms, is limited to about 100 meters (328 feet) between active devices under the TIA-568 standard. On a city lot that limit almost never comes up. On a Long Island complex it comes up on the first walk: the parking lot camera is 500 feet from the office, the far building is across a lawn, the maintenance garage is at the edge of the property. The answers are fiber between buildings, a PoE extender or switch at a midpoint, a point-to-point wireless bridge, or a cellular camera where nothing else is practical. Each one has a cost and a failure mode, and we write the choice into the scope rather than leaving it for install day. Published per-drop pricing of $150–$300 is on our structured cabling page for Long Island; long outdoor runs are priced on the walk.

Storms, outages and battery backup

Long Island properties sit closer to the weather than most city buildings. Nor'easters and tropical storms knock out power across whole neighborhoods, south-shore basements take on water, and lightning finds long outdoor cable runs. A security system that dies with the power leaves a complex with doors that will not release and cameras that recorded nothing during the one night the owner needs to see. We size battery backup for door controllers, recorders and network switches so the entry keeps working and the recorder keeps a clean record through a typical outage, and we tell the office plainly how long that backup is expected to last.

Placement matters as much as batteries. Where a garden building's intercom power supply and recorder sit on a basement floor, we move them up the wall or to a higher room when we replace them, because one flood can take out every building that shares that basement. Outdoor runs that leave a building get surge protection at both ends, and cameras on poles get grounded properly. Fail-safe and fail-secure choices on each door are made with the owner and the local code office, so the manager knows in advance which doors unlock and which stay locked when the power goes.

Maintenance agreements and service calls across the portfolio

We write maintenance agreements for Long Island portfolios after a survey of the properties they cover. The agreement lists each property and its systems, the scheduled checks (door closers and strikes, power supplies and batteries, recorder storage, outdoor cable and connectors after winter), how service calls are requested, and how they are billed. Prices and terms are set per portfolio; we do not publish tiers, offer guaranteed response times, or sign service level agreements. Calls and texts are answered at any hour by our AI receptionist, a person responds from 8 a.m. to 8 p.m. every day, and visits are booked first come, first served once approved and paid, early in the day, seven days a week.

Building Specialists

Property Management Security for Long Island's Building Stock

Long Island rentals fall into a handful of building types, and each one fails in its own way. The notes below come from what we find on walks, not from a catalog. They explain why the same request, “we want fobs on the doors,” produces very different scopes from one property to the next.

Postwar garden-apartment complexes

Two- and three-story brick buildings around lawns and parking courts, many from the late 1940s to the 1960s. Many separate entrances, original doors, buzzer wiring buried between buildings, laundry rooms in basements, and very little exterior lighting.

Station-area mid-rises

New five- to seven-story rental buildings near LIRR stations in places like Mineola, Baldwin and Patchogue. One lobby, a garage or surface lot, an elevator, and a system the developer chose, often cloud-based with a monthly fee.

Village downtown mixed-use

Two to four floors of apartments over stores on a main street. A narrow residential door beside a storefront, a shared rear lot, and retail tenants with their own alarm companies.

Small multifamily and accessory apartments

Two- to four-family houses and legal accessory units held by investors, often under a town rental permit. A video doorbell, a keypad lock and one or two cameras are usually the whole system.

Rental communities with clubhouses

Larger managed communities with a leasing office, a pool, a fitness room and sometimes a gated entrance. Amenity access schedules and visitor control at the gate matter as much as the apartment doors.

Commercial and office properties

Strip centers, small office buildings and industrial units that sit in the same management portfolio. Recorder-based cameras, after-hours alarms and card access for tenants' employees.

Garden complexes: how we work a property with a dozen front doors

The first walk on a garden complex is mostly tracing. We open every vestibule panel, find where each building's power comes from, and check whether the buildings share a master unit somewhere in a basement. On older complexes it is common to find that two or three buildings were already upgraded by an earlier vendor and the rest were not, which leaves the office with two systems, two sets of fobs and two support numbers. The scope we write for a complex like that usually standardizes on one platform and phases the buildings so that tenants in a given building switch over on one day.

The between-building cable decides most of the cost. Where the old buzzer wire still tests clean, some panels can reuse it. Where it does not, a phone-based panel with its own internet connection, or a wireless bridge from a central building, is often cheaper than trenching across a lawn and a parking court. Exterior lighting is the next weak spot; cameras at night are only as good as the light near them, and adding site lighting is the building electrician's job, so we flag dark areas in the survey rather than leaving the manager to discover them on the first recording.

Station-area buildings: inheriting the developer's choices

New rentals near train stations tend to open with a full system already in place: a touchscreen panel at the lobby, phone-based entry, readers at the garage and amenity doors, and dozens of cameras on a cloud platform. The problem shows up when the developer's installer finishes the punch list and leaves. The management company inherits administrator accounts in someone else's name, a monthly platform fee it never negotiated, and no drawings. When we take over a building like that, the first visit is an inventory: what is installed, which accounts control it, what is under warranty with whom, and which subscriptions are actually needed. Lease-up schedules make timing tight, so we work around the move-in calendar the leasing office gives us.

Downtown mixed-use: keeping the apartments separate from the stores

In village downtowns from Rockville Centre to Patchogue, the residential entrance is often a single door between two storefronts, with a stair to the apartments and a shared rear lot. Retail tenants usually have their own alarm and camera vendors. The work for the property manager is keeping the residential door, the stair and the apartments on the building's own system, with credentials the office controls, and making sure a store's alarm company does not end up with a key or a code to the residential side. Where the owner wants cameras on the rear lot, we put them on the building's recorder rather than borrowing a store's view.

Small multifamily and accessory units under town rental permits

Investors who own two-family houses or legal accessory apartments in towns such as Islip and Brookhaven are already used to inspections: the Town of Islip issues a rental occupancy permit after an on-site inspection and renews it every two years, and Brookhaven's rental registration runs for 15 months and requires an inspection by the town or a licensed professional within 90 days of a temporary registration. Both laws focus on safety items such as working smoke and carbon monoxide alarms. For these properties we keep the security side simple: a wired video doorbell or a small door station, a keypad lock on a shared entry, and one or two cameras on a small recorder the owner can watch from a phone.

Rental communities with amenities and gates

Larger rental communities, especially in Suffolk, often have a clubhouse, a pool and a vehicle gate. Here the work is about schedules: the pool reader on summer hours, the fitness room on a waiver list, the clubhouse open only for a booked event, and a gate call box that reaches the leasing office during business hours and a phone after. Snowplows, salt and sprinklers are part of the design, because a reader post at a drive entrance takes a beating every winter. The board-run equivalent of this, gatehouses and HOA common areas, is covered on our condo pages.

Commercial properties in the same portfolio

Many Long Island management companies also run strip centers, small office buildings or light-industrial units along corridors such as Route 110, Sunrise Highway and Old Country Road. Those properties need recorder-based cameras covering the lot and the loading area, after-hours alarms with their own permits, and card access for tenants' employees. Treating them as part of the same vendor relationship keeps one certificate of insurance on file, one point of contact and one invoice format, even though the equipment differs from the residential buildings.

For Management Companies

Vendor Setup, Maintenance and Multi-Property Work on Long Island

Getting us on your vendor list

Long Island management companies onboard vendors the same way city firms do, and we fit the usual process. We fill out your vendor application, supply a W-9 for the contracting company, and review and sign a master service agreement. The certificate of insurance naming the owner and the management company as additional insureds is issued when a job is awarded, to your exact wording, rather than attached to a quote reply; requirements vary by owner and the certificate is easier to get right once the scope is fixed. Being set up in your system lets your accounts payable pay us; it is not a claim that we sit on any company's approved list.

Scheduling across two counties

The distance from the Queens line to the East End is well over a hundred miles of road, and traffic on the Long Island Expressway and the parkways sets the schedule more than the work does. We group visits by area, so a technician working in central Nassau handles the next property nearby rather than crossing to Suffolk and back. Visits are booked into two-hour arrival windows, early in the day, and technicians arrive inside the window, never ahead of it. Properties far out on the North or South Fork are usually set as the first or last stop of a day. Requests go on the schedule first come, first served once approved and paid.

Working in occupied rentals without the city's notice rules

New York City's rule that sets 24 hours' notice for inspections and one week for repairs in rental apartments is a city regulation; outside the city, access is governed mostly by the lease and by general state landlord-tenant law, and owners should follow their own counsel on how much notice to give. In practice we work the same way everywhere: apartment visits are scheduled through the management office, the office sends the tenant the notice it uses, we keep to daytime hours unless the tenant asks otherwise, and anything that needs a tenant at home is grouped into as few visits as possible. Common-area work, which makes up most of a security project, goes ahead without entering apartments.

Turnover on a large complex

A rental community with a few hundred apartments may see several move-outs a month, and every one of them touches security: the departing tenant's fobs, the entry directory, the mailbox key, the parking permit and any app account. On keyed properties it also means a locksmith. We set the access system up so that one task in the office, cancelling a tenant, removes that person's credentials everywhere at once, and the new tenant's credentials can be issued at the lease signing. Directory entries on the entry panels are edited from the same place, so a new tenant's name appears at the door the day they move in rather than whenever someone remembers to drive out.

Contractors and vendors are the other half of turnover. Painters, cleaners, appliance installers and landscapers rotate through vacant apartments and common areas all year. Instead of lending them master keys, the office can issue a time-limited credential that works on the doors they need, during the hours they work, and expires on its own. When a dispute comes up later about who was in a vacant unit, the access log and the camera record answer it.

Apartment doors are the last piece of turnover. Rekeying a lock at every move-out adds up across a large complex, and code or app locks let the office change access in minutes instead. The trade-offs are batteries, the fire rating of the door, and tenants who would rather carry a key, so many owners start with vacant units and convert others as leases turn. Options and models are on our page for smart lock installation on Long Island.

One point of contact, one written record per property

Every property we work on gets a running record: what is installed, where each power supply and recorder lives, which accounts control which system, what we changed on each visit, and what we recommended but the owner has not approved yet. That record goes to the management office, not just into our files, so a new property manager or a new super can pick it up without calling three former vendors. When a property is sold or moves to another management company, the record and the administrator accounts go with it, in the owner's name.

Owners who live out of the area

Many Long Island rentals belong to owners who do not live nearby: family partnerships, out-of-state investors, or funds that bought a portfolio of older complexes. Telling tenants about a changeover

Most complaints after a security upgrade come from tenants who did not know it was happening. A good changeover notice says which building is switching and on what day, what the tenant has to do (pick up a fob at the office, download an app, be home for an in-unit station), what will work differently afterward, and who to call with problems. We draft that notice for the office in plain language, with the dates from our schedule, and the office sends it through its own channels. On complexes with tenants who speak several languages, the office usually translates it; we keep the wording short so translation is easy.

On the day itself, our technician starts with the building's front door so the entry problem is solved first, keeps the office informed when each building goes live, and leaves printed instructions at the panel where tenants will see them. Tenants who miss the day are picked up on a follow-up visit grouped with other work, not one at a time.

Rent-stabilized buildings in Nassau

Some Nassau municipalities opted into the state's Emergency Tenant Protection Act, which brings rent stabilization to buildings of six or more units built before 1974 in those places, administered by NYS Homes and Community Renewal. Owners of regulated buildings must keep required services in good working order, and tenants can file complaints with the agency when a service is reduced. When we work in those buildings we keep the existing entry function running during a changeover, put any reduction or change of service on the scope in writing, and leave the regulatory question, such as whether a change needs an application to the agency, to the owner and its counsel.

Rental permits, building departments and alarm permits

Before a job starts we tell the office which local offices are likely to care about it. Exterior work on a building in an incorporated village goes through that village's building department; in an unincorporated hamlet it is the town. The state Department of State notes that the Uniform Code is “automatically in effect in each city, town, and village in the State (except New York City),” with enforcement handled locally. Low-voltage camera and access work rarely needs a building permit, but door hardware that changes egress, and anything tied to a fire alarm, may. Rental-permit inspections in towns like Islip and Brookhaven focus on smoke and carbon monoxide alarms rather than security systems, and alarm permits go to the county police or the local department as described above.

Local rules and paperwork

The town is inspecting one of our rentals for its rental permit. Does the security equipment come into it?

Usually not directly. The Islip and Brookhaven rental laws we have read focus on safety items such as smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, occupancy and the general condition of the unit, not on cameras or intercoms. What can matter is anything we did to a door: a lock or a closer that no longer latches, or hardware that changes how someone exits. We check egress hardware as part of our own work and fix our side before the inspector arrives.

Which of our Long Island buildings need an alarm permit, and whose name goes on it?

Any monitored burglar alarm in Nassau needs a county police permit, $100 for a residence and $200 for a business, renewed every two years. In Suffolk, alarm owners inside the county police district register under chapter 290, at $50 residential and $100 commercial; some towns and villages with their own police fall outside it. The permit goes in the name of the alarm owner, normally the property owner or the entity on the lease for an office. We tell you which applies to each address when we install.

Who pays when the police bill a false alarm at a building we manage?

The fees are charged to the permit or registration holder, so in practice it is whoever owns the alarm, and your management agreement decides whether that cost passes to the owner. Suffolk gives registered locations two false alarms a year before a service fee and charges unregistered ones from the first. The real fix is technical: correct sensor placement, entry delays that suit the staff who use the office, and pets and heaters kept out of motion zones. We check those on every alarm service call.

Spread-out layouts

How far apart can two buildings be before a camera or intercom cable cannot run between them?

For network cable, about 100 meters (328 feet) between active devices is the practical limit. Past that we use fiber between the buildings, a powered switch partway, or a wireless bridge if there is a clear line of sight. Older two-wire buzzer cable behaves differently and can run farther, but on garden complexes it is often the cable that has been in wet ground the longest. We measure on the walk and write the method into the quote.

What happens to the old buzzer wiring once a garden building moves to phone-based entry?

Usually it stays in the walls, disconnected and labeled at both ends, because pulling cable out of occupied apartments and buried runs costs more than it saves. We remove the old panels and in-unit stations the owner no longer wants, cap the wiring so nobody mistakes it for a live circuit later, and note it on the property record. If the owner may want in-unit answering again someday, a clean old pair can be kept as a spare path rather than abandoned.

Can the basement laundry room in each garden building be limited to that building's tenants?

Yes. A reader on each laundry door can be set to accept only the credentials assigned to that building, so tenants from the far end of the complex, and people who are not tenants at all, cannot walk in. A small camera over the machines and the door settles most disputes about damaged machines or missing clothes. Where the laundry has an outside door as well as an inside one, both are controlled, or the outside door is set to exit only.

When a vacant apartment is found damaged, can the system show who went into that building?

If the building door and the unit door are on readers, the access log lists every credential that opened them, with times, and a camera at the entrance shows who came through with it. That is why we recommend giving painters, cleaners and other contractors their own time-limited credentials instead of a shared master key. With keyed doors there is no log, and the camera record is all the office has.

New buildings

What should be working before the first tenants move into a new building near the train station?

Before the first move-in we want the lobby panel showing a correct directory, every apartment able to answer it, the garage and amenity readers on the schedule the leasing office wants, delivery access separated from residents, cameras recording with the office able to view them, and administrator accounts held in the owner's or manager's name. We also want a written equipment list and the platform fees in writing. Anything missing at lease-up becomes a tenant complaint in the first week.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Property Management Security on Long Island

Search summaries, chatbot answers and national cost guides from sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr tend to describe building security in New York as if every property were in Manhattan, or as if every property were a single-family house. Long Island rentals are neither. These are the claims we most often hear from managers who looked something up before calling.

1. “New York law requires an intercom in every apartment building”

The intercom requirement people quote comes from section 50-a of the Multiple Dwelling Law, which covers buildings of eight or more apartments built or converted after 1968. The state Attorney General's tenant guide summarizes it in general terms, and summaries often stop there.

The Multiple Dwelling Law applies to cities of 325,000 or more people. Long Island's towns and villages fall under the Multiple Residence Law instead, and we have not found an equivalent intercom mandate there, so we do not claim one.

That does not make the intercom optional in practice. Tenants expect a working door, leases often promise one, and an entry system that fails generates the same calls to the office whether or not a statute names it.

2. “An intercom costs a couple of thousand dollars”

National guides such as HomeAdvisor put intercom installation in a normal range of $1,200 to $8,500, written mostly with houses and small offices in mind.

On a Long Island complex the number depends on how many separate entrances there are, whether the cable between buildings still works, and whether tenants will answer on a phone or need a station inside. Our own published multi-unit range is $1,200–$4,500+ for a building; a complex with many vestibules is several buildings, not one.

That is why we quote building-wide work only after the walk, and why a garden complex's scope often reads like a list of small buildings with a shared link between them.

3. “Wireless cameras are the easy answer for big properties”

Summaries often recommend battery or Wi-Fi cameras because they skip cable. On a rental complex, Wi-Fi rarely reaches a parking lot from inside a brick building, and batteries struggle in a Long Island January.

Wireless links have their place: a bridge between two buildings with a clear line of sight, a cellular camera at a far gate, a solar unit in a corner with no power. They are tools for the edges of a property, not the core.

Our default on the buildings themselves is wired cameras powered over network cable, with the wireless pieces written into the scope only where the walk shows they are the better choice.

4. “The city's tenant notice and privacy rules apply on Long Island too”

Many articles about apartment access and smart locks are written about New York City rules: notice periods set by a city regulation, and the city's law on smart-access systems in residential buildings.

Those are city rules. Outside the city, access to apartments is mostly a matter of the lease and state landlord-tenant law, and no city privacy statute follows a fob reader into Nassau or Suffolk.

We still recommend owners treat tenant data carefully and run any biometric reader past counsel. We schedule apartment visits through the office either way.

5. “Any alarm company can monitor any building with no paperwork”

Generic answers skip local permits entirely. On Long Island, alarm permits and registrations are a county police matter, with fees and false-alarm charges attached.

Nassau County Police issue two-year permits and report being asked to respond to more than 125,000 false alarms a year. Suffolk registers alarm owners inside its police district and charges escalating fees for repeat false alarms, with some towns and villages outside the program.

A manager with offices and vacant units in both counties needs someone tracking which property falls under which program, which is part of every alarm job we do here.

6. “Fire alarms are just one more add-on from the camera installer”

Summaries treat fire alarms as one more security add-on. They are a separate, code-driven system that begins with engineered drawings and ends with an inspection.

In Nassau, fire alarm permits require a County Fire Marshal company number. We do not hold one at this time, so we do not take Nassau fire alarm work. In Suffolk we do, following the local fire marshal's process.

We never bundle fire alarm work with cameras, and we never publish a fire alarm price. Each system is quoted from its drawings.

7. “Property management security means guards or a virtual doorman”

Search results lump guard services, remote monitoring and virtual-doorman subscriptions in with electronic systems, and some vendors sell all of it as a managed service.

We do not provide guards, and we do not run a virtual-doorman or managed-security service. We install and maintain the doors, panels, readers and cameras that a building's staff, or an outside service the owner hires, would rely on.

For most Long Island rentals, which have no doorman to replace, the security that matters is simpler: doors that latch, entry that works, credentials the office controls, and cameras that recorded what happened.

Field notes: walking a garden complex for the first time

On a first visit to a garden-apartment complex, we start in the management office or the super's apartment, because that is usually where the old master station or the recorder ended up. Then we walk the property in a loop with the site plan in hand, stopping at every vestibule. At each one we open the panel, note the model and the wiring, try the release, and watch whether the door closes and latches on its own. In basements we look for the transformer and for signs of water around it, because a wet basement and a buzzer power supply are a common pairing on older complexes. Along the way we pace off the distance between buildings and to the parking rows, mark the dark stretches, and look for poles or eaves that could carry a camera. By the end we know which buildings share a power supply, where the cable between them runs, and whether reusing it is realistic. That becomes a one-page summary per building, which the manager can forward to the owner before any price is on the table.

Field notes: the spring check after a Long Island winter

Winter is hard on outdoor security equipment here. Salt from walkways corrodes the bottom edge of entry panels and reader posts, freeze-thaw cycles loosen strikes and shift frames, plows clip anything near a drive entrance, and ice in a camera housing can fog a lens for weeks. When a maintenance agreement includes a spring check, we look at exactly those things: the panel faces and their seals, the strike alignment on every exterior door, the reader posts at gates and drive entrances, the condition of outdoor cable and connectors, and the battery backups that ran during any outages. We also check that the recorder kept recording through the winter and that its clock did not drift after a power loss. The report that follows lists each item, what we fixed on the visit, and what needs the owner's approval.

How long a Long Island property management security project takes

Most repairs finish on the first visit, or on a second one if a part has to be ordered. A single panel change on a building with good wire is usually a day on site once the equipment is in. A garden complex takes longer, because each building is its own small job and tenants in each building get their own changeover day. Projects with fiber or wireless links between buildings add time for the link work. Special-order equipment arrives on the maker's schedule, not ours, and we give a date only once it ships.
