The Neighborhood

Why Brighton Beach Properties Need Fire Alarm Installation

Brighton Beach is a dense strip of apartment buildings between Ocean Parkway, Corbin Place, the Belt Parkway and the boardwalk, and most people here live above the ground, in elevator buildings of six stories or more. That shapes fire detection in a way that statistics about houses miss. When a fire starts in one apartment of a six-story building, the people most at risk are the ones inside it, then the people on the same floor, then the people above. A smoke alarm in the apartment where it starts is the first and often the only warning, because most of the older buildings here were never built with a fire alarm system in the halls. Whether that one alarm is there, working and loud enough behind a closed bedroom door is the question that matters most, and in recent Brighton Beach fires it is the question that went wrong.

The local fire record of the last two years is short and specific. In September 2024 firefighters found several burning e-bikes in a sixth-floor apartment, and the battalion chief told News 12 that the fire itself was small but the smoke was not. In October 2024 a lithium-ion battery charging in a second-floor bedroom on Brighton 3rd Street, near Brightwater Court, failed before dawn. The FDNY said it blocked the way out of the room, and a 69-year-old man died. ABC7 reported that a smoke detector had been removed from that apartment. On Christmas Day 2024 a second-floor fire on Neptune Avenue sent a firefighter and one other person to the hospital. In December 2025 a two-alarm fire on Brighton 8th Street started on the fourth floor, spread through several apartments, and displaced 49 people, 15 of them children; neighbors went door to door to get people out. In March 2026 more than 140 firefighters spent two hours on a fire in a vacant building on Brighton 5th Walk that a complaint to the Buildings Department had described as illegally occupied by 20 to 30 people.

None of those reports says whether a building fire alarm sounded, and we do not guess. What they show is the pattern a detection plan has to answer here: fires that start in a bedroom or a living room upstairs, often at night, sometimes from a battery; smoke that fills a floor faster than the fire spreads; and neighbors who learn about it from a knock on the door. Add the people. The Census Bureau's latest survey of Community District 13, which covers Brighton Beach and Coney Island, counts a median age of 48.3, 56.8 percent of residents born abroad and 67.5 percent speaking a language other than English at home. Many apartments here are home to someone in their seventies or eighties who sleeps with a door shut and a window unit running, and may not hear a single alarm in a hallway two rooms away.

The buildings bring their own questions. The 1920s and 1930s elevator houses near Ocean Parkway and Brightwater Court are mostly protected by the detectors inside each apartment rather than by a system in the halls. The postwar towers to the north have long risers and, in some cases, building systems that a managing agent has to keep in service. The Oceana condominium, sixteen buildings with more than nine hundred units built after Sandy with its switchgear raised and its elevator controls on the roof, runs engineered systems. Along Brighton Beach Avenue, walk-ups sit over shops, restaurants and halls under the elevated B and Q, and some of those halls seat hundreds of people at a time. Each of these gets a different plan below.

Six Brighton Beach facts that change a fire alarm plan

  • The population skews older. The Census Bureau's 2024 survey puts the median age in Community District 13 at 48.3, and Bklyner reported in 2019 that seniors make up about 20 percent of the district's residents, near the top citywide. Older residents need more time to get out and are more likely to miss a single quiet alarm, so we plan for linked devices and test from the farthest bedroom.
  • Most homes share a building. The 1920s and 1930s apartment buildings on Ocean Parkway and Brightwater Court and the postwar towers further north put dozens of households on one stair. A fire in one apartment is everyone's problem, which is why the condition of the alarm in each unit matters to the whole building.
  • Lithium-ion batteries have already killed here. The FDNY traced the October 2024 death on Brighton 3rd Street to an unregulated battery bought online and charging in a bedroom, and the September 2024 sixth-floor fire to burning e-bikes. Citywide, the FDNY counted 277 lithium-ion fires in 2024.
  • Language is part of the installation. Two in three residents of the district speak a language other than English at home, and a Russian-speaking diaspora gives the neighborhood its nickname, Little Odessa. HPD requires owners to give each household written instructions when detectors go in, and instructions nobody can read do not do their job.
  • The avenue holds big rooms. The Master Theater at 1029 Brighton Beach Avenue lists a capacity of 1,326, the National at 273 Brighton Beach Avenue is a split-level supper club with a stage and dance floor, and Tatiana is a banquet hall on the boardwalk. Rooms like these fall under place-of-assembly rules that can require a building fire alarm.
  • Water decides where equipment lives. First Street's flood model puts 99 percent of Brighton Beach properties at some flood risk over 30 years, and FEMA maps 756 of 1,774 properties in a special flood hazard area. A panel, a power supply or a standby battery in a basement that takes seawater can be out of service just when the building needs it.

Prewar elevator houses near the avenue: one detector per apartment carries most of the load

The older elevator buildings that line the numbered streets between Brighton Beach Avenue and Brightwater Court were put up when Brighton Beach turned from a resort into a year-round neighborhood. In most of them, the fire protection residents actually depend on is the smoke detector inside their own apartment and the self-closing door between that apartment and the hall. HPD's rules say how that detector has to be placed: in a Class A multiple dwelling, smoke and carbon monoxide detectors go within fifteen feet of the primary entrance to each room lawfully used for sleeping, and every device must have an end-of-life alarm. The owner installs them and keeps a record of when each one went in and when it expires; the tenant tests them and replaces any device that goes missing while they live there.

In practice, that means the first job in a building like this is an inventory. We go apartment by apartment and write down what is on the ceiling, whether it works, how old it is, and whether it matches the bedrooms the apartment actually has now, which is not always the layout it was built with. Apartments with a bedroom at the end of a long inside hall sometimes need a second device to stay within fifteen feet of every sleeping room. Kitchens that open straight into a living room where someone sleeps on a sofa bed need a device placed so that ordinary cooking does not make people take it down. The October 2024 fire on Brighton 3rd Street is the reason we press on that last point: ABC7 reported that a smoke detector had been removed from that apartment, and a detector that keeps going off for toast is the one most likely to end up in a drawer.

Cable is rarely the problem in these buildings, because most apartment devices are sealed-battery or wired to an existing circuit by an electrician. Access is the problem. A building with dozens of apartments needs dozens of doors opened, and many residents are home all day but cautious about strangers. We schedule floor by floor, give the super a list with times, and leave each apartment with written instructions. When the building has a hall system or a panel in the cellar, we also look at where that panel sits, because a cellar that took water in 2012 is a poor place for the one box that has to work in an emergency.

Art Deco buildings on Ocean Parkway and Brightwater Court: the board, the shareholder and the lobby finishes

Some of the older apartment houses near Ocean Parkway and along Brightwater Court are cooperatives, and that splits the fire alarm job in two. The board is responsible for the halls, the lobby, the cellar, the laundry and the trash rooms. Inside each apartment, practice varies. Habitat Magazine reported that many co-op and condo boards pass responsibility for smoke and carbon monoxide detectors on to the apartment owners, and the attorney it quoted, Jeffrey Reich, put it plainly: "Many co-op and condo boards pass the responsibility for carbon-dioxide and smoke detectors on to the apartment owners." Whatever the proprietary lease says, the board still wants every unit protected, so we can work either way: one contract with the board for every apartment, or separate small jobs for each shareholder at the per-unit rate.

The lobbies are the other issue. Decorative plaster, tile and terrazzo are part of what people buy in these buildings, and a board will not thank anyone for surface raceway across a lobby ceiling. Where a building has a panel or hall devices, we look for routes that already exist: closets, the elevator machine space, the cellar ceiling and the back of the mailroom. Where a lobby device has to be visible, we place it where it can be seen and tested without cutting into original work. When a change goes beyond a repair, we tell the board early, because a panel relocation or a technology upgrade is the kind of work the Department of Buildings' 2024 bulletin says needs new plans filed with the FDNY.

Postwar towers to the north: risers, panels and the managing agent's file

The taller buildings toward Neptune Avenue and the Belt Parkway put more apartments on each floor and stack them higher. Some have a building fire alarm panel, hall smoke detectors and devices tied to the elevators, which is a different job from the apartment-only protection in the prewar houses. The questions here are about records and repair. Does the managing agent have the building's FDNY letter of approval for the existing system? What does the panel's own history show? Are replacement parts for the panel still listed and available, or has the manufacturer moved on? The DOB bulletin is clear that a system which cannot be repaired, or for which listed parts are no longer available, has to be fully replaced, and that is a filed project rather than a service call.

Our role in a tower is usually two things. Inside the apartments, we bring every unit up to HPD's detector rules, which apply whether or not the building has a panel. On the building system, we test, diagnose and repair what can be repaired like for like, and where the work needs a design, we work with the owner's registered design professional, the licensed electrical contractor who signs the electrical paperwork, and the FDNY-approved fire alarm company of record. We say at the start which of those parts we do ourselves and which belong to the other firms, so the board is never surprised by a second bill.

The Oceana and newer condos: engineered systems with a raised head end

The Oceana is a different kind of property from anything else in the neighborhood. Commercial Observer described it in 2014 as sixteen buildings with 927 units by Muss Development, with a newer building at 50 Oceana Drive East built to post-Sandy standards: the electrical switchgear moved up onto a raised platform and the elevator controls moved to the roof, along with about 24,000 square feet of commercial space for ground-floor retail and second-floor medical offices. Buildings like these have engineered fire alarm systems that were designed, filed and inspected, and a management office that keeps the paperwork.

Unit owners in buildings like these usually call us about the devices inside their own apartment during a renovation: a new kitchen, a wall moved, a bedroom added where a den used to be. Moving a wall can change what the detection inside a unit has to do, and in a building with an engineered system, devices tied to the building panel belong to the building, not the unit owner. We start with the condo's alteration rules and the management office, confirm which devices in the unit report to the building system, and only then plan anything. Commercial tenants in the retail and medical space get the same treatment: the landlord's system comes first, and nothing is added to it without the landlord's fire alarm company.

Walk-ups over stores: the closed shop below and the family above

Along Brighton Beach Avenue and the southern end of Coney Island Avenue, many buildings are low brick walk-ups with a store or restaurant at street level, two or three apartments upstairs and a cellar used for stock. The danger in these buildings runs upward. A fire that starts in a closed shop or in a cellar full of cartons at three in the morning is seen by no one until the smoke reaches the apartments, and by then the stair the families use to get out may be the route the smoke takes. NY Engineers' summary of the 2022 building code adds a second reason to look hard at the cellar: a mercantile fire area below grade that exceeds 1,500 square feet is one of the conditions that requires a fire alarm system.

The plan for a walk-up over a store therefore starts with the store, not the apartments. We look at what the business does, what it keeps in the cellar, where the electric panels and any gas equipment are, and how the residents' stair is separated from the shop. Then we decide how a fire in the store would reach the people upstairs: through a building system, through linked devices, or through monitoring that calls someone when the store is empty. The apartments still get their own detection under HPD's rules. What changes is that the people upstairs can also learn about a fire in a space they never enter.

Bungalows on the lanes and walks: small houses, close neighbors, confusing addresses

Forgotten NY's walk through the neighborhood describes the laneways that most visitors miss: Brighton 1st Walk, Terrace, Lane, Place and Path between Brighton 1st and 2nd Streets north of Brighton Beach Avenue, Brighton 3rd Court, Walk and Lane between Oceanview and Neptune Avenues, and the wedge of Brighton 10th Street, Court, Path, Terrace and Lane along the old right-of-way of the elevated line. Many of the houses on them began as summer bungalows and were winterized for year-round use, and some have been replaced by taller buildings. They sit a few feet apart, often share walls or walkways, and are reached on foot.

For fire detection these houses are mostly simple: linked smoke and CO alarms on every level, inside and outside the bedrooms, with a carbon monoxide device near the boiler or furnace. Two details are particular to the lanes. The first is the address. The March 2026 fire happened on Brighton 5th Walk, while the Buildings Department complaint News 12 cited for the same building named 23 Brighton 5th Lane. If a monitored alarm sends the wrong one of those to a central station, firefighters lose time finding the door, so we confirm the exact address, the walk name and the nearest street with the owner before any monitoring account is set up. The second is the neighbors. A house a few feet from a vacant or overcrowded one needs alarms that will wake its own family quickly, because a fire next door reaches it faster than on a wide street.

Places of assembly: the room's use decides the system

Brighton Beach has more large rooms than most residential neighborhoods: banquet halls, supper clubs with stage shows, a theater on Brighton Beach Avenue listed at a capacity of 1,326, and restaurants on the boardwalk that fill up every summer weekend. The Department of Buildings' code notes on places of assembly set the main thresholds. A Place of Assembly Certificate of Operation is required where 75 or more people gather indoors. Restaurants count as assembly spaces. A fire alarm system with an emergency power source is required at an occupant load of 300 or more, or where the room is used as a cabaret. An enclosed sidewalk café counts as part of the assembly space.

Those rules make assembly work in Brighton Beach a design job, not a box swap. A hall that adds a stage, a dance floor and late-night shows can change how the code sees it, even if the number of seats stays the same. A restaurant that encloses its sidewalk tables for winter may have added to its assembly space. We work on these jobs alongside the owner's registered design professional and the building's FDNY-approved fire alarm company: surveying, installing and labeling devices to the approved drawings where we are engaged to, coordinating with the sound and lighting people so a show can be stopped when the alarm sounds, and keeping the dining room and back of house protected while the work goes on.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Where the Battery Charges, Where the Bed Is, and Where the Door Is

Find the chargers before the detectors. In a Brighton Beach apartment, the technician's first walk-through is not about the ceiling. It is about the floor: where e-bikes, scooters, mobility scooters, power tool packs and phone power banks sit while they charge, and how close they are to a bed or to the only door out of a room. The FDNY's warning after the Brighton 3rd Street fire was blunt: "Do not put these devices between you and the front door." We write down each charging spot by room, because it changes where the detectors go and what we tell the household.

Count who sleeps where, tonight. Next, we ask who sleeps in each room, including a grandparent on a sofa bed in the living room or a home attendant who stays over. HPD's rule follows rooms lawfully used for sleeping, but fire follows people. Every room someone sleeps in gets a device placed for it.

Close the door and run the air conditioner. Then, with every bedroom door shut and the window unit running, a second person sounds an alarm at the far end of the apartment. If the person in the farthest bedroom cannot hear it clearly, the apartment needs a linked alarm or another device, whatever the minimum says.

Read the label and write the date. Last, every device gets its install date and expiration date on the owner's list, apartment by apartment, so the next check does not depend on anyone's memory.

Batteries and Bedrooms

Four Brighton Beach Fires in Eighteen Months, and What Each One Changes in a Detection Plan

We do not use other people's tragedies as advertising, and nothing below says what any building's alarm did or did not do, because the news reports do not say. We use these fires for one reason: each one points to a decision that is easy to get wrong in a Brighton Beach apartment, and each decision is something an owner, a board or a household can fix before the next one.

Brighton 3rd Street, October 2024: the battery, the bedroom and the missing detector

Shortly after 3:30 in the morning on October 16, 2024, neighbors near Brighton 3rd Street and Brightwater Court heard an explosion. Fire marshals later determined that a lithium-ion battery, bought online for a scooter and charging in a second-floor bedroom, had failed and ignited. A 69-year-old man died in that room. Two adults burned their hands trying to put the fire out, and two children got out unharmed. Chief Fire Marshal Daniel Flynn said the device "was blocking the egress of the person who succumbed to injuries from the fire," and gave the advice the FDNY now repeats at every battery fire: "Do not put these devices between you and the front door," and "You will not be able to put these things out on your own." ABC7 reported that a smoke detector had been removed from the apartment.

Three things in a detection plan follow from that fire. A device has to be in or right outside every room where someone sleeps, and it has to stay there, which means placing it where cooking and steam will not tempt anyone to take it down. Batteries for e-bikes and scooters should charge away from beds and away from the only way out of a room, and we say so in the written instructions we leave. And the household needs to know, before it happens, that the right response to a battery that hisses, pops or smokes is to leave, close the door and call 911, not to fight it. Flynn also noted that the occupants "heard it fail, it made a noise." A smoke alarm is still the backstop for the times nobody hears it, or nobody is awake.

The sixth-floor e-bike fire, September 2024: a small fire and a lot of smoke

On the afternoon of September 10, 2024, firefighters went into a sixth-floor apartment in a Brighton Beach building and found several burning e-bikes. Battalion Chief Michael Stone told News 12, "The fire condition wasn't that severe, but the smoke condition was," and added, "The smoke from those lithium-ion batteries, fires, are very toxic." Four people went to Coney Island Hospital with minor injuries. The building's superintendent said it was the second fire in that apartment.

For a six-story building, that fire is a reminder that smoke is what travels. A detector inside the apartment warns the people in it. The people on the rest of the sixth floor, and on the floors above in a building with an open stair or a leaky door, are warned only if the smoke reaches a device near them or someone knocks. Where a building has hall detection, we test whether it is working and whether it sounds where it needs to. Where it does not, the owner should know what adding it would involve, which in some buildings is a filed alteration and in others is not. And when one apartment has already had a battery fire, the owner has a building problem, not just a tenant problem, and should treat it that way.

Brighton 8th Street, December 2025: fire on the fourth floor, warnings by knock

Just after 8:24 on the evening of December 20, 2025, a fire broke out on the fourth floor of 2822 Brighton 8th Street and spread across several apartments. The FDNY sent 25 units and 106 firefighters and EMS workers, and the fire was out around 9:30. One resident told News 12, "I heard a knock on my door and they said 'fire fire get out get down'." The Red Cross later registered twelve households, 34 adults and 15 children, 49 people in all, who could not go home.

The neighbors who knocked did exactly the right thing, and the lesson is not that they should not have had to. It is that in many Brighton Beach buildings, a knock is the building's fire alarm. If that is true of yours, the first question is whether each apartment has detection that will wake its own household, and the second is whether there is any way for the rest of the building to hear about a fire in one unit. Linked devices across apartments are not usually how multiple dwellings are protected. Which Path Your Building Is On

Detectors, a Repair, or a Filed System: How the Rules Sort Brighton Beach Buildings

Most of the confusion we hear about fire alarms in Brighton Beach comes from mixing up three different things: the detectors HPD requires inside apartments, the repair of a building system that already exists, and a new or altered building system that has to be designed and filed with the FDNY. They are governed by different rules, done by different people and priced differently. Here is how we tell them apart.

Path one: HPD's detector rules for apartments and rented houses

HPD's detector page sets the baseline. Owners of all multiple dwellings must provide at least one approved, operational smoke detector in each dwelling unit. Carbon monoxide detectors are required in multiple dwellings and in one- and two-family homes the owner does not occupy, wherever there is a fossil-fuel-burning device. Natural gas detectors join the list on January 1, 2027, in those same buildings wherever there is gas piping. In Class A multiple dwellings, the smoke and CO detectors go within fifteen feet of the primary entrance to each sleeping room, detection also goes where the fuel-burning or gas appliance is, and every device must have an end-of-life alarm. Combination devices are allowed.

The paperwork is part of the rule. HPD expects owners to give an adult in each apartment a written notice about testing, maintenance and carbon monoxide, to post a notice in the building's entrance area, and to keep records of when the notice was posted, when each device was installed and when each expires. Owners may collect $25 per smoke, CO or gas detector in Class A buildings, $50 for a combined two-type device and $75 for a three-type device, with the tenant given a year to pay; in Class B buildings they may not collect at all. The state adds one more layer: since April 1, 2019, new and replacement smoke alarms sold in New York must be either hardwired or powered by a sealed battery that lasts at least ten years, though existing removable-battery alarms installed under the codes do not have to come down.

Path two: repairing a building system without new plans

Some Brighton Beach buildings, mostly the larger and newer ones, have a building fire alarm system with a panel, hall detectors and notification devices. The Department of Buildings' Bulletin 2024-001, issued in February 2024, says when work on such a system is a repair. Repairing or replacing in kind a smoke detector, a waterflow switch, a pull station, a horn or strobe, or even a control panel does not need new plans filed with and approved by the FDNY, provided a licensed electrician does the work under the Electrical Code, the system's functionality does not change, and the replacement matches the existing arrangement.

This path is where most service work in co-op and tower lobbies lives, and it is often cheaper and faster than owners fear. The limit is real, though. If the panel is being moved, if the technology is changing, if a floor is being altered, or if a space's occupancy is changing to one that requires a fire alarm, the bulletin says new plans go to the FDNY. And if the system does not work and cannot be repaired, or listed replacement parts are no longer available, the whole system has to be replaced.

Path three: a filed system, from design to inspection

When a building needs a new or altered fire alarm system, the FDNY's own guide sets out the order. A New York State registered design professional, an engineer or architect, decides the occupancy and system type under the Building Code, prepares the plans and files them with the TM-1 application. Since June 2, 2019, new fire alarm applications go straight to the FDNY without a separate filing step at the Department of Buildings. Initial review can take up to eight weeks, and the guide says plan submission to final inspection has averaged about 20 to 24 weeks. Additions to an existing system need that system's letter of approval.

After approval, the guide tells owners to use only FDNY-approved installation, inspection and testing companies, to have the central station connection active before scheduling the inspection, and to have at least one technician with a Certificate of Fitness S97 or S98 on site for the test, which is held under NFPA 72 as modified by Appendix Q of the Building Code. The Fire Alarm Inspection Unit is at 9 MetroTech Center in Brooklyn. A filed job therefore involves several licensed parties. We are one of them on many jobs, and we tell owners at the first meeting exactly which work is ours and which belongs to the design professional, the electrical contractor and the fire alarm company that holds the FDNY approvals.

Which Brighton Beach buildings tend to land on which path

A house on one of the lanes, owner-occupied or rented, is on path one: household smoke, CO and, where rented, gas detection. A prewar six-story elevator building without a hall system is on path one for every apartment, and the owner's choice about adding hall detection may push part of the job onto path two or three. A co-op or tower with an existing panel spends most of its life on path two, with path three arriving when the panel is obsolete. The Oceana and other engineered condo buildings are on path two for service and path three for any alteration. Halls, theaters, cabaret-style restaurants and large cellar stores can land on path three as soon as their use or size crosses one of the code's thresholds. NY Engineers' summary of the 2022 code lists some of those for new work and triggered alterations: an apartment building with units three or more stories above the lowest exit or with at least 16 units, an assembly occupancy of 300 or more, and a store with a below-grade fire area over 1,500 square feet.

Your Questions Answered

Fire Alarm Installation in Brighton Beach: Questions Answered

These are the situations Brighton Beach owners, boards, shopkeepers and families bring to us. Each one is answered here in full and is not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Batteries and missing detectors

News reports said a smoke detector had been taken down in the Brighton Beach apartment where a man died in the 2024 battery fire. As an owner, can I find out when a tenant removes one?

With an ordinary sealed-battery alarm, no. It is a stand-alone device, and once it is off the ceiling it tells nobody anything. HPD's rules put the duty to replace a device that is stolen, removed or disabled during an occupancy on the tenant, and the duty to replace it before a new tenant moves in on the owner, but neither duty helps on the night it is missing. There are two practical ways to close that gap. The first is detection the household has no reason to remove: devices placed away from the kitchen and the bathroom door, photoelectric types that are less prone to cooking nuisance alarms, and a short conversation with the tenant about why it matters. The second, for owners who want to know, is supervised detection: wireless or wired devices connected to a control unit or a monitoring service that reports when a device goes offline, is tampered with or reaches its end of life. That costs more than a sealed alarm, and in a multiple dwelling it has to be planned so it does not turn into a building system nobody filed. We price both and let the owner choose.

How much warning does a smoke alarm really give when a lithium-ion battery fails?

Less than most people expect, which is why placement and habits matter as much as the device. In the October 2024 fire, the occupants were awake enough to hear the battery fail, tried to fight the fire and burned their hands; the FDNY's Chief Fire Marshal said afterward, "You will not be able to put these things out on your own." In the September 2024 sixth-floor fire, the battalion chief said the fire was small but the smoke was severe and toxic. A smoke alarm reacts to smoke reaching its chamber, so the alarm in the hall outside a bedroom sounds after the smoke gets there, and the alarm inside a closed bedroom sounds sooner for the person in that room. That is why we put a device in every room where someone sleeps and one in the hall outside, and link them where the product and the apartment allow. No detector stops a battery fire. What a detector does is give the people in the apartment the seconds they need to get out, close the door and call 911, and the charging spot decides whether the way out is still open.

Stores, halls and the boardwalk

Our shop on Brighton Beach Avenue uses the whole cellar for stock. Can the size of the cellar alone mean we need a fire alarm system?

It can, for new work and for alterations that bring the space under the current code. NY Engineers' summary of the 2022 New York City code lists a mercantile fire area below grade of more than 1,500 square feet as one of the conditions that requires a fire alarm in a store, alongside a single fire area over 12,000 square feet, a fire area more than three stories above grade and a combined area over 24,000 square feet. A Brighton Beach Avenue cellar running the full depth of a lot can pass 1,500 square feet. Whether your store is required to have a system today depends on when the space was approved, what has changed since, and what its certificate of occupancy says, and that is a question for a registered design professional. What we do first is measure the cellar, note what is stored there and how it connects to the floor above and the residents' stair, and tell you which path you are likely on. Even where no system is required, a cellar full of cartons under apartments deserves detection that someone will hear.

Our restaurant has a stage, a dance floor and a show most weekends, but fewer than 300 guests. Can the entertainment alone mean the room needs a fire alarm?

Possibly. The Department of Buildings' code notes on places of assembly say a fire alarm system with an emergency power source is triggered at an occupant load of 300 or more or where the space is used as a cabaret. So the number of guests is not the only test; the use is a test too. A dining room that becomes a room with live entertainment and dancing can be seen differently by the code than a restaurant of the same size that only serves dinner. How your space is classified, and whether its approvals already account for the shows, is something the owner's registered design professional confirms against the certificate of occupancy and the Place of Assembly Certificate of Operation. If the answer is that a system is required, it is a filed job, and on the installation side we coordinate the alarm with the sound and lighting so the music stops and the room lights come up when the alarm sounds.

Every winter we close in our sidewalk tables with walls and heaters. Does that enclosure change anything for the fire alarm?

It can. The DOB's code notes say an enclosed sidewalk café is treated as part of the assembly space. Enclosing the tables can therefore add to the occupant load of the restaurant and to the area the restaurant's protection has to cover, and if the restaurant is close to one of the code's thresholds, it can be the change that matters. The heaters add a second issue: where they are fueled by gas or propane, they are fossil-fuel-burning devices inside an enclosed space full of people, and carbon monoxide detection deserves a look even where nothing else changes. Before the walls go up each season, we suggest the owner confirm with the design professional or the expediter who handled the café approvals that the enclosed area is covered, and we can survey what detection the enclosure needs and how it connects to the rest of the restaurant.

We run a theater that seats more than a thousand people. While a show is on, who has to be watching the fire alarm?

That is set by the FDNY, not by an installer. The FDNY's public assembly permit page lists the Certificate of Fitness for supervision of fire alarm systems and related systems, the S-95, among the requirements a place of assembly deals with, and the permit itself scales with the number of occupants. A theater at the Master Theater's listed capacity of 1,326 sits well above the 300-person threshold at which the code requires a fire alarm system with emergency power. Who must be on duty, with which certificate, during which hours, is a matter for the operator and the FDNY, and we do not advise on staffing. What we do on the installation side is make the system easy for the person on duty to read: labels that match the device list, zone descriptions that use the names your staff actually use for each part of the building, and a written explanation of each signal at the panel.

Our restaurant on the boardwalk wants its new system working before the summer crowds arrive. When should we start?

Apartments, co-ops and addresses

Plan it with the board's access procedure rather than around it. FAQ

Brighton Beach Fire Alarm Installation FAQ

What should we do in the first minute if an e-bike or scooter battery starts hissing, popping or smoking?
+

Get everyone out, close the door to that room behind you, and call 911 once you are outside. Do not try to put it out. After the October 2024 Brighton Beach fire, the FDNY's Chief Fire Marshal said, "You will not be able to put these things out on your own." Two adults in that apartment burned their hands trying.

Lithium-ion fire deaths have dropped across the city. Is it still worth upgrading the detectors in our building?
+

Yes. The FDNY counted 18 lithium-ion battery fire deaths in 2023 and six in 2024, and reported one in 2025 as of its year-end summary, but it also counted 277 lithium-ion fires in 2024, more than in 2023. One of the 2024 deaths was in Brighton Beach. Fewer deaths means the warnings are working, not that the fires have stopped.

Do you service kitchen hood and fire suppression systems in Brighton Beach restaurants?
+

No. Kitchen hood and fire suppression systems are a separate trade with their own filings, and we do not service them. We handle the restaurant's fire alarm side: smoke, heat and carbon monoxide detection in the dining room, back of house and cellar, and coordination with the suppression contractor where the two systems connect.

What is a TM-1, and who signs it for our building?
+

The TM-1 is the FDNY's application for plan examination, filed with the fire alarm plans. The FDNY's guide says a New York State registered design professional, a licensed engineer or architect, prepares the plans and completes the filings, including the TM-1. An installer does not sign it.

We want to add devices to our building's old system. Why does everyone keep asking for its letter of approval?
+

Because the FDNY asks for it. The FDNY's guide to fire alarm approval says that for additions or changes to an existing system, you must submit the letter of approval for the existing fire alarm system. If the board or managing agent cannot find it, that search comes before the design, and older jobs filed before June 2019 may be in the Department of Buildings' records.

The floor numbers in our building's elevator do not match the floors on our certificate of occupancy. Can that hold up an FDNY filing?
+

It can. Among its tips for avoiding defects, the FDNY guide says to make sure the floor numbers, the TM-1 and the certificate of occupancy or Schedule A match. Buildings that skip a floor number, call the ground floor the lobby or have a cellar and a basement should sort out the naming with the design professional before plans are drawn.

Where does the FDNY inspection of a new fire alarm in a Brooklyn building take place, and who has to be there?
+

The test happens at the building. The FDNY guide says the approved plans are brought to the Fire Alarm Inspection Unit at 9 MetroTech Center in Brooklyn on Tuesdays or Thursdays from 9 a.m. to noon, and that at least one certified fire alarm technician with a Certificate of Fitness S97 or S98 must be on site for the test, with enough staff to run it.

News reports called the Brighton 8th Street fire a two-alarm fire. Does that have anything to do with the building's own fire alarm?
+

No. A two-alarm fire describes the size of the FDNY's response, not anything inside the building. News 12 reported that the December 2025 fire on Brighton 8th Street drew 25 units and 106 firefighters and EMS workers. Whether a building had a fire alarm system, and whether it sounded, is a separate question the reports did not answer.

Do you need a deposit before ordering a panel or detectors for our building?
+

Yes. Work is booked with a 50 percent deposit under our service agreement, and equipment is ordered only once the deposit is in. Special-order panels and devices can take longer to arrive than stock items, so we tell you whether anything is special order before you pay, and we give you the delivery date once it is ordered.

Our hallway has a combination smoke and carbon monoxide alarm. When it goes off, how do we know which danger it is warning about?
+

Listen to the pattern and read the device. Combination alarms use one pattern for smoke, usually three beeps and a pause, and another for carbon monoxide, usually four beeps and a pause, and many also speak or light a label. For smoke, get out and close the door. For carbon monoxide, get everyone into fresh air and call 911. We show each household its own device's signals when we install it.
