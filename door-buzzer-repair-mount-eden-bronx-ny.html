The Neighborhood

Why Mount Eden Properties Need Door Buzzer Repair

Mount Eden takes its name from Rachel Eden, who in 1820 bought the sloping eastern part of the Anderson farm, according to the historical sign NYC Parks keeps on the Mount Eden Malls, the planted traffic islands that run along Mount Eden Parkway from Walton to Weeks Avenues. Sections of that parkway were once called Wolf Street, Jane Street and Walnut Street. The wider district’s name comes from the same hills: Tremont, to the north, was coined by a nineteenth-century postmaster from three “mounts” — Mount Eden, Mount Hope and Fairmount. The ground still drops from the Grand Concourse ridge down to Jerome Avenue; Wikipedia calls the district very hilly, with stair streets joining blocks that sit at different heights.

The neighborhood that stands today went up fast. The Jerome Avenue elevated line opened its Mount Eden Avenue station on June 2, 1917, and the Concourse subway followed under the boulevard. City tax records date 182 of the study area’s 270 residential lots, holding 6,903 of its 10,491 homes, to between 1901 and 1929, and another 27 lots with 1,367 homes to 1930–1945. Nearly all of them are brick apartment houses of five or six stories: 69 of the buildings with twenty or more homes have five floors and 69 have six. Of the 173 lots with eight or more homes, 153 were built before 1968, the date that decides which buildings the Multiple Dwelling Law requires to have an intercom without being asked.

The second wave came out of the ruins of the 1970s and 1980s. New Settlement Apartments opened in 1990 after Settlement Housing Fund renovated and acquired 14 abandoned buildings in Mount Eden; the fund now counts 1,140 homes in its New Settlement portfolio, including 1561 Walton Avenue, which it built new. Seventeen lots in our study area are still recorded under New Settlement owner names, most with a 1990 alteration year, and 36 carry the name of a housing development fund company. The third wave is the newest: after the City Council approved the Jerome Avenue Neighborhood Plan in 2018, new apartment buildings rose along an avenue long lined with auto shops under the elevated tracks, among them 1325 Jerome Avenue (2019) and 1351 Jerome Avenue, which the Bronx Times described in January 2025 as an 11-story building with 131 homes.

All three waves now share one problem. The intercom in a 1920s building is wired through the building, one handset per apartment, and when a single handset dies the city writes that apartment, not the building. That is how a neighborhood of about ten thousand homes produced 358 intercom violations in under six years, and why so many of them are still open: each one needs its own repair, its own test and its own line on a certification form. Where an owner also wants cameras watching the lobby door, that work is covered on our Mount Eden security camera page.

Eight census tracts

Our study area is 2020 tracts 209, 219, 221.01, 221.02, 223, 227.02, 227.03 and 229.02, part of the city’s Mount Eden–Claremont (West) area: roughly Jerome Avenue and Macombs Road to the Grand Concourse, East 167th Street to the Cross Bronx Expressway, plus the blocks east of the Concourse along Monroe, Topping and Eastburn Avenues north of Mount Eden Parkway.

10,491 homes on 270 lots

City tax records list 457 lots in the eight tracts, 270 with homes. ZIP code 10452 covers 151 of the residential lots and 10457 the other 119. Ninety-six percent of the homes are in buildings with twenty or more of them.

6,052 homes in elevator buildings

From Complaint to Closed

Fixed Is Not the Same as Closed: The Five Stages of a Mount Eden Buzzer Repair

Most pages about buzzer repair stop when the handset rings again. In Mount Eden that is the middle of the story. A tenant’s complaint, an inspector’s visit, a notice of violation, the repair, the owner’s certification and HPD’s decision to close or keep the violation all leave a trace in public records, and those records show where buzzer repairs here go wrong. Very often it is not at the panel. It is at the moment someone has to prove, apartment by apartment, that the repair was made. We read the two city datasets for the eight study tracts and followed every buzzer complaint and every intercom violation from start to finish. What follows is what they show, stage by stage, and how we shape a repair so that it holds up at each one.

Stage 1 — the complaint: three in ten end with a violation

Between January 2020 and October 20, 2026, tenants in the study area filed 471 bell, buzzer and intercom complaints with 311, from 97 addresses. HPD’s closing notes put each one into a small number of outcomes. In 135 the inspector found the condition and wrote a violation. In 181 the inspector came, tested, and found nothing that broke the housing code that day. In 122 the inspector never got to test at all: 57 closed with the note that HPD was not able to gain access, 38 with a card left at the door and a letter sent, and 27 with HPD asking the tenant to submit a new request because the inspection could not be completed. Fifteen more closed because the tenant, reached by phone, said the condition had been corrected; ten found a violation already on file; six are still open and two were folded into an earlier building-wide complaint.

The median complaint closed seventeen days after it was filed. Put plainly, a buzzer complaint in Mount Eden is about as likely to end with no record of the fault as with one. A handset that fails at night and works at noon, or an apartment nobody is home to open, leaves nothing for an owner to fix and nothing for a tenant to point to. That matters to us because a repair that starts from a written violation knows exactly which apartment and which part the inspector found broken; one that starts from a closed complaint starts from a tenant’s description, and the first job on site is to reproduce the fault.

Stage 2 — the notice: two dates, and they arrive fast

When an inspector does write a violation, the notice follows quickly: for the 358 intercom violations written in the study area since January 2021, the median notice was issued two days after the inspection. HPD mails it to the managing agent named in the building’s registration, or to the owner if no agent is registered, so a building that changed agents without updating its registration can miss it entirely. Each notice carries a date by which the condition must be corrected and a later date by which the owner must certify the correction to HPD.

In the records the gaps are regular. For a class B violation, the hazardous class into which 259 of the 358 fall, the correct-by date sits 35 days after the notice and the certify-by date 14 days after that. For the 93 class A violations the correction window is 95 days, again with 14 more to certify. The six class C intercom violations, written where the inspector judged the condition immediately hazardous, allowed six days to correct and seven to certify. Three hundred and forty-two of the 358 were written against a particular apartment rather than the building as a whole, so a notice that lists several apartments is really several repairs, each with the same two dates.

Stage 3 — the repair: one apartment at a time

An intercom violation in a Mount Eden apartment usually names a handset in the foyer or kitchen, the bell and the return buzzer, and the path from the street or vestibule door to that apartment. Across all 803 intercom violations ever written in the study area, the word “bell” appears in 333, “foyer” in 250, “return” in 214, “vestibule” in 63, “kitchen” in 57 and “street entrance” in 34. What the wording asks for is a working conversation and a working door: the visitor presses the apartment’s button, the apartment rings and can speak, and the button upstairs releases the door the visitor is standing at.

So our repair in a cited apartment is not finished when a new handset is screwed to the wall. The technician rings the apartment from the panel, holds a conversation in both directions, presses the door button and watches the strike release, and then does the same from the inner door if the building has one. Where the fault turns out to be shared, a riser pair or a supply that several cited apartments hang from, it is fixed once and each apartment is still tested on its own, because each has its own violation number.

Stage 4 — the certification: the step that is most often skipped

Certifying a correction is free, and HPD lets owners do it on paper or online through eCertification. The paper form for violations that cannot all be certified at once asks for the notice ID, each violation number and information about who completed the repair, and it must be signed and notarized. HPD says it may audit any certification, that tenants are told when one is received and may challenge it, which triggers an audit inspection, and that a violation found not corrected after certification is placed in false-certification status, stays open and exposes the owner to civil penalties and possible placement on its Certification Watchlist.

In the study area 195 of the 358 recent violations have a certification date, and 186 of those were filed on or before the certify-by date. That leaves 163 that were never certified at all. Of those, 55 were later marked closed and 17 dismissed, typically months later; 66 still sat in “NOV sent out” status on October 22, 2026, which means nothing had been filed since the notice was mailed. Our repair sheet is built for this step: it lists, for each apartment we repaired, the violation number if the owner gives it to us, the part we replaced or the fault we cleared, how we tested it, the date and the technician, under license #12000287431. The owner still signs and certifies; the sheet is what the owner’s certification rests on.

Stage 5 — the outcome: dismissed, closed or still open

Of the 358 violations written since January 2021, 145 had been dismissed by October 22, 2026, 128 of them after the owner certified. In those 128 the dismissal followed the certification by a median of 72 days, almost all of them between two and three months later. Ninety-three more were marked closed, 38 with a certification and 55 without, and the closed ones took much longer: a median of 236 days from inspection, against 111 for the dismissed ones.

The 117 still open tell the rest. Sixty-six have no certification at all. Twenty-six are marked “not complied with.” Sixteen show that an inspector came back to check and could not get into the apartment. Three certifications were ruled invalid and one was found false. Two certified late and two certified on time are waiting. Ninety-five of the 117 are past their certify-by date, 69 are more than a year old and 36 more than two. HPD’s page on clearing violations says a violation that was not certified on time, or was falsely certified, may need a dismissal request inspection, applied for by the owner with a fee, and that new violations can be written during that inspection. From July 1, 2026, HPD sends those applications to its Central Violations Administration Unit at 345 Adams Street in Brooklyn.

None of this means owners in Mount Eden ignore their buzzers; many of the 66 uncertified violations may well have been repaired. It means the repair and the record went separate ways. Our job is to keep them together: fix the apartment, test it in a way an inspector would recognize, and hand over a note the owner can certify from. Where a tenant association or a group such as Community Action for Safe Apartments, the tenant organizing project of New Settlement, is following a building’s repairs, the same note shows tenants what was done in each apartment.

The Records

What Mount Eden’s HPD and 311 Records Show About Its Buzzers

HPD has written intercom violations in the study area since at least 1984, 803 in all, at 125 different buildings. For most of that time the pace was modest: between 2012 and 2019 the yearly count moved between 23 and 48. After a dip to 13 in 2020, it climbed to 56 in 2022, 65 in 2023, 69 in 2024 and 73 in 2025, and inspectors had written 63 more by October 14, 2026. Recent years also carry most of the open ones: 43 of 2026’s 63 and 31 of 2025’s 73 were still open on October 22.

Measured against the housing stock, the recent rate is high. The 358 violations since January 2021 come to 3.4 for every hundred homes in the study area. For the Bronx as a whole, HPD’s file holds 10,895 intercom violations over the same period against about 591,000 homes in city tax records, roughly 1.8 per hundred. The violations are spread widely rather than concentrated: 90 of the study area’s 173 buildings with eight or more homes have at least one since 2021; 28 of those buildings have exactly one and 22 have two, while 16 buildings account for six or more each.

The floor numbers in the records are worth a look too. In six-story buildings, where most of the neighborhood lives, 237 apartment-level violations since 2021 give a floor. The sixth floor has 62 of them, the most of any floor and more than a sixth of the total would suggest; the second has 50, the fifth 37, the first 36, the third 26 and the fourth 22. A top-floor apartment is at the far end of the riser from the panel and the supply in the cellar, so it is the first to notice a weak supply, a corroded splice or a junction box someone opened on the floor below. When a sixth-floor apartment is cited, we check the riser at the floors below it before condemning the handset.

Two smaller sets of records point at the street door rather than the apartments. Eight violations since 2005 were written not because the intercom was broken but because visitors could not reach it: a locked gate in front of the panel, a gate installed at the lot line of a front stoop, an iron entrance gate, a locked door between the street and the vestibule, a panel removed from the front of the building, and, in the inspector’s words, an “intercom unreachable from outside.” And the door itself is watched far more closely than the intercom: since January 2021 HPD has written 2,670 self-closing-door violations in the study area, all but three of them class C, at about 160 buildings. A street door that does not close and latch on its own is a separate violation with a 14-day correction period, so every buzzer repair we make here ends with the door closing itself onto a strike that holds.

Complaints to 311 follow the same curve as the violations: 23 in 2020, 38 in 2021, 64 in 2022, 90 in 2023, 103 in 2024, 95 in 2025 and 58 so far in 2026. They are also concentrated: five addresses filed 127 of the 471. The city does not record the reason behind each complaint, and we do not name buildings here; the point is that a few buildings call often, and in those buildings the gap between a repair and a closed record is usually the whole problem.

Split by kind of building, the recent record is heaviest in the elevator buildings: 4.1 violations for every hundred homes since 2021, against 2.9 in the walk-ups and 1.2 in buildings put up since 2000. Most of the open violations are recent, which fits the notice clock: a violation written this year has not yet reached the point where it would normally be dismissed.

By street, the Grand Concourse leads simply because it holds the most homes: 52 violations since 2021 across 1,782 homes on its study-area lots. Townsend Avenue has 46 across 1,204, and Walton Avenue 29 across 1,207. Monroe Avenue’s 36 come almost entirely from a single building, which is why we read the record building by building before drawing any conclusion about a block.

What We Repair

Door Buzzer Repair Services in Mount Eden

Every repair below is priced from the Bronx list and starts with the $250 service call. What makes them Mount Eden repairs is the order in which we do them: the door first, then each apartment the record names, then whatever those apartments share.

Cited-apartment repairs

A handset, button or station replaced or rewired in each apartment a notice lists, then tested from the panel: it rings, both sides can talk, and its button releases the door. Each one gets its own line on the repair sheet.

Riser pairs and junctions

When several cited apartments sit one above another, the fault is often in the riser or a junction box on a floor between them. We find the break, splice or re-terminate it, and label the box with the apartments it serves.

Street and vestibule door releases

Electric strikes, keepers and the latch that has to meet them, on the outer door and on an inner lobby door where there is one. The door has to close and lock by itself before we leave.

Lobby and street panels

Worn buttons, a cracked faceplate, a dead speaker, a missing directory, or a panel a visitor can no longer reach because of a gate or a locked outer door.

Supplies, transformers and amplifiers

The cellar end of the system: measured with several apartments in use, replaced if it sags, and moved out of a damp or hot spot if the location is what is killing it.

Networked and cellular panels

In buildings opened since 2015 the panel often calls phones. Repairs there are about power, network, the account and the door hardware, and we check all four.

Store, church and garage doors

A single door with its own button, on Jerome Avenue, East 170th Street or a side street: the same parts on a smaller scale, booked by whoever runs the door.

Records for certification

For any repair made because of a violation, a sheet listing apartment, violation number, the part or fault, the test and the date, so the owner can certify from it.

Building Specialists

Door Buzzer Repair for Mount Eden’s Architecture

Mount Eden’s housing is unusually uniform in height and unusually varied in what has happened inside it. Six building types cover almost all of it, and each has its own way of failing and its own way of showing up in HPD’s records.

1920s five-story walk-ups

The 134 walk-up lots in the study area hold 3,943 homes, many on Elliot Place, Marcy Place, East Clarke Place and the East 168th and 169th Street blocks. One stair, one riser route, a handset in each foyer or kitchen, and a supply in the cellar.

Six-story elevator buildings

Eighty-seven elevator lots hold 6,052 homes, from 1920s buildings on Walton and Townsend Avenues and Wythe Place to late-1930s and 1940s buildings on the Grand Concourse and Townsend. Longer runs, more apartments per riser and, in the records, the most top-floor violations.

Buildings with stores below

Forty-nine buildings with 2,591 homes have shops at street level, most along the Grand Concourse and the side streets near Jerome Avenue. The residents’ door sits between storefronts, and shop work can disturb the wiring behind it.

Rebuilt in the 1990 wave

Seventeen lots with 1,040 homes are still recorded under New Settlement owner names, most with a 1990 alteration year, and Settlement Housing Fund says it has since upgraded the systems and apartments of its original buildings. Other lots carry housing development fund company names. Systems here vary from building to building.

Built since 2015

Thirteen lots with 1,282 homes date from 2015 or later, on Jerome and Inwood Avenues, East Clarke Place, East 172nd Street, Monroe, Weeks, Topping and Walton Avenues, among other streets. Networked panels, fobs and phone apps are common.

Small houses and two-to-four-family buildings

Ninety-seven lots hold one to seven homes, 70 of them in the tract east of the Grand Concourse, on Topping, Monroe, Clay and Weeks Avenues and East Mount Eden Avenue. Bells, chimes and a button plate at the door rather than a building intercom.

Method by Building

How We Repair Each Kind of Mount Eden Building

The order of work changes with the building. What does not change is the end of the job: every cited apartment tested from the panel, every door closing and latching on its own, and a written record of both.

Walk-ups on the side streets

In a five-story walk-up on Elliot Place or Marcy Place the riser usually climbs the stair hall, so it can be reached from the halls rather than from inside apartments. We start at the street door and the vestibule door, then go to the cellar supply, then to the first cited apartment and work up. In these buildings a violation in a second-floor kitchen and one in a fourth-floor foyer can share a junction box on the third-floor landing, and that box is opened, cleaned and re-made before any handset is condemned.

Elevator buildings on Walton, Townsend and the Concourse

Six-story elevator buildings put more apartments on each riser and the supply further from the top floor. We measure the supply with several apartments talking at once, then test the top-floor cited apartments before the lower ones, because a weak supply or a corroded splice shows there first. Where the building has an inner lobby door as well as the street door, both releases are tested from every cited apartment, since the violation wording often runs “from street entrance door” all the way to the apartment.

Buildings with shops at street level

When the residents’ door sits between two storefronts, the cable from the panel to the riser often runs through a shop ceiling or cellar. Before opening anything we ask who controls those spaces and when they are open. A renovation in a shop below is one of the few things that can knock out a whole building’s panel overnight, and the record of what we find helps an owner decide who pays.

Rebuilt and nonprofit-owned buildings

Buildings rebuilt around 1990 and since upgraded can hold a mix of original and newer parts on one riser. We record which handset model is in each cited apartment and which supply and panel the building runs, so that a replacement matches its neighbors and the owner’s asset manager can see what is left of the older system.

Newer buildings near the Mount Eden Avenue station

For a networked or cellular panel we check power at the panel and the strike, the network link or signal, the service account and who holds its administrator login, and the door hardware, in that order. Many of these calls end without a part: an expired plan, a changed router or a resident list that was never updated. When a part is needed, it is quoted like any other.

Houses and small buildings

In the one-to-four-family houses east of the Concourse the system is often a transformer, a few buttons and a bell or chime in each home. We trace which button rings which home, replace the failed part and, where an owner rents out a floor, leave a note of which circuit is whose.

Door Buzzer Repair in Mount Eden: Questions Answered

These are the questions the records raise: what happens to a complaint, who receives a notice, what certifying involves and how an old violation comes off a building. Answers about HPD’s process quote HPD’s own pages and closing notes; answers about the repair are ours.

Complaints and inspections

The 311 app says HPD could not complete the inspection and asks me to file a new request. Did the first complaint count for anything?

It stays on the city’s record as a complaint, but it closed without an inspection, so no violation came from it and the owner received nothing to correct. In the study area 27 buzzer complaints since 2020 closed with exactly that note, asking the tenant to submit a new service request. If you file again, give times when someone will be home to let the inspector in and test the handset in front of them.

Meanwhile, tell the super or managing agent in writing which apartment is silent and what happens when a visitor presses your button. A repair does not need to wait for HPD, and a written note gives the owner, and us, the starting point the inspection would have given.

Five apartments on our line have dead handsets. Should every household file its own 311 complaint, or does one complaint cover the line?

HPD writes intercom violations apartment by apartment: 342 of the 358 written in the study area since 2021 name a particular apartment. A complaint from one household usually brings an inspection of that household’s handset. HPD does fold some complaints into an earlier building-wide one, and two study-area buzzer complaints closed that way, but a dead line usually shows up as separate apartments.

For the repair it matters less. If five apartments above one another are silent, we treat it as a likely riser or junction fault, fix it once and test each apartment separately, so whichever of them carry violations each get their own line on the repair sheet.

The owner fenced in our front stoop with a locking gate to keep people from sitting there, and HPD wrote the intercom up as unreachable. Can the gate stay?

Often it can, if the panel moves to the visitor’s side of the gate or the gate is put on the intercom. Since 2005 HPD has written eight violations in the study area for exactly this kind of thing: a locked gate in front of the panel, a gate added at the lot line of a stoop, an iron entrance gate, a locked door between the street and the vestibule. Each was written as a nuisance under Administrative Code § 27-2005 and Multiple Dwelling Law § 309 rather than as a broken intercom.

The fix depends on the layout. A panel can be mounted on the gate post or the fence beside it, with its cable run back under the stoop; or the gate can get its own strike released from the same apartment buttons as the door. We price both on site, and either way a visitor has to be able to ring an apartment from the sidewalk.

Notices, dates and who receives them

We took over managing a building on Walton Avenue, and the intercom notices went to the previous agent. How do we find what is open and make sure the next notices reach us?

HPD mails notices of violation to the managing agent named in the building’s registration, or to the owner if none is registered, and its page on clearing violations says the fix for missing notices is to submit a new property registration. Open violations, with their apartments and dates, can be looked up by address on HPDONLINE.

Send us that list and we will tell you which apartments can be visited together, which share a riser, and which repairs are past their certify-by date. In the study area, 95 of the 117 open intercom violations were already past that date on October 22, 2026.

One notice lists intercom violations in nine apartments, and only six can be fixed this week. Can the owner certify those six now?

Yes. HPD’s paper form for this situation is meant for exactly that: it is used when all the violations on a notice cannot be certified at one time, and it asks for the notice ID, each violation number and information about who did the repair, signed and notarized. Owners can also certify online through eCertification.

We schedule the repair the same way: the six reachable apartments first, each tested and written up with its violation number, and the other three booked as soon as their tenants can let us in. The sheet for each visit stands on its own.

Did the penalty changes HPD made in December 2023 make an open intercom violation more expensive to leave?

HPD’s page on penalties and fees says that new legislation effective December 8, 2023 raised civil penalties for violations issued after that date; the correction periods it lists are 90 days for class A, 30 for class B and immediate correction for class C. It also notes inspection fees for repeated violations and separate penalties for false certification.

We cannot say what a particular building would be charged; that depends on HPD and, where penalties are sought, Housing Court. What we can say is that most of the study area’s intercom violations since 2021 were issued after that date, and that a $250 visit that ends with a certifiable repair is usually the cheaper path.

Certifying, audits and old violations

HPD’s record shows our intercom certification as “invalid,” although the repair really was done. What usually goes wrong?

HPD’s public record does not say why, so the owner or agent should ask at the Bronx Borough Service Center. The paper form gives the usual places to look: a missing notice ID or violation number, information about who did the repair left blank, or a signature that was not notarized. Three intercom certifications in the study area are currently marked invalid.

If the repair was ours, our sheet already carries the apartment, the date, the work and the license number. If it was someone else’s, we can test the apartment and write up what we find, so the owner certifies from a fresh test rather than from memory.

A tenant challenged our certification, and HPD is sending an inspector to audit it. How should the apartment and the door be ready?

HPD says a tenant challenge triggers an audit inspection, and a violation found uncorrected goes into false-certification status, stays open and brings penalties. So the useful preparation is to make sure the repair really holds: test the handset from the panel, hold a conversation in both directions and release the street door and any inner door from the apartment’s button.

If the owner books us before the audit, we do that test with the tenant present, fix anything that has slipped, and note the result. It is also worth confirming the tenant will be home; sixteen open violations in the study area show that a reinspection could not get in.

I received HPD’s notice that my landlord certified the intercom repair, but my button still cannot open the street door. How do I dispute it?

HPD says tenants are told when a certification is received and may challenge it, which triggers an audit inspection. Follow the instructions on the notice, and say exactly what fails: the apartment rings but the door will not open, or the panel cannot reach you at all.

A release that fails while the ring works usually points to the door button in your handset, the relay or the strike at the door, not the whole system, which is why an inspector, or a technician, needs to test from your apartment rather than only at the panel.

An intercom violation from 2017 is still open on our building, though the whole system has been replaced since. How does it come off the record?

A late certification alone usually will not clear it. HPD’s page says violations not certified on time, and falsely certified ones, may need a dismissal request inspection, which the owner applies for and pays a fee for, and that new violations can be written during that inspection. Since July 1, 2026, those applications go to HPD’s Central Violations Administration Unit at 345 Adams Street, Brooklyn.

Because the inspector may look at more than the old item, owners usually ask us to test the apartment named in the old violation and the street door before the request goes in, so the visit finds a working system.

Our super replaced the handset in a cited apartment himself. Can you check his repair before the owner certifies it?

Yes. The visit is the $250 service call. We test the apartment the way an inspector would, from the panel, in both directions and through the door release, check that the new handset matches the building’s system, and write up the result.

If it passes, the owner has a dated test to certify from. If it does not, we tell you whether the problem is the new handset or something the handset could not fix, such as the riser or the supply, and price the rest from the Bronx list.

Types of Door Buzzer Equipment We Repair in Mount Eden

Mount Eden’s systems span a century, so the equipment list is long. These are the parts a cited apartment or a stuck door most often comes down to.

Apartment handsets and stations

Wall handsets with a talk button and a door button, in a foyer or kitchen. Replacements are matched to the building’s system and voltage so that one new handset does not upset the rest of the line.

Lobby and street panels

Button plates, directory panels and speaker grilles, flush or surface mounted, at the street door, the vestibule or a gate post.

Electric strikes and keepers

Fail-secure strikes stay locked when power is lost; fail-safe ones unlock. On a street door a fail-secure strike keeps the door locked if the power goes, and with either kind the lever inside still lets people out.

Transformers and power supplies

Bell transformers in small buildings and dedicated intercom supplies in larger ones, usually in the cellar near the electrical room.

Amplifiers and audio modules

The part that carries voices between the panel and the apartments; when it fails, buttons may still ring while nobody can hear.

Riser cable and junction boxes

The multi-pair cable that climbs the building, with boxes on the landings or in closets where it splits to apartments.

Door closers and latches

Not intercom parts, but a release is only as good as the door it opens. A closer that slams or stops short defeats the strike.

Networked and cellular door stations

Panels that call phones over the internet or a cellular plan, common in buildings opened since 2015, with an account and an administrator login behind them.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Door Buzzer Repair in Mount Eden

We could not open Google’s AI Overview or People Also Ask from where this page was built, so instead we tested seven claims that appear in the cost guides, vendor articles and advice pages AI answers draw on, against Mount Eden’s own records.

“If the buzzer is broken, a 311 complaint will get it fixed.”

A complaint starts a process; it does not guarantee a repair. Of the 471 buzzer complaints filed from study-area addresses since 2020, 135 ended with a violation. In 181 the inspector found nothing that broke the code that day, and in 122 the inspector could not get in.

A complaint that ends without a violation leaves the owner with nothing to correct and the tenant with nothing to point to, even when the handset fails again that evening. That is not a reason to stop filing; it is a reason to describe the fault in writing to the owner as well.

When we are called after a complaint like that, the first job is to make the fault happen in front of us. We ring the apartment repeatedly, press its door button with the strike under load and check the riser joints that intermittent faults usually live in.

“Minor intercom repairs typically run $150 to $400.”

Swiftlane’s cost guide gives that range. Our Bronx list starts with a $250 service call that includes the testing, and a single handset or station runs $250 to $525. For one silent apartment, the two ranges overlap.

The figure misleads in Mount Eden for another reason: a notice that names nine apartments is nine repairs, each tested and recorded, unless they share a riser or a supply, in which case one riser repair at $850 to $2,400 can bring several back at once.

What a cost guide cannot price is the record. A cheap repair that leaves no note of which apartment was fixed, how it was tested and when can cost an owner far more later, when the violation is still open and the certify-by date has passed.

“Only buildings built after 1968 with eight or more apartments need an intercom.”

ButterflyMX describes the rule as generally applying to buildings of eight or more units built or converted after January 1, 1968. That is the automatic requirement in Multiple Dwelling Law § 50-a, but the same section requires older multiple dwellings to provide one when a majority of tenants ask, and it lets owners recover the cost.

In Mount Eden only 20 of the 173 buildings with eight or more homes were built in 1968 or later, yet HPD wrote 358 intercom violations here in under six years, almost all in older buildings. Where an intercom exists, the owner’s duty to keep the building in repair under Administrative Code § 27-2005 applies to it.

So the useful question for an older building is not whether the law requires an intercom, but whether the one it has works. In this neighborhood the answer is visible, apartment by apartment, in HPD’s file.

“Once the landlord fixes it, the violation goes away.”

Not on its own. HPD expects the owner to certify the correction by the date on the notice. In the study area, 163 of the 358 recent intercom violations were never certified, and 66 of those still sat at “NOV sent out” on October 22, 2026.

Even a certified violation is not closed at once. The 128 that were dismissed after certification were dismissed a median of 72 days later, and HPD may audit any certification, especially when a tenant challenges it.

That is why our repair sheet exists. It does not replace the owner’s certification, but it gives the owner something true to certify from, and it gives a tenant a clear account of what was done in their apartment.

“Most intercom problems are user error.”

Brick Underground’s 2019 guide quotes a security company saying “a lot of times it’s user error.” Some calls do end that way: a volume turned down, a handset left off the hook, a resident list never updated on a newer panel.

But 342 apartment-level violations written by inspectors since 2021 in this small area are not user error; an inspector found each one broken. And the 181 complaints that ended with no violation include faults that simply were not happening that day.

We do not charge for being told on the phone that a handset is off the hook. On site, we treat a fault as real until a test in front of the tenant shows otherwise.

“An app-based intercom requires no building wiring.”

ButterflyMX says its call box requires no building wiring to the apartments, because it calls residents’ phones. That is true of the handset wiring, and it is why many newer Mount Eden buildings use such panels.

It still needs power at the panel, a network or cellular link, and a strike or lock on the door, wired to the panel. When any of those fails, the door stops working just as surely as when a riser breaks.

Our checks on these systems follow the same order every time: power, link, account and administrator login, then the door hardware. Most of what fails is one of the first three.

Street Door Unlocked or a Certify-By Date Coming Up? Call the Bronx Line

If the street door will not lock, close it and use the key rather than wedging it open. If HPD has written apartments in your building, find the notice or look the building up on HPDONLINE so the dates are in front of you. Then call (646) 490-0629 with the address, the apartments and what the door is doing now. A technician will usually arrive with the parts the description points to, and leave with a record you can certify from.
