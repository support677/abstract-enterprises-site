Why Flatlands Properties Need Alarm Installation

Flatlands is a neighborhood of houses. Avenues J, K and L are mostly residential; Flatbush, Utica and Ralph avenues and Avenue N carry the shops; Kings Plaza sits on the southern edge; and the Flatlands/Fairfield Industrial Business Zone holds auto shops, distributors and yards. That shape matters for an alarm, because the side of a Flatlands house is where the trouble has come from. The burglaries that local papers have described here are almost never stories about a front door being kicked in. Put those together and a pattern appears. The front of the house faces the street, the neighbors and the bus riders on Flatbush Avenue. A person who reaches the alley is out of sight within a few steps, and the windows there are often low enough to reach from the ground, from the top of a garbage can or from patio furniture left in the yard. An alarm kit that comes with two door contacts and one motion detector protects the side of the house the burglar is least likely to use. Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Flatlands’s Architecture

An alarm plan in Flatlands depends mostly on two things: how much of the house is exposed on its own sides, and how many households share it. A detached frame house on a corner can have openings on four sides; an attached brick house in the middle of a row may have openings only at the front and back. A two-family adds a second set of keys, and an apartment in one of the larger buildings changes the job entirely. These six arrangements cover nearly every alarm request we get from the neighborhood.

The detached frame house

Wood-frame houses with siding or shingles on their own lots, usually with a driveway along one side, a yard at the back and a garage either attached or at the rear. Every side can have reachable windows, so the plan covers all four walls, the garage and the basement.

The semi-detached brick pair

Two brick houses sharing a center wall, each with one open side, often a driveway along it, a back yard and a basement entrance. The open side and the rear carry most of the risk, and the siren has to respect the family through the wall.

The attached brick row

Brick houses joined on both sides, with a front porch or stoop, a rear yard and sometimes a shared driveway behind the row leading to garages. The openings are concentrated at the front and back, which keeps the sensor count low.

The two-family with a rental floor

An owner on one floor and a tenant on the other, sometimes with a shared front hall and cellar, sometimes with separate entrances. The alarm has to give each household control of its own space without letting either one disarm the other.

The bungalow with an enclosed porch

Small postwar houses, often one story with an attic or a finished basement, many with a porch enclosed in glass and aluminum at the front. The porch is its own zone, and the low windows are the main concern.

The apartment in a larger building

Units in the postwar apartment buildings near Kings Highway and Nostrand Avenue and in the co-ops and rentals around the neighborhood. The alarm stays inside your own door, wireless and removable, with nothing in the halls.

The detached frame house: four sides, a driveway and a garage

A free-standing frame house is the most common alarm job we do in Flatlands, and it is the one where a store-bought kit falls furthest short. The front door gets a contact, and so does the side door the family actually uses, which is often the one at the top of a few steps off the driveway. The basement door, usually under the back steps or at the bottom of an outside stairwell, gets a contact too, because it leads into the part of the house where nobody is at night. If the garage is attached and has a door into the house, that inside door is the important one; a burglar who gets into the garage has all the time in the world to work on it. A separate garage at the back of the lot can report to the same panel over a wireless sensor if the signal reaches, or over a small repeater mounted in the house if it does not.

The windows are where the real decisions are. We walk the outside of the house and mark every window that a person could reach from the ground, from a porch roof, from the top of a fence, or by standing on something left in the yard. The East 31st and East 32nd Street burglaries are the reason we count the patio furniture: in one of them someone dragged a chair under a driveway window, and in another four chairs were stacked to reach a side window. A window that looks safely high from inside the house is often not high at all once you look at what is sitting below it. Reachable windows on the driveway side and the back get a contact on the sash or a glass-break detector covering the room, depending on whether the family likes to leave them open in summer. Front windows facing the street usually rank lower, unless a porch roof or a hedge gives cover.

Inside, one or two motion detectors cover the rooms a person would cross after climbing in: usually the kitchen at the back and the hall that connects it to the stairs. On frame houses we rarely need more than that, because the perimeter does most of the work. Wiring is a choice, not a requirement. Most detached houses we alarm in Flatlands use wireless contacts on a 2GIG panel, which means no opened walls and no patching, and the panel reports over its own cellular communicator rather than the home’s internet. Where a family is renovating and the walls are open anyway, a hardwired system with a wireless panel at its center is worth considering.

Semi-detached and attached brick houses: the open side and the party wall

Brick houses that share a wall with a neighbor have fewer openings to protect and one extra person to think about. On a semi-detached house, the windows on the open side, the rear windows and the basement door carry almost all of the risk; the shared wall has no openings at all. On an attached row house, the openings are at the front and back, plus any skylight or roof hatch on a top floor. That usually makes a brick house in Flatlands a smaller and cheaper job than a detached frame house of the same size.

The party wall changes how we set the siren. An interior siren inside the house is enough to wake a family and frighten off most intruders, and we put it where it is loud for you and muffled by the shared wall for the neighbor, usually on an interior wall rather than against the party wall itself. An exterior siren is optional and, on attached houses, we set it to a short cutoff. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires an audible burglar alarm on a building to stop on its own within fifteen minutes of going off; we set ours well inside that limit, because a siren that runs for fourteen minutes at three in the morning does not help anyone once the monitoring station has already called you.

Masonry also changes the wireless plan a little. Brick and plaster walls absorb more signal than a frame house, so we place the panel near the middle of the house and test every sensor from where it is mounted before we leave. In a long attached house with a basement at the back, a sensor on the rear cellar door can be the hardest one to reach, and we would rather add a repeater on the day than have a door that drops out of supervision in the winter.

The two-family with a rental floor: two households, one building

Two-family houses are everywhere in Flatlands, and the alarm questions they raise are about people more than sensors. Who arms the shared front hall? Can the tenant come home at midnight through the same door without setting anything off? Should the owner be able to see when the tenant’s door opens? Our answer in most cases is a single panel split into two areas, one for each household, each with its own codes and its own app access, and a shared area for the front hall, the cellar or the laundry that is armed only when both households are armed. Neither family can disarm the other’s floor, and the history shows only your own openings to you.

The rental floor also brings legal obligations that have nothing to do with burglary. HPD’s rules on detectors say that smoke detectors are required in each unit of a multiple dwelling, and that carbon monoxide detectors are required in one- and two-family homes that are not occupied by the owner where there is a fuel-burning appliance or gas piping, placed within 15 feet of the entrance to each sleeping room. Plenty of Flatlands two-families have a gas boiler in the cellar and a gas range on each floor. Monitored smoke and carbon monoxide detectors can go on the same alarm panel as the burglary sensors, so that a detector in the rented unit reports to the owner as well as sounding in the apartment; that is an option, not a requirement, and it never replaces the detectors the law asks you to have.

When a tenant leaves, the work is quick but it has to be done. The tenant’s codes come off the panel, any fob is deleted, the app access is removed, and the monitoring call list is updated so the station is not phoning someone who moved to Queens a year ago. We can do that on the phone for systems we installed, without a visit.

The bungalow and the enclosed porch

After the Second World War, bungalows and semi-detached brick homes filled the last empty lots in Flatlands, and many of them have a front porch closed in with glass and aluminum. A porch like that is a room that is neither inside nor outside. Packages are left in it, coats and shoes are kept in it, and its door is often a light storm door that latches but does not lock well. We treat it as a separate zone: a contact on the porch door that chimes during the day and alerts at night, and the real perimeter at the house door behind it. A glass-break detector inside the house can also hear the porch glass if the two rooms share a large window.

Bungalows sit low, so almost every window is reachable from the ground. On a one-story house we often cover the windows with glass-break detectors, one per room or one per pair of rooms, rather than a contact on every sash, which keeps the cost down and still catches a broken pane. A finished basement with its own outside door becomes a second floor for alarm purposes, with its own motion detector and its own contact.

The apartment in a larger building: everything inside your own door

Renters and co-op owners in the larger buildings get a very different installation. The street door, the lobby and the halls belong to the building, and so does any intercom or reader. Your alarm sits inside your apartment: a contact on your door, a contact or glass-break detector on any window that faces a fire escape, a roof or a low setback, a motion detector in the main room, a small keypad and a panel in a closet. Everything is wireless, mounted with screws that leave small holes or with adhesive, and everything comes with you when you move.

If you are in a co-op, read the proprietary lease and the house rules before the visit, and ask the managing agent whether the board wants to be told; most do not need to approve equipment that stays inside one apartment and touches nothing in the building, but some like to know. We can send a certificate of insurance to the managing agent on request, and we work inside whatever contractor hours the building sets.

Driveways, Alleys and Back Windows

The Way In Nobody Watches: Planning a Flatlands Alarm Around the Side and the Back of the House

If you take one thing from this page, take this: walk around your house before you buy anything. Start at the sidewalk, go down the driveway or the alley, turn the corner into the back yard and come back along the other side if there is one. Count every window you could reach, every door, and every place where you would be out of sight of the street. Then go inside and look at what is under each of those windows on the inside: a kitchen counter, a table with a laptop on it, a sink. That walk is the first half of our survey in Flatlands, and most homeowners find two or three openings on it that they had never thought about.

The local reports are specific enough to plan from. On East 31st Street, a burglar came in through a rear kitchen window between three and five in the morning while the family slept; the homeowner heard a noise and found the window open, and car keys and cash were gone. On East 32nd Street, someone put a chair under a driveway window, opened it overnight and took a laptop and a tablet from the kitchen table. Another kitchen window screen on East 31st was pushed in and nothing was taken. A few days later, on the same block, police believed up to five people broke a kitchen window screen between 5:30 and 9:10 in the morning and took an expensive bike, three laptops and a phone. Every one of those openings was a window. None of them was the front door.

Two details in those reports change how we lay out sensors. First, several of the break-ins happened while people were home, at night or early in the morning. An alarm that is only armed when the house is empty would not have helped. A Flatlands household needs a night mode that keeps the side and back windows and the basement armed while people sleep upstairs, and an early-morning routine that does not force anyone to disarm everything just to make coffee. Second, the thing that actually stopped one burglar was a mechanical one: window stoppers that kept a side window from opening wide enough. Hardware and alarms work together. A stopper or a sash lock slows someone down; a contact on the same window tells you that someone is trying.

The Avenue I burglary adds the other half of the picture. Those burglars came through a window on a weekday afternoon, when the house was empty and the block was quiet, and they had enough time to walk through the rooms. The East 57th Street video shows the pattern in miniature: try the front door, give up, go down the alley, get in at the back, leave by a side window. A good Flatlands alarm is built for both situations, the sleeping household and the empty house, with the same sensors armed in different combinations.

What we put on the side and back of a typical Flatlands house

  • Kitchen windows at the back. A contact on each sash that opens, or a glass-break detector if the family keeps them open in summer. These are first on our list because they appear first in the local reports.
  • Windows along the driveway or alley. A contact on each one that can be reached from the ground or from anything that could be stood on, including garbage cans and patio chairs.
  • The side or rear door. A contact and the entry delay for the door the family really uses, with the keypad just inside it.
  • The basement door. A contact on the outside door and, if the basement is finished, a motion detector at the foot of the stairs.
  • The garage. A contact on the door from the garage into the house, and a separate sensor on the overhead door if the garage holds anything worth stealing.
  • The yard itself, optionally. An outdoor-rated detector or a floodlight camera covering the alley mouth, set to chime or light up rather than set off the siren, so cats and wind do not cause false alarms.

Most families in Flatlands pair this with one or two cameras, usually one on the driveway and one on the back yard, so that an alert from a side window comes with a picture. That is an add-on, never a requirement, and it is listed separately on the quote.

Why a camera system is not enough on its own

Most Flatlands stores that call us already have cameras, and many owners assume the cameras are the security. The Flatbush Avenue burglary near Quentin Road shows the limit of that. The thief forced the back door, pried open the register, took the cash that was in the store and then removed the disc from the surveillance system before leaving. The owner found out at about a quarter past ten the next morning. Whatever the cameras saw, nobody saw it while it was happening, and the recording itself walked out of the door.

An alarm does the one thing a recorder cannot: it tells someone in real time. When the back door opens after hours, the panel sends a signal over its own cellular communicator to the owner’s phone and, if the store is monitored, to the central station within seconds. Nothing about that depends on the recorder, the store’s internet or anything a burglar can unplug once inside, because the signal has left the building before he reaches the office. The station calls the owner and, following the instructions on file, requests police dispatch. Even when nothing is recovered, the owner knows within minutes instead of the next morning, the door is secured that night, and the police report has an exact time.

Cameras still matter, and the two work best together. When the alarm and the cameras are on the same app, an alarm alert comes with the clip from the camera over the back door, and the owner can tell a real break-in from a delivery driver who rattled the gate. We also move the recorder out of sight and lock it up, in a cabinet or a ceiling space, so that the next person who tries what happened near Quentin Road has to find it first.

Compliance notes for Flatlands homes and businesses

  • The siren must stop on its own. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building’s audible burglar alarm to be capable of shutting off, and to shut off automatically, within fifteen minutes of activation. Every system we install in Flatlands has its cutoff set well inside that limit.
  • Chronic alarms are a noise complaint. NYC 311 tells callers to dial 911 for an alarm that is going off right now, and treats alarms that go off “too often or for too long” as a Department of Environmental Protection noise matter, which needs an inspector to observe the condition before a summons. A well-zoned system with a short cutoff keeps your address off that list.
  • The installer must be licensed. New York’s Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers under Article 6-D of the General Business Law, with 81 hours of qualifying education, an exam and fingerprinting. Our license number is #12000287431; ask any installer for theirs.
  • Building fire alarms are a separate system. A burglar alarm can carry monitored smoke, heat and carbon monoxide detectors for a house, an apartment or a shop, but it does not replace a building fire alarm system that a larger building or business may be required to have. Unnecessary activations of a building fire alarm can be costly: in one New York City case reported in 2012, an owner was fined $2,500 after two unnecessary alarms, one of them caused by construction work.
  • Detectors in rented homes. HPD requires smoke detectors in each unit of a multiple dwelling and carbon monoxide detectors in non-owner-occupied one- and two-family homes with fuel-burning appliances or gas piping. An alarm panel can monitor them, but the owner’s legal duty stays the same.
  • Exits stay usable. Nothing on an alarm may lock, slow or block a required exit. We use contacts and sensors on doors, never locking hardware, unless a separate access-control job is designed for it.

Windows, alleys and the side of the house

Someone on East 32nd Street stacked four patio chairs under a side window to reach it. How do we decide which of our upper windows count as reachable?

Stand outside under each window and look at what is around it rather than at how high it is: a porch roof, a fence top, a garage roof, an air-conditioner bracket, a trash can, a grill, a stack of chairs. If a person could stand on any of those and reach the sill, the window counts. On most Flatlands lots that means every ground-floor window on the driveway and yard sides, and often one or two on the second floor over a porch or garage roof. We mark them on the survey and you decide with us which get a contact and which are covered by a glass-break detector in the room.

Window stoppers kept that same burglar out. If our windows already have stoppers and good locks, do they still need sensors?

Stoppers and locks are worth having, and on East 32nd Street they did their job. What they cannot do is tell you anything. A burglar who meets a stopper can break the glass, try the next window or come back with a tool, and the household sleeps through all of it. A contact on the sash, or a glass-break detector in the room, turns the attempt into an alert. We usually leave existing stoppers in place, fit the contact so the window can still open to the stopper for air, and set that zone so a window opened a few inches does not stop you from arming.

The East 57th Street burglars came in at the back and climbed out through a side window. If every door and window has a contact, do we still need motion detectors inside?

Yes, but fewer than a kit suggests. Contacts cover the openings you know about; a motion detector catches someone who got in through an opening you did not cover, such as a broken pane where a glass-break detector was not fitted, and it confirms that a person is actually moving inside. In a Flatlands house we usually put one in the hall or room every intruder has to cross, often the back hall between the kitchen and the stairs, and set it to arm only when the house is empty, so the family can move around at night.

The Avenue I and East 49th Street burglary happened on a Tuesday afternoon. Is it worth arming the house every time we leave for work, even for a few hours?

The afternoon is when a Flatlands block is quietest: adults at work, children at school, neighbors out. Arming on the way out takes one press on a fob or a tap on the phone, and you can set the app to remind you if the house is still disarmed half an hour after the last phone leaves. The point is not to arm for long trips only. Most of the time a house stands empty is ordinary weekdays, and that is when the Avenue I burglars had time to walk through the rooms.

Two-family houses and landlords

We rent out the second floor of our two-family off Avenue L and there is a gas range in each apartment. Do we have to supply a carbon monoxide detector upstairs, and can it report to our alarm?

If you live in the house, HPD’s carbon monoxide requirement for one- and two-family homes applies to homes that are not owner-occupied, so read HPD’s detector page for your exact situation; many owners fit one upstairs anyway because the cost is small and the risk is not. Where a detector is fitted within 15 feet of the bedroom entrance, a monitored model on our panel sounds in the apartment and also sends the alert to you, which matters if your tenant is out and you are downstairs. Smoke detectors in each unit are required for multiple dwellings, and HPD expects tenants to replace detector batteries; a monitored detector also tells you when a battery is low.

Our tenant has a separate entrance from the driveway. Should that door be on our alarm at all?

It can be, as part of the tenant’s own area. A door that belongs to one household should be armed and disarmed by that household only, so we put it in the tenant’s area with the tenant’s code, and the history of that door is visible only to the people the tenant and you agree on in writing. If the tenant does not want an alarm, we leave that door off and protect your own floor and the shared spaces. Either way, tell the tenant what is installed before it goes in; a surprise sensor on a tenant’s door is a quick way to start an argument.

Shops, malls and the industrial zone

The burglar near Quentin Road took the store’s surveillance disc. If our recorder disappears, what proof do we still have?

An alarm keeps its own record away from the store. The panel’s history, the alerts on your phone and the central station’s log all show which door opened and at what minute, and none of that is stored in anything a thief can carry out. If your cameras are on the same app, the clips tied to each alarm alert are stored off the premises too. Together that gives police and your insurer a time line even if the recorder is gone, and we can also put the recorder in a locked box or ceiling space so it is not sitting on an open shelf.

We lease a store inside Kings Plaza. Doesn’t the mall’s own security cover us after closing?

Mall security patrols the common areas, and that is not the same as watching your space. Over the Memorial Day weekend Brooklyn Paper wrote about, two thieves came in through the parking garage after hours, pried open a door into the mall, avoided security and took about $55,000 of jewelry from one kiosk. Your lease and the mall’s tenant rules decide what you can mount and where, so we work from those, but a store inside a mall usually benefits from its own contacts on the shutter or door, motion inside, and a glass-break or shock sensor on display cases, reporting to you directly.

Our Ralph Avenue auto shop leaves the bay door code with three mechanics. How do we stop one person’s code from opening the whole building?

Give each mechanic a personal code that disarms only the areas they need, at the hours they work, and keep the tool room, the parts cage and the office on separate areas with their own codes. If someone leaves the job, you delete their code from the app in a minute without changing anyone else’s. The opening and closing report then shows by name who disarmed the shop each morning and who armed it at night, which is often what an owner most wants after a theft like the one at the tire shop near Preston Court.

Our building in the Flatlands/Fairfield industrial zone is all metal. Will a wireless alarm even work inside it?

Metal walls and roofs can block both cellular signal and the radio signal between sensors and the panel, so we test before we promise. The cellular communicator goes where the signal meter reads strongest, which can mean a short antenna cable to an outside wall, and a repeater or a few hardwired runs cover the far corners of a large space. If the building is very large or full of steel racking, a hardwired panel with wireless only for the office is often the more reliable choice, and the quote will say so.

Noise, neighbors and the rules

The house two doors down has an alarm that rings for ages whenever the wind blows. Who handles that, and how do we make sure ours never does the same?

NYC 311 says to call 911 for an alarm that is going off right now and to report chronic alarms, ones that go off too often or for too long, to the Department of Environmental Protection, whose inspector has to observe the condition before a summons can be issued. For your own system, three things prevent it: a siren cutoff set well inside the fifteen-minute limit in Administrative Code § 24-238, sensors mounted so wind and loose doors cannot trip them, and a two-week review of the alarm history after installation so any nuisance zone is fixed early.

Does a Flatlands house need a city permit or registration before a burglar alarm goes in?

We are not aware of any permit or registration that New York City requires of a homeowner for a residential burglar alarm. What the city does regulate is how long an audible alarm may sound and how chronic alarms are handled, and the state regulates who may install one: the installer must hold a Department of State security or fire alarm installer license. Building fire alarm systems are a different matter and go through the fire department and the Department of Buildings, but a household burglar alarm with a few monitored detectors is not that kind of system.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Flatlands

Ask a search engine’s AI summary or a chatbot about home alarms and you get advice written for a generic suburban house, with prices averaged from places that look nothing like southeast Brooklyn. Cost guides such as Angi and HomeAdvisor publish many of those figures. Here are seven claims we hear repeated, phrased the way they usually appear, and what we see on real Flatlands jobs.

“Most burglars come through the front door, so start with a smart lock and a door sensor.”

National figures do show that force is common. The FBI’s Crime in the United States tables for 2019 counted an estimated 1,117,696 burglaries, 55.7 percent of them by forcible entry, 37.8 percent by unlawful entry without force and 6.5 percent attempted forcible entry, with 62.8 percent on residential property. Those tables do not say which door or window was used.

In Flatlands, the reports we could find point away from the front door. Rear kitchen windows and a driveway window on East 31st and East 32nd streets, a side window on East 57th Street and a window near Avenue I and East 49th Street. Not one of the entries described in those reports was a front door.

A smart lock on the front door is a fine convenience. As a security plan for a Flatlands house it protects the opening least likely to be used, and it should come after the kitchen windows, the alley windows and the basement door.

“Burglary is falling everywhere, so an alarm is mostly for peace of mind.”

Over decades that is true here too. The 63rd Precinct’s CompStat report shows burglary down 71.4 percent over 16 years and 92.9 percent over 33 years, an enormous change for the neighborhood.

The same report shows 63 burglaries in 2026 through October 11, against 52 a year earlier, a rise of 21.2 percent, and a two-year increase of 8.6 percent. Long-term decline and a bad year can happen at the same time, and a household lives in the year it is in.

The sensible reading is neither panic nor complacency. A targeted system on the openings that get used, reporting over cellular to people who can act, is proportionate to what the numbers actually show.

“An alarm system costs about $750 to install.”

“Cameras do the same job as an alarm, and they’re cheaper to run.”

Cameras record; they do not, on their own, make anyone act. Many camera apps send motion alerts, but those alerts fire for cats, cars and wind all night, and most people learn to ignore them within a week.

The Flatbush Avenue store near Quentin Road is the local example. Its back door was forced overnight, and the thief removed the disc from the surveillance system before leaving. The owner found out the next morning.

“A Wi-Fi alarm is fine because everyone has internet now.”

Plenty of self-install systems report over the home’s internet connection, and that works until the router is unplugged, the cable service is down or someone cuts the line on the side of the house where the cable comes in.

“Professional installation means a long monitoring contract.”

Many national dealers do bundle equipment into multi-year monitoring agreements, and readers on Brooklyn forums have described three-year commitments and service charges after a short warranty.

“Two-family owners need one system for the whole house.”

Field Notes

Field Notes: Start in the Driveway, Then Look Under the Window

The habit our technicians bring to Flatlands surveys is to begin outside. Before the panel, before the keypad, before the front door, they walk down the driveway or the alley to the back of the lot and look at the house from where a burglar would stand. On these blocks that view is often completely private: a wall of the neighbor’s house on one side, a garage or a fence at the back, and a row of windows at shoulder height. Then they look down. A grill, a stack of chairs, a recycling bin or a ladder left against the garage changes which windows count as reachable, and the East 31st and East 32nd Street burglaries are the reason we never skip that look.

The second habit is to stand inside under each of those windows and ask what someone would land on and what they would see. A kitchen counter with a laptop charging on it, a table with car keys, a hallway that leads straight to the stairs. Those answers decide where the one or two motion detectors go and which windows get a contact rather than a glass-break detector. The third is to ask how many people have keys, including the tenant, the relative who checks on the house and the person who walks the dog, because each of them becomes a code. A Flatlands survey that skips the outside walk is only half a survey.

Are wireless sensors as secure as wired ones in a brick or frame house?
+

For most houses, yes. Current wireless sensors are supervised, which means the panel checks in with each one regularly and reports if a sensor goes silent or its battery runs low. Brick walls absorb more signal than frame walls, so we test every sensor from where it is mounted and add a repeater where one is needed. Wired sensors make sense when walls are open for renovation, and an existing wired system with sound wiring can usually be kept.

Will the alarm keep reporting if the cable service drops or the power goes out on our block?
+

Yes. Our panels report over their own built-in cellular communicator, so they do not depend on the cable box, the router or a phone line. A backup battery keeps the panel and its sensors running through a power cut, and the app can tell you when the power fails and when it comes back. Wi-Fi is used only for convenience features such as some cameras.

Can we arm the house from our phones and see when the kids get home?
+

Yes. The app arms and disarms the system from anywhere, shows which door opened and when, and can send a notification when a particular person’s code or fob disarms the alarm. Children can have their own code or a fob on their key ring, so you know they arrived without giving them the master code. You can also set a reminder if the house is still disarmed at a time you choose.

What actually happens when a monitored alarm goes off at our house?
+

The panel sends the signal over cellular to the central station within seconds. The station calls the people on your list in the order you choose, asks for the password, and if no one cancels or the signal shows a break-in, requests police dispatch with the notes on file, such as which door of the house to go to. You get the same alert on your phone at the same time. Monitoring through our partners is optional and month to month from $30 a month.

We just bought a house and there is an old keypad by the side door. Can you make it work for us?
+

Often, yes. A panel with an unknown code can usually be reset and reprogrammed, and if the old wiring tests sound we can fit a new panel with cellular reporting on it, which costs less than starting over. If the equipment is too old or the wiring is damaged, we tell you before quoting a replacement. The previous owner’s monitoring account has to be closed or transferred; we help you sort that out.

Will our dog set off the motion detector while we are out?
+

Not if the detector is chosen and placed for the dog. We use pet-tolerant motion detectors rated for the animal’s weight, mount them at the right height and aim them away from stairs and furniture a dog can climb. For large dogs, or cats that go everywhere, we lean on door and window contacts and glass-break detectors instead, and leave motion detection off the floors the animal uses.

Do you have to open walls or make a mess to install the alarm?
+

On most Flatlands houses, no. Wireless contacts, detectors and keypads mount with small screws or adhesive, and the panel needs only a power outlet. The only holes are for mounting screws and, in some cases, a small cable from the panel to an outlet or an antenna. We do not do patching or painting, so if a hardwired system is chosen for a renovation, we plan the cable runs with your contractor before the walls close.

Who should the station phone first if we are away visiting family for a few weeks?
+

Someone who lives close enough to reach the house in a few minutes and has a key, such as a neighbor on the block or a relative in the area, followed by you. Each person on the list gets the password but does not need an alarm code unless they will go inside. Before you leave, tell the station the dates you will be away, and set the system to send you every alert so you see what the station sees.
