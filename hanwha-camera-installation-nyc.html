The Neighborhood

Why NYC Properties Need Hanwha Camera Installation

New York City was mostly built before anyone imagined a network camera, and that single fact shapes every Hanwha system we put into it. The five boroughs became one city on January 1, 1898, after a consolidation vote so close that it passed 64,744 to 64,467, according to the city's archaeology timeline, and the subway that opened in 1904 tied them together. The housing that followed went up fast. The Citizens Housing & Planning Council's housing stock visualization says more than 80 percent of the city's housing was built more than 50 years ago, and that over half of all units, about two million, were built before 1947. HPD's 2021 Housing and Vacancy Survey gives the same picture in more detail: 2,042,000 units, or 56 percent, in buildings built before 1947, and another 974,000, or 27 percent, in buildings built between 1947 and 1974. In practical terms, five out of six apartments in this city sit in a building that is at least half a century old. A camera system has to be threaded through that building, not designed on a blank sheet of paper.

Hanwha itself has a longer history in this market than its name suggests. The cameras now sold as Wisenet began life at Samsung Techwin. Wikipedia records that Hanwha completed its takeover on June 29, 2015 and renamed the business Hanwha Techwin, and that on March 1, 2023 Hanwha Techwin changed its name again, to Hanwha Vision. The company is headquartered in Seongnam, South Korea, and Tec-Tel's comparison of Axis and Hanwha places its American operation, Hanwha Vision America, in Teaneck, New Jersey, just across the George Washington Bridge from Washington Heights. So when a building manager in Midtown or a co-op board in Queens tells us they want "Samsung cameras" or "Wisenet" or "Hanwha," they are usually asking for the same family of equipment under three names. Part of our job on the first call is untangling which product line they actually mean, because the hardware runs from the lower-cost Q Series through the mainstream X Series to the premium P Series, which Tec-Tel describes as carrying artificial intelligence on the camera itself.

The reason the brand comes up so often now is federal policy, not marketing. The FCC's Covered List names equipment from Hikvision, Dahua and several other Chinese manufacturers, and in 2026 the agency moved from blocking new authorizations to prohibiting the continued import and marketing of previously authorized "legacy" equipment. The sources we reviewed do not agree on the exact date: Iron Gate's NDAA guide gives June 26, 2026, while Coram's replacement guide cites July 16, 2026 and FCC Public Notice DA 26-635. We are not going to pick one for you, and anyone whose contract turns on that date should read the FCC notice itself. What the sources do agree on is twofold. First, iCtally's summary states that Hanwha Vision is not on the FCC Covered List, so its cameras are not caught by the 2022 authorization freeze or the 2026 import ban. Second, Iron Gate states plainly that the ban does not require existing cameras to be ripped out and that there is no "kill switch." That combination produces most of the Hanwha calls we expect across the city: an owner who is not forced to replace anything today, but who knows that replacement parts, firmware support and future purchases of the old brand are getting harder, and who wants the next camera on the wall to be one nobody will question.

The kind of building changes the job far more than the brand does. The prewar stock that HPD counts is not one type. Sky NYC's guide to prewar buildings describes four- to six-story walk-up tenements built before 1929, eight- to twelve-story elevator apartment houses from the 1910s and 1920s, and the larger doorman buildings, and it places many of them on the Upper West Side, the Upper East Side, the Villages, the Lower East Side and East Village, and Harlem. RentReboot's sample of prewar listings found that 47.7 percent had fewer than ten apartments and 41.5 percent listed an elevator, which tells you how many small walk-ups there are. HPD's survey adds that only 12 percent of units in prewar buildings were condos or co-ops, about 70 percent of those being co-ops. Each of those details matters for a Hanwha install. A small walk-up means long cable runs up stairwells and through masonry, with no elevator to carry a ladder. A co-op means a board, a managing agent, rules about hours and a certificate of insurance. An elevator building means risers, and in older buildings, as Seneca Security's guide to choosing a camera system for a city building puts it, "full risers, plaster and coax nobody has traced."

Then there is the outside of the building. The Landmarks Preservation Commission was created when Mayor Robert Wagner signed the Landmarks Law on April 19, 1965, according to Village Preservation, and the city's 2026 Preliminary Mayor's Management Report says the Commission has designated 38,119 buildings and sites, including 1,470 individual landmarks and 157 historic districts and extensions across all five boroughs. LPC's 2018 rule amendments list security cameras, lighting and intercom panels among the objects "commonly used for security measures," and the proposed rules would prohibit installing many of those objects on a primary facade. For an owner on a brownstone block in a historic district, that is the difference between a camera over the front door and a camera tucked under the stoop, in the areaway or on the rear wall. A Hanwha dome in a facade-matched finish is a very different conversation with LPC from a white bullet camera bolted to the cornice.

The people living in these buildings have their own reasons for calling. Security.org's 2026 package theft report found that apartment residents were more than three times as likely as single-family residents to have had a package stolen in the previous three months, 10 percent against 3 percent. CBS New York has reported a lobby thief in Astoria at 36th Avenue and 31st Street who, police said, used a screwdriver to disable a door lock, and thieves in a Park Slope lobby seen cutting packages open inside the building. At the Robeson on Lenox Avenue in Harlem, CBS reported that a key code was required to enter and police said there was no forced entry. Public housing residents are asking for coverage too: a 2025 participatory budgeting proposal for the Gowanus Houses asked the city to install security cameras in elevators and near apartment doors. At the same time, New Yorkers are wary of cameras that do more than record. The Guardian reported in 2019 that tenants at two brick high-rises in Brownsville, which already had cameras watching the grounds, doorways, elevators and halls, fought their landlord's plan for facial-recognition entry. A Hanwha P Series camera can run analytics on board, and a building that switches those features on without thinking about its tenants, or about the city's biometric signage law, is buying itself a problem.

We looked for Reddit threads in r/NYC, r/AskNYC and r/homesecurity about Hanwha installation, for Yelp reviews that praised or complained about Hanwha installers, and for Patch, BKReader or Bronx Times stories about the brand, and none of those searches produced anything we could cite. We did not pull HPD violation, 311 complaint or DOB permit counts tied to cameras. We could not open Google's People Also Ask boxes or its AI Overview for this search from our build environment. Where this page draws a conclusion about what sits behind a wall or inside a riser, it is based on the age and type of buildings in the public record and on what we find in buildings of that age, not on a survey of your building. The only way to know your building is to walk it, which is the first thing we do.

What buildings usually add once the cameras are in. When a building replaces its cameras, the next question is often the front door: a video intercom, key fobs or app entry. That is a separate scope with its own rules. HPD's Tenant Data Privacy Law covers smart access systems in residential buildings with at least three units, so if the door system and the Hanwha cameras are going to share data, tell us at the start and we will plan the two so the camera footage and the entry logs stay separate where the law expects them to.

From Hikvision or Dahua to Hanwha: Planning a Replacement in a New York Building

Most Hanwha calls in this city will not be for a building that has never had a camera. They will be for a building that has one already, often a Hikvision or Dahua recorder, or a rebrand built on one of those platforms, that was installed years ago and has quietly kept running. Iron Gate's NDAA guide is clear that the 2026 import ban does not force anyone to remove working equipment, and we tell owners the same thing. Replacement is a decision, not an emergency, unless a contract, a lender, an insurer or a government-funded program says otherwise. The question is how to do it without paying twice for work the building already has. The table below is how we think through each part of an older system.

Why the cable decides the budget

In a modern office with drop ceilings, pulling a new cable to a camera is quick. In a 1920s walk-up with plaster walls, a full riser and a landlord who does not want hallway ceilings opened, the cable is the job. That is why the first thing we do on a replacement survey is test what is already in the walls. A Cat5e or Cat6 run that certifies cleanly can carry a Hanwha PoE camera's power and video on the same cable, as Seneca's technical guide to PoE installation describes, and reusing it means the new camera goes up where the old one was with little more than a new mount. A run that fails, or turns out to be old coax from an analog system, has to be replaced, and the price of that depends almost entirely on the path. We would rather tell you that on the survey than discover it halfway through the install.

Keeping the footage you are required to keep

Before an old recorder comes off the shelf, we ask whether anyone needs what is on it. A building in the middle of an insurance claim, a commercial tenant with a dispute, or a board that has received a records request may need footage preserved. We export the clips you name before the swap, onto a drive you keep, and we note on the job sheet that the old recorder's disk was handed to you, not wiped. If nobody needs the old footage, we say so on the paperwork too. This step takes minutes, but skipping it is the one mistake in a replacement that cannot be undone.

Doing it in stages

Because the ban does not force a rip-out, many owners spread the change over time. A common order is to replace the recorder and the cameras covering the most important areas first, usually the entrances, lobby, mailroom and garage ramp, and leave the rest of the old cameras running until a later budget year. Whether that works depends on whether the new recorder can take the old cameras in the meantime, which varies by model and firmware. We check that compatibility on the survey and tell you in writing whether a staged plan is realistic, or whether the two systems would have to run side by side on separate screens until the second stage.

When the replacement is not optional

An NYC competitor's warehouse page notes that some builds move to Hanwha, Axis or Avigilon when a contract or landlord requires NDAA-compliant equipment, naming institutional owners, government-adjacent tenants and some records and pharmaceutical contracts as examples. If your building or business falls into that group, the requirement usually arrives as a clause in a lease or contract, and it often names Section 889 of the National Defense Authorization Act. Send us the clause. We will tell you which parts of your existing system it reaches, which parts it does not, and we will list the make and model of every device we install so you can hand that list to whoever is checking compliance.

Walk the building at night once

A camera sees only what the light shows it. Seneca's compliance guide points out that the Multiple Dwelling Law requires adequate lighting in vestibules, hallways, stairwells and courtyards, and that a camera pointed at an unlit corridor records nothing useful. Before the survey, walk the areas you want covered after dark and note where the light is weak, where a bulb is out, and where a bright fixture shines straight at the spot a camera would look from. Hanwha cameras handle low light well, but no camera can identify a face lit from behind by a glaring fixture. Your notes tell us where we may need a different angle, or where the fix is a light bulb rather than a camera.

Know who has to sign off

In a one-family brownstone, the owner decides. In a co-op, the shareholder, the board and the managing agent may all have a say, and building-wide upgrades may need board approval, as Seneca's guide notes. In a rental building, the owner decides about common areas, but tenants will notice cameras in the halls, and Seneca's article on tenant objections warns that a camera aimed at a single tenant's door, rather than the broader hallway, can look like harassment in a dispute. In a commercial space, the landlord usually controls the facade, the roof and the shared ceilings. Tell us who owns what before the survey and we will plan each camera position with the right person.

Building Specialists

Hanwha Camera Installation for NYC's Architecture

A city with about two million homes built before 1947 does not have one kind of wall. Each building type changes three things about a Hanwha camera job: what the camera is fixed into, how the cable gets from the camera to the recorder, and who has to agree before we drill. These are the types the public record and housing studies describe across the five boroughs, and how we handle each.

Brownstones and Row Houses

Rowhouse neighborhoods went up between the Civil War and World War I, according to the Architectural League. Brick or brownstone fronts, plaster inside, stoops and areaways, and in historic districts, LPC review of anything on the facade.

Walk-Up Tenements

Four- to six-story buildings built before 1929, many with fewer than ten apartments. Long vertical cable runs, masonry party walls and no elevator for ladders and spools.

Prewar Elevator Buildings

Eight- to twelve-story buildings from the 1910s and 1920s and larger doorman buildings. Risers, plaster ceilings, old coax, and often a board or a managing agent with contractor rules.

Postwar and Mid-Century Towers

The 27 percent of units HPD counts in buildings from 1947 to 1974. Concrete and block, which blocks WiFi and calls for hammer drilling, but often better pathways than prewar buildings.

Mixed-Use Buildings

Stores below and apartments above. Seneca notes that cable crossing fire-rated assemblies or floor separations between commercial and residential occupancies needs proper fire-stopping.

Garages, Warehouses and Industrial Space

Concrete, steel and high ceilings, conduit where it is required, lifts that need landlord approval, and in union buildings, work-hour rules.

Brownstones: hiding a wired camera on a protected front

A brownstone owner's first instinct is often a camera above the front door, pointed down the stoop. On a block in a historic district, LPC's rules aim to keep many security objects off the primary facade unless there is no feasible alternative, and secondary sources describe limits on how many cameras may sit along each 25 feet of frontage. We are not the Commission and we cannot promise what it will approve, so we plan the other way round. We start with positions that are not on the primary facade at all, under the stoop looking out at the gate, in the areaway, inside the vestibule looking through the glass, or on the rear wall over the yard, and we use the smallest Hanwha dome or turret that gives the view in a finish that suits the masonry. If a facade position really is the only one that works, we tell you that LPC review is likely needed, and the owner files for it before we mount anything there. The cable comes in through the basement or an existing opening wherever possible, never through ornamental stone.

Walk-ups: long runs and no elevator

In a five-story walk-up, a camera at the roof door can be a long way from a recorder in the basement, and every foot of that run goes up a stairwell. PoE cable has a limit on how long a run can be before the signal and power fall off, so in tall walk-ups we sometimes place a small PoE switch in a closet or utility space partway up and run the upper cameras to that instead of all the way down. Where there is no shaft, cable travels in painted surface raceway along the stairwell walls, fixed into the masonry, not stuck on with adhesive that lets go in the first humid summer. We carry spools, ladders and tools up by hand, which takes time, and we tell you that time on the quote rather than discovering it on the day.

Prewar elevator buildings: the riser problem

Older elevator buildings usually have a riser, a vertical shaft for telephone, cable and electrical wiring, and it is usually crowded. Seneca's description of "full risers, plaster and coax nobody has traced" matches what anyone working in these buildings expects. Before we plan a single cable through a riser, we look at it with the super, tone out any old camera cable to see if it can be reused, and check whether there is physical room for new runs without disturbing what is already there. Where the riser is full, the alternative is corridor raceway, which is visible but honest, or a carefully planned run through a closet stack. Plaster ceilings in corridors are another reason we prefer surface mounts in halls: opening and patching century-old plaster to hide a cable for one camera rarely makes sense.

Postwar towers: concrete and the WiFi myth

Mid-century and later towers are often concrete, sometimes with block partitions. ADR Security's guide to NYC camera installation notes that signal degrades through concrete, steel and distance, and that is the single best argument for wired Hanwha cameras in these buildings. Concrete also means hammer drilling and, for anything that has to pass through a slab or a structural wall, core drilling, which we only do with written approval from the owner or board and, where the work calls for it, a filing. Most of the time we avoid core drilling entirely by using the building's existing sleeves and risers.

Mixed-use buildings: the fire line between the store and the homes

Many city blocks are lined with buildings that have a store on the ground floor and apartments above. A camera system for the whole building often means running cable from the storefront up into the residential floors, or from the apartments' lobby down into the store's back room. Seneca's mixed-use guidance is that cable crossing fire-rated assemblies or floor separations between commercial and residential occupancies needs proper fire-stopping. We seal every penetration we make through a rated wall or floor with a listed fire-stopping product, and we do not route camera cable through a store's ceiling into the apartments' stairwell without the owner's agreement. Field Notes: Reading an Old Camera Closet Before Quoting the New One

We do not publish stories about a particular customer's building unless we can document them, so this is not a tale about one address. It is the routine our technicians follow when they open the door to the camera closet in an older New York building, which is where every Hanwha replacement begins.

We photograph the closet exactly as we find it before touching anything, because the super will want to know later what was there. We read the recorder's model label and note the date on its screen, which tells us whether it has been recording at all. We count the cables arriving at the back of the recorder or switch and compare that number with the cameras the building thinks it has; the two rarely match. We feel the battery backup for heat and check the date on its battery. We tone out two or three cables to the cameras they serve, starting with the lobby and the front door, and test them. We look at how the cables leave the closet, through a sleeve, an open hole in the ceiling, or a riser, and whether that opening was ever fire-stopped. Only after that do we walk the camera positions. By the time we leave, we know which cables can carry a Hanwha camera, which cannot, and where the new recorder should live, and that is what the quote is built on.

The brand and its product lines

Our old system says Samsung and the new quote says Wisenet. Are Hanwha and Wisenet one and the same?

Yes, they are the same family under different names. The camera business that started at Samsung Techwin was taken over by Hanwha, which completed the deal on June 29, 2015 and renamed it Hanwha Techwin, and on March 1, 2023 it became Hanwha Vision, according to Wikipedia. Wisenet is the brand name on the cameras, recorders and the WAVE software. So an older Samsung-labelled camera in a city building, a Hanwha Techwin recorder and a new Wisenet camera all come from the same lineage. That does not mean old and new equipment will always work together; firmware and recorder compatibility still have to be checked model by model, which we do on the survey.

Which Hanwha series makes sense for a city apartment building: Q, X or P?

It depends on the view, not the building as a whole. Tec-Tel describes the Q Series as Hanwha's lower-cost line, the X Series as the mainstream line and the P Series as the premium line with artificial intelligence on the camera. In most apartment buildings, stairwells, roof doors and rear yards are well served by Q Series cameras, while lobbies, mailrooms and street-facing entrances, where you want a clear face at the door in mixed light, often justify X Series. P Series makes sense where analytics matter, such as a garage entrance or a loading dock that needs reliable person and vehicle detection. Mixing series on one recorder is normal, and the quote lists the series for each position so you can see why.

Federal rules and older equipment

Our lease says equipment must be NDAA compliant. Does a Hanwha system satisfy that clause?

Hanwha is widely treated as compliant, but the clause is what decides. Hanwha Vision is a South Korean manufacturer and is not on the FCC Covered List, per iCtally, and NDAA compliance guides such as Ring and Ping's and Tec-Tel's list Hanwha among compliant brands. Section 889 is about the manufacturer of the equipment, so the cameras alone are not the whole answer: the recorder, the PoE switch and any other networked device in the system are part of it too. Send us the exact wording of the clause. We will specify every device by make and model, keep anything from a Covered List manufacturer out of the system, and give you a written equipment list you can hand to whoever checks compliance.

We still run Hikvision recorders in two buildings. Must they come out now, and can we buy more of the same?

They do not have to come out because of the ban. Iron Gate's guide states that the 2026 import ban does not require removing installed equipment and there is no kill switch, and Blinkless notes the FCC rules do not ban private use. Buying more is the problem: the FCC prohibited continued importation and marketing of previously authorized Hikvision and Dahua equipment in 2026, though our sources disagree on whether that took effect June 26 or July 16, so new supply, replacement parts and support will keep getting harder. For a private building with no contract requirement, the sensible approach is to keep what works, plan a Hanwha replacement, and avoid buying new legacy equipment in the meantime.

Recording software

Does Wisenet WAVE recording come with a monthly or yearly fee?

Not in the way a cloud camera subscription does. Surveillant's Hanwha pricing guide describes WAVE as using a one-time license for each camera, with the Professional tier carrying no annual renewal, and CDW lists the WAVE Professional single-camera license as a separate product. So a building that records on WAVE buys one license per camera when the system is set up and owns it. A Hanwha recorder is the other option and handles licensing differently. Internet service for remote viewing is a separate cost the building already pays. We show the licenses as their own line on the quote, so you can see exactly what you are buying and why.

Wiring in masonry

In a brick or concrete building, should the Hanwha cameras be wired or WiFi?

Wired, wherever the building allows it. ADR Security's NYC guide points out that wireless signal degrades through concrete, steel and distance, and nearly every city building is made of at least one of those. A PoE camera gets its power and its network connection over one Cat5e or Cat6 cable, as Seneca's technical guide explains, so it keeps recording through router changes, does not need a battery swapped, and does not drop off the recorder when a neighbor's network crowds the airwaves. WiFi still has a place, for a single camera where no cable path exists at all, but in a masonry building we treat it as the exception and tell you the trade-off before installing it.

"Hanwha is a Chinese camera brand."

The FCC Covered List names specific companies, not whole countries or regions. iCtally's summary of the 2026 import ban states that Hanwha Vision is not on the Covered List and that its cameras are not affected by the 2022 authorization freeze or the 2026 import ban.

What third-party cost guides leave out

National guides, Angi's city averages and competitor blogs give per-camera ranges for "commercial-grade" or "NYC" installs. NYC Hanwha Camera Installation FAQ

Who can install a Hanwha camera system for us in New York City?
+

AESS installs Hanwha systems across all five boroughs, working under NYS license #12000287431 and dispatching from 1282 Troy Avenue in Brooklyn. We have been owner-operated since 2002 and book a survey within 2 hours of a request. Several other city firms also advertise Hanwha work, so ask any installer you speak to for their state license number, a certificate of insurance, and a written equipment list by make and model before you sign.

Does the person putting up our cameras legally need a state license?
+

Yes, if they are doing it as a business. NYC's business portal states that installing, servicing or maintaining security systems requires a license from the New York State Department of State, and the state rule at 19 NYCRR 195.2(b) includes closed-circuit television used to detect or monitor intrusion or theft. Ask for the license number and check it. Ours is NYS license #12000287431, printed on every quote.

How many days does a Hanwha install take in a city building?
+

It depends far more on the building than on the number of cameras. Board paperwork, freight elevator windows, union work-hour rules and whether existing cable can be reused all change the schedule. A small brownstone system is usually a short job once the survey is done, while an occupied elevator building with full risers takes longer. Your written quote includes our time estimate for your building and explains what would change it.

How many Hanwha cameras does a typical brownstone end up with?
+

Most owners start with the areas a stranger would use: the front gate and stoop, the vestibule, the rear yard and any basement or side door. That is often four cameras, which is why our starter scope is built around four, but the count follows the house. A brownstone in a historic district may need positions under the stoop or in the areaway instead of on the facade, and a house with a garden apartment may need its own entrance covered.

If our building grows or we add a floor later, can more cameras join the system?
+

Yes, as long as the system is planned with room to grow. We size the PoE switch and recorder with spare ports and storage where you expect expansion, and Wisenet WAVE lets you add a camera by buying one more one-time license. We also leave the port map and cable paths documented, so whoever adds the next camera can see where it fits. Tell us about planned expansions at the survey and we will build in the headroom.

Hanwha or Hikvision: which would you put on a New York building today?
+

For a new system today we recommend Hanwha. Hikvision is on the FCC Covered List and its previously authorized equipment can no longer be imported or marketed in the United States under the 2026 prohibition, so future supply, parts and support are uncertain. Hanwha is not on the Covered List. If you already have Hikvision equipment that works, you are not required to remove it, but new purchases should be planned around a brand you can keep buying.

Our contract lists Axis or Hanwha. Is there a reason to choose one over the other?
+

Both are treated as NDAA Section 889 compliant brands in guides such as Tec-Tel's, so the contract does not decide it. The choice usually comes down to the recording platform the building wants, the camera types needed for each view, budget, and what any existing equipment already uses. Hanwha's Q, X and P Series give a clear ladder from lower-cost to analytics-heavy cameras. We install Hanwha and can explain the trade-offs for your specific positions.

We are adding app-based door entry along with the cameras. What does Local Law 63 require of us?
+

The Tenant Data Privacy Law covers smart access systems in residential buildings with at least three units. Butterfly MX notes it does not apply to ordinary camera feeds, but it does apply when cameras come bundled with keyless entry. Owners must give tenants a plain-language privacy policy, get express consent before collecting data or biometrics, use the data only for access and entry monitoring, and destroy authentication data within 90 days. We keep camera footage and entry logs separate where possible.

Does adding door access control with our cameras bring in a Buildings Department filing?
+

It can. Vertex notes that DOB filing or approval is needed when access control work alters fire-rated door assemblies, modifies means of egress under Building Code Chapter 10, or ties directly into the building's fire alarm system. Cameras on their own rarely trigger that. When a project combines Hanwha cameras with door hardware, we identify any door that is fire-rated or part of an exit route at the survey and tell you whether filing is likely before work is scheduled.

Can our store's Hanwha cameras be shared with the NYPD during an emergency?
+

NYC 311 describes the NYPD Community Camera Sharing Program as letting any business with a security camera share live video with the NYPD during emergencies, with camera locations registered at NewYorkCityConnect.org. Joining is voluntary and is your decision. If you choose to register, we can explain what your Hanwha recorder or WAVE server would need on the network side, and how to keep sharing limited to the cameras you choose.

Is there a tax credit that helps pay for a camera system in New York City?
+

Not one you can count on right now. The 2024 to 2025 New York retail security tax credit has ended, according to Avigilon. State Assembly bill A406 from 2025 proposes a credit for buying and installing a camera system on residential and commercial property in cities of a million or more, and City Council bill Int 0623-2026 proposes a small business security pilot, but our research did not confirm that either has become law. Check with your accountant before budgeting on one.

Is our apartment building legally required to have security cameras at all?
+

Generally no. Seneca's compliance guide notes there is no general NYC law mandating cameras in apartment buildings, though the law does require intercoms in many buildings and adequate lighting in common areas. Most camera systems are driven by owners, boards, tenants and insurers; Seneca also notes that many commercial insurers set camera coverage conditions for theft and vandalism claims. If your insurer or lender has requirements, send them and we will plan coverage to meet them.
