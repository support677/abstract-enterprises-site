The Neighborhood

Why Fieldston Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Fieldston was laid out to ignore the city grid, and that one decision shapes nearly every camera plan we draw here. Joseph Delafield bought the land in 1829. In 1909 his estate sold part of it to Manhattan Teachers College, the start of today's Horace Mann School, and developed the rest as a private residential park. The first lots sold in 1910. Instead of straight blocks, the engineer Albert Wheeler set the streets to follow the contours of the ridge, keeping woodland and natural rock outcrops in place. The result, a century later, is a neighborhood of curving roads where houses sit at angles to the street, driveways bend out of sight, front doors face gardens rather than sidewalks, and the view from one corner of a house to the road can be blocked by a hill, a ledge of stone or a hundred-year-old tree. A camera plan that works on a Bronx block of attached houses, where every door faces the same sidewalk, does not translate. Each Fieldston lot has to be walked on its own terms.

The streets themselves are not city streets. Residents formed the Fieldston Property Owners' Association in August 1923, and the deeds to the streets passed to the Association in 1948. Today the Association owns the streets and common areas, plows them, maintains the sewers, cares for the trees and runs a security patrol around the clock. Its physical security is handled by Excel, whose van patrols the streets day and night and also deals with illegally parked cars, traffic, fallen trees and downed power lines. Once a year the streets are closed to outsiders to keep them legally private, and parking is limited to residents and their guests. Since a 1938 petition for a special zoning district barred apartment buildings, the core of the neighborhood has stayed almost entirely detached houses; city building records show about 256 residential lots inside the historic district, every one of them a one- or two-family house. That is the customer here: an owner of a large, old, single house, often with a separate garage, sometimes with a terrace or balcony on an upper floor, on land that may adjoin strips owned by the Association.

The houses are also landmarks. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Fieldston Historic District in 2006, and most of the neighborhood sits inside it. The Commission's permit guidebook has a section titled "Installing Security Cameras," and it says staff can approve security cameras when the installation meets the stated criteria. The rule behind it, LPC Rule 2-20, covers health, safety and utility equipment, cameras among them, and asks that the equipment be finished to match the building or otherwise not call undue attention to itself. Fieldston also has its own master plan, adopted as an LPC rule in 2014, which lets staff approve things like fences, gates, driveways and lighting within set limits; cameras are not named in it, so the general rule governs them. This is not paperwork for its own sake. A 2020 op-ed in the Riverdale Press described a long list of Landmarks violations at Horace Mann's Tibbett Avenue properties, and one application was meant to clear outstanding violations that included security cameras installed without a permit. A camera put up first and approved later is a camera that may have to come down.

The record shows why owners still want cameras despite the patrol. The Riverdale Press reported that some cars smashed on Fieldston Road on January 9 were still missing windows the next day, that much of that stretch of Fieldston Road is poorly lit, and that one resident's outdoor cameras were placed badly for the street and caught only the driveway. CBS2 aired surveillance video of a thief walking up to a truck on Fieldston Road, trying the unlocked door and getting in; another resident's van was entered in her driveway, behind a closed gate. An Association letter reported in the Riverdale Press described a series of thefts from unlocked cars and an attempted break-in at a home on Delafield Avenue. NY1 reported a 2019 burglary at a house on Grosvenor Street just after 5 p.m. on a Saturday, where the burglars got in through a balcony window and took jewelry and luggage worth more than $140,000. And after a stabbing near Fieldston Terrace and West 252nd Street, police used surveillance video from nearby homes to describe the suspect. A patrol van passes; a camera stays. When something happens on these streets, the useful picture almost always comes from a private house.

The land adds its own constraints. The Special Natural Area District covers the Riverdale Ridge, including parts of Fieldston, with its steep slopes, rock outcrops, ponds, brooks, marshes and mature trees. Rock outcrops inside it cannot be altered without City Planning Commission authorization, and a designated area of no disturbance must be protected from excavation, tunneling for utilities and tree removal. That matters the moment a camera has to reach a detached garage, a gate pier or a far corner of the lot, because the obvious answer, a trench across the lawn, may not be available. The size of the lots matters too. On a quarter-acre or more, with a stone house between the router and the drive, Wi-Fi cameras that work fine in a city apartment struggle, and cable runs grow long enough that the route needs real planning.

One more lesson comes from just outside the neighborhood. Fieldston has no shops of its own; the nearest commercial strip is Riverdale Avenue, where the Riverdale Press reported that burglars at one restaurant even took the box that recorded the security cameras. A recorder sitting on a shelf by the front door can leave with the jewelry. So in a Fieldston house we put the recorder somewhere hidden and dry, often in the cellar or a closet far from the entry, keep it on battery backup, and give the owner a way to keep important clips off the house as well. Along the eastern edge, the picture is different again: roughly eight apartment buildings on the Broadway, Cayuga Avenue and Post Road strip, among them the 1961 co-op at 6035 Broadway with 103 apartments and a doorman, and the 1930 to 1931 co-op at 4410 and 4414 Cayuga Avenue. Those are board decisions, planned as buildings, not houses.

Fieldston facts that shape a camera plan

  • Private streets with a patrol: the Fieldston Property Owners' Association owns the streets and common areas and runs a 24-hour patrol through Excel; residents still need their own record of their own doors and drives.
  • A landmarked district: most of Fieldston has been in an LPC historic district since 2006, and LPC staff can approve cameras that meet Rule 2-20's criteria for finish and visibility.
  • A known enforcement case: Horace Mann's Tibbett Avenue properties carried Landmarks violations that included security cameras installed without a permit, according to a 2020 Riverdale Press op-ed.
  • Protected rock and trees: Special Natural Area rules protect rock outcrops and areas of no disturbance from excavation and utility tunneling, which limits cable trenches to garages and gates.
  • Detached houses on big lots: about 256 residential lots in the district, all one- or two-family houses, often on a quarter-acre or more with long driveways and detached garages.
  • A lower district-wide crime rate, with specific incidents: the Furman Center counts 10.1 serious crimes per 1,000 residents in Riverdale and Fieldston combined in 2025, against 12.3 citywide, alongside reported car break-ins on Fieldston Road and a balcony-window burglary on Grosvenor Street.

What owners often add alongside cameras. Fieldston owners who start with cameras often move on to the doors and the drive: whether a camera view and an alert can arrive together when the garage or a terrace door opens late at night, or whether the doorbell picture can ring through to several floors of a big house. We plan cable routes and recorder space so either can be added later without opening the walls a second time.

The district house: what shows from the street and what does not

On a Fieldston house we sort every proposed camera into one of three groups before anything else. The first group is inside: a camera in the garage, in the cellar near the recorder, or looking at the inside of a terrace door. These do not change the exterior at all. The second group is on the outside but out of view from the street: under a rear porch ceiling, on the garden side of a wing, inside a recessed entry where the camera sits within the shadow of the opening. These are usually the easiest exterior positions to justify, though on Fieldston's curving streets and corner lots "the back" is sometimes visible from another road, so we look from every street edge, not just the front. The third group is anything plainly visible from the street: a camera at the corner of the front facade, over the garage doors facing the road, or on a gate pier. Those are the ones that need the most care with size, finish and cable, and the ones that need to go to LPC staff before they go up.

Where your lawn ends and the Association's land begins

The LPC's Fieldston master plan notes that the Association owns the streets and, in many cases, areas of yards beside them, including sidewalks. For cameras, that matters in two ways. First, a camera post, a junction box or a cable trench near the road should sit on land you actually own, so we ask for a survey or the Association's answer before planning anything near the street edge. Second, it affects what a camera is aimed at. A camera that watches your own drive as it meets the road is doing its job; one framed down the length of the street, across other people's frontages, is a different matter, and we keep views to your own property and its approaches. Most cameras never come near the line at all, because they are mounted on the house. But for the drive and gate positions that owners care about most after the Fieldston Road break-ins, we check the line first.

The Broadway co-ops: a building with a board

The apartment buildings on Fieldston's eastern edge are a separate kind of job. A board decides where common-area cameras go, who can see the footage and who can export it. The managing agent usually wants after-hours alerts; the super or doorman wants a desk view of the entrances and the package area; shareholders want to know that no camera looks at their apartment doors from closer than it should. We plan these buildings the way we plan any co-op: one recorder in a locked room, cameras at the entrances, the lobby, the mailroom, any garage or laundry, and the grounds, cable routed through the cellar and existing chases, rated fire-stopping wherever cable passes between floors, and a written access policy the board can adopt. A shareholder who wants a camera inside their own apartment gets a small indoor camera and our advice to check the house rules before fixing anything to the door.

Security Camera Installation for Fieldston's Architecture

Fieldston's houses were designed by named architects, Dwight James Baum, Julius Gregory, Delano & Aldrich and Van Vleck & Goldsmith among them, over roughly half a century, and the walls a camera crew meets change from one house to the next: dressed stone and rough fieldstone, stucco, half-timbering, brick, clapboard, slate and tile roofs, and, on the modern infill, glass and flat overhangs. These are the house types the sources describe, and what each means for where a camera can sit and how its cable gets inside without marking the house.

Half-Timber, Stucco and Stone: The Tudor Houses

Steep roofs, deep eaves, leaded windows and mixed materials, such as the 1911 to 1912 Tudor at 355 West 246th Street. Cameras belong on stone or under eaves, never on the timbers.

Brick, Clapboard and Shutters: The Colonial Family

Colonial, Georgian and Dutch Colonial houses with symmetrical fronts and center entries. A camera on the front has nowhere to hide, so most go to porches, side wings and the rear.

Stucco and Tile: The Mediterranean Houses

Smooth or textured stucco, tile roofs and walls like the schist stone fence at 4621 Waldo Avenue. Stucco needs careful drilling and color-matched housings.

Early houses like the 1911 to 1912 Craftsman at 4418 Waldo Avenue, with stone bases and fieldstone chimneys. Irregular stone takes anchors only in its joints.

Houses like the 1963 Julius Panero design at 4540 Fieldston Road. Long glass walls and flat overhangs change both mounting and night glare.

Stone, schist and fieldstone: anchors only in the joints

Much of Fieldston is built of stone, and stone does not forgive a careless drill. Dressed stone can crack or spall from an anchor set too close to its edge; rough fieldstone and schist are irregular, so a camera bracket screwed to one face sits crooked against the next. The rule we follow is simple: anchors go into mortar joints, never through the face of a stone, and the mount sits on a junction box shaped or shimmed to the wall so the camera is level and the cable never shows at the camera. Mortar can be repointed when a camera is moved; a drilled stone stays drilled. Where a stone wall is too irregular for any neat mount, we look for a better position nearby, a sheltered soffit or a porch ceiling, rather than forcing a bracket onto the stone. The single hole that carries cable inside goes through the stone base below the first floor where possible, sloped slightly toward the outside, sleeved and sealed with an exterior-grade sealant, so water follows gravity out rather than following the cable in.

Stucco: backed mounts and a color that disappears

Stucco looks solid and is not. It is a thin hard skin over lath or masonry, and a camera screwed straight into it can crack the skin, let water behind it and leave a patch that never matches. On the Mediterranean houses and the stucco panels of the Tudors, we find what is behind the stucco before choosing a spot, drill with a masonry bit at low speed to avoid shattering the surface, set the anchor into the solid backing rather than the stucco alone, and seal the edge of the mount. The housing color matters as much as the fixing. A bright white or black camera on ochre or cream stucco is exactly what Rule 2-20 is trying to avoid, so for visible positions we choose housings that can be finished to match the wall, or small enough to read as a shadow under an eave.

Slate and tile roofs, deep eaves and the attic route

Fieldston's steep slate and tile roofs and their deep eaves are both a gift and a hazard for cameras. The gift is shelter: a camera tucked under a deep eave stays dry, sits out of easy reach and is often barely visible from the street. The underside of the eave usually leads straight into the attic, where cable can travel across the house and down an interior closet to the cellar without a single run on the outside wall. The hazard is winter. Snow and ice slide off a steep slate roof in sheets, and anything mounted at the drip edge can be knocked off or buried. So we keep eave cameras back from the edge, under the soffit rather than on the fascia, and we never mount anything on the roof surface itself. In the attic we check the condition of old wiring and insulation before anyone crawls across it, keep camera cable away from hot fixtures and never leave it loose in insulation.

Plaster walls inside and the path to the recorder

The inside of most Fieldston houses is plaster, often original, and fishing cable through it takes patience. A cable that has to get from an attic to a cellar three floors down needs a path: an old chase beside a chimney, a closet stacked above a closet, a back stair, the space behind built-in cabinets, or a laundry chute. We find that path on the survey rather than cutting the plaster, and where there is truly no path, we say so and suggest a different camera position before anyone opens a wall. In houses being renovated, this whole problem disappears for a season: with the walls open, camera cable can go exactly where it is needed, labeled and tested, before the plasterers return. That is the cheapest moment in a house's life to wire it, and it is one reason we ask every buyer of a Fieldston house about their renovation plans.

Long outdoor runs to the garage and the drive

On a big lot, some cameras sit a long way from the recorder. Network camera cable carries power and picture together for about 100 meters, roughly 328 feet, and beyond that a camera needs a switch or extender part-way along. Where a cable has to run outside, from the house to a detached garage or along a wall toward the drive, it goes in protective conduit rather than lying on the ground or stapled to a fence, and it enters each building through a sealed, sleeved opening. Where the shortest route crosses exposed rock or the roots of a protected tree, we do not trench through it. We look instead for a route along an existing path or driveway edge on the owner's own land, a route through the house and garage that avoids the ground altogether, or a point-to-point wireless link between two buildings that each have their own cabled cameras.

Rules and limits for cameras in and around Fieldston

  • Landmarks for anything visible on a district house. LPC staff can approve security cameras that meet Rule 2-20's criteria; visible equipment should match the building's finish or not draw undue attention. Work done without the required permit can lead to violations, as the Horace Mann case shows.
  • City Planning for digging in protected areas. Rock outcrops in the Special Natural Area District cannot be altered without City Planning Commission authorization, and areas of no disturbance are protected from excavation and utility tunneling.
  • The Association's land is the Association's. Before any post, box or trench near the street edge, we confirm the property line with a survey or the Association.
  • Private places stay off camera. Recording where people reasonably expect privacy, such as bathrooms and changing areas, is a crime under New York Penal Law 250.45, and we will not aim a camera at one, in a house, a shop or a co-op.
  • Neighbors' homes stay out of frame. New York's Civil Rights Law §52-a lets residential owners and tenants sue over cameras that intrude on their yards, so views stop at your own property.
  • Electrical work belongs to an electrician. Our cameras run on low-voltage cable from the recorder or a switch. If a position needs a new outlet or circuit, the quote says so and names it as an electrician's work.
  • Fire-rated construction stays rated. Where cable passes between floors or through a rated wall in a co-op, the opening is sealed with rated fire-stopping.
  • Licensing. New York's General Business Law §69-m licenses security system installers. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431 and is insured, which is what a co-op board, an agent or an insurer usually asks first.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Fieldston: Questions Answered

Landmarks and the historic district

Does a camera on a Fieldston house have to go to a public Landmarks hearing, or can LPC staff approve it?

In most cases it can be approved at staff level. The LPC's permit guidebook has a section on installing security cameras and says staff can approve them when the installation meets the stated criteria, which come from Rule 2-20: the equipment should be finished to match the building or otherwise not call undue attention to itself. In practice that means small housings, colors that blend with the wall, positions under eaves or within recessed openings, and cable concealed inside the house rather than run across the facade. Cameras inside the house, and many on rear walls that cannot be seen from any street, do not change the street face of the house at all. Anything plainly visible should go to staff before it goes up. We assemble the product sheets, mounting details and photographs an owner needs for the application, and we plan the rear and interior cameras so they can be installed while it is reviewed.

Some cameras at Horace Mann's Tibbett Avenue properties went up without a Landmarks permit. What happens if a camera goes up first and the paperwork comes later?

The Horace Mann case shows what tends to happen: unpermitted work becomes a violation, and the owner then has to apply to legalize or correct it. A 2020 Riverdale Press op-ed described a long list of Landmarks violations at those properties, and one application was meant to clear outstanding violations including security cameras installed without a permit. For a homeowner, the cost of going first and asking later is not just a fine risk; it is the chance that a camera, its mount and its cable have to come down and the wall be repaired, and that a sale or a later renovation application gets tangled up in an open violation. Applying first costs a little time. That is why our plan separates the cameras that need review from the ones that do not, and why we do not mount a visible camera on a district house without the owner confirming it is approved.

Stucco, half-timber, stone and glass

How do you anchor a camera to the stucco on a Mediterranean Revival house without cracking it?

By not relying on the stucco to hold anything. Stucco is a hard, thin coat over lath or masonry, and it cracks if it is drilled fast or asked to carry a load on its own. We locate what is behind it first, drill slowly with a masonry bit, set the anchors into the solid backing, and seat the camera on a mounting block or junction box sealed around its edge so water cannot get behind the stucco. If the backing is hollow or crumbling at the chosen spot, we move the camera rather than force it. For positions visible from the street, the housing is chosen to be painted or finished to the stucco color, or small enough to read as a shadow under the eave or a loggia ceiling. The cable goes straight into the house through the back of the mount, so nothing runs across the stucco face.

Our Tudor's front is half-timber and stucco under a steep roof. Where does a camera sit without landing on the timbers?

Usually under the eave, or on stone, not on the timbering. The timbers are the most recognizable part of a Tudor front, they are often old and decorative rather than structural, and screw holes in them are visible from the street and invite rot. A camera tucked under a deep eave or the overhang of a gable is sheltered, out of reach and much less visible, and its cable can go straight up into the attic. A recessed front entry often has a stone or plaster soffit where a small dome or turret sits almost out of sight while still seeing faces at the door. Where the house has a stone base or a stone wing, joints in that stone are a better anchor than anything on the timber. We avoid the leaded windows entirely, both for the glass and because a camera behind leaded glass sees mostly its own reflection at night.

Our modern house has long glass walls and flat overhangs, more like 4540 Fieldston Road than a Tudor. Where do cameras go?

The flat overhang is usually the answer. On a mid-century house with walls of glass, there is little solid wall to mount on, and a camera inside looking out through the glass sees its own infrared glow and the room's reflections at night. The underside of a flat roof overhang gives a clean, sheltered surface; a low-profile dome or small turret there looks down on the doors and the terrace without breaking the house's lines. Cable goes up into the roof structure and across to an interior wall. Solid wall sections between glass panels, often at the corners or the entry, take a camera where an overhang does not reach. Because these houses depend on clean lines, we keep housings small and match their color to the trim or soffit. We also check the glass from outside after dark, because large lit windows can blind a camera aimed toward the house.

Our Arts and Crafts house has fieldstone chimneys, like the early houses on Waldo Avenue. Can a camera mount on the chimney?

We advise against it. A chimney is one of the most visible features on an early Fieldston house, and a camera on it is hard to justify under Landmarks criteria. It is also a poor technical choice: a working flue runs hot, smoke and soot foul a lens, the flashing where the chimney meets the roof is easily damaged by a ladder or a drill, and old fieldstone chimneys often have soft or failing mortar. A camera on the chimney is also hard to service safely. Better positions are almost always nearby: the stone base of the house, where joints take anchors well and cable can go straight into the cellar, the sheltered side of a porch beam, or under the eave. If the chimney seems to offer the only view of an approach, we usually solve it with a second camera from a less prominent spot instead.

Big lots, long drives and the rock between buildings

Our house sits well back from Fieldston Road behind trees. Can any camera on it see the road at night?

Often not well, and that is what the Riverdale Press found after the January car break-ins, when one resident's outdoor cameras covered only the driveway. A camera on the house, far back from the road, sees the road as a small, dark strip, and at night its built-in infrared does not reach that far. Two changes usually help. First, frame the place that matters, the point where the drive meets the road and where cars park, using a zoom-lens camera that makes that spot large in the picture, rather than a wide view of the whole lot. Second, bring that camera closer: a mount on the garage, a porch near the drive, or a post on your own land near the drive's end, with light planned for it. A camera with its own light, or a motion light on your property aimed at your own drive, gives a usable color picture without lighting up the neighbors.

Our detached garage sits beyond a ledge of exposed rock. How does the camera there get its cable?

Not by cutting through the rock. Rock outcrops in the Special Natural Area District cannot be altered without City Planning Commission authorization, and areas of no disturbance are protected from excavation and tunneling for utilities, so the trench most installers would dig first may simply not be allowed. We look for other routes: along the edge of the existing driveway on your own land, through a garage that already has power and conduit from the house, or along an existing path in protective conduit without disturbing the ground. Where no route works, the garage gets its own small cabled cameras and a switch, connected to the house by a point-to-point wireless link aimed between the two buildings. That link needs a clear line of sight and a steady mount, which we test before relying on it. The quote names the route and why it was chosen.

The 2019 Grosvenor Street burglars got in through a balcony window. How do cameras cover upper floors on a large house?

By watching the approach to the balcony and the balcony itself, not just the doors at ground level. NY1 reported that the burglary on Grosvenor Street happened just after 5 p.m. on a Saturday and that the burglars broke a balcony window. On Fieldston's big houses, balconies, sleeping porches, flat roofs over a porch or garage, and terraces with French doors are all possible ways in, and many are reached from a garden that cannot be seen from the street. We map them on the survey: what someone could climb, a porch roof, a trellis, a garden wall, and where they would stand. A camera under the eave above the balcony looks down at it and at the climbing route; a second on the ground floor sees the person arriving. Alerts from those cameras can be set for the hours the upstairs is empty. An early-evening break-in is a reminder that the schedule should cover dusk, not just the middle of the night.

The patrol and your own footage

Can the Association's patrol see our cameras, or get our footage if they need it?

Only if you choose to share, and only in the way you choose. Your camera system and its accounts belong to you; nobody else, including the Association's security contractor, has any access unless you grant it. We do not know the patrol's policy on viewing residents' cameras, and the sources do not describe one, so ask the Association before assuming anything. In practice, most owners keep live access to their own household and share clips on request: if the patrol or the 50th Precinct asks about an incident, you export the relevant clip and send a copy, keeping the original on the recorder. Some recorders and apps allow a guest login limited to one or two cameras, which an owner can give and later revoke. We set up whatever you decide, and show you how to export a clip quickly so a request does not turn into a search.

FAQ

Fieldston Security Camera Installation FAQ

Snow and ice slide off our steep slate roof every winter. Could they knock a camera off the eave?
+

They can if the camera sits at the drip edge or on the fascia board, where sliding snow and ice hit hardest. We mount eave cameras back under the soffit, out of the path of anything coming off the roof, and never on the roof surface itself. The housing is sealed and the cable enters through the back of the mount into the attic, so there is no exposed loop for ice to pull on. We also check gutters and snow guards on the survey, because an overflowing gutter can sheet water and ice across a lens below it.

Our front door sits deep inside an arched stone entry. Will a doorbell camera there show anything but shadow?
+

It can, with the right camera and position. A deep stone entry is dark inside while the garden behind the visitor is bright, which turns faces into silhouettes on an ordinary camera. We use doorbells and entry cameras with wide dynamic range, which balance bright and dark areas, and place them so the visitor faces the lens rather than standing beside it. A small dome in the soffit of the arch can add a downward view. A modest light inside the entry, on your own property, often does as much as a better camera.

Can the rear and indoor cameras go in while we wait for Landmarks to approve the ones on the front?
+

Yes, and that is how we usually plan it. Cameras inside the house and on parts of the exterior that cannot be seen from any street do not change the street face of the house, so they can be installed first, along with the recorder, the cable routes and the network. The street-visible positions are cabled to just inside the wall and left ready, so once LPC staff approve them, fitting the cameras is a short second visit. If staff ask for a change in size, finish or position, nothing already installed has to come out.

The Association uses Excel for its patrol, and Excel also offers alarms. Do our cameras have to come from the same company?
+

No. The Riverdale Press reported that alarm installation and monitoring were available from Excel, the company the Association uses for its patrol and street closures, but nothing requires residents to use the same company for anything. Your cameras can be a separate system you own outright. If you already have an alarm with Excel or another company, ask them what their system can connect to, and we will plan the cameras so an alarm event and a camera clip can be matched by time, or linked where the equipment allows it.

With a patrol already on the streets, do we need professional monitoring, or is watching the cameras ourselves enough?
+

It depends on how often the house is empty and who will respond. Self-monitoring means alerts come to your phones and you decide what to do, with no monthly fee for the camera system itself. Professional monitoring means a central station watches alarm signals and calls you or the police, usually under a contract. Many Fieldston owners keep cameras self-monitored for evidence and peace of mind, and rely on an alarm with professional monitoring for break-ins. The patrol watches the streets, not your alerts. We set up whichever arrangement suits the household.

If the cameras run on PoE, does each one still need its own power cord or outlet?
+

No. PoE, power over Ethernet, sends power and picture down the same network cable from the recorder or a PoE switch, so each camera needs only one cable and no outlet beside it. That is a real advantage on a Fieldston house, where adding outdoor outlets under eaves or on stone walls is both an electrician's job and something Landmarks may notice. The limit is distance: a single run reaches about 100 meters, so very far cameras get a switch or extender part-way, which itself needs power at that point.

Our street belongs to the Association rather than the city. Does that change where a camera may point?
+

It changes whose land things sit on more than where a camera points. The Association owns the streets and, according to the LPC's master plan, often strips of yard and sidewalk beside them, so posts, boxes or trenches near the road need the line confirmed first. For the view itself, we frame cameras on your own house, drive and grounds and the point where your drive meets the road, not down the length of the street or across neighbors' frontages. Keeping views to your own property is good practice on any street, private or public.

Our street curves past the house, so cars appear only at the last moment. How do you aim cameras on a winding street?
+

We aim at the places a vehicle or person has to pass slowly, not at the curve where they flash by. On Fieldston's contour-following streets, that usually means the mouth of your drive, where a car must turn in and slow down, and the spot where cars park near the house. A narrow, zoomed view of the drive entrance captures a car and its occupants at a useful size; a wide view of the bend captures a blur. A second camera on the house picks people up as they walk toward the door.

Will Landmarks accept camera cable running on the outside of a district house, or does it have to be hidden?
+

We plan for it to be hidden, because exposed cable is the kind of thing Rule 2-20's concern about undue attention points at. On most Fieldston houses it can be: cable goes straight through the back of the camera mount into an attic, a soffit or the cellar, and travels inside the house. Where a short exterior run cannot be avoided, it goes in a small conduit or raceway finished to match the wall, in a corner or along trim, and that detail is shown in the application material so staff see exactly what is proposed.

Our dogs have the run of the garden at night. Will they set off the camera alerts constantly?
+

Not if the cameras are set up for it. Many current cameras and recorders can tell people and vehicles from animals and send alerts only for the first two, and we turn that on wherever the equipment offers it. Motion zones can also leave out the lawn where the dogs roam and cover the doors, the drive and the garden gate. The cameras still record everything, so you can check the footage later; it is only the alerts that are filtered, which keeps the phone quiet without leaving the house unwatched.
