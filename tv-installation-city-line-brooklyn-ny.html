The Neighborhood

Why City Line Properties Need TV Installation

City Line takes its name from a border. Before the five boroughs were consolidated in 1898, the edge of the City of Brooklyn met Queens County here, and the neighborhood still sits right against that line, a sub-section of East New York inside Brooklyn Community Board 5. Wikipedia places Cypress Hills to the north and Ozone Park in Queens to the east, with Liberty Avenue as the main shopping street and the A and C trains serving the western edge at Euclid Avenue and Grant Avenue. Definitions of the exact edges vary from one source to the next, and listings on the same block sometimes say East New York, Cypress Hills or even Ozone Park. None of that changes the price: every City Line address is billed at the same Brooklyn rate. What changes the job is the house, and City Line's houses were built in two very different waves.

The first wave is old. City-Data's housing tables for the neighborhood count 3,842 homes built in 1939 or earlier, far more than any later decade, and those are the brick two-families, attached houses and small apartment buildings that line Forbell, Hemlock, Pine and Crescent Streets and the avenues between them. The second wave is recent. The same tables count 1,584 homes built between 2010 and 2019 and another 992 built in 2020 or later, which is a lot of new construction for an area City-Data measures at about half a square mile. In between there is a thinner middle layer, including 1,146 homes from the 1960s. For a TV installer, the gap between those waves matters more than anything else on the block. A wall put up before the Second World War is usually plaster on wood lath over framing, or plaster laid straight onto brick. A wall put up in the last fifteen years is usually drywall, and what sits behind the drywall can be wood studs, light steel or a block wall. The two need different drill bits, different anchors and different plans for the cables.

The buildings themselves have a strong two-family character. City-Data reports that two-unit buildings hold 34.7 percent of City Line's housing, with attached houses at 11.8 percent and fully detached houses at 8.2 percent, and Redfin's City Line market page describes the homes for sale as mostly two-family, three-family and mixed-use buildings, including Liberty Avenue buildings with a store on the ground floor and apartments above. Openigloo lists more than six hundred rated rental buildings in the neighborhood, many of them prewar, on streets such as McKinley Avenue, Sheridan Avenue, Autumn Avenue and Grant Avenue, several of them marked rent-stabilized. In a neighborhood like that, the person who wants the TV on the wall is often not the person who owns the wall. An owner may live downstairs and rent the top floor, a family may rent a whole floor from a relative, and a shopkeeper may lease the ground floor from someone who lives above it. We plan every job with that in mind, because one text to the owner before the visit avoids most arguments after it.

City Line is also one of the places where Bangladeshi New York took root. City Limits reported in 2013 that more than 2,000 Bangladeshis lived in City Line, most of them from Sylhet, and that Forbell Street above Liberty Avenue had been co-named Mizanur Rahman Way. Bklynr's 2014 feature described the community as "centered on Liberty Avenue, in a neighborhood known as City Line," with Bangladeshi-owned bodegas and halal restaurants along the avenue and Masjid Al-Aman on Forbell Street as the largest mosque. The neighborhood is far from one community, though. Wikipedia lists immigrants from Bangladesh, the Dominican Republic, Guyana and Puerto Rico among its residents, and City-Data reports that 9.3 percent of residents speak English "not well or not at all," compared with 7.2 percent for the state. In practice that means many of our City Line customers prefer to show us the wall rather than describe it, which is why the first step on this page is photos, not paperwork, and why our hand-over is done in front of the screen, with you pressing the buttons.

The last thing that shapes a City Line TV job is the street. East of Grant Avenue the A train climbs out of its tunnel and runs on the elevated structure above Liberty Avenue into Queens, so parts of the shopping street sit under steel and shadow, with stores that want screens their customers can see from the door. The blocks to the south include two large public housing developments, the Cypress Hills Houses, opened in 1955 with fifteen seven-story buildings, and NYCHA's East New York City Line development, completed in 1976, whose address list runs along Hegeman Avenue, Fountain Avenue and Logan Street. And the Grant Avenue Municipal Parking Field, which Bklynr reported is rented for Eid prayers, is one of the few large open spaces nearby. When we book, we ask which side of Liberty Avenue you are on, whether there is a driveway or a hydrant in front, and which floor the TV is going to, because a 65-inch carton has to get from the van to your living room before any of the careful wall work can begin.

Six City Line facts that shape a TV job before a bracket is chosen

  • Most of the older housing is prewar. City-Data counts 3,842 homes built in 1939 or earlier. In those houses the first wall we expect is plaster, which is a +$45 add-on on our list because it takes slower drilling, different anchors and care around old cracks.
  • A large share is brand new. 2,576 homes date from 2010 onward. Their drywall looks easy, but it can hide steel framing or a block wall, so we scan and confirm with a hidden pilot hole rather than assume.
  • Two-family houses dominate. Two-unit buildings hold 34.7 percent of homes. In a two-family, the TV wall upstairs is often directly above the owner's living room, and the owner may want to know about the drilling first.
  • Party walls are common. Attached houses make up 11.8 percent of homes, and many two-families are attached too. The long wall in the front room is often the brick wall shared with the house next door, which is a +$65 add-on and a very solid place for a heavy screen.
  • Many renters live in older buildings. Openigloo lists hundreds of rated rental buildings in City Line, several of them rent-stabilized. The lease's alteration clause, not the TV, decides what can go on those walls.
  • Liberty Avenue mixes homes and shops. Redfin listings include buildings with a store on the ground floor and apartments above. A store TV and an apartment TV in the same building can have two different people approving the wall.

Same-Day Repair in City Line

Need Repair in City Line? A Screen Sagging Off Old Plaster, a Bracket in Crumbling Mortar, or a Cord Pushed Through the Wall

Urgent TV calls from older houses come in three familiar shapes, and City Line's prewar stock makes each of them likely. The first is a screen that has started to lean forward or drop on one side. On a plaster wall, that almost always means the bracket was held by anchors meant for drywall, which grip the hard skin of the plaster and nothing behind it. Every time the TV is pulled out on an arm, the plaster around those anchors is crushed a little more, until one day the top of the screen is an inch lower than the bottom. The second is a bracket on brick that was screwed into the mortar joints because they drill more easily. StreetEasy's guide to brick walls quoted the masonry contractor Michael Russo warning that in older buildings "the mortar may be crumbling and [too] unstable" for a heavy item like a TV, and that the better choice is to "drill into the brick, away from the edges." The third is the leftover of someone else's job: an empty bracket, a TV power cord fed into a hole above the screen and out of another near the floor, or a power strip taped to the wall where the old set used to hang.

If a screen is leaning, do not push it back up. If two people can lift it safely, take it off the bracket, unplug it, lay it face up on something soft and call (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list, and putting the same TV back up properly is priced as a new mount on that wall, with the wall add-on that applies. A power cord running inside the wall is not something we leave in place, because the electrical code does not allow it; it comes out, and the power is either brought up behind the screen with an in-wall power and cable kit ($169) where the wall is framed, or run on the surface in a paintable cord cover ($59). If the problem is the wall itself, a long crack through the plaster or bricks loose around the old holes, we say so plainly and stop, so the owner can bring in a plasterer or a mason before anything is hung again.

Building Specialists

TV Installation for City Line's Architecture

Six kinds of building cover nearly every City Line TV call. They differ in three ways that matter: what the wall behind the TV is made of, how the TV gets from the curb to the room, and who besides you has a say about the drilling. Those three answers decide the plan before anybody talks about screen brands.

The prewar two-family

Two stacked apartments with plaster inside and brick on the party wall, the most common City Line home, often with the owner on one floor and a tenant or relatives on the other.

The three-family rental

Three units, sometimes a basement unit too, which Redfin's listings show for sale as income buildings. Each floor may have a different tenant and the owner may live elsewhere.

The attached one-family

A narrow attached house with its long front-room wall shared with the neighbor, usually brick under the plaster, and a stair along the other side.

The small prewar apartment building

Walk-up buildings on the side streets and avenues, several of them rent-stabilized, with a landlord or managing agent, plaster partitions and masonry outer walls.

The Liberty Avenue store with apartments above

A shop on the ground floor and one or two apartments upstairs, often under the elevated A train east of Grant Avenue, with two kinds of TV job in one building.

The house built since 2010

Newer two- and three-family houses and small buildings with drywall inside, where the scanner and a pilot hole decide what really holds the bracket.

The prewar two-family: plaster in front, brick beside, and an owner who may be listening

In a typical City Line two-family from the 1920s or 1930s, the living room runs across the front of each floor, and the wall everyone wants for the TV is the long one opposite the windows. In an attached house, that is usually the party wall shared with the house next door. Under its plaster there is commonly solid brick, which is excellent for a heavy screen as long as the anchors go into the brick and not the mortar. That wall is +$65 on our list. The short walls and the wall beside the stair are usually framed partitions with plaster on wood lath, +$45, where the fixing has to reach the framing behind the plaster or use an anchor rated for hollow plaster. We find out which wall is which with a scanner, a knock test and one small pilot hole where the bracket plate will cover it. The dust tells the story: white powder and then wood means plaster on lath, white powder and then red grit means plaster on brick.

The second thing about a two-family is that the people on the two floors usually know each other well, and often one of them owns the house. If you live upstairs, the owner below will hear the drill, and may well be the person who decides whether the TV goes on the wall at all. A two-line text before the visit, saying which wall, how many holes and whether the wires will go inside the wall or in a cover, settles most of it. If you are the owner setting up the upstairs unit for a tenant or for relatives, choosing one good wall and mounting the TV yourself, with a proper in-wall kit, saves the wall from a new set of holes every time someone moves in.

The three-family rental: three tenants, one owner and the same wall three times

Three-family houses appear regularly among City Line's listings, and they are often bought as income buildings. For a TV, that means three different households, three sets of walls stacked one above the other, and an owner who may live in another neighborhood. The walls themselves follow the age of the house, plaster and brick if it is prewar, drywall if it is newer, but the decision-making is what makes a three-family different. A tenant who calls us for a TV needs the owner's approval for the wall, and an owner who calls us is often trying to stop the cycle of brackets going up and coming down with each lease.

For owners, the cleanest approach is one well-chosen TV wall per unit, mounted by us, with the cables either in an in-wall power and cable kit on a framed wall or in a paintable cord cover on brick, and a line in the lease saying the tenant uses the existing bracket rather than adding their own. Each TV is priced the same way on our list, by wall and by what is added, so three units cost three times one unit, without surprises. For tenants, the simplest path is to send the owner the photo of the wall, the TV size and the cable plan before booking, and to forward us the reply. If the owner says no to drilling, a TV on its stand is $119.99 and leaves nothing to patch.

The attached one-family: the neighbor's wall is your best wall

Attached houses make up close to an eighth of City Line's homes by City-Data's count, and in many of them the front room is narrow enough that only one wall is long enough for a screen: the wall shared with the house next door. That is usually a good thing. A brick party wall gives the strongest fixing in the house, which matters for a 65-inch or larger screen and especially for a full-motion arm that will be pulled out every evening. The care goes into depth and position. We drill only as deep as the anchor needs, we keep every hole in the face of a brick and away from its edges, and we look for what runs along the wall before drilling, such as a chimney flue, a gas line to the kitchen or electrical conduit added in a later upgrade.

The other walls of an attached house are usually framed partitions, and the stair wall in particular often carries the house's wiring between floors. If you would rather hide the cables inside the wall, a framed partition is the only wall in the room that can take an in-wall kit, so the choice is sometimes between the strongest wall and the tidiest wiring. We show you both, with both totals, before deciding. A 55-inch TV on the party wall with a paintable cord cover is $303.99 on our list ($179.99, plus $65 for brick, plus $59). The same TV on a plaster partition with an in-wall kit is $393.99 ($179.99, plus $45, plus $169).

The small prewar apartment building: the landlord's walls and the stairs

Openigloo's list of City Line rental buildings includes many prewar addresses on McKinley, Sheridan, Autumn and Grant Avenues and on Forbell, Hemlock and Pine Streets, and several are marked rent-stabilized. In these buildings the walls between rooms are usually plaster partitions, the walls along the outside and between buildings are masonry, and the stairs are the first obstacle. A big screen in its carton is long and awkward on a narrow stair with a turn at every landing, so if the TV has not arrived yet, have it delivered in its original box and leave the box sealed until we come. It is the safest way to carry it up.

The bigger question in these buildings is permission. Brick Underground's 2022 "Realty Bites" column put the question of wall mounting to New York real estate professionals, and the property manager Arik Lifshitz put it simply: "the real concern is what happens when something is taken down." A nail hole is generally fine, he said, but a larger hole, such as the ones a mounted TV leaves, counts as damage, and "you must fix it before you move out." So in a rental building we always ask what the lease says about alterations, suggest a short written request to the landlord or managing agent, and send a certificate of insurance if they want one. If the answer is no, the TV goes on its stand.

The Liberty Avenue store with apartments above: two jobs, two decisions

Liberty Avenue is City Line's main street, and Redfin's listings show the building type that defines it: a commercial ground floor with one or two apartments above. East of Grant Avenue, the A train runs on the elevated structure over the avenue, so many of these storefronts sit under the el. For a TV installer that building holds two very different jobs. The store wants a screen that can be seen from the counter or the door, mounted out of reach and out of the walking path, and installed before opening or after closing. The apartment upstairs wants a living-room TV like any other, usually on a party wall of brick or a plaster partition.

The decision-making differs too. A shop owner who leases the ground floor needs the building owner's approval for anything fixed to the wall, and that owner may live in one of the apartments upstairs. A tenant upstairs needs the same owner's approval for a different wall. When both jobs come up together, we price each TV from the list on its own, by wall and add-ons, and do them in the same visit if both approvals are in hand. The noisy part, the drilling, happens in short bursts, and we tell the store when it will be so it does not happen during the lunch rush.

The house built since 2010: drywall that hides what is behind it

The 2,576 City Line homes that City-Data dates to 2010 or later include many two- and three-family houses and small apartment buildings put up on lots where older houses once stood. Inside they look easy: smooth drywall, square corners, outlets at modern heights. The catch is what the drywall hides. Depending on the builder, the framing can be wood studs, which take a lag bolt in the usual way, or light-gauge steel studs, which strip a lag bolt and need toggles made for steel. Some walls between units are block or masonry with drywall fixed over them, which looks identical from the room side until a screw spins in a void.

So in a newer house the scanner comes first and the pilot hole decides. A stud finder that reads a steady line every sixteen inches is probably wood; one that reads metal is steel; one that reads nothing for the whole wall often means drywall over block. If it is drywall on wood studs, the job is our base price, $179.99 for a TV up to 55 inches. If it turns out to be something else, you hear what that changes before any more drilling happens. Newer houses are also where an in-wall power and cable kit is most often possible, because the cavities are empty and the walls are framed, which makes a fully hidden install a realistic choice at $169 on top of the mount.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The First Ten Minutes Inside a City Line Front Room

These notes describe how our technicians work in houses like City Line's. They are our standing practice, not a story about any one address.

Look at the room before the wall. Before the scanner comes out, the technician notes which walls face the street, which one is shared with the next house, where the radiator sits and where the outlets are. In a prewar house, an outlet in a metal box screwed to the surface of the wall usually means wiring added later in conduit, and conduit runs somewhere we must not drill. A newer outlet set flush in the wall of an old room tells another story: that wall may have been opened and rebuilt.

Knock, then scan. A knock along the wall at screen height says a lot. Drywall drums. Plaster on lath gives a duller sound with hollow spots between the framing. Plaster on brick gives almost nothing back at all. The scanner then confirms what the knock suggests, but on old plaster a scanner can read the whole wall as solid, so the knock and the next step matter more than the beep.

One pilot hole, hidden. The technician drills a single small hole where the bracket plate will later cover it and reads the dust and the feel of the bit. That one hole decides the anchor. If it changes the add-on from what you booked, for example from plaster to brick, you hear the new number before any more drilling.

Tape, then sit down. Painter's tape marks the outline of the screen. The technician asks you to sit where you usually watch, and checks the height, the glare from the street windows and whether a door or a radiator gets in the way. Moving tape costs nothing; moving a bracket costs a wall.

Ask about the floor below. In a two-family or an apartment building, the technician asks who is downstairs and next door, and whether anyone is sleeping. The drilling is then done in one short burst rather than spread across the visit.

Wall by Wall

Plaster, Brick or Drywall: What Holds a TV on a City Line Wall and What Each One Costs

The base price on our Brooklyn list, $179.99, covers a TV up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs, labor only. In a neighborhood where thousands of homes went up before 1940, that wall is the exception rather than the rule, which is why the wall add-ons come first in every City Line quote. Each one exists because the wall needs different tools, different fixings and more time, not because an older house is an excuse to charge more.

Plaster on wood lath (+$45). This is the classic wall of a prewar Brooklyn house: thin wooden strips nailed across the framing, covered in two or three coats of plaster that together can be close to an inch thick. Plaster is strong when pressed and weak when pulled. A plastic plug in plaster alone, or a screw that bites only into a lath strip, can feel tight on the day and still let go over months, especially under a full-motion arm. The fixing that lasts reaches through the plaster and lath into the framing, drilled slowly so the plaster does not crack outward around the hole, or it uses a toggle rated for the load where the bracket cannot reach a stud. Framing in houses this old is not always on modern spacing, so we find each stud rather than measuring from a corner.

Plaster laid on brick (priced as brick). On party walls and outside walls, prewar plaster is often applied straight to the masonry. A stud finder reads nothing useful there, and a forum thread on Brownstoner about mounting a TV over a fireplace made the same point years ago: many old party walls have no studs at all, just plaster over brick. Once the pilot hole shows red brick dust behind the plaster, the wall is priced at the brick rate.

Solid brick (+$65). A brick wall holds a TV very well if the anchor is set in the brick itself. The mortar between bricks is softer, and in an old wall it can crumble around a fixing. StreetEasy's guide to brick walls quoted the masonry contractor Michael Russo saying exactly that, and adding a line every installer learns early: "You never really know what problems might come up." We use a hammer drill, masonry anchors sized for the bracket and the screen, and holes kept away from the edges of each brick. On soft old brick we spread the load over more fixings rather than trusting a few.

Stone (+$65). Stone facing is rare in City Line living rooms but turns up around fireplaces and in some renovated basements. It is priced the same as brick on our list and drilled the same careful way, with the bit started slowly so the face does not chip.

Drywall over wood studs (base price). Common in houses built or gutted since 2010. Lag bolts into two studs, or more for a large screen or a full-motion arm, and this is the wall where an in-wall power and cable kit fits most easily.

Drywall over something else. In newer buildings, drywall can sit over light steel studs or over a block wall. Neither one is a reason to give up on the wall; both need fixings made for them. When the pilot hole shows either one, the technician tells you what the wall needs and what it adds before going further, and you can choose another wall if you prefer.

Hiding the wires without breaking the electrical code

The request that follows "mount it" is almost always "make the wires disappear," and there is a right and a wrong way to do it. The wrong way is to push the TV's own power cord into a hole behind the screen and pull it out of another hole near the floor. The National Electrical Code does not allow it. EC&M's 2022 Code Q&A on the 2020 code sums up Section 400.12 plainly: flexible cords "are not permitted to be concealed by walls, floors, or ceilings," may not be run through holes in walls, and may not be used as a substitute for the fixed wiring of a building. Gizmodo raised the same point years earlier, after a reader poll in which "over 1/3" of respondents admitted their HDTV cords were hidden behind a wall, and its suggested fix was the one we use: a proper outlet behind the TV.

On a framed wall, that means an in-wall power and cable kit. It puts a recessed power inlet low on the wall that plugs into your existing outlet, carries the power up inside the wall in the kit's own rated cable to a recessed outlet behind the screen, and gives a separate opening for HDMI and other low-voltage cables. It does not connect to your house wiring at all; it simply plugs into the outlet you already have. On our list it is $169. It needs a hollow framed cavity between the two openings, which suits plaster on lath and drywall walls but not brick, block or stone. On those walls, the honest options are a paintable cord cover on the surface ($59), which runs straight down to the console and can be painted to match, or mounting the TV directly above the furniture so the cables drop out of sight behind it.

In a rental, an in-wall kit leaves two neat openings to patch when you move out, and a cord cover leaves a few small screw holes. Either is easy to restore, but either is also something a landlord may want to agree to first.

Renters and Owners

Leases, Owners and Relatives: Mounting a TV When the Wall Belongs to Someone Else

In a neighborhood of two-families, three-families and rental buildings, the question of who decides comes up on a large share of our calls. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. It is what the public guidance says and what we have learned doing this work, so you can ask the right question before booking.

Start with the lease. In Brick Underground's 2022 column, the attorney Steven Kirkpatrick of Romer Debbas said the first thing to check is the lease's alteration provision, and that a restriction that is not in the lease "wouldn't be enforceable." The broker Bill Kowalczuk added that a landlord is "within their rights to ban you from hanging items on walls," and suggested asking permission before installing anything that causes more damage than a nail, such as a TV bracket. If your lease is silent, ask anyway. If it restricts fixtures, ask in writing and keep the reply.

Know what counts as damage. The same column said a nail-sized hole is generally fine, but larger holes, like those a bracket leaves when a TV comes down, are damage you are expected to repair. It also noted that landlords must give an itemized list of any deposit deductions and return the rest within two weeks of move-out. A typical bracket leaves four to six holes about the width of a pencil, all hidden behind the screen while it is up. Patching them before you leave, and photographing the repaired wall, is the simplest way to avoid an argument later.

When the owner is family. In City Line it is common for a household to rent a floor from a relative, or for the owner's parents, siblings or grown children to live on the other floor. Family arrangements are often informal, which makes it easy to skip the conversation. It is still worth a text with the wall, the TV size and the cable plan. Nobody wants a disagreement about holes at the next family dinner.

When you are the owner. If you own the house and rent one or two units, mounting TVs for your tenants on walls you choose, with the wires done properly, protects the walls from a new bracket every lease. Our list prices each TV the same way, and a certificate of insurance can be made out to you.

When the answer is no. A TV on its own stand on a good console is a perfectly proper result. On our list it is $119.99, and a soundbar placed on the console in front is $79. You get the same tidy cables and the same setup, and nothing to patch when you leave.

Compliance notes for City Line businesses and buildings

  • Keep screens out of the walking path. Under the 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design, objects mounted on a wall with their leading edge between 27 and 80 inches above the floor must not stick out more than 4 inches into a circulation path. A full-motion arm in a narrow store aisle or a waiting area can break that rule when pulled out, so in public spaces we use low-profile brackets or mount above 80 inches.
  • No power cords inside walls. Section 400.12 of the National Electrical Code bars flexible cords from being concealed in walls, floors or ceilings or run through holes in walls. Hidden power behind a business screen goes through an in-wall power kit with its own rated cable, or in a cord cover on the surface.
  • Insurance paperwork first. Commercial landlords and boards often want a certificate of insurance naming the owner before work starts. We send it ahead of the visit.
  • Exits and life-safety devices stay clear. We do not mount a screen where it would block an exit sign, a sprinkler head, a fire alarm device or the swing of a door, and cables never cross a route people use to leave.
  • Rated hardware with a margin. Every bracket we install is rated for more than the screen's weight, and on masonry we use anchors rated for the load. A shop screen gets bumped, leaned on and cleaned with a ladder, so it needs that margin more than one at home.

Rooms, walls and what is behind them

Our apartment in an older building on McKinley Avenue is rent-stabilized. Does putting up a TV bracket change anything about our rent or our lease?

A bracket you pay to have installed is your own work, not an improvement the landlord makes to the apartment, so it has nothing to do with how a rent-stabilized rent is set. What it does touch is the lease itself. Most leases have an alteration clause, and that clause, not the stabilization status, decides whether you need the owner's permission to drill. Read it, and if it mentions fixtures, alterations or wall attachments, send the owner or managing agent a short written request with the wall, the TV size and the cable plan, and keep the reply with your lease. When you move out, the holes are yours to patch. Nothing about the TV changes your renewal rights. If the owner says no, a TV on its stand at $119.99 avoids the question entirely.

The breaker panel for our two-family is on the other side of the wall we picked for the TV. Does that rule the wall out?

Not the whole wall, but it rules out the spot directly in front of the panel and the path the wires take out of it. A panel on the far side of a wall usually has a bundle of cables leaving it upward and sideways inside that same wall, and those are exactly the places a bracket screw must never go. Before drilling, the technician looks at the panel from its own side, scans the TV side for metal and live wiring, and moves the bracket sideways if the cables run where the screen was planned. It is also the wall where an in-wall power and cable kit is least welcome, because the cavity is already crowded. A paintable cord cover ($59) or a different wall is usually the better answer, and we will tell you which on the day.

In our small upstairs bedroom, the only clear wall space is above the window. Can the TV go there?

Sometimes, but it is rarely the best place. Above a window there is a header, a beam that carries the wall over the opening; in a prewar brick house it may be a steel or masonry lintel. A bracket can sometimes be fixed into the framing above a header, but a screen up there sits high enough to strain the neck from a bed, faces straight into the room's brightest light from behind, and gets in the way of curtains and blinds. If that is truly the only wall, a tilting bracket angled down toward the bed helps with the height. More often the better choice is a smaller screen on the wall at the foot of the bed, or on a narrow strip of wall beside the closet, even if it means a slightly smaller TV. We tape out each option so you can see it from the pillow before deciding.

Our children share a room with a bunk bed. Where should the TV go so both can see it, and can you lock down what they are allowed to watch?

The screen goes on the wall facing the bunk, centered between the two mattress heights rather than at the usual living-room height, usually on a tilting bracket so the lower bunk can see it without looking straight up. In a children's room we also keep the bracket low-profile, since a full-motion arm sticking out from the wall is something to climb on, and we put every cable in a paintable cord cover ($59) so nothing hangs within reach. For what they can watch, smart TVs and streaming devices have parental controls, PIN codes and kids' profiles, and the technician can set them with you during the visit, using your accounts and your choices. If the TV is already on the wall and only the settings need doing, that is a setup-only visit at $195 for the first hour.

The console under our TV is full, so we want the soundbar mounted above the screen instead of below it. Does that work?

It works, with a trade-off. The practical points are the ones that matter. The soundbar's bracket has to be fixed into the same solid wall as the TV, a few inches above the top edge so the screen can tilt, and the soundbar's cable has to reach the TV without crossing the picture, which on a framed wall means routing it inside the in-wall kit's opening and on brick means a short cord cover. Many soundbars also have a remote sensor and a display, which are harder to see from above. On our list a wall-mounted soundbar is $129 either way.

Shops, restaurants and community spaces

Our community organization holds English classes in two different rooms. Can a TV go on a rolling stand so it moves between them?

Yes, and for a space you share or rent, a rolling stand is often better than a wall mount, because nothing is drilled and the screen goes where the class is. Our shop wants a promotions screen that turns on at opening and off at closing without anyone touching it. Can that be set up?

Usually yes, and there are three common ways to do it. Many TVs have on and off timers in their settings, and a screen playing from a USB stick or a streaming device can be set to come on with the TV and start the loop by itself. Signage players and some streaming devices have their own schedules that wake the screen. Which one works depends on your TV's model, so send us the model sticker. Our corner store has had things go missing. Can the TV be mounted so it cannot simply be lifted off its bracket?

Yes. Most TV brackets hold the screen on hooks that drop over a wall plate, and a set of screws or a latch keeps it from lifting, which stops an accident but not a determined person. For a store, we use a bracket with a locking feature, where the TV cannot be lifted off without a key or a padlock through the latch, and we mount it high enough that nobody can reach it from the floor without a ladder. On masonry walls, the anchors themselves are the strongest part of the job. Brackets are sold separately, so if you are buying one, look for "locking" or "security" in the description and send us the model; if you would like us to supply one, tell us the TV's model number and we will quote it with the installation.

FAQ

City Line TV Installation FAQ

Are rubber pass-through grommets enough to make running the TV's power cord inside a City Line wall legal?
+

No. A grommet only tidies the hole. Section 400.12 of the National Electrical Code bars flexible cords from being concealed in walls or run through holes in them, whatever the opening looks like. Hidden power needs an in-wall power and cable kit with its own rated cable, which is $169 on our list.

Can I book and pay for a TV install at my parents' house in City Line while I am at work somewhere else?
+

Yes. Book and pay online in your name, give us your parents' address and phone number, and tell us who will open the door. The technician calls the number you choose before arriving, and the hand-over is done with whoever will use the TV, in front of the screen.

Can an in-wall power and cable kit go into an outside wall of a newer house that is packed with insulation?
+

Often, but it is slower and not always possible. Insulation fills the cavity the kit's cable has to pass through, and some outside walls also have a vapor barrier we try not to tear. We check the cavity through the top opening first, and if the cable cannot pass cleanly, we suggest an inside wall or a paintable cord cover instead.

The wall we want has the kitchen stove on the other side. Does the heat matter for the TV?
+

Usually not through a full wall, but we check two things first: whether the stove's exhaust duct or gas line runs inside that wall, which decides where the bracket screws may go, and whether the TV side feels warm when the oven has been on for a while. If it does, a different wall is the better choice.

What happens to the TV's feet or pedestal once it goes on the wall?
+

We take them off, bag the screws with them and hand them to you. Keep them with the TV's box or in a closet. If you move to an apartment where you cannot drill, or the TV later moves to a stand in another room, you will need them, and replacement feet are hard to find for older models.

Can our Wi-Fi router sit hidden behind the wall-mounted TV?
+

We advise against it. The metal back of a TV and its bracket block and reflect the router's signal, so the rest of the house gets weaker Wi-Fi, and a router behind a screen has little air around it. A router on a shelf nearby, in the open, works better, with a short cable to the TV if it streams in high resolution.

Our new TV came with a wall bracket in the box. Can you use it instead of buying one?
+

Yes, if it suits the wall and the room. A bracket made by the TV's maker is designed for that model, so the fit is right, but it is usually a fixed bracket with no tilt and comes with screws meant for wood studs. We use the bracket and supply anchors suited to your plaster or brick, at the labor prices on our list.

Can a small TV be mounted on a closet door or a sliding partition to save wall space?
+

We do not mount TVs on doors or moving partitions. Most interior doors are hollow, a moving door swings the TV's weight on its hinges every time it opens, and the power cable would have to cross the gap. A small fixed bracket on the wall beside the door, or a TV on a dresser strapped to the wall, does the same job safely.

Several relatives share our house and each has their own streaming account. Can the TV keep everyone's apps separate?
+

Yes. Most streaming apps and smart TVs support separate profiles, and some TVs support separate user accounts too. During setup, each person signs in with their own password or code on their phone, and the technician arranges the home screen so the apps each person uses most are easy to reach.

Our new TV makes films look strangely smooth, like a soap opera. Can the technician fix the picture settings?
+

Yes. That look comes from motion smoothing, a setting most new TVs turn on by default. During setup we switch it off or lower it, pick a picture mode suited to your room's light, and show you where the setting lives so you can change it back for sports if you prefer.

A boxed-in soffit runs along the top of the wall where we want the TV. Does it limit where the bracket can go?
+

It limits how high the screen can sit and what can be hidden above it. A soffit usually hides a beam, a duct or pipes, so we never fix into it or run cables through it. The bracket goes on the flat wall below, with enough clearance for the TV to be lifted onto and off its hooks.
