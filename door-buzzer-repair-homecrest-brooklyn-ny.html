Every front door in Homecrest has to open in two ways. The first is the one everyone thinks of: a resident upstairs presses a button and the strike lets the latch go. The second is the one people only notice when it is gone: a key, a lever or a mechanical lock that opens the door with no electricity at all. In this neighborhood the second way matters more than most. Homecrest has a growing Jewish population served by quite a few Orthodox synagogues, and residents who keep Shabbat do not use electric releases, fobs or keypads from Friday evening to Saturday night. Power cuts take out the first way for everyone. And the city's housing inspectors write orders about door locks and latches in Homecrest far more often than about intercoms. This page is written around a simple rule we follow on every repair here: fix the electric way in without taking the mechanical one away.

The Neighborhood

Why Homecrest Properties Need Door Buzzer Repair

Homecrest is the strip of southern Brooklyn between Kings Highway to the north, Avenue X to the south, Coney Island Avenue to the west and Ocean Avenue to the east. Some people file it under Sheepshead Bay, and the two run into each other along Avenue U and Avenue X, but the name is old and still used, by the public library branch, the public school and the church that carry it. It belongs to Brooklyn Community District 15, its main ZIP code is 11229, and it is patrolled by the 61st Precinct, whose station house is at 2575 Coney Island Avenue. The B and Q trains of the Brighton line run down the middle of it, stopping at Kings Highway, Avenue U and Neck Road, and the B3, B7, B36, B49, B68 and B82 buses cross it. The 2010 census counted 44,316 people on about 688 acres.

The neighborhood's reputation is that it is mostly quiet residential streets between a few busy shopping corridors, and the city's property file bears that out. Kings Highway is the big shopping street along the northern edge. Coney Island Avenue and Avenue U carry stores too, and Avenue U between Coney Island Avenue and Ocean Avenue has grown since the late 1990s into southern Brooklyn's second Chinatown, with food markets, bakeries, restaurants and electronics shops. A broker quoted by Brick Underground in 2022 described walking down one block and hearing Russian, Hebrew, Italian, Spanish, Chinese, Greek and Pakistani. Homecrest was long an Italian neighborhood, and it has since drawn residents from China, Russia and the other former Eastern bloc countries, Israel, Syria, Latin America and South Asia. Orthodox synagogues, both Ashkenazic and Sephardic, serve a growing Jewish population.

Those communities live in a mix of buildings that a door technician has to read block by block. When we counted the lots between Kings Highway, Ocean Avenue, Avenue X and Coney Island Avenue in MapPLUTO on October 22, 2026, we found 3,097 lots with homes on them and 13,610 homes in all. Most lots are small: 1,158 one-family houses and 1,312 two-family houses. But the homes are concentrated in big buildings. The 95 elevator apartment buildings in the area hold 6,351 homes, 47 percent of the total, and most of them are the same type: six stories, brick, with a lobby, an elevator and one heavy street door. Fifty-three of them went up before 1946, mostly between the late 1920s and 1941; another 34 went up between 1946 and 1979, most of them in a wave around 1960 to 1964. Twenty-four are cooperatives holding 1,893 apartments. Since 2000 a third layer has arrived: condominiums and small elevator buildings, the biggest of them seven to nine stories on Ocean Avenue. In the early part of this century, as the Wikipedia article on the neighborhood puts it, many condominiums were built, while many two-story houses were rebuilt as mini-mansions.

Each of those layers has a different front door, and each one fails differently. In our experience the prewar six-story buildings usually have heavy wood or steel doors with mortise locks and strikes that have been replaced several times over; the buildings of around 1960 often have aluminum and glass entrances whose lock bodies are now some sixty years old; and the newest condos often have glass doors held by magnets and opened by fob, keypad or app. The houses have a bell and perhaps a porch release on a transformer in the cellar. A good repair in Homecrest starts by working out which of these it is looking at, and whether the door still has a way in that does not need electricity.

The Second Way In

Why Every Homecrest Door Repair Is Judged on Two Paths

Most buzzer repairs are judged on one question: when someone presses the button upstairs, does the door open? That is the electric path, and it runs from the button through the wire in the building's riser, through a transformer or power supply, to an electric strike in the door frame that lets the latch go. It is the path that fails most often and the one we spend most of our time on. But a street door has a second path that does not involve the intercom at all: a key turning a cylinder, or a lever turning a lock body, or a code entered on a mechanical push-button lock, pulling the latch back by hand. The two paths meet only at the latch.

In most neighborhoods the second path is a backup that people forget about until the power fails. In Homecrest it is in daily use. Residents who keep Shabbat, in a neighborhood with quite a few Orthodox synagogues, do not press an intercom release, tap a fob, enter a code on an electronic keypad or open a door that triggers an electric lock between Friday evening and Saturday night, or on the major holidays. A rabbinic question-and-answer site, Din Online, put the problem plainly in June 2026: a buyer had found an apartment they loved, but every entrance used an electronic tag. The answer explained that a door with an ordinary intercom strike can often be opened with a regular key, or with a mechanical latch set before Shabbat, and suggested checking this during the week by turning off the power to the intercom and lock and seeing whether the door still opens by hand. Electromagnetic locks were described as problematic, and a time switch that releases them for certain hours needs the neighbors' agreement.

We are not rabbis and this page gives no religious guidance; practice differs, and residents ask their own rabbi. What we can do is make sure the hardware gives a building a choice. That means three things on every Homecrest door we touch. First, we test both paths before we start, so we know whether the key works today. Second, we do not repair the electric path in a way that removes the mechanical one, for example by swapping a strike and lockset for a magnet, or by fitting a reader in place of the key cylinder. Third, when a building asks for a way in that needs no electricity, we offer ways that keep the door closing and locking, rather than a taped latch or a door left on the latch for twenty-five hours.

The rule is not only about Shabbat. A power cut takes out the electric path for everyone, and the city's housing inspectors have been writing about exactly this in Homecrest for twenty years. Since 2006 HPD has issued 17 orders at 10 buildings in these blocks to remove an “unacceptable electromagnetic locking device” from a building entrance or vestibule door, each one written up as an illegal fastening and most of them in the immediately hazardous Class C. It has issued six orders at six buildings to “provide security in the event of a power outage by means of an acceptable heavy-duty lock and latch set”, most recently in October 2023. And in December 2025 and March 2026 it wrote six orders at one building for magnetic holders and a magnetic stopper keeping fire stair doors open. Those are the city's own words for the problem this page is about: a door that depends entirely on electricity can fail in both directions, stuck shut or held open.

What breaks the second path

  • A magnet in place of a strike. A magnetic lock holds a door shut only while it has power. On its own it has no keyway, so a building that converts to one often loses the key, and when the power fails the door is simply unlocked.
  • A reader in place of the cylinder. Fob and app systems are often fitted by removing the outside cylinder of the old lock and covering the hole. The door then opens only for the reader.
  • A fail-safe strike on a door that should stay locked. Fail-safe strikes unlock when power is lost. Allegion's guidance describes fail-secure strikes as the ones that stay locked, with the lever still free on the inside.
  • A seized lock body. Many old mortise locks are never operated by key any more, because everyone uses the buzzer. After years without use the cylinder or latch retractor seizes, and nobody finds out until the day it is needed.
  • A taped or wedged latch. It gives a way in without electricity, but it also leaves the door open to everyone, and an HPD inspector will treat it as a door that does not close and lock.

What keeps it

For most Homecrest lobbies the answer is the arrangement the buildings were built with: a mortise lock that a key operates from outside and a lever from inside, with a fail-secure electric strike in the frame for the buzzer. A strike repair keeps that intact. Where a building has already moved to fobs, a keyed cylinder can usually be restored alongside the reader. Where residents want a way in without a key, a mechanical push-button lock that sets its own code by hand can be added to the door, and it works with no power at all. And where a building has a magnetic lock it wants to keep, a keyed lock on the same door keeps the second path. We explain each of these in the questions below, and the price lines they fall under are in the pricing section.

The Public Record

What the City's Files Say About Homecrest Front Doors

Before we price a repair in a multiple dwelling, we look the building up in the city's open data. For this page we did that for the whole neighborhood, on October 22, 2026, using HPD's violation file and the 311 service request file, and keeping only records that fall inside Kings Highway, Ocean Avenue, Avenue X and Coney Island Avenue and belong to Community Board 15. Three things stand out, and each one shapes how we work here.

The intercom record is thin

Since 1990 HPD has written 92 violations about a broken or defective intercom, bell or buzzer in these blocks, at 46 buildings. That is not many for 13,610 homes. Sixty-nine were Class B and 23 Class A; none were Class C. Thirty-one are still listed as open, some of them decades old, which usually means the paperwork was never closed rather than that the fault is still there. The yearly totals are small and uneven: eight in 2009, ten in 2018, two in 2021, eight so far in 2026. Complaints to 311 tell the same story. From January 2020 to October 22, 2026, these blocks produced 84 complaints filed under bell, buzzer or intercom, between four and twenty a year.

A thin record does not mean the systems are in good shape. A large share of Homecrest's apartments are in cooperatives and condominiums, where a shareholder or owner with a dead handset calls the managing agent or the board rather than the city, and where HPD rarely has reason to inspect. The record is a list of what tenants reported and inspectors found, not a survey. That is our reading of the numbers, not something the data proves.

The door record is not thin

Set the intercom numbers beside what HPD has written about the doors themselves. In the same blocks the file holds 1,580 violations that mention a self-closing door, 1,291 of them Class C, at as many as 155 buildings, and 342 about a lock at a building entrance or vestibule door. The order to repair a self-closing door is an immediately hazardous Class C violation, which HPD expects to be corrected within fourteen days of the notice. Most of these are about doors that do not close or do not lock, which is the mechanical half of the entrance.

The record already names the second path

The most useful entries for this page are the rarest. Seventeen times since 2006, at ten buildings, inspectors have ordered the removal of an “unacceptable electromagnetic locking device” from an entrance or vestibule door, calling it an illegal fastening. Six times, at six buildings, they have ordered the owner to “provide security in the event of a power outage by means of an acceptable heavy-duty lock and latch set”, in three cases specifying that it be mechanical. All six were written on the same day as an order to remove a magnetic lock at the same building. Read together, they describe the repair we recommend: a magnet on its own is not an acceptable way to secure a Homecrest apartment-house door, and whatever electric release a building has, the door also needs a lock and latch that work with no power.

We read a building's open violations before we quote, and we tell the owner or managing agent which of them our work should clear and which it will not. A violation for an electromagnetic lock is not cleared by fixing the intercom. It is cleared by changing the door's hardware.

What We Install

Door Buzzer Repair Services in Homecrest

Every service below is a repair to an existing entrance. Where a building needs a new system, that is a separate quote on our installation pages. Each one is done with the second path in mind: the door still opens by key or code when we leave.

Strike repair that keeps the key

Replacing a burnt-out or misaligned electric strike in a mortise-lock lobby door, with the keeper set so the latch drops cleanly, and the key and inside lever tested with the release power switched off.

Restoring a lost key cylinder

Where a fob reader or app panel was fitted by removing the outside cylinder, we restore a keyed cylinder on the same lock where the lock allows it, so the reader and the key both open the door.

Magnetic lock corrections

For doors HPD has written up for an electromagnetic lock, we fit a heavy-duty lock and latch set with an electric strike, or keep the magnet for a board that wants it and add a keyed lock beside it, with the release and exit device tested.

Mechanical push-button locks

A code lock that works entirely by hand, added to a street or synagogue side door so residents, staff or guests can come in without a key and without electricity. The door still closes and locks behind them.

Riser and supply repairs in six-story buildings

Tracing a dead line of apartments on a six-floor riser, re-terminating pairs at the cellar box, and replacing failed transformers or power supplies, with every pair labelled at both ends.

Handsets and buttons

Replacing a dead or crackling apartment station in a co-op or rental, matched to the system on the wire, and replacing worn release buttons in apartments, offices and shops.

Lobby panels

Repairing or re-facing the call panel in the vestibule, replacing broken buttons and directory windows, and resealing the panel where weather gets in.

House bells and porch releases

Bells, chimes and porch-door releases in one-, two- and three-family houses, including the one-story brick rows south of Avenue V and the large rebuilt houses on the side streets.

Door closers and latches

Adjusting or replacing the closer so the lobby door closes and latches on its own from full open, which is what the self-closing door rule requires, charged at our hourly rate where it is not a list line.

1920s four-story walk-ups

43 walk-up apartment buildings, most of them four stories and built in the 1920s, with 939 homes, on East 12th, East 15th, East 17th and East 18th Streets and Homecrest Avenue. One stair, one riser, a cellar transformer and a vestibule door.

Two- and three-family houses

1,312 two-family lots and 293 three-family lots. The bells, the porch door and the cellar transformer are the owner's, and a three-family house counts as a multiple dwelling under the state's law, so the state's entrance-door rules reach it.

One-family houses and rebuilt houses

1,158 one-family lots, from wood-frame houses of the 1900s to the large houses rebuilt in recent decades on the side streets. A rebuilt house may have a video station, a gate bell and more than one entrance.

One-story rows south of Avenue V

Below Avenue V the Wikipedia account describes bungalow-type houses and rows of one-story brick homes with shared backyards and driveways. Bells there are simple, but the door people actually use is often at the side or rear.

Stores with apartments above

About 230 store-and-apartment lots, most built before 1946, on Kings Highway, Avenue U and Coney Island Avenue. The apartments' street door sits beside the shop window and the wire usually runs through the store's ceiling or cellar.

Method by building type

  • Prewar lobby door. We take the cylinder out and check that the key still works the lock body before touching the strike. A mortise lock that has not been operated by key in years is cleaned or replaced first, because a new strike on a seized lock leaves the door with one path.
  • 1960s aluminum entrance. The lock is usually a narrow-stile type in a thin door. We check the latch throw and the strike keeper together, because a sagging aluminum door pushes its latch onto the keeper and makes a working strike look dead.
  • Elevator co-op. We work through the managing agent, give the board each line of the price separately, and leave a written note of how the door opens with the power off, which the board can post in the lobby.
  • Condo with a magnet. We test whether the door has any keyed lock at all. If it does not, we explain the HPD orders on these blocks before suggesting anything, and price both choices: a strike and lockset, or a keyed lock added beside the magnet.
  • Four-story walk-up. One riser serves the whole building, so a fault low in the riser silences everyone above it. We test from the top apartment down, and check the vestibule door's lock and latch with the release power off.
  • Two- and three-family house. The transformer is usually in the cellar near the boiler. We measure it under load, check every button, and confirm the porch door opens by key; in a three-family house the street door also has to close and lock by itself.
  • Store with apartments above. We separate the residents' circuit from anything the shop has added, and agree with the shopkeeper when the shared cellar or ceiling can be opened.

Rules that apply to Homecrest entrances

  • Self-locking doors and intercoms. The state Attorney General's tenants' guide reads Multiple Dwelling Law § 50-a this way: a multiple dwelling put up after January 1, 1968 needs entrance doors that close and lock by themselves, plus a two-way voice intercom with a door release once it has eight or more apartments; an older one owes residents the self-locking doors and the intercom when most of its apartments ask, and its owner can recover what they cost. Most Homecrest apartment buildings are older than 1968.
  • Self-closing doors. HPD treats a defective self-closing door as a Class C immediately hazardous violation, to be corrected within fourteen days of the notice (Housing Maintenance Code § 27-2041.1).
  • Electromagnetic locks at entrances. HPD inspectors in these blocks have repeatedly written up magnetic locks on entrance and vestibule doors as illegal fastenings and ordered a heavy-duty lock and latch set for power outages. Any magnetic lock that stays must release for egress, and on a door that is a required exit, the building code's conditions for sensor-released and electrically locked egress doors apply.
  • Fire stair doors. Holding a fire stair door open with a magnet that is not part of an approved fire alarm release has been cited in Homecrest as an illegal fastening and, in 2026, as a Class C self-closing violation.
  • Reach and access. New call buttons and releases we fit are kept within the 48-inch high forward reach in the 2010 ADA Standards wherever the door and wall allow.
  • Line-voltage work. Low-voltage repairs are ours. Work on the 120-volt circuit that feeds a transformer or power supply is done by a licensed electrician under a Department of Buildings permit where one is required.
Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Repair in Homecrest: Questions Answered

These are the questions we expect from Homecrest owners, boards, managing agents and residents, grouped by the kind of building they come from. Each one is answered here once and is not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Keys, Shabbat and the Second Way In

Since our co-op's lobby door was converted to fobs, the brass key no longer opens it. Can a key cylinder be put back without removing the reader?

Usually, yes. When a fob reader is added to an old lobby door, the installer often takes the outside cylinder out of the mortise lock, covers the hole with a blank plate, and lets the reader drive the electric strike. The lock body inside the door is normally still there. If it is, a new cylinder keyed to the building can go back into it, and the door then opens three ways: by fob, by the buzzer from upstairs, and by key. The reader and the strike are not touched.

If the installer replaced the whole lock with a storeroom-function lever set that has no cylinder, or with a magnet, the job is larger. We price a lock body that accepts a cylinder, or a keyed lock beside the magnet. Before we start we take the plate off and look, and the price is written down before any work begins. A key path is also what HPD's power-outage orders in these blocks ask for.

Several residents keep Shabbat and have asked the board to leave the street door unlocked from Friday evening to Saturday night. What can we do instead that keeps the door locking?

A door left on the latch for a day and a night is a door that anyone can walk through, and an inspector who finds it that way will write it up, so most boards look for another answer. There are three common ones, and a building can use more than one.

The simplest is the key: if the lobby door has a working mortise lock with a cylinder, residents can open it by key with no electricity involved, and the strike stays locked. Some residents carry a key on a Shabbat belt, as the Chabad.org guide to doors on Shabbat describes. The second is a mechanical push-button lock on the street door, which opens with a code entered by hand and needs no power. The third is a staffed door, where a building has a doorman or porter. Whether any of these is acceptable for a particular family is a question for their rabbi, not for us. Our part is to make sure the door can do it, and to keep the door closing and locking whichever choice the board makes.

Would a mechanical push-button combination lock on our street door get in the way of the intercom release?

No, if it is fitted properly. A mechanical push-button lock is a complete lock of its own, with its own latch or a cylinder that works the existing latch. Entering the code withdraws the latch by hand. The electric strike in the frame does not care how the latch was withdrawn: the buzzer still releases the strike, and the code still works the latch, so the two ways in sit side by side.

The fitting is what matters. The code lock has to suit the thickness and backset of the door, its latch has to line up with the strike keeper, and on an aluminum door with a narrow stile there may be no room for one without a different lock body. We check those before we price it. The code should be changed when a resident moves out, which on many mechanical models is done at the lock itself.

We switched the intercom power off as a test. The key turned, but the door still would not open. What does that point to?

It tells us the key is working the cylinder but the latch is not being freed, and there are four usual causes. The first is a lock body that has seized inside, so the cylinder turns without pulling the latch back; this is common in lobby doors whose locks have been operated by the buzzer alone for years. The second is a second lock: a magnet or a deadbolt somewhere on the door that the key does not control. The third is a door that has dropped onto its latch, so the latch is pinned against the strike keeper and cannot slide even when the lock pulls on it. The fourth is a strike installed upside down or with its keeper jammed.

Each one is quick to tell apart on site: we pull the door toward the hinges while the key is turned, look at the latch with the strike plate off, and check the door for anything else holding it. Write down which doors you tested and what you heard, because a seized lock and a sagging door are fixed very differently.

Our building's Shabbat timer holds the strike released through Saturday. Who looks after it, and what happens if it sticks?

A timer that powers a strike open for set hours is part of the door circuit, so it is the building's equipment and the owner's or board's responsibility, like the strike itself. It should be on the building's records with the times it is set for and who may change them. Din Online's June 2026 answer noted that putting a door on a time switch for certain hours needs the neighbors' agreement, which in an apartment building means the board or owner deciding it, not one resident.

The weak point is what happens afterwards. If the timer fails or its clock drifts, the door can stay unlocked into Sunday, or lock early on Friday. We check the timer's setting, the relay it drives and the strike's duty rating, since a strike held open for a whole day runs hotter than one buzzed for a few seconds. We would also point out that a timer on a fail-safe strike leaves the door unlocked for the whole period, which an inspector may treat as a door that does not lock, so the board should decide it with that in view.

Six-Story Co-ops, Rentals and New Condos

Our co-op is modernizing its elevator and contractors are in and out for weeks. How do we keep the lobby door working and secure during the job?

The usual mistake is to wedge the lobby door open for the trades. That leaves the building open and the closer and strike under strain. A better arrangement is to give the elevator contractor a key to the lobby lock, or a temporary code on a push-button lock, so the door can close and lock behind them. If the trades need the door open for deliveries, someone from the contractor holds it, and it is shut again afterwards.

Before the work starts we can check the door's closer, strike and lock, label the intercom riser where it passes the shaft or the machine room, and photograph the panel and the cellar box. Modernization crews sometimes disturb low-voltage wire near the shaft, and a labelled riser makes it quick to find a cut pair if part of the building goes quiet. At the end of the job we test every apartment from the lobby.

A shareholder wants the fob-only entry the condo down the street has, and the board wants to keep keys. What does each choice cost to keep running?

A keyed lobby door with a repaired audio intercom has no running cost beyond repairs: strikes, transformers and handsets wear out and are replaced at our list prices, and a cylinder can be rekeyed when keys go missing. A fob system adds fobs to buy and program, a controller to maintain, and often a software charge; app-based systems commonly charge per apartment per month.

The other cost is less visible. A fob-only door gives residents who keep Shabbat no way in on Saturdays, and gives everyone no way in during a long power cut unless the system has battery backup that is tested. A common compromise is both: fobs for convenience and a keyed cylinder for the times fobs cannot be used. We can price the repair of the existing door, the fob addition, and the keyed backup as separate lines so the board can see each one.

When the strike fails on the original lobby door of our 1960s co-op, should the lock in the door be replaced at the same time?

Not automatically. A strike can fail on its own: its coil burns out, its keeper wears, or its mounting loosens. If the lock body is sound, a new strike on the old lock is the cheaper repair, and it keeps the cylinder and keys the building already has.

We test the lock before deciding. With the release power off we work the key and the inside lever, watch the latch retract fully, and check how far the latch throws into the strike. If the latch drags, the cylinder is loose, or the lock body is cracked, a new strike will not last, and we say so before quoting. On narrow aluminum doors from around 1960, replacement lock bodies of the same size can be hard to find, so a worn lock sometimes means changing the lock and strike as a pair. Either way, you see both prices before choosing.

Houses, Synagogues and Side Streets

Our synagogue's side door is buzzed open on weekdays, but on Shabbat a volunteer has to stand there. Is there a way to secure that door without an electric release on Saturdays?

Yes, and the answer is usually mechanical. The weekday buzzer can stay exactly as it is, on its strike. For Shabbat the door can be given a mechanical push-button lock, so members who know the code let themselves in by hand, or a keyed lock for the people who hold keys. Either way the door closes and locks behind each person, which a door propped or taped open does not.

A door used as an exit by a congregation has to open from inside without a key, code or special knowledge, so whatever we fit on the outside, the inside stays a plain lever or a push bar. If the side door is a required exit with a magnetic lock, the exit rules for electrically locked doors apply as well. Your rabbi decides what is permitted; we make sure the hardware offers the choice and still meets the exit rules.

We are turning our two-family on a Homecrest side street into one large house. What happens to the second bell and its wiring?

The second bell's button, chime and wire can come out, or they can be kept and given a new job. Many owners who rebuild a two-family as one house end up with more entrances than before: a front door, a side door by the driveway, sometimes a gate. The old second circuit is often the easiest way to put a bell at one of those, because the wire already runs to the cellar transformer.

If the house is being opened up, it is the cheapest moment to decide. We can test the old wire before the walls close, tell you whether it is worth keeping, and mark it for the contractor. Old bell wire inside walls is often cloth- or thin-jacketed, and a new run during the renovation costs much less than a repair after it. A large rebuilt house may also want a video station in place of a plain bell; that is a separate installation, not a repair.

HPD Orders About Magnets

HPD cited our building for magnetic door holders on the stair doors. Are those the same magnets as the lobby door lock?

No. A magnetic door holder is a small magnet on the wall that holds a stair or corridor door open, usually so it should release when the fire alarm sounds. A magnetic lock is a much stronger magnet at the top of a door that holds it shut. They look similar and both run on low voltage, but they do opposite jobs.

In these blocks HPD wrote five orders in December 2025 at one building for magnetic door holders on the fire stair doors, one on each floor from the second to the sixth, and a Class C order in March 2026 for a permanently installed magnetic stopper on a sixth-floor fire door. A fire stair door has to close by itself. A holder that never lets go, or that is not tied to a fire alarm release, keeps it open, which is why the inspector calls it an illegal fastening. Removing those holders is not intercom work, but we can take them out and close up the wall while we are on site, and tell you what an approved, alarm-released holder would involve.

An inspector wrote up the magnetic lock on our vestibule door as an “illegal fastening.” Can a magnetic lock ever stay on a Homecrest apartment-house door?

The orders in these blocks give the city's answer for this situation: in 17 orders at 10 buildings since 2006, inspectors called an electromagnetic lock on an entrance or vestibule door an unacceptable illegal fastening and ordered it removed, and at six buildings they also ordered a heavy-duty lock and latch set to provide security in a power outage. So the dependable way to clear the order is to remove the magnet and secure the door with a lock and latch set and an electric strike for the buzzer.

Some buildings want to keep a magnet for a reason, such as a glass door that cannot take a strike. We do not decide that for you. We can price the strike-and-lockset repair that matches what the orders ask for, and, separately, what keeping the magnet with a keyed lock, an exit device and a fire alarm release would involve, and the owner can take the second option up with HPD or its own expeditor before choosing it.

Equipment

Types of Door Buzzer Hardware We Repair in Homecrest

A Homecrest entrance is a set of parts that have to agree with each other. These are the ones we repair or replace most, and what each means for the second way in.

Mortise locks and cylinders

The lock body inside a heavy lobby door, worked by a key from outside and a lever or knob from inside. It is the mechanical path in most prewar and postwar Homecrest lobbies, and the part most often neglected.

Fail-secure electric strikes

The strike that stays locked when power is lost, while the inside lever still lets people out. Our usual replacement on a lobby door that has a keyed lock.

Fail-safe electric strikes

Strikes that unlock when power is lost. They suit some interior doors; on a street door they leave the building open in a blackout, so we fit them only when the building asks for that behaviour knowing what it means.

Magnetic locks and exit sensors

Electromagnets that hold a door shut while powered, released by a push button or a motion sensor on the inside. Where one stays, it needs a keyed lock beside it, an exit device and a fire alarm release.

Mechanical push-button locks

Code locks that work entirely by hand, with no batteries or wiring. They give a way in without a key or electricity and still lock the door when it closes.

Door closers

The arm at the top of the door that pulls it shut and holds it against the latch. A weak or mis-set closer leaves a working strike unable to lock the door.

Transformers and power supplies

The 16- to 24-volt bell transformer in a house cellar, or the power supply and relay in a building's cellar box. When every apartment goes quiet at once, these are tested first.

Lobby panels and apartment handsets

The call panel in the vestibule and the station on each apartment wall, from audio handsets to video monitors. Matched to the system already on the riser.

Time switches

Clocks or controllers that hold a release on for set hours. Part of the door circuit when a building uses one, and checked with the strike it drives.

Field Notes: Turn the Power Off Before You Pick Up a Tool

This is our standard method for a Homecrest entrance, written as a method rather than an account of a particular job. The first test at a lobby door is done with nothing powered. The technician switches off the release circuit and tries the door the way a resident without electricity would: key from outside, lever from inside, code lock if there is one. In a building where everyone has used the buzzer for years, this is often the first time in a long while that anyone has turned the key, and it tells us more than any meter. A key that turns stiffly says the lock body needs attention. A key that turns freely while the latch stays put says the lock has seized inside or the door has sagged onto the latch. No keyway at all says the second path has already been lost.

Only then does the power go back on and the electric side get tested: supply under load, strike at rest and under the buzzer, panel, riser, apartments. When we finish, both tests are repeated in the same order, and the note we leave says what the key does, what the buzzer does, and what we changed. A repair that passes the second test and fails the first is not finished.

If the lobby key cylinder is replaced, does every resident need a new key?
+

Not necessarily. A new cylinder can usually be keyed to match the building's existing key, so residents keep the keys they have. That is the right choice if the old cylinder simply wore out. If keys have gone missing or the building has lost track of who holds them, a new key is the better choice, and the board will want to plan how keys are handed out, especially to residents who rely on the key rather than the buzzer on Saturdays. How can we tell whether our lobby strike stays locked or unlocks when the power fails?
+

The power-off test answers it in a minute. Switch off the release circuit, close the door, and push it from outside without using a key. If the door stays shut, the strike is fail-secure and the door depends on its key when the power is out. If it swings open, the strike is fail-safe, or there is no strike and the door was held by a magnet, and the building is unlocked whenever the power fails. Most strikes also carry a small marking for fail-secure or fail-safe on the body, which we read when we take the strike out.

Some lever locks can be set to stay unlocked. How do we stop the lobby lock from being left that way?
+

Many commercial locks have a hold-back or a function that keeps the latch retracted, set by a button, a thumb-turn or a key. On a lobby door that is a convenience for deliveries and a risk the rest of the time, because a door left in that state closes but does not lock. If residents keep leaving it set, the simplest fix is a lock function without the hold-back, or one that can only be set with a key held by the super. We can change the function when we work on the lock, and the board decides who, if anyone, should be able to set it.
