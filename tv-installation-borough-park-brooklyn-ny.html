The Neighborhood

Why Borough Park Properties Need TV Installation

Borough Park did not stop being built when its first houses went up. The land between Green-Wood Cemetery and Bensonhurst was laid out as a suburb in the first years of the twentieth century, when State Senator William H. Reynolds bought farmland next to the older hamlet of Blythebourne in 1902 and gave it the name that stuck. Brick row houses, semi-attached two-families and small apartment buildings filled the grid over the following decades. That much is ordinary Brooklyn history. What is not ordinary is what happened next. According to Wikipedia's summary of city records, the Department of Buildings has issued more private construction permits for new homes and additions in Borough Park since 1990 than in any other residential neighborhood in the borough, and a special zoning district created in 1992 allowed buildings to cover 65 percent of a lot, shrinking setbacks and back yards so families could build out.

The reason is the size of the households. The city's 2018 Community Health Profile, quoted in NYC Food Policy's Borough Park Foodscape, counted children under eighteen as 34 percent of the district's population, and Maimonides Medical Center on the neighborhood's edge runs the birthing center that delivers more babies than any other hospital in New York State. Large families need large kitchens, large dining rooms and more bedrooms, and many have found them by pushing the back wall of the house out, adding floors on top, or both. The Brooklyn Eagle described one such job in 2016 at 1311 44th Street, where an existing two-story, two-family house was being more than doubled to 5,633 square feet and turned into a five-story building for four families, with workers hoisting steel beams into place on the day the reporter walked by. Multiply that by the permits filed over thirty-five years and you get blocks where the house next door, and sometimes the back half of your own house, is less than twenty years old.

For a TV installer, that history changes the first question on every visit. In most Brooklyn neighborhoods we can guess the wall from the age of the building: plaster in a 1920s walk-up, drywall in a 2015 condo. In Borough Park the age of the building is not one number. The front rooms may sit behind a 1920s brick facade with plaster laid straight onto the masonry, while the living room that was added at the back may be framed in steel, built of concrete block, or finished with drywall glued directly onto block. The old rear wall of the house may now stand in the middle of the floor as an interior wall of solid brick. Each of those holds a bracket in a different way, and a stud finder that behaves perfectly on one side of the room may be useless three feet away. We price and plan the job by the wall the screen actually goes on, and in this neighborhood that often means two different answers in one room.

The second thing that sets Borough Park apart is what the screen is for. Many households here do not keep a television at all. Meyer Labin, who grew up in the Satmar community in Williamsburg, wrote in JTA in 2020 that "there are virtually no televisions in Hasidic households, as well as very limited access to the internet," and BKLYNR's 2013 report on technology in Hasidic Brooklyn described a Borough Park company, Xchange Telecom, building a filtered internet service for the community. Borough Park is more varied than that description: Wikipedia notes that a 2002 UJA-Federation study found nearly three quarters of the neighborhood's Jewish residents identified as Orthodox, which leaves many who do not, and census figures in the same article count sizeable Asian and Hispanic populations, especially on the blocks toward Sunset Park and Fort Hamilton Parkway. So we hang plenty of family televisions here. But a large share of the screens we put on Borough Park walls are something else: a zmanim and minyan-times board in a shul, a monitor for the security cameras in a store, a screen in an office conference room, a display in a guest suite, or a large monitor at the dining-room table for shiurim and family video calls. The mounting is the same craft; the setup afterward is very different, and we ask what the screen is for before we ask how big it is.

The third thing is the calendar. Thirteenth Avenue, the main shopping street, runs for about a mile from 39th to 55th Street, and 16th and 18th Avenues are busy commercial streets too. Many of the stores, offices and institutions on them close early on winter Fridays and stay closed for Shabbos and Yom Tov, and the weeks before Pesach and Sukkos are the busiest of the year for anyone who works inside homes. A screen for a store or a shul office is best fixed when the doors are closed to customers, and a family that is painting, cleaning or hosting may want the TV work done well before a Yom Tov rather than during it. We book around that, and we finish Friday jobs with time to spare when you ask.

Six Borough Park facts that shape a TV job before we pick an anchor

  • More additions than anywhere else in Brooklyn. Wikipedia, citing city permit records, says Borough Park has received more private permits for new homes and additions since 1990 than any other residential neighborhood in the borough. Expect a wall from a different decade on each side of the room.
  • Lots that are mostly building. The 1992 special zoning district let buildings cover 65 percent of a lot. Deep houses with few windows on the sides leave long solid walls, which is good for a screen, but the long walls are often shared with the neighbor.
  • A third of residents are children. The 2018 Community Health Profile put children at 34 percent of the population. A TV that cannot tip, an arm that cannot be swung on and cords out of reach matter more here than in most places.
  • Many screens are not televisions. Shuls, yeshiva offices, stores and family offices use wall screens for times, camera views, presentations and video calls. The setup has to fit the use, including filtered or offline sources.
  • Commercial streets with their own week. Thirteenth Avenue from 39th to 55th Street, plus 16th and 18th Avenues, carry most of the shops and offices. Display work is booked before opening, after closing or on the days the business is shut.
  • Guest beds for visiting families. The Park House Hotel, described as the first kosher hotel, opened in Borough Park in 1987, and the Avenue Plaza Hotel followed on 13th Avenue in 1999. Hotels, guest suites and rooms kept for visiting relatives often need several identical screens set up the same way.

Same-Day Repair in Borough Park

Need Repair in Borough Park? A Screen Pulling Off New Drywall, an Old Set Hanging Low on a Brick Wall, or a Bracket Left Behind by the Contractor

The urgent calls we take from Borough Park homes tend to start with a renovation. The most common is a TV that was hung on the drywall of a new extension and has begun to sag. Drywall over block or over light steel framing looks exactly like drywall over wood, and a bracket fixed with the screws that came in the box, or with plastic plugs, can feel secure on the first day and then slowly pull its fixings through the board. You see it as a gap opening at the top of the bracket plate, a hairline crack running out from one corner, or a screen that has to be nudged level every week. The second kind is an older television on a brick party wall in the front of the house, often hung years ago into the mortar joints rather than the brick, where the joints have softened and the fixings have started to turn. The third is the bracket that nobody wants: an arm left on the wall by a previous owner, a plate the contractor put up before the walls were finished, or a cable sticking out of a hole where the old set used to be.

If a screen is leaning or the bracket has moved, do not push it back against the wall and do not let the children near it. If two adults can lift it straight up and off the bracket safely, do that, unplug it and lay it flat on a blanket; if not, leave it, keep the room clear, and call (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list. If the same screen then goes back up properly on that wall, it is priced as a new mount with the wall add-on, so a screen up to 55 inches re-hung on plaster is $179.99 plus $45 and on brick or stone $179.99 plus $65, with the take-down added. We look at what failed before we fix anything, and if the problem is the wall itself, such as drywall that has come loose from the block behind it or brick that has cracked, we tell you plainly so the contractor or a mason can deal with it before a screen goes back on it.

A word on contractors. When a house is being finished, the general contractor or an electrician will sometimes offer to "throw the TV up" on the way out. Many do it well. The problem we see is not skill but sequence: the bracket goes on before anyone has decided where the sofa goes, or before the blocking that was meant to be in the wall was actually installed, and it ends up in the wrong place or in the wrong material. If your contractor is still on site, ask them to leave the TV wall alone, photograph it before it is closed, and send us the photos. We can tell from them where the solid backing is and save you a second set of holes later.

Building Specialists

TV Installation for Borough Park's Architecture

Borough Park's buildings fall into a handful of types, and most of the houses have changed type at least once. For a TV the useful question is not what the building is called but what the wall behind the screen is made of, who else lives on the other side of it, and how the screen gets to the room.

The original brick row or semi-attached house

Brick facades and party walls from the first decades of the twentieth century, plaster inside, and often a decorative fireplace in the front room.

The house with a rear extension

The back pushed out toward the lot line under the 1992 zoning, usually in block with drywall finishes, joined to the old house at what used to be its back wall.

The house built up

One or more floors added on top, often on steel, with framed partitions and new stairs, as at 1311 44th Street, which went from two stories to five.

The walk-up over the avenue

Apartments above the stores of 13th, 16th and 18th Avenues and New Utrecht Avenue, reached by a stair from a street door beside the shop.

The newer apartment building

Infill buildings from recent decades, with elevators, drywall partitions, block or concrete around the stairs and shafts, and owners who want to know who is coming.

The shul, office or hall

Former houses converted into shtieblach and offices, purpose-built shuls and halls, each with its own committee or office that approves anything fixed to a wall.

The original brick house: plaster on masonry and a front room built for a mantel

The houses that still look as they did when they were built, with their brick fronts and stoops, usually have the most predictable walls in the neighborhood. The party walls shared with the next house are solid brick under a coat of plaster, and in many of them the plaster was laid straight onto the brick with no lath at all. That is excellent backing for a screen of any size, as long as the anchors go into the face of a brick and not into the mortar line between two bricks, which in a hundred-year-old wall may crumble when a drill touches it. The interior partitions, between the front room and the hall or between bedrooms, are more often wood framing with plaster on wood lath, which needs a different approach: the fixing has to reach the stud through the plaster, or use an anchor rated for hollow plaster where the stud is in the wrong place.

Many of these front rooms were built with a fireplace, and in a lot of houses the fireplace has not been used for decades. A screen above a mantel is a +$130 add-on on our list, and because the chimney breast is masonry, the brick or stone add-on applies too. The question we always ask is whether the flue is still open to anything, because in some houses an old chimney was later used to vent a boiler or a water heater, and warm, damp air rising through the breast is not kind to electronics. We also look at height: over a mantel the screen often ends up high, and a Brownstoner commenter's warning about neck strain is one we repeat. Where the room allows, the side wall beside the chimney breast is often the better choice, and we will tape out both so you can compare before deciding.

The house with a rear extension: two buildings joined at the old back wall

A rear extension is the most common change we see. The back of the house is pushed out toward the lot line, usually in concrete block, and the new rooms are finished inside with drywall, either on a light framework set in front of the block or glued directly to it. The old back wall of the house, which used to be outside, becomes an interior wall. In a typical layout the kitchen and the big dining room move into the new space, and the living area runs from the old house into the new one, with the line between them marked by a beam, a soffit or a change in ceiling height. People often want the TV on the long side wall of that combined room, which means the wall may be plaster on brick for the first part of its length and drywall on block for the rest.

Each part is fine on its own. What we avoid is fixing a single bracket plate across the line between the two, because the old wall and the new one move slightly differently over the seasons, and a plate spanning both can crack the finish at the joint. Usually the screen can be centered on one side of the line with a small adjustment, and we show you the choice with painter's tape. If the screen must sit across the seam, the bracket is fixed entirely into one material and the plate is chosen so its fixings all land on that side. For the block itself, the fixings go into the solid webs and faces of the blocks rather than the hollow cores, which we find with the test hole and a careful scan. Block is not listed as its own line on our Brooklyn TV list, so we price that wall from your photos before booking, and you see the number before the visit.

The house built up: steel, framed partitions and a new stair

When a house gains one or more floors, the new rooms are usually framed, and in the larger enlargements the structure is often steel, as it was at 44th Street. Partitions on those floors may be light-gauge steel studs or wood studs, finished in drywall, and from the room the two look exactly alike. The difference matters for a TV, because a lag screw that holds firmly in a wood stud will strip a thin steel one. We find out which it is with a magnet and the scanner before the first hole, and on steel we use fixings made for it, spread across more than one stud. For heavier screens or arms on steel framing, a backer board fixed across the studs gives the bracket a solid surface to pull against.

New floors also bring new stairs, and the wall beside a new stair is frequently built as a fire-rated assembly, with thicker or doubled drywall. A screen can still go there, but the cords cannot be fished inside that wall with an in-wall kit, because cutting openings in a fire-rated wall changes what it was built to do. On those walls the cords go in a surface cover or straight down to the furniture, and we tell you which walls in the room are which. If your contractor or architect left drawings, send a photo of the page that shows the walls; it saves guessing.

The walk-up over the avenue: a stair from the street and a store below

The apartments above the stores on 13th, 16th and 18th Avenues and on New Utrecht Avenue are reached by a stair from their own street door, usually narrow and often steep. Carrying a large screen up is the first part of the job: keep the TV in its original carton until we arrive, because a boxed screen can be carried on its edge up a tight stair far more safely than one wrapped in a blanket. The walls inside are typically plaster, with brick along the street front and the party walls, and the front room facing the avenue tends to have the windows on one wall and the long brick walls on either side.

Two practical things come with living over a store. The first is noise: drilling into brick is loud, and if the store downstairs is open, we would rather drill before it opens or tell the owner it is coming. The second is the street: the avenues are busy, and on 13th Avenue in particular there is little room to stop a van outside. We plan where to park before the day, and if the visit is on a Friday in winter, we schedule it early so the street is not at its most crowded when we are carrying a screen in.

The newer apartment building: an elevator, a managing agent and mixed walls

Borough Park has a steady supply of new apartment buildings, many of them on lots that once held one or two houses. They make the carry easier, since there is usually an elevator, but they often come with an owner or managing agent who wants notice of contractors, a certificate of insurance and set working hours. The walls are mixed: partitions between rooms are commonly drywall on steel studs, while the walls around the stair, the elevator and the corridor are block or concrete. The living room wall that faces the sofa may be either. We scan and test before we commit, and if the wall you want turns out to be the one shared with the corridor or a shaft, we explain why the cords will go in a cover on that wall rather than inside it.

The shul, office or hall: a committee, a room full of people and a screen that must not be touched

Many of Borough Park's shuls began in houses, and the shtiebel in a former two-family has the same brick and plaster as the house next door, sometimes with a new extension of its own at the back for a larger beis medrash. Purpose-built shuls and halls have larger rooms, higher ceilings and walls of brick, block or both. A screen in these rooms is usually a display rather than a TV, it must be readable from the far end of the room, and it must keep running without anyone fiddling with it. The person who books the job is often not the person who decides where the screen goes, so we ask early who signs off, whether that is the rav, a gabbai, a board or the office manager, and we make sure they have seen the taped outline before we drill.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Finding the Seam Between the Old House and the New One

These notes describe how our technicians approach a room in a house that has been extended or built up. They are our working method, not an account of any particular address.

Read the ceiling first. Before touching the wall, the technician looks up. A change in ceiling height, a dropped soffit or a beam wrapped in drywall almost always marks where the original back wall of the house stood before the extension. Recessed lights that stop in a straight line, a change in the direction of the floorboards, or a step down into the dining area tell the same story. That line is where the materials change.

Knock along the whole wall, not just the middle. On the old side, plaster laid on brick gives a dead, hard sound; plaster on lath sounds firmer than drywall but hollow in places. On the new side, drywall on a frame gives a hollow drum, while drywall glued straight to block sounds solid in patches and hollow between the blobs of adhesive. The knock tells us where to scan.

Scan with the right expectations. On plaster over brick a scanner finds nothing, which is correct: there is no framing to find. On drywall over steel, the scanner finds studs and a magnet confirms they are steel. On drywall over block, the scanner may light up in odd places where the adhesive or furring strips are. We use it to rule things out, especially pipes and cables, rather than to decide on its own where the bracket goes.

One test hole, behind the plate. The technician drills one small hole where the bracket plate will cover it. Red dust means brick, grey grit means block or concrete, white powder followed by a sudden drop means drywall or plaster with a cavity behind it, and white powder followed by red means plaster laid on brick. If what comes out changes the add-on from what we quoted, you hear about it before any more drilling.

Tape it, then sit where the family sits. In a dining room that seats a large family, the person at the far end of the table may be fifteen or twenty feet from the screen, and the children may be on the floor in front of it. We tape the outline and check it from the sofa, from the table and from the floor, and we keep it clear of the doorway so nobody brushes it on the way past.

Wall by Wall

Brick, Block, Plaster and Steel: What Holds a Screen in a Borough Park Wall and What It Costs

The starting price on our Brooklyn list is $179.99 for a screen up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs, labor only. A great deal of Borough Park is not that wall, so here is what each of the others involves, and which add-on applies. The add-ons exist because each material needs different bits, different anchors and more time, not because an older house is an excuse for a bigger bill.

Solid brick (+$65). The party walls of the older houses, the street fronts of the walk-ups and the old rear walls now inside extended houses are commonly solid brick, sometimes two or three bricks thick. Brick holds a screen extremely well, but only if the fixings go into the brick. Mortar from the early twentieth century is often soft lime mortar that can be scratched with a fingernail, and an anchor set into a joint can loosen as the mortar around it crumbles. We use a hammer drill, sleeve or wedge anchors sized for the bracket and the load, and we keep each hole a safe distance from the edge of its brick. Brownstoner's forum has long carried the advice that a party wall usually has "plaster over brick" with no studs at all, and that matches what we find.

Plaster (+$45). Plaster on wood lath over a framed partition is the classic interior wall of the older houses. It is hard on the surface and weak in pull, so a plastic plug or a light toggle set only into the plaster can feel solid and then slowly let go. The fixing that lasts goes through the plaster and lath into a stud, or uses an anchor rated for that thickness of plaster where the stud is not where the bracket needs it. Studs in old houses are not always on the spacing a modern bracket expects, so we locate them one at a time. Where the plaster sits directly on brick, the test hole shows red under the white, and we price the wall by what is behind the plaster, at the brick rate.

Stone (+$65). The brick or stone add-on also covers stone, which shows up in Borough Park as fireplace surrounds, feature walls and some facades. Thin stone veneer glued over block or drywall is common in renovations and is not the same as solid stone; if the test shows veneer, the bracket goes through it into whatever carries it, and we take care that the veneer face does not chip.

Hiding the cords the legal way

Almost everyone wants the cords to disappear, and there is a right way and a wrong way to do it. The wrong way, which we still find behind screens in Borough Park, is the TV's own power cord pushed into a hole behind the screen and pulled out of another near the floor. The National Electrical Code does not allow flexible cords to be concealed by walls, floors or ceilings, because a cord inside a wall can be pinched by a fixing, overheat where it is bundled and cannot be inspected. A StreetEasy forum thread on mounting TVs in New York apartments has posters arguing over exactly this, one insisting that "Electrical outlets cannot be hidden behind drywall" and another that New York walls need "metallic armored cable ('BX')." The safe summary is simpler: the TV's own cord never goes inside the wall.

On a hollow wall, a framed partition with drywall or plaster on lath, the answer is an in-wall power and cable kit, $169 on our list including the kit. It has a recessed inlet low on the wall that plugs into your existing outlet, carries power inside the wall in its own rated cable to a recessed outlet behind the screen, and leaves a separate opening for HDMI and other low-voltage cables. It does not tie into the house wiring. It needs a hollow cavity between the two openings, so it does not suit brick, block or concrete, and it should not go into a fire-rated wall beside a stair or a shaft.

On masonry, the honest options are a paintable cord cover, $59 on our list, which runs on the surface from the screen to the outlet and can be painted to match the wall, or placing the screen directly above a console or cabinet so the cords drop straight down behind it and never show. In a house with small children the cabinet route has a second advantage: the power strip and the boxes sit inside a closed door rather than on the floor where a toddler can reach them.

Safety at Home

A Screen in a House Full of Children: Tip-Overs, Arms and Cords

If a third of the people on your block are under eighteen, the safety of a television is not a side issue. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission's 2022 report counted 581 deaths since 2000 in which furniture, a TV or an appliance tipped over, and 472 of them, 81 percent, were children seventeen or younger. Seventy-one percent of those child deaths involved a television. The same report estimated that about 22,500 people a year were treated in emergency departments for tip-over injuries in 2018 to 2020. The commission's advice is to anchor TVs and furniture to the wall, and where a TV cannot be anchored, to "Always place TVs on a sturdy, low base" and push it as far back as it will go.

A properly fixed wall mount takes the tip-over risk away almost completely, which is one of the best reasons to mount a screen in a family home. The risk does not vanish on its own, though. It moves to three other places: the bracket itself, the cords and the furniture underneath. The bracket has to be fixed into a material that will hold it and rated for more than the screen weighs, which is why we spend so much of this page on walls. The cords have to be out of reach, which is why we run them in a cover or a cabinet rather than letting them hang. And the console under the screen, which children will climb to reach a remote or a toy, has to be stable too.

Fixed or tilting brackets for low walls. In a living room where small children play, a fixed or low-profile tilting bracket is usually the right choice. It holds the screen close to the wall, gives a child nothing to pull on and nothing to swing from, and leaves no gap behind the screen for small fingers.

Full-motion arms with care. An arm that swings out is useful in a long room where the screen has to face both the sofa and the table, but an arm pulled out from the wall puts its whole weight, and any weight hung on it, on the top fixings. If you want an arm in a house with young children, we fix it into masonry or doubled framing, set it so it rests against the wall when not in use, and mount it high enough that a standing child cannot reach the screen's edge. If the room does not need an arm, we will tell you so.

Height that suits everyone. In a family room the screen is often a little higher than the usual eye-level rule, so that it sits above the reach of a child standing on the floor and is still comfortable from the sofa. A tilting bracket brings the picture back down toward the viewers. We check the height with tape from where the family actually sits, including the floor if that is where the children watch.

Stands that are strapped. If you are not drilling at all, a TV on its stand is $119.99 on our list. The CPSC's advice still applies: a sturdy, low base, the TV pushed back, and anti-tip straps from the TV to the wall or the furniture where possible. We tell you what we would strap and where before we leave.

Remotes and boxes up high. The CPSC also warns against leaving remotes and toys on top of furniture where children will climb to reach them. We put the cable or streaming box behind the screen or in a closed cabinet, and if you want, a small wall shelf for the remote above a child's reach. It is a small thing and it removes the reason to climb.

Houses that have been extended or built up

Since the back extension went on, the old outside wall of our house stands in the middle of the living room. Is that brick a good place for the TV?

Usually it is one of the best walls in the house. A former rear wall is solid brick, often thicker than a modern wall, and once it is indoors it stays dry and stable. The things to check are what was done to it during the extension. Openings were usually cut through it to join the old rooms to the new ones, and the lintels over those openings must not be drilled. Old window openings may have been filled in with block, so part of the wall may be brick and part block. Some builders furred the wall out and covered it with drywall to match the new rooms, in which case the fixings have to pass through the drywall into the brick. Our test hole tells us which. The brick add-on is $65, so a screen up to 55 inches on that wall is $244.99 in labor, and the cords usually go in a paintable cord cover or straight down into a cabinet, since nothing can be fished inside solid brick.

The rooms in our extension were finished with drywall glued straight onto concrete block. What holds a bracket in a wall like that?

The block does, as long as the fixings reach it properly. Drywall glued onto block, sometimes called direct-applied or "dabbed" drywall, sits a little off the block on spots of adhesive, with a small air gap between them. A screw into the drywall alone will hold almost nothing, and a masonry anchor that is simply tightened against the drywall can crush the board and pull the bracket crooked. The right fixing is a masonry anchor long enough to pass through the drywall and the gap and set into the solid part of the block, with a sleeve or spacer so the drywall is not squeezed as it tightens. On hollow block we place the fixings into the solid webs, which the test hole and scan locate, or use anchors made for hollow masonry. Because block is not a listed line on our Brooklyn TV list, we price that wall from your photos before booking, and cords run in a cover or down into furniture.

The wall we want is beside the new stair, and our contractor said it is fire-rated. Can a TV be mounted on it at all?

In most cases yes, with two limits. A fire-rated wall in an enlarged house is usually built with thicker or doubled fire-resistant drywall on its framing, and its job is to slow a fire spreading between the stair and the rooms. A bracket fixed with a few anchors into the framing or into block behind it does not change that much, and screens are hung on such walls all the time. What we will not do on a fire-rated wall is cut the two openings an in-wall power and cable kit needs, because a box-sized hole through a rated assembly is exactly the kind of opening it is designed not to have. So the cords on that wall go into a paintable cord cover, $59 on our list, or straight down into a console beneath. If your architect's drawings are handy, a photo of the page that marks the rated walls helps us plan before we arrive, and if there is any doubt about what the wall is, we treat it as rated.

Rooms full of children

Our little ones grab anything that sticks out. Is a full-motion arm a bad idea in a house with many small children?

Often it is, and we would rather say so before you buy one. A full-motion arm is the one bracket that gives a child something to pull, and when it is extended, every pound hanging from it, including a small child, levers on the top fixings. In a living room where toddlers play, a fixed or low-profile tilting bracket keeps the screen flat against the wall with nothing to grab and no gap behind it. If the room really needs the screen to turn, for example toward a long dining table on Shabbos and Yom Tov, we fix the arm into brick or into doubled framing, set it to rest folded against the wall, and mount it high enough that a standing child cannot reach the edge of the screen. We also show you the arm's lock or tension setting so it does not swing out on its own. Brackets are sold separately, so you can decide after we have seen the room.

Once the TV is up, how do you keep the cords, the cable box and the remote away from a crawling baby?

By deciding where they go before the bracket is fixed. The cords from a wall-mounted screen either go into an in-wall kit on a hollow wall, so there is nothing to pull, or into a paintable cord cover fixed to the wall, so they cannot be looped or chewed. The power strip and any boxes go behind the screen on a bracket shelf or inside a closed cabinet, not on the floor. The Consumer Product Safety Commission advises keeping TV and cable cords out of children's reach and not leaving remotes or toys on top of furniture where children will climb to get them, and we set up the room with that in mind. If the screen sits above a console, we check the console is stable and can be strapped to the wall. The aim is a wall with nothing loose on it below shoulder height.

The best spot for the screen is right beside a doorway with a mezuzah on it. Will the screen or the bracket get in its way?

It does not need to. We keep every fixing and the edge of the screen clear of the door casing, so the doorpost and the mezuzah are never touched, drilled or covered. In practice that means leaving a margin of a few inches between the door frame and the side of the screen, more if the bracket is an arm that swings toward the doorway. Door casings in older Borough Park houses are often wide and decorative, and the wall right beside them may hide the framing of the doorway, which is actually a good place for a fixing if the screen can sit there without crowding the opening. We also check that the screen does not stick out into the path of people walking through, which in a busy family room is the bigger practical concern. If the screen would end up uncomfortably close to the doorway, we suggest moving it along the wall and show you both positions with tape.

Scan, knock and one small test hole behind the bracket plate to tell plaster, brick, block and drywall apart, and to find the seam with any extension.

4. Mark and agree

Painter's tape shows the screen, checked from the sofa, the table and the floor, clear of doorways; the cord route is agreed before drilling.

5. Fix the bracket

Anchors made for the material, in brick not mortar, in framing not plaster alone, in solid block not the cores, leveled to true.

6. Hang and dress the cords

The screen goes up and is checked; cords go into an in-wall kit, a cord cover or the cabinet below, with nothing loose at a child's height.

7. Test, tidy and hand over

Every source is tried with you, inputs and locks are set for the screen's use, the dust is cleaned and any old TV that was booked is taken down.

FAQ

Borough Park TV Installation FAQ

What is the labor for a shul lobby screen up to 55 inches on a brick wall, with a paintable cord cover?
+

The starting price is $179.99, brick adds $65 and the cord cover is $59, so the labor comes to $303.99 on our Brooklyn list. The bracket and any display player are separate, and a wired network cable to the screen is quoted from photos of the route.

Is the price the same on every Borough Park block, from Fort Hamilton Parkway over to McDonald Avenue?
+

Yes. Every block in Borough Park is billed at the same Brooklyn rate, and so are the neighboring blocks of Kensington, Mapleton, Bensonhurst and Sunset Park. The wall and the add-ons change the total; the street does not.

Should we buy the screen before or after you have seen the wall?
+

If you can, send the wall photos first. We can tell you how wide a screen the wall can take between the doorway, the window and the corner, and whether the wall is likely to be brick, block or drywall, before you choose a size or a bracket.

Does the brick charge change if the brick wall has been painted?
+

No. Painted brick is still brick, so the add-on is $65 either way. We score the paint around each hole before drilling so it does not flake, and the fixings go into the face of the brick rather than the painted mortar line.

Can a screen be mounted on the wall above a bed?
+

It can, but it is rarely the best spot, because nobody can see a screen directly above their head. The wall facing the bed is usually better. If a screen must go over a bed, we use a fixed bracket into solid backing and never mount over a child's bed.

Can the screen go up before our new furniture is delivered?
+

Yes, as long as you know where the sofa and the console will go and how tall the console is. We mark the furniture's outline on the floor with tape and set the screen height from that, so it lines up when the furniture arrives.

Can the soundbar be fixed above the screen instead of below it?
+

Yes. A wall-mounted soundbar is $129 whether it goes above or below. Above works well when the screen sits low over a console, though sound usually seems more natural from below, so we check both positions with you before fixing it.

Can you also put up the computer monitor in our home office during the same visit?
+

Usually, yes. Send a photo of the office wall and the monitor's model number with your request, and we price it from the same list by the wall it goes on, so both screens are covered by one booking and one arrival window.

Can you mount screens in a hall we are renting for one simcha?
+

Only on a wall the hall agrees to have drilled, and only with the hall's approval in writing. Many halls prefer screens on their own stands for one-night events, so ask the hall manager first and send us what they allow.

Can the shul's screen be locked so visitors cannot change the input or the settings?
+

Most current TVs have a lock or a hotel or kiosk mode that disables the buttons and menus, and we set it where the model allows. The remote stays with the gabbai or office, and we leave written steps for unlocking it.

Can the same display also appear on a second screen in the ezras nashim?
+

Yes. The picture can be split to a second screen with an HDMI splitter, or the second screen can run its own copy of the display service. Each screen is priced by its own wall, and the cable route between them is quoted from photos before you book.
