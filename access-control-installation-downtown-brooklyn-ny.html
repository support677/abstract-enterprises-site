The Neighborhood

Why Downtown Brooklyn Properties Need Access Control

Downtown Brooklyn is a business district before it is a neighborhood. Wikipedia calls it the third-largest central business district in New York City, after Midtown and Lower Manhattan, packed into less than half a square mile between Borough Hall, Flatbush Avenue and the edge of Fort Greene, with ZIP code 11201 north of DeKalb Avenue and 11217 to the south. Its streets carry college campuses, courthouses, hotels, the Fulton Mall and the office blocks of Brooklyn Commons, the superblock once called MetroTech. What sets the access work here apart from the rest of the borough is simple: almost nobody owns the whole building. A typical caller leases a suite, a floor, a storefront or an apartment inside something much bigger, and the doors on the way in belong to several different people.

That shapes every job. Before we price a single reader, we list every door between the sidewalk and your space and write down who controls it. The lobby doors and turnstiles usually belong to the landlord. The elevators belong to the landlord and are serviced by an elevator contractor. The stair doors are governed by the Building Code and wired to the fire alarm. The corridor and the suite entrance may be yours, or may be part of a landlord standard that every tenant on the floor must follow. Access control in Downtown Brooklyn is therefore half hardware and half paperwork: alteration agreements, insurance certificates, after-hours work windows and a freight elevator booking, all before a drill touches a door frame.

The office market is moving again, and that brings new doors. Commercial Observer reported in January 2026 that the Arizona College of Nursing had signed a 40,000-square-foot lease at 532 Fulton Street for its first New York City campus, and that 203 Jay Street had reached full occupancy within the past year. The broker who represents both buildings, CBRE’s Brad Gerla, told the paper that Downtown is drawing tenants because “the floor plates are there, the locations are there,” and that “the smart landlords fix up the lobby.” A refreshed lobby usually means new turnstiles, new readers and a new badge format, and every tenant upstairs needs to know whether their suite credentials will still work when it opens.

Smaller companies are arriving in flexible offices. Allwork.Space reported in June 2026 that Jay Suites had opened a 30,000-square-foot, full-floor workspace with 65 private offices inside the Pioneer Building at 41 Flatbush Avenue, a building completed in 1909. Sixty-five offices behind one floor entrance is sixty-five sets of people who need the front door, their own office and the shared rooms, and nothing else. That is a different design problem from a single company’s suite, and we treat it as one further down this page.

Some office buildings are becoming homes. Commercial Observer reported in August 2025 that BH3 and Capstone Equities had taken control of 141 Willoughby Street, a 24-story office building finished in 2023 that never filled, and planned to turn it into about 200 rental apartments, a quarter of them affordable. In September 2026 the Mann Report described a $208 million construction loan for that conversion. When an office tower becomes a multiple dwelling, its entry system stops being a corporate badge system and starts answering to the Multiple Dwelling Law and the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law, which ask very different things of the same readers.

And the towers keep coming. Downtown’s residential boom since the 2004 rezoning is covered in detail on our other Downtown pages; for this page what matters is height. In March 2026 the City Council approved a 72-story, 840-foot tower at 395 Flatbush Avenue Extension with 1,263 homes, retail and roughly 75,000 square feet of office or community space designed for the city’s health department, according to 6sqft. In a building like that, the street door is only the first of many controlled doors. The elevator decides which floor you reach, the amenity floors decide who swims or works out, and the stair doors must, by code, let people back onto a floor in a fire. In June 2026, News 12 reported that tenants of a 26-story Downtown rental had lived for months with elevators out of service for upgrades, and that a neighbor with limited mobility had to walk down 18 flights. When the elevator is the real front door, access control has to be planned with the elevator contractor, not around it.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Downtown Brooklyn’s Architecture

The building decides the hardware, and in Downtown it also decides who has to sign off. Below are the six kinds of building we work in most here, with what each usually needs.

Prewar office buildings

Masonry office blocks from the early 1900s, like the Pioneer Building at 41 Flatbush Avenue, now subdivided into suites and flexible offices. Wood or hollow-metal suite doors in old frames, plaster corridor walls, and cable routes found above dropped ceilings rather than cut into masonry.

Late-century office towers

The Brooklyn Commons superblock between Jay Street and the Flatbush Avenue Extension, assembled from 1992, with lobby security desks, turnstiles and a landlord’s base-building access system. Suite readers have to be compatible with, or at least not interfere with, what the building already runs.

New Class A office floors

Large open floor plates of around 22,000 square feet, like the lower floors of 141 Willoughby Street, built with raised or dropped ceilings, steel-stud demising walls and aluminum-and-glass suite entrances that leave little room for a lock in the door itself.

Office buildings becoming apartments

Towers converting from office to rental use, where the badge system was planned for one employer per floor and now has to serve hundreds of households, with apartment entry doors, package rooms and the privacy rules of a multiple dwelling.

Residential high-rises

Rental and condo towers from about 25 stories up to the 1,035-foot Brooklyn Tower, with attended or keyless lobbies, amenity floors, garages and elevators that decide who reaches which floor; often run on one company’s cloud platform from the day the building opened.

Hotels and mixed podiums

Hotels on Duffield and Schermerhorn Streets and at the corner of Smith Street, and towers that stack stores, offices or community space under apartments. Separate staff, guest, tenant and resident entrances that must never share a credential by accident.

What the materials mean for the install

Prewar office corridors are usually plaster on masonry or clay tile. We do not chase cable into those walls; it goes up into the ceiling space above the door and along the corridor ceiling to the suite’s closet, and the reader is surface-mounted or set into the door frame. Old wood frames have often been bored for three or four generations of locks, so the strike area is filled or the jamb section replaced before a strike goes in. Hollow-metal frames in later buildings are often filled with grout where they meet masonry and need a clean cut for the strike body.

Newer floors bring the opposite problem. Steel-stud demising walls make cable easy, but aluminum storefront-style suite entrances have stiles too narrow for a mortise lock, so the lock usually goes into an electrified panic bar, the frame header or a strike designed for that profile, with power passed into the door through a hinge or a door loop. Glass doors and sidelights mean the reader often goes on the frame or the adjacent wall, and a request-to-exit sensor has to be aimed so it does not release the door every time someone walks past the glass inside.

Sixty-Five Offices Behind One Door: Coworking Floors and Shared Suites

A flexible office floor like the one Jay Suites opened in the Pioneer Building has a door plan that looks nothing like a company suite. There is one floor entrance off the elevator lobby or stair, a reception and concierge zone behind it, a corridor of private offices, and a set of shared rooms: meeting rooms, phone booths, a kitchen, a mail area and perhaps a roof deck. Every member needs the floor door and their own office. Most need the shared rooms only at certain times. Nobody needs the operator’s storeroom, the network closet or another member’s office.

The design starts with groups, not doors. Each member company is a group; each person in it gets a credential that opens the floor entrance, that company’s office and the shared rooms on the operator’s schedule. A company that rents two adjoining offices gets both. A day-pass guest gets a credential that opens the floor entrance and the lounge for one day and expires on its own. The operator’s staff get everything except the rooms a member has asked to keep private, and every opening of a member’s office by staff shows in the log. We set those rules up in the management software before the first credential is issued, so a new member is a few clicks rather than a reprogramming job.

Turnover is the real workload. A floor with dozens of offices may add or lose members every month, and members add and lose staff of their own. We give the operator a simple onboarding routine: create the company, add its people, assign its office, set an end date that matches the membership agreement. When a membership ends, every credential in that group stops on the same day without anyone remembering to cancel it. Members who want to manage their own people can be given a limited administrator role that reaches only their group, so the operator is not the bottleneck for every new hire.

The building itself comes first. A coworking floor inside an older office building still sits behind the landlord’s street door and elevators, and the operator usually cannot put readers on those. Members then carry two credentials, the building’s and the operator’s, unless the landlord’s system can issue mobile keys the operator can request on a member’s behalf. We find out which before the plan is drawn, because it changes the whole first day for every new member.

What we leave in place

Every private office keeps a mechanical key override held by the operator, so a failed lock never traps a member’s work behind a door nobody can open. Exit is always free: the floor entrance and every office door open from inside with one motion and no credential. And nothing about the access log is shared between members; one company cannot see when another company’s staff came and went.

When an Office Tower Becomes Apartments: Planning Entry for a Conversion

An office building’s access system is built around employers. Each floor or suite belongs to one company, that company decides who gets a badge, and the landlord runs the lobby with a security desk and turnstiles. Badges carry an employee’s name and photo, logs are kept for as long as the company wants, and nobody expects privacy from their employer’s door records. Almost all of that has to change when the same building fills with households.

The legal frame changes first. Once a converted tower is a class A multiple dwelling, section 50-a of the Multiple Dwelling Law applies to its entrances: for buildings erected or converted after January 1, 1968, the street and other entrance doors must be self-closing and self-locking and kept locked except when an attendant is on duty, and a building with eight or more apartments needs an intercom with voice and remote release. The city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law, which covers fobs, apps, cards and biometrics in multiple dwellings, requires written consent, a published privacy and retention policy, collection of only the data the system needs, and destruction of authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized. It also forbids using those systems to track tenants outside the building or to harass or evict them. An office badge database that logs every entry for years cannot simply be carried over.

The hardware changes less than people expect. Lobby doors, electrified panic hardware, the controller cabinet, the cable risers and many readers can often stay, as long as they are listed for access control and the readers can take encrypted credentials. What goes are the turnstiles in most cases, the photo-badge workflow, and any reader still on an old 125 kilohertz proximity format that can be copied with an inexpensive device. What arrives is new: apartment entry doors with listed locksets, a package room, a bike room, an intercom or video entry panel at the street door, amenity spaces with their own schedules, and an elevator plan that decides whether residents can reach every floor or only their own.

Timing matters more than anything else. A conversion like the one planned at 141 Willoughby Street is a construction project before it is a building, and the cheapest moment to place readers, back boxes and conduit is while walls are open and the electrical contractor is still on site. We work from the architect’s door schedule, mark which doors need power and cable, and coordinate with the elevator and fire alarm contractors, so the system that opens with the first lease is the one the building will keep, not a temporary arrangement replaced a year later.

A conversion checklist we give owners

  • Inventory. Every existing reader, controller, lock and turnstile listed with its model, format and whether it is listed to UL 294.
  • Credentials. A plan to retire copyable proximity cards and issue encrypted fobs or phone keys, with a key-based alternative for anyone who does not want an app.
  • Data. The office-era logs exported or destroyed, and a new retention setting that matches the privacy policy the Tenant Data Privacy Law requires.
  • Separation. Any remaining commercial tenant on its own doors and its own administrator, with no shared credentials between offices and apartments.
  • Life safety. Every electrified door re-tested with the fire alarm, and stair doors set for re-entry under the high-rise rules described below.

Towers Where the Elevator Is the Real Front Door

In a walk-up, access control is a street door. In a Downtown tower it is a chain: the street door, the lobby or vestibule, the elevator, the amenity floor and finally the apartment or suite. A credential that opens the lobby but not the elevator leaves a visitor stranded by the mailboxes; an elevator that takes anyone anywhere makes the lobby reader decorative. The useful question in a high-rise is not “which door gets a reader” but “what should each credential be able to reach, and how does it get there.”

Elevator access is done in partnership with the elevator contractor. The access controller tells the elevator which floors a credential is allowed to call, either through a reader in the cab or through a hall-call panel in the lobby. The elevator controller decides everything else, and above all it hands control to the fire service when firefighters’ operation is switched on, at which point no access restriction applies. We never wire into an elevator controller ourselves; we supply the access side, the elevator contractor supplies and certifies the interface, and both are tested together. That is also why elevator modernizations, like the months of upgrades News 12 described at a 26-story Downtown rental in June 2026, need an access plan: when a car is taken out and modernized, its reader and interface go back in with it.

Stair doors follow their own rule. Section 403.5.3 of the NYC Building Code says doors into interior stair enclosures in high-rise buildings may not be locked from either side, except that they may be locked from the stair side if they unlock automatically on fire detection, on elevator recall or by a signal from the fire command center. Where stair doors are locked that way, signs must say re-entry is available only in a fire emergency, and section 403.5.3.1 adds a two-way communication system, connected to a constantly attended station, at least on every fifth floor. In practice that means a tower cannot use readers to keep people in the stairs off other floors except through hardware the fire alarm and the fire command center can release, and the access design has to show those connections on paper.

Phone keys are now common in new buildings. Allegion describes the Maxwell, a 40-story high-rise in Downtown Brooklyn, as built so that residents use mobile credentials on their phones to open their apartment, the building and its amenities, and vendors receive digital credentials of their own. That works well for most residents. But amNY reported in 2019 that a Department of Buildings spokesman said technology-based entry may not replace a key-based tool, and that fobs and cards count as keys under the department’s protocols. We therefore set up every phone-key building with a physical credential available to anyone who asks, so no resident’s way home depends on a charged phone or on agreeing to install an app.

Amenity floors and the people who are not residents

Gyms, pools, roof decks, screening rooms and co-working lounges are where tower access gets complicated, because they are used by people who are not on the lease: personal trainers, children, cleaners, guests invited for an evening. We set amenity doors up on schedules that match the posted hours, give each amenity its own rule so a guest pass for the roof does not open the gym, and log amenity openings separately from the residential entrances so the record of who used the pool is not a record of who came home.

Hotels: keep staff doors off the guest-key system

The Downtown Brooklyn Partnership’s hotel guide lists ten hotels in and around the district, from the New York Marriott at the Brooklyn Bridge with nearly 700 rooms to the 117-room Hampton Inn, with the Sheraton and its more than 300 rooms and the Aloft both on Duffield Street. Every one of them runs two kinds of door. Guest rooms open with key cards the front desk encodes at check-in; that is the hotel lock vendor’s system and we leave it alone. Everything else, from the staff entrance and time-clock corridor to linen rooms, liquor and food stores, engineering, the loading door and the offices, is staff territory, and it should be on a staff access system with named credentials, schedules by department and a log that shows which person opened which door.

Keeping the two apart matters for a simple reason. A guest-key encoder that can also make cards for back-of-house doors is one mistake away from handing a guest a card to the storeroom, and a guest-room lock system is rarely built to report a door held open or forced. A staff system with readers on hollow-metal doors, door contacts and exit sensors does both, and it can lock out a departing employee the moment they hand in their uniform.

Rules that apply to Downtown Brooklyn business doors

  • Egress first. Under the NYC Building Code, a sensor-released electrically locked egress door must unlock when the sensor detects someone approaching, when power fails, and when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate, with a push-to-exit button within five feet that cuts power to the lock for at least 30 seconds; a magnetic lock released by door-mounted hardware must release with one hand and immediately. Office and store exits always open from inside without a card, code or key.
  • Stair doors in high-rises. Building Code section 403.5.3 forbids locking interior stair doors from either side except stair-side locking with automatic fail-safe release on fire detection, elevator recall or a fire command center signal.
  • Listed equipment. Controllers and electrified locks on egress doors should be listed to UL 294; we fit listed equipment and keep the listings in the job file for the landlord’s engineer.
  • Apartments in the same building. Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a requires the street entrance of a newer class A multiple dwelling to be self-closing, self-locking and locked except when an attendant is on duty, so a store or office in a mixed building cannot put the residential entrance on a daytime unlock schedule.
  • Tenant data. Where a building with apartments uses fobs, apps or other smart access, the Tenant Data Privacy Law requires written consent, a privacy policy, limits on data collected and destruction of authentication data within 90 days unless anonymized. A company’s own suite readers are not covered, but a shared residential entrance is.
  • Licensing. New York State requires a Department of State license to install or service security systems for others; AESS works under license #12000287431.

Access Control in Downtown Brooklyn: Questions Answered

Office floors, coworking and schools

Our coworking floor has dozens of private offices behind one entry door. Can each member’s credential open the floor and only their own office, while day-pass visitors stay in the lounge?

Yes, and that is how a flexible floor should be set up. Each member company becomes a group in the management software; its people get credentials that open the floor entrance, that company’s office and the shared rooms during the hours the operator sets. Day-pass visitors get a credential, usually a phone key or a printed code, that opens the floor entrance and the lounge for one day and expires by itself. Phone booths and meeting rooms can open on bookings, so a member who reserved a room at two o’clock gets in at two o’clock.

The operator keeps a staff group that can reach every room for cleaning and maintenance, and each staff opening of a member’s office is logged. Members never see each other’s records. When a membership ends, the group’s end date shuts off every credential in it on the same day.

Our school is opening classrooms and clinical labs on leased office floors. Can the doors follow the academic calendar, room by room, instead of one schedule for the whole floor?

They can, and on a teaching floor they should. Each room type gets its own rule: classrooms unlock for scheduled sessions and otherwise open only for faculty; skills labs and equipment rooms open for the instructors and the students enrolled in that course; offices and records rooms open for staff. Holidays, breaks and exam periods go into the controller as a calendar, so the floor locks down over a winter break without anyone changing settings by hand.

Student credentials carry the end date of the term or the program, so a cohort that finishes in May stops getting into the labs in May. Because the floor sits inside an office building, we also confirm with the landlord how students reach it: whether the building issues them its own lobby credential, or whether the school has to request visitor access for each cohort. That decides the first day of every term.

Our landlord is refreshing the lobby this year. What should a suite tenant get in writing before the building changes how people reach our floor?

Four things. First, the new lobby credential format and whether your current suite readers can read it, so your staff are not carrying two cards by accident. Second, the date of the changeover and how your employees will be enrolled, ideally from a list you send rather than a queue at the desk. Third, what happens to visitors and deliveries: whether the desk still calls up, whether the new system issues guest passes, and who approves them. Fourth, after-hours rules, because a lobby refit often changes which doors stay open in the evening.

CBRE’s Brad Gerla told Commercial Observer that “the smart landlords fix up the lobby,” and many Downtown buildings are doing exactly that. We regularly read landlord specifications for tenants and tell them, in plain terms, whether their suite hardware will keep working or needs a new reader.

Another organization sublets two offices inside our suite. Can they manage their own people without seeing when our staff come and go?

Yes. The subtenant becomes its own group with a limited administrator role that reaches only its people and the doors it rents: the suite entrance during its permitted hours, its two offices and any shared rooms the sublease includes. It can add and remove its own staff without asking you, and it cannot see your staff list or your door records. You keep the master administrator account and see every opening.

Put the arrangement in the sublease: which doors, which hours, who pays for credentials, and what happens to the subtenant’s access the day the sublease ends. We then build the end date into the group so the access stops when the paperwork does.

We are moving our company from Manhattan to an office in Downtown Brooklyn. Can our employees keep their existing cards on the new readers?

Possibly, and it is worth checking before you order anything. Whether an old card works on a new reader depends on its technology and format: many readers can read several common encrypted card types and the older proximity formats, but some cards are tied to one manufacturer’s keys. Send us a photo of a card front and back, any number printed on it, and the name of your current system, and we will tell you whether the new readers can accept it.

If the cards are old 125 kilohertz proximity cards, we usually advise replacing them anyway, because that format can be copied with an inexpensive device. A move is the cheapest moment to change cards, since everyone is being enrolled on the new site anyway.

Towers, conversions and life safety

Our office tower is being converted to rental apartments. Which parts of the office badge system can stay, and what has to change once people live there?

Much of the infrastructure can stay: cable risers, controller cabinets, lobby doors with electrified panic hardware, and readers that are listed for access control and can read encrypted credentials. What has to change is how the system is run. Under the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law, a smart-access system in a multiple dwelling needs tenants’ written consent, a privacy and retention policy, minimal data collection and destruction of authentication data within 90 days unless anonymized, so an office-era database that keeps every entry for years cannot carry over.

The physical plan changes too: turnstiles usually go, an intercom with voice and remote release goes in at the street door for a building of eight or more apartments under Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a, and apartment doors, package rooms and amenity floors are added. We inventory what is there, list what can be reused, and price the rest from the Brooklyn list.

Our tower’s elevators are being modernized one car at a time. What happens to the readers in the cabs, and who coordinates the work with the elevator contractor?

The reader in each cab is part of the access system, but the connection to the elevator controller belongs to the elevator contractor. When a car is modernized, its controller is replaced, so the interface that tells the elevator which floors a credential may call has to be reconnected and tested on the new equipment. We schedule with the elevator contractor so the reader comes out before demolition, is stored and labeled, and goes back in with the new controller, then we test floor restrictions together and confirm that firefighters’ operation overrides them.

During the work, fewer cars are running, so check what the remaining cars allow. If floor restrictions leave some residents with no working car to their floor, the restrictions can be relaxed on the remaining cars until the modernized car returns.

Our building has a public plaza and a through-block lobby that must stay open during the day. How does access control start at the elevators instead of the street door?

The controlled line moves inside. The street doors and the public lobby stay open on the hours the building’s approvals require, and the first credential check happens at the elevator bank or at a set of doors between the public area and the residential or office lobby. Elevators then answer only to credentials, with a desk or a video panel for visitors. After public hours, the street doors lock on a schedule and the line moves back out.

Large mixed towers, like the one approved at 395 Flatbush Avenue Extension with a 4,750-square-foot public plaza, retail and office or community space, need this planned with the architect, because the right place for the line depends on where each use’s lobby sits. We draw it as a simple floor plan with open, controlled and staff-only zones before any hardware is chosen.

Our tower’s fire command center is off the lobby. Which locked doors does the fire service expect to release from there?

In a high-rise, the doors that matter most are the stair doors. NYC Building Code section 403.5.3 allows a stair door to be locked from the stair side only if it unlocks automatically on fire detection, on elevator recall or by a signal from the fire command center, so any locked stair door has to be wired to release from that panel. Electrically locked egress doors elsewhere must also release when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate.

We design the access system so those releases come from the fire alarm side, which the fire alarm contractor owns and tests, rather than from an access app. The access controller reports the door states, and we document every connection on the door schedule so the building’s fire safety director can see which doors release and from where.

Hotels, stores and food halls

Our hotel’s staff entrance and back-of-house doors open with the same key-card encoder as the guest rooms. Should staff doors be on their own system?

In most hotels, yes. A guest-room lock system is designed for one thing, letting a registered guest into one room for one stay, and the front desk can usually encode any card for any door it controls. Putting storerooms and engineering on the same encoder means one wrong selection at check-in hands a guest a back-of-house card, and those locks usually cannot report a door held open or forced. A separate staff system with readers, door contacts and exit sensors gives named credentials, departmental schedules and real alerts.

Staff can still carry one card if the hotel lock vendor and the staff system read the same card technology; that is checked on the survey. We do not change guest-room locks, which stay with the hotel’s lock vendor.

Can our store’s high-value stockroom cage require two staff credentials before it opens?

Yes. Many controllers support a two-person rule: the cage door releases only after two different authorized credentials are presented within a short time of each other, and the log records both names. It suits a cage that holds phones, fragrances, jewelry or electronics, where a single key holder is a risk to the business and to that employee. A door contact then reports if the cage is left open, and the cage can be set to refuse all credentials after closing.

The rule only works if it fits how the store really runs. If one manager is often alone at opening, the rule needs a fallback, such as a manager plus a remote release from the district office. Exiting the cage is always free from inside; no one is ever held in by the rule.

Our food-hall stall rents a storage room off a basement corridor shared with many other vendors. Can our room have its own reader, and whose permission do we need?

Usually it can, with the hall operator’s written consent, because the door, the frame and the corridor belong to the operator or the building. Ask the operator for its alteration rules first: some halls want every vendor room on the hall’s own system so their staff can open any door in an emergency or for pest control, and will add your staff to it. Others allow a vendor’s own reader as long as the operator keeps a key override and receives a credential.

Either way, the room should open for your staff on your hours, log who went in, and send an alert if the door is left open, which matters when the room holds stock or a cooler. We work under the operator’s insurance and scheduling rules and send them certificates before work starts.

After a theft from our suite, police asked for our door records. Can we hand over what they need without exposing everyone else’s movements?

Yes. Most management software exports the log for a chosen door and a chosen time window, so you can provide entries for the suite door between, say, six in the evening and eight the next morning rather than a month of every employee’s comings and goings. Exports usually include the credential name, the time, the door and whether access was granted, denied or forced. Keep a copy of exactly what you handed over and to whom.

If your system is in a building with apartments, the Tenant Data Privacy Law limits what can be kept and shared about residents, so building management should handle any request about residential doors. For your own office doors, your company’s policy decides; we can set the system to keep logs long enough to be useful and no longer.

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Downtown Brooklyn

These are generalizations we hear repeated in quick online answers, consumer articles and national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each is set against what a Downtown Brooklyn door actually needs. We do not attribute any specific claim or figure to any one of those sources.

“Pick a system and install it on your building’s door.”

Generic advice assumes you own the building. In Downtown Brooklyn most callers lease a suite, a floor or a store inside someone else’s building, and the street door, lobby, elevators and stairs are not theirs to change.

A tenant’s access project starts with the lease and the landlord’s alteration rules: which doors the tenant may control, whether readers must match a building standard, and when work may be done. Skipping that step is how a tenant ends up paying to remove hardware the landlord never approved.

We map every door from the sidewalk to your space, mark who controls each one, and design only for the doors you have the right to control, with the landlord’s consent in writing.

“Phone keys mean you can drop cards and fobs.”

Phone keys are convenient, and some Downtown towers are built around them; Allegion describes the 40-story Maxwell as using mobile credentials for apartments, the building and amenities.

But a phone runs out of battery, gets lost and gets replaced, and not everyone wants an app. amNY reported in 2019 that a Department of Buildings spokesman said technology-based entry may not replace a key-based tool, and that fobs and cards count as keys under the department’s protocols.

Every phone-key system we install also issues a physical card or fob to anyone who asks, and works when the phone does not.

“Any card reader can be added to an elevator.”

Quick answers describe elevator access as a reader in the cab. The reader is the easy part.

The connection to the elevator controller is the elevator contractor’s work, and it has to give way completely to firefighters’ operation. In a building whose cars are being modernized, the interface is redone on each new controller.

We supply the access side and test it with the elevator contractor, car by car, and never wire into an elevator controller ourselves.

“Locking every stair door keeps a tower secure.”

It is tempting to lock stair doors so nobody wanders between floors. In a New York City high-rise that is not allowed in the simple form.

Building Code section 403.5.3 permits stair doors to be locked from the stair side only with automatic release on fire detection, elevator recall or a fire command center signal, with re-entry signs, and section 403.5.3.1 requires two-way communication at least every fifth floor where doors are locked.

We design floor security around those rules, using the corridor and suite doors to control access and leaving stair re-entry to the fire alarm.

“An office badge system works the same after a conversion.”

Articles on office-to-residential conversions talk about plumbing, windows and floor depth. Entry systems get a line, if that.

Once the building is a multiple dwelling, the Multiple Dwelling Law sets rules for entrance doors and intercoms, and the Tenant Data Privacy Law requires consent, a privacy policy, minimal data and a 90-day limit on keeping authentication data unless it is anonymized. An office database that logs years of entries does not meet that.

For a conversion such as 141 Willoughby Street, we inventory what can be reused, then reset the system and its records for households rather than employees.

“Access control costs a few hundred dollars a door.”

National guides often quote the price of one part, a lock or a reader, and call it a door.

A controlled suite door with a reader or keypad, a strike, the cable, a programmed controller and ten fobs is $1,500 to $2,500 on our Brooklyn list. Readers on several office doors in a shared building are $4,000 to $12,000, and a full building system is $15,000 to $40,000.

Downtown pays the Brooklyn base list, with no surcharge for the neighborhood.

“A security desk in the lobby makes suite readers unnecessary.”

An attended lobby is a real advantage, and many Downtown buildings have one. But a desk watches the lobby, not your suite door at 7 a.m. on a Saturday.

Brooklyn Law School’s safety tips for building residents put the problem plainly: “Do not allow strangers to follow you into the building,” and “Never leave doors propped open.” Those are habits a desk cannot enforce on every floor. News 12 reported in January 2025 that the NYPD had concentrated patrols in the 84th Precinct, including the Fulton Mall corridor, after overall crime there rose nearly 25 percent in 2024, and that retail thefts fell 27 percent after the plan began, by the department’s own figures.

Numbers move both ways. A reader on the suite door does the same quiet job either way: only your people get in, and you know who did.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Read the Lease Before the Door

This describes how we approach a Downtown office job, not a particular customer. The first document we ask for is not a floor plan; it is the part of the lease or the building’s tenant handbook that covers alterations. It tells us which doors a tenant may change, whether the landlord insists on a particular reader or lock, whether the building’s own engineer has to be present, and whether drilling is allowed during business hours at all. Ten minutes with that page saves a week of rework, and it is often the difference between a reader the landlord approves and one the landlord asks you to remove.

Then we go above the ceiling. In a prewar building we look for the old routes, the space above a corridor’s dropped ceiling or a disused chase near the elevator core, so nothing is cut into masonry. In a newer building we look for the tenant’s IT closet and the nearest place a controller can sit on its own circuit. Only then do we look closely at the door: does it close and latch by itself, is the frame sound, and is it the kind of door, wood, hollow metal or glass, that suits the lock we have in mind. A good reader on a door that drifts open protects nothing.

FAQ

Downtown Brooklyn Access Control FAQ

Can visitors get a one-time code or link instead of someone walking out to let them in?
+

Yes, on most systems we install. Reception or the person expecting the visitor creates a guest pass with a start time, an end time and the doors it opens, and the system sends it by text or email as a code or a link that turns the visitor’s phone into a key. It stops working when the visit ends. In a building with a staffed lobby, the landlord’s desk usually still checks visitors in, so a guest pass for your suite door works alongside the building’s own sign-in rather than replacing it.

In an emergency, can we lock every controlled door in our office from one phone?
+

Yes. Most controllers support a lockdown command that sets every controlled door in the suite to refuse all credentials except a chosen group, such as managers or security staff, and it can be triggered from the app, from a button at reception or from the controller. Lockdown only stops people getting in. Every exit still opens from inside with one motion, and stair doors in a high-rise remain under the fire alarm’s control, so nobody is trapped. We set it up, label the button and show staff how to cancel it.

What does a wired access system give an office that a smart lock on the suite door does not?
+

A smart lock controls one door from one brand’s app, usually on batteries. A wired system controls many doors from one place, keeps working through a network outage on its own battery-backed controller, reports doors forced or held open, gives subtenants or departments their own administrators, and uses locks listed for commercial egress doors that release when the fire alarm sounds. For a two-person office behind an attended lobby, a good smart lock can be enough; once there is a server room, staff turnover or a landlord’s rules, a wired system is the better fit.

Can the system warn us when someone tries a card that has already been cancelled?
+

Yes. When a cancelled or expired credential is presented, the reader refuses it and the controller logs the attempt with the time, the door and the name the credential was issued to. You can choose to receive an alert for those attempts, which is useful when a former employee or a member whose agreement ended still has a card. Repeated refused attempts at one door in a short time can trigger their own alert, which often means someone is trying cards they found or copied.

During a fire evacuation, can the readers tell us who badged in and has not badged out?
+

Only if people badge out as well as in, which most offices do not require. Many systems offer a mustering report that lists everyone whose last recorded event was an entry, and it can be opened on a phone at the assembly point. It is only as accurate as the habits behind it, and it never replaces the building’s fire safety plan or the floor wardens’ headcount. Where an employer wants it, we add exit readers and train staff to use them.

Can each door opening in the log come with a picture of who walked through?
+

Yes, if a camera covers the door and the camera and access systems are linked, or if the door station has a built-in camera. Many platforms attach a still image or a short clip to each access event, so a suspicious entry at 6 a.m. can be checked in seconds instead of searching hours of recording. We place the camera to show the person at the door rather than the whole corridor, and in buildings with apartments the same privacy limits apply to images as to entry records.

Can our access cards double as printed photo ID badges for staff?
+

Yes. Many encrypted cards can be printed on with a company badge printer, carrying the employee’s photo, name and department on the front, while the chip inside opens the doors. If your building requires its own lobby badge, check whether the landlord’s system can read the same card; if not, staff may carry the building card for the lobby and the printed company card for the suite. We tell you which card stock suits your readers before you order a printer.

Can old and new credentials both work for a few weeks while we switch systems?
+

Often, yes. Many new readers can read both the old card format and the new encrypted one, so staff keep using their old cards while new ones are handed out, and on a set date the old format is switched off. Where the old controller has to come out first, we run the changeover after hours and enroll everyone in advance so the first morning works. A short overlap is fine; leaving the old copyable format active for months defeats the purpose of the upgrade.

Our suite’s exit door already has a panic bar. Can a reader be added without replacing it?
+

Usually. If the panic bar takes an electrified trim or an electric strike in the frame, the reader is added outside and the bar stays as the free exit inside. Some older or lighter bars cannot take electrified parts and are swapped for an electrified version of the same device. Either way the door keeps opening from inside with one push and no card, which is what the Building Code expects of an exit door. We check the bar’s model on the survey and price whichever route applies.

Can our IT team add and remove users automatically from our company directory?
+

On many cloud-managed systems, yes. They can sync with common company directories, so a new hire added to the directory gets a credential and a departing employee loses access when the account is disabled, without anyone opening the access app. That is the cleanest way to stop access outliving employment. We tell you before you buy whether the platform proposed for your suite supports your directory, and set up the groups so department changes in the directory move people between door groups too.

Can door alerts go to our team’s chat app or email instead of yet another app?
+

Usually. Most management platforms can send alerts by email, and many can also post to a chat channel or call a web address that a chat tool understands. That suits an office manager who already lives in email or a facilities team that works in one channel. We set alerts for the events that need a person, such as a door forced, held open or refused repeatedly, and keep ordinary openings out of the channel so the alerts are not ignored.
