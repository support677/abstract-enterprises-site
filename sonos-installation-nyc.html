The Neighborhood

Why New York City Homes Need Sonos Installation

Most Sonos trouble in this city starts with the building, not the speaker. Sonos publishes a short table of how much common materials weaken a wireless signal. Wood, glass and synthetic materials are rated low. Brick and marble are medium. Plaster and concrete are rated high, and metal very high. Those last three are the inside of a typical New York apartment: plaster on wood or metal lath in pre-war buildings, poured concrete floors and block walls in post-war towers, and steel framing everywhere from a Midtown condo to a gut-renovated brownstone floor-through. A speaker that streams flawlessly in a showroom can stutter in a bedroom two plaster walls away from the router.

Then there are the neighbors' networks. Sonos lists Wi-Fi routers, extenders and access points among the devices that cause interference, alongside cordless phones, baby monitors, wireless cameras and smart-home hubs. In a walk-up you might see twenty networks on your phone; in a high-rise, more. One Sonos owner on Reddit's r/sonos put it plainly: apartments and dense urban areas "can be a nightmare where everyone else's Wi-Fi bleeds into your space." That is not something a speaker can solve on its own. It is solved by where the router sits, which band and channel the system uses, and whether one Sonos product is wired.

The third problem is the network type. Sonos's own system requirements rule out guest networks, networks with a log-in portal page, wireless range extenders, powerline (Ethernet over Power) adapters, mobile hotspots and routers that broadcast only 5 GHz. Several newer rental buildings in the city provide building-wide Wi-Fi as an amenity, and that kind of managed network is where we hear about speakers that can't be found by the app. A Sonos community answer to exactly that complaint suggested what we usually do: put your own router behind the building's connection so the speakers live on a network you control.

Ceiling and wall speakers bring a different set of city problems. Cutting into a hundred-year-old plaster ceiling without cracking it is slow, careful work. Many post-war buildings have concrete slab ceilings with no cavity at all, and some landmark-era buildings combine steel beams with terracotta or brick arches above the plaster. Every wire has to reach an Amp somewhere in the apartment, through walls that were not built with a cable path in mind. Co-op and condo boards typically want to know about any work that cuts into walls or ceilings, and doorman buildings book contractors around a service elevator.

None of this means Sonos is the wrong choice for a New York home. It is a very good system for apartments, partly because so much of it can be wireless and partly because it grows one room at a time. It means the installation has to start with the network and the structure, and then the speakers. That is the order we work in.

New York City numbers that shape a Sonos job

  • 54 percent: the Rent Guidelines Board's 2026 Housing Supply Report says more than half of the city's apartments are in pre-1947 buildings, so the typical Sonos room here is walled and ceilinged in old plaster or masonry.
  • 3,705,000: housing units in the city in 2023, the figure the same report takes from the Housing and Vacancy Survey — nearly all of them sharing walls, floors and airwaves with neighbors.
  • High: the interference rating Sonos gives both plaster and concrete in its guide to reducing wireless interference; metal is rated very high.
  • 18 inches: the minimum distance Sonos recommends between its products and other electronic devices, which matters on a crowded media console.
  • 32: the maximum number of Sonos products in one system under Sonos's system requirements — plenty for a classic six, but worth knowing in a townhouse or an office.
  • 42 dBA: the limit the city's Noise Code fact sheet sets for music from a store, restaurant or bar, measured inside any apartment that receives the sound — the number behind every commercial Sonos zone we set up under residences.

Taken together, those figures describe a typical New York Sonos visit: older walls that weaken Wi-Fi, a network shared with hundreds of neighbors, a building with rules, and in commercial spaces, apartments overhead. The speakers are the easy part once those are handled.

What We Install

Sonos Installation Services in NYC

A Sonos job in the five boroughs is usually two or three of the services below in one visit. The order is always the same: get the network right, put the hardware where it belongs, then set up and tune the rooms in the app with you holding the phone.

Arc, Beam and Ray Mounting

The Sonos soundbar goes on the wall under the TV, on the TV's own mount, or on the console. We leave the gap Sonos asks for between bar and screen, fix the bracket with anchors chosen for the wall we find, and connect the bar to the TV port it actually needs.

Sub and Rear Surrounds

Adding a Sub, Sub Mini or a pair of Era speakers as rears turns the soundbar into a surround system. We place each one, pair it in the app, and plan where the rear speakers get power in a living room that often has two outlets at most.

Ceiling Speakers on a Sonos Amp

Passive in-ceiling speakers cut into the ceiling and wired back to an Amp, which joins them to the rest of the Sonos system. Kitchens, bathrooms and bedrooms where nobody wants a speaker on the counter. Scoped at a walkthrough, because the ceiling decides the method.

In-Wall Speakers

Flush speakers set into a stud wall, usually as the front pair of a media wall or as stereo music in a living room. Possible in drywall and many plaster walls; not in solid masonry or concrete, which we tell you before anything is marked.

Port to Your Existing Receiver

Keep the receiver and speakers you already love and add Sonos streaming through a Port, connected by analog or digital coaxial output, with the 12-volt trigger set so the amplifier wakes when music starts.

Multi-Room Setup and Grouping

Every product added to one system, each room named, rooms grouped the way you actually listen, music services signed in, and voice control switched on or off as you prefer. The $125 Sonos setup line on our price list covers this work.

Wired Backbone

An Ethernet run from the router to one Sonos product, or to a ceiling access point, so the system stops depending on a weak link through plaster. Sonos lists a wired connection as an option in its requirements, and in many pre-war apartments it is the single biggest fix.

Network Tune-Up for Sonos

Router placement, a band and channel plan that avoids the busiest neighbors, separate settings checked for anything Sonos doesn't support, and removal of the range extenders and powerline adapters that Sonos lists as unsupported.

Trueplay Tuning

Every room tuned with Sonos's Trueplay, which measures how sound reflects off the walls and furniture and adjusts the speaker for its spot. We bring a compatible iPhone for the advanced method, so an Android-only household isn't left untuned.

Building Specialists

Sonos Installation for New York City's Architecture

Sonos ships one way. The ceilings, walls and wiring paths it ends up in vary building by building. These are the building types we see most often in New York Sonos work and what each one changes about the job.

Pre-War Elevator Co-ops

Plaster on lath walls and ceilings, generous room sizes and thick masonry between apartments. Wi-Fi struggles through the plaster, so a wired backbone is common. Ceiling speakers are possible in many rooms but every cut is planned around joists, pipes and old wiring, and the board usually wants to know about it.

Brownstones and Townhouses

Several floors stacked on wood joists with plaster ceilings, party walls of brick and a router that usually lives on one floor. Multi-floor Sonos systems here nearly always need a wired link or a second access point; the joist bays also give the best cable paths in the city.

Post-War Towers

Concrete slab ceilings and block or concrete walls. Excellent for a soundbar bracket, very poor for running wire and difficult for Wi-Fi. Ceiling speakers normally mean a dropped soffit or an existing drop ceiling; otherwise wireless Era speakers on brackets carry the music.

New Condos

Drywall on metal studs, sometimes a dropped ceiling in kitchens and baths, and occasionally speaker or network pre-wire left by the developer. We trace what is already in the walls before suggesting anything new, because it is often there and unused.

Lofts and Conversions

Exposed brick, timber or concrete ceilings, columns and open plans. Sound bounces off hard surfaces, which is where Trueplay earns its keep, and cable paths are usually surface raceway or the voids above kitchens and bathrooms.

Two-Family Houses and Detached Homes

Common in Queens, Staten Island and the outer parts of Brooklyn and the Bronx. Drywall or plaster over wood framing, basements and attics for cable runs, and space for outdoor speakers on a deck. Closest to suburban work, with a tenant often on the other floor.

How each New York surface changes the Sonos install

Plaster and lath ceilings. The hole for an in-ceiling speaker is far larger than a screw hole: Sonos lists an 8.25-inch cut-out for its 6-inch in-ceiling speaker and 10.125 inches for the 8-inch. In old plaster, a cut that size has to be scored and cut slowly, with the lath supported so it doesn't shake loose keys of plaster around the opening. We mark the location, check the cavity through a small pilot hole first, and walk away from any spot where the plaster is already loose.

Concrete slabs. There is no cavity to put a speaker in and no path to fish a wire through. The realistic choices are a dropped soffit built by a carpenter, an existing drop ceiling in a kitchen or bathroom, or wall-mounted Sonos speakers that need only power. We say which before anyone opens a wall.

Steel and arch floors. Some of the city's oldest apartment houses were built with steel beams and brick or terracotta arches between them under the plaster. The Dakota on Central Park West, finished in 1884, is a documented example: rolled steel beams three to four feet apart with brick or terracotta arches, under concrete slabs. Ceilings like that are surveyed carefully before any speaker is promised.

Drop ceilings. The easiest ceilings in the city for speakers. Tiles lift out, wire runs above the grid, and the speaker drops into a tile or a support bridge. Common in renovated kitchens and in commercial spaces.

Metal studs and drywall. Good for in-wall speakers and soundbar brackets, as long as the fixings are made for light-gauge steel. Sonos's Era 300 wall mount is described as stud-mounting only and not for drywall, so on a metal-stud wall we locate the studs first and plan the speaker position around them.

Brick and stone. Strong for a soundbar or speaker bracket, impossible for hidden wire. Brick also rates medium for Wi-Fi loss in Sonos's table, so a router behind a brick party wall needs a better path to the speakers.

If you are renovating, the cheapest Sonos install happens before the drywall goes up. Speaker wire to ceiling locations and a network cable to the TV wall cost very little while the studs are open. Our structured cabling installation in NYC is often booked alongside the Sonos plan for exactly that reason.

Commercial Sonos Installation

Business Sonos Installation in NYC

Sonos is common in small New York businesses for the same reason it is common at home: staff can run it from a phone. Sonos markets installed systems to businesses as well, and a restaurant, boutique or office often ends up with ceiling speakers on Amps, a Port into existing gear and a couple of wall speakers. These are the settings we work in most.

Restaurants and Cafes

Dining-room ceilings with several speakers per Amp, a separate bar zone and a volume ceiling set low enough for the apartments upstairs. Wiring runs above drop ceilings or soffits where the room allows.

Boutiques and Showrooms

Even, low-level music across a long, narrow storefront, with speakers spaced so there are no loud and quiet patches between the door and the fitting rooms.

Offices and Studios

Background music in a reception area, a conference room soundbar on the display, and the network work that keeps Sonos discoverable on a business network rather than lost behind a guest Wi-Fi.

Salons, Barbershops and Gyms

Music all day from a front-desk phone or tablet, speakers mounted out of reach, and a short written note for staff on how to regroup rooms if someone changes them.

Building Amenity Spaces

Residents' lounges, gyms and roof-deck rooms in condo and rental towers, where the managing agent controls scheduling and the system has to be simple for staff to reset.

Medical and Professional Waiting Rooms

Quiet music and a TV, with speakers flush in the ceiling and every cable hidden for a clean, easily wiped finish.

Compliance callouts for Sonos systems in New York businesses

  • Music under apartments. The city's Noise Code fact sheet limits music from a commercial establishment such as a store, restaurant or bar to 42 dBA measured in any apartment that receives it. We place speakers away from party walls, agree a maximum level with the manager on the day, and show staff where that level sits on the app's volume slider.
  • Business networks. Sonos requires the app and every speaker to be on the same subnet and does not support WPA/WPA2 Enterprise security, guest networks or portal log-ins. On a business network that usually means a dedicated network for the music system, set up with your IT provider.
  • Cable in ceilings. Speaker and network cable above a commercial ceiling is run in cable types suited to the space it passes through, and Sonos's own in-ceiling manual tells installers to consult local building codes before running speaker wire through ceilings.
  • Power and permits. Amps and Ports need outlets. New outlets and any line-voltage wiring are work for a licensed electrician; we coordinate with yours and never add or move an outlet ourselves.
  • Landlord paperwork. Before a storefront fit-out the property manager will usually ask to see our insurance with the owner listed. That certificate costs you nothing and is generally emailed within the day.

A shop owner putting in music is often also fed up with the store's Wi-Fi or its old camera recorder. Our low-voltage installation services in NYC cover audio, data and security under one licence, and AESS AV and sound installation takes on larger systems that go beyond Sonos.

Your Questions Answered

Sonos Installation in NYC: Questions Answered

Below are the real worries we hear on the phone before a Sonos booking, sorted into six themes: whether the network will hold up, whether the ceiling or wall will take speakers, whether the building will allow it, how it will sound to the neighbors, which products to buy and how it fits a rental. Cost, warranty and scheduling are in the FAQ further down.

Network and Wi-Fi in a crowded building

Why does my Sonos keep dropping out in my apartment building?

Usually because the signal between the router and the speakers is weak or crowded, not because a speaker is faulty. Sonos rates plaster and concrete as high-loss materials for wireless signals, and it lists other routers, extenders, cordless phones, baby monitors and wireless cameras as common sources of interference. In a New York building you have all of those at once, most of them belonging to neighbors. A speaker with a marginal link plays fine at 2 PM and drops at 9 PM when everyone upstairs starts streaming.

The fix is in the network, in roughly this order: move the router out of the hall closet if it is buried there, keep Sonos products at least 18 inches from other electronics as Sonos recommends, pick a channel the neighbors aren't crowding, and if a room is still unreliable, wire one Sonos product or add a wired access point. We test the signal in each room before and after, so you can see the change rather than take it on trust.

My building provides the Wi-Fi — will Sonos work on it?

Often not as it comes. Sonos's system requirements exclude guest networks and networks with a log-in portal, require the app and every speaker to sit on the same subnet, and don't support WPA or WPA2 Enterprise security. Building-wide Wi-Fi in amenity rentals is frequently set up in one of those ways, which is why the app can't see speakers that are plainly powered on and connected.

The dependable answer is your own router, connected to the building's network or to a separate internet line, with your Sonos and your phone on it. We set it up, move the speakers across and check that the app finds every room. If your lease or building management restricts adding a router, we will tell you that plainly before you buy more speakers.

Should one Sonos be wired to the router, and can you run that cable in a finished apartment?

If any room struggles on Wi-Fi, yes. Sonos lists a wired setup, with one or more products connected to the router by Ethernet at all times, as a supported way to build the system, and in mesh networks it says the wired Sonos must connect to the primary node. In a pre-war apartment with the router in one corner, wiring the soundbar or an Amp often settles the whole system.

In a finished apartment we run that cable the least visible way the building allows: inside a stud wall where there's a cavity, along baseboards or door casings in a slim raceway painted to match, or through a closet. Concrete and brick walls mean surface routing. If the router and the TV are on opposite sides of the apartment, a wired ceiling access point near the speakers can be the neater answer.

My router sits by the front door and the living room is behind two plaster walls — what then?

Then the router is in the wrong place for Sonos, and most likely for everything else too. Internet providers in the city usually install the router wherever the cable enters, which is often the entrance hall or a bedroom closet. Two plaster walls between it and the TV is exactly the situation Sonos's interference table warns about.

Options, from simplest to most involved: move the router to a central spot if the provider's cable can be extended; add a wired access point near the living room; or wire one Sonos product back to the router. What we don't do is add a plug-in range extender or a powerline adapter, because Sonos lists both as unsupported and they cause many of the dropouts we are called to fix.

Ceilings, walls and speaker wire

Can ceiling speakers go into a pre-war plaster ceiling?

In many rooms, yes, with care and a survey first. A Sonos 6-inch in-ceiling speaker needs an 8.25-inch hole and 4.73 inches of depth behind the ceiling; the 8-inch model needs a 10.125-inch hole and 5.32 inches of depth. In plaster on wood lath there's usually room between the joists, but the opening has to be cut slowly and the lath held so the surrounding plaster doesn't let go. Sonos's own installation manual tells installers to survey for studs, conduit, pipes, ducts and other obstructions before cutting, and to probe through a small pilot hole if unsure.

Where plaster is already sagging, cracked in a web pattern or sounds hollow across a wide area, we don't cut it; no speaker should hang from plaster that isn't holding on. We show you the spot, suggest the nearest sound location, or recommend a plasterer before the speakers go in.

My ceiling is solid concrete — are in-ceiling speakers still possible?

Not directly into the slab. Many post-war towers have poured-concrete ceilings with the finish skimmed straight onto them, and there is neither depth for a speaker nor a path for wire. That is a property of the building, not something a better saw can change.

There are three honest alternatives. A carpenter can build a dropped soffit along one side of the room, which gives a cavity for speakers and cable; kitchens and bathrooms sometimes already have a drop ceiling we can use; or the room gets wireless Sonos speakers on wall brackets that only need a nearby outlet. We look at the room and tell you which one fits before you commit to buying in-ceiling speakers.

How does the speaker wire get from the ceiling back to the Amp without opening every wall?

By choosing where the Amp lives first. An Amp in a hall closet, a media cabinet or a bedroom closet near the speakers keeps the wire short, and the run can often follow the joist bay the speakers sit in to a wall cavity that drops to the closet. In brownstones, the joists give clean runs front to back; side-to-side runs cross joists and need careful drilling or a soffit.

Where a run would mean opening finished plaster, we look for an existing path first: a closet ceiling, a chase beside a riser, the space above kitchen cabinets. Only when none exists do we suggest an access cut, and we tell you its size and position so a plasterer can be booked alongside. A cable path is also why some customers choose wireless Sonos speakers for one room and in-ceiling for another.

Will the Era 300 wall mounts hold on my wall?

Only if they go into a stud. Sonos describes its Era 300 wall mount as stud-mounting only and not for drywall, with a ball joint that swivels 15 degrees up, down, left or right. That matters in New York, where the spot you want for a rear speaker may be a plaster wall with no stud nearby, a metal-stud partition or brick.

We locate the framing before agreeing the position and adjust it a few inches if needed. On brick and concrete we use masonry fixings rated for the load and suited to the mount; where neither works, a floor stand or shelf is the better answer, and we will say so rather than force a bracket into a wall that can't carry it.

The building, the board and the landmarks

Does cutting ceiling speaker holes count as an alteration in my co-op?

Often it does, and you should ask before booking. Co-op and condo alteration rules exist for work that cuts through walls and ceilings or touches electrical systems, and trade coverage of New York boards notes they may regulate any work touching fragile or old walls, even cosmetic work. Speaker holes in a ceiling, and cable run inside walls, can fall under that, while a soundbar on two anchors or wireless speakers on a shelf usually do not.

Ask the managing agent whether low-voltage speaker work needs an alteration agreement. If it does, we supply the scope, speaker locations, product sheets showing cut-out sizes and our insurance certificate, so the board reviews a clear plan. If the answer is no, we still send the certificate and confirm the building's contractor hours.

My building is in a historic district — does the Landmarks Preservation Commission need to approve speakers?

For interior Sonos work, usually not. The Commission says an LPC permit is needed for interior work only when the work also needs a Department of Buildings permit, when interior work affects the exterior, or when the interior itself is a designated interior landmark. Ceiling speakers and low-voltage wiring inside an apartment don't normally meet any of those.

Outdoor speakers are a different matter. Anything fixed to a facade, a stoop or a roof visible from the street in a historic district may need review, because most changes to front and rear facades there do. If you want speakers on a brownstone's rear deck or garden wall, we check before drilling. This is general information, not legal advice; your building's counsel or the LPC has the last word.

When can the work be scheduled in a doorman building?

Inside the building's contractor hours, which in many co-ops are weekdays during business hours. Trade guidance on New York alteration rules describes the traditional window as Monday to Friday, 9 AM to 5 PM, with weekend work limited, and contractors typically arranging the service elevator at least 48 hours ahead. Your building may differ, so we ask when you book.

That shapes the visit. A soundbar and app setup fits comfortably in a building window. Several rooms of ceiling speakers may be split across two weekday visits rather than squeezed into one. We book the service elevator through the super or the front desk, and we tell you in advance if a weekend date you asked for isn't allowed.

Sound, bass and the neighbors

Will a Sonos Sub bother the people downstairs?

It can, because bass travels through floors and structure far more easily than voices do. Sonos's guide allows the Sub 4 in a corner, against a wall, or under furniture with at least an inch of clearance above it, and its wireless connection lets it sit anywhere in the room. That flexibility is useful in an apartment: the Sub doesn't have to go in the corner that drives the most vibration into the floor.

We try two or three positions with you, sitting where you watch, and keep the one that gives even bass at the lowest level. A dense rug or an isolation pad under it helps on wood floors. After Trueplay, we set the Sub's level in the app and show you the night-listening setting on the soundbar so late films stay in your living room.

Do in-ceiling speakers send music into the apartment above?

Some sound does go up into the ceiling cavity, and in a wood-joist building that cavity is shared with the floor above. How much reaches the neighbor depends on the ceiling, the floor above and the volume, and it is worth discussing before a bedroom or a late-night living room goes into the plan.

Practical steps: keep ceiling speakers for background listening in kitchens, baths and hallways, set a sensible maximum in the app, and use the soundbar and Sub for loud films. Where noise upstairs is a real concern, wireless speakers on wall brackets are often the better choice for that room. We would rather tell you that now than install speakers you can't use after 10 PM.

Choosing the gear

Arc Ultra, Beam or Ray — which one suits my living room?

Size the bar to the TV and the room. Sonos lists the Arc Ultra at about 46.4 inches wide and the Beam (Gen 2) at about 25.6 inches, both connecting to the TV by HDMI; the Ray is the compact model and connects by optical cable. A 75-inch TV over a long wall suits an Arc Ultra; a 50-inch TV in a narrow living room is usually better served by a Beam; the Ray fits a bedroom or a studio and an older TV with an optical output.

Check one thing before you order: which audio outputs your TV has. If it has HDMI ARC or eARC, an Arc Ultra or Beam connects with one cable and the TV remote controls the volume. If it only has optical, the Ray connects directly. Send us the TV model and we will confirm before you buy.

Do I need a Sonos Amp, or can I keep my receiver and add a Port?

It depends on what you are keeping. The Port is a streamer: Sonos describes analog and digital coaxial outputs to a receiver or amplifier, an analog input for something like a turntable with a built-in preamp, and a 12-volt trigger that wakes a third-party amplifier when Sonos starts playing. If you love your receiver and speakers, the Port puts them on Sonos.

The Amp has its own amplifier and drives passive speakers directly, which makes it the usual choice for in-ceiling and in-wall speakers. Reviews list it at 125 watts per channel into 8 ohms, with an HDMI ARC input for a TV. We will tell you which one your plan needs, and how many.

How many ceiling speakers can one Amp run?

Sonos says the Amp reliably powers up to three pairs of its architectural speakers, six in total, when wired in parallel. In practice that is one room with six speakers, or a pair each in three nearby spaces that will always play together, such as a kitchen, a dining area and a hall.

If rooms need different music or volume, they need separate Amps, because one Amp is one Sonos room. That is the decision we make with you at the walkthrough: which spaces will ever play separately, and which never will. It sets how many Amps you buy and where the speaker wire goes.

Does Trueplay work if everyone in the house uses Android?

Partly. Sonos says most of its products need an iOS device for Trueplay tuning. Its newer speakers — Arc Ultra, Beam Ultra, Era 100, Era 100 Pro and Era 300 — can tune themselves with quick tuning from iOS or Android using their built-in microphones, while advanced tuning, where you walk the room with the phone, is iOS only. Architectural speakers on an Amp are tuned through the app with a supported iOS device.

We bring a compatible iPhone and run advanced tuning in each room, which Sonos says takes about three minutes per room. The room has to be reasonably quiet while it runs, and if you later move a speaker or change a stereo pair, Sonos advises tuning again.

Renting and moving

I rent — what Sonos setup leaves no holes?

More of one than people expect. A Sonos soundbar on the console, a Sub on the floor and Era speakers on stands or shelves need no drilling at all, and wireless rears can sit on bookcases at the back of the room. The network work — router placement, channel settings, removing an extender — leaves nothing behind either.

If your lease allows small holes, the soundbar can go on the wall or on the TV mount, and a paintable cord cover hides the cable. In-ceiling speakers are for owners, or for renters with written permission from the landlord, because the ceiling openings stay when you leave.

We're moving apartments — can you take the system down and set it up at the new place?

Yes. We take down the soundbar and any wall mounts at the old apartment, pack the hardware with its cables, and reinstall at the new one with anchors for whatever wall we find there. The system then has to join the new Wi-Fi, which is where moves usually go wrong; we do that on the visit and retune each room, because a new room sounds different.

Ceiling speakers normally stay with the apartment, since they are built in. If you are leaving behind an Amp and speakers, we can remove the Amp from your Sonos account so the next owner or tenant can set it up cleanly.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Sonos Installation in NYC

How these were chosen: our research tools could not load Google's AI-generated summaries, so we don't quote them. Instead, each heading below is a statement we found in print — in a national cost guide, a forum thread or product marketing — of the sort that search assistants pick up and repeat. Under each one is what changes when the system is going into a New York apartment or storefront.

"Sonos is plug-and-play — nobody needs an installer"

For one speaker in a house with a good router, that is close to true. It is also why so many Sonos systems in the city were set up by their owners and work most of the time.

Sonos's own pages draw the line differently. Its beginner's guide to in-ceiling speakers says architectural speakers often require a professional installer, and its guide to in-wall speakers says you'll likely need one, because an Amp has to be installed, holes cut and speaker cable run. Its system requirements also rule out common city network setups. Plug-and-play stops where the network or the ceiling starts.

If your system is a soundbar and two speakers on a good home network, you may need no one. If it is dropping out, crossing plaster walls or going into a ceiling, that is the point to call.

"Multi-room audio costs $500 to $1,000 per room"

That is Angi's figure for additional zones in a whole-home audio system, and it is a reasonable national number. Angi's same guide puts labor at $50 to $100 an hour for jobs of two to three hours.

A New York room doesn't have an average ceiling. A drop-ceiling kitchen is quick; a pre-war plaster ceiling is slow and careful; a concrete-slab room may need a carpenter's soffit or a different product entirely. Add a building insurance certificate, contractor hours and a service elevator, and the same "room" means different work in different buildings.

That's why AESS publishes the lines it can price in advance — the $125 Sonos setup, soundbar mounting, brackets, wall surcharges — and prices ceiling and wall speakers after a walkthrough, within the $800 to $2,500 per zone range published on our NYC AV and sound installation page.

"Any Wi-Fi will do — add an extender if the signal is weak"

Extenders are the standard advice for weak Wi-Fi, and quick answers repeat it for speakers too.

Sonos's system requirements list wireless range extenders and Ethernet-over-power adapters as unsupported, alongside guest networks, portal log-ins, mobile hotspots and 5 GHz-only routers. Following the generic advice can make a Sonos system worse.

What works in a plaster-and-concrete apartment is a better router position, a wired access point, or one Sonos product wired to the router — all supported by Sonos and all things we do on a visit.

"Ceiling speakers can go in any ceiling"

Many DIY videos make in-ceiling speakers look like a drywall job: trace the template, cut, wire, clip in. In a suburban house with drywall ceilings and an attic above, that's largely accurate.

In New York, the ceiling might be plaster on lath, a concrete slab with no cavity, or steel beams with masonry arches. Sonos's in-ceiling speakers need an 8.25- or 10.125-inch opening and up to 5.32 inches of depth, and its manual tells installers to survey for pipes, ducts and conduit first and to consult local building codes before running speaker wire through ceilings.

We survey each ceiling before you buy speakers for it, and we will tell you when a room is better served by wireless speakers on brackets.

"Trueplay works on any phone"

Sonos's marketing for its newer speakers mentions tuning from iOS or Android, and summaries generalize that to the whole range.

Sonos's support article is more precise: most Sonos products need an iOS device for Trueplay; the Arc Ultra, Beam Ultra, Era 100, Era 100 Pro and Era 300 can use quick tuning from either platform; and advanced tuning is iOS only. Architectural speakers on an Amp need a supported iOS device.

An Android-only household can still have every room tuned — we bring a compatible iPhone and run advanced tuning room by room during the visit.

"Sonos's app problems are fixed, so setup is simple again"

Sonos released a redesigned app, version 80.0, on May 7, 2024. Trade press reported problems with volume control, grouped rooms and missing features, and the company's chief executive publicly apologized and set out a schedule of fixes.

The app has been updated many times since, and most systems we set up today go smoothly. But many owners lost confidence in grouping and regrouping, and some older products set up on the S1 app can't use the newer Wi-Fi modes or WPA3 security that a modern router offers.

Part of every visit is checking which app and software each product runs, confirming every room appears and groups correctly, and showing you how to regroup without starting again.

"Put the subwoofer in the corner for the most bass"

Corner placement for maximum bass is classic home-theater advice, and Sonos's own guide allows the Sub in a corner.

In a pre-war building with wood-joist floors, the corner is where bass couples most into the structure — and into the bedroom below. The goal in an apartment is even bass at the couch at the lowest level, not the most bass possible.

Because the Sub is wireless and Sonos permits it under furniture with an inch of clearance, we can usually find a spot along the front wall or under the console that sounds right without shaking the floor. We test positions with you and set the Sub level after Trueplay.

Network First

Building a Sonos Network That Survives a New York Building

Most Sonos service calls we take in the city come back to the network. This is the checklist we work through on every install, drawn from Sonos's support pages and from what we find in apartments.

  • A supported router. One that broadcasts 2.4 GHz as well as 5 GHz, uses WPA2 or WPA3 Personal security, and puts the app and every speaker on the same subnet. Guest networks, portal log-ins and enterprise security are out.
  • A sensible router position. Central rather than at the front door, out of metal cabinets and away from the TV's back panel, with at least 18 inches between Sonos products and other electronics as Sonos recommends.
  • No extenders or powerline adapters. Sonos lists both as unsupported. We remove them and replace them with a wired access point where coverage needs help.
  • One wired product where it helps. In plaster-and-concrete apartments, a single Ethernet connection to the soundbar, an Amp or a Port often steadies the whole system. On a mesh network, Sonos says it must go to the main node.
  • Channels picked for the building. We look at which channels the neighbors crowd and set the router to avoid them, then check signal in each Sonos room.
  • Managed switches set correctly. Where a building or office uses managed network gear, the switch settings that Sonos systems rely on for discovery are checked with whoever runs that network.
  • Current software. Every product updated, and older products set up on the S1 app identified, because they can't use the newer Wi-Fi modes or WPA3.

None of this is visible once the speakers are playing, which is the point. A system built this way should still be working the day a new neighbor moves in with a powerful router.

Field Notes

Field Notes on New York Sonos Rooms

How our installers survey a Sonos room in an older apartment — standing practice, not one particular job

The first thing out of the bag isn't a drill, it's a phone. Before touching a speaker we walk from the router to each Sonos room checking signal strength and counting the networks around us. If the bedroom reads poorly through two plaster walls, there's no point mounting anything until the network plan is settled.

The second check is the ceiling. A knock tells us a lot: drywall sounds hollow and light, plaster rings dense, and a dull, unchanging thud usually means concrete. In a plaster ceiling we press around the planned speaker spot for give, and drill one small pilot hole to probe the cavity for pipes, cable or a joist before marking the full cut-out.

The third is the closet. Every in-ceiling plan depends on where the Amp can live with power, ventilation and a cable path, so we find that spot before promising speakers anywhere. If no closet works, the plan changes before you buy anything.
