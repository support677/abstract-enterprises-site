The Neighborhood

Why City Line Properties Need Security Camera Installation

City Line takes its name from a border, and the border still shapes how a camera job is planned here. Forgotten NY explains that between 1886 and 1898 this corner really was the city line of Brooklyn, the edge of the old city before consolidation, and the name stayed after Brooklyn became a borough. The elevated railroad came first: Wikipedia records a station called City Line opening on the Fulton Street el in 1894, later renamed Grant Avenue and closed in 1956, when the underground Grant Avenue station of the A line replaced it. Sources still disagree about where the neighborhood starts and stops. Forgotten NY runs it from Conduit Boulevard south toward Flatlands Avenue, east of Euclid Avenue and west of a borough line that zigzags along Drew Street, Liberty Avenue, 75th Street, Dumont Avenue and 78th Street. CityNeighborhoods.nyc uses Atlantic Avenue to the north, Conduit Boulevard to the south, Euclid Avenue to the west and the Queens line to the east. Most accounts treat it as a part of East New York, served by Brooklyn Community Board 5 and the 75th Precinct. Urban Omnibus points out that the border between Brooklyn and Queens cuts across streets and even property lines, which is why we confirm the borough for every address before quoting rather than relying on any map's shading.

The houses set the terms. City-Data's breakdown of City Line's housing shows structures with two units making up about 34.7 percent of all units and attached townhouses another 11.8 percent, and CityNeighborhoods.nyc describes brick two-family homes with decorative cornices, pastel-painted fronts and tiny front gardens. The building records on a single street show the spread of ages. On Eldert Lane, StreetEasy lists number 462 as a two-story one-family from 1915, number 452 as a two-story, two-unit house from 1920 classed as a two-family converted from a one-family, number 477 as a two-unit house from 1955, and number 302 as a one-family attached or semi-detached house in the 75th Precinct. For cameras, three things follow. An attached house offers only a front and a back to mount on. A converted house often has two front doors where it once had one. And a 1915 house and a 1955 house on the same block may hide entirely different paths for cable, from open basement joists to poured floors and newer drywall. Current listings on Forbell Street, Eldert Lane, Drew Street, Lincoln Avenue and Atlantic Avenue show the same mix, and Redfin's City Line listings lean heavily toward two-families, three-families and mixed-use buildings with a store on the ground floor.

The people and the shopping streets matter just as much. City Limits reported in 2013 that a Bangladeshi enclave had grown up around Forbell Street, where families bought neighboring houses one by one until there was room to build a mosque, and that halal groceries and sellers of South Asian women's clothing had taken over storefronts on Liberty Avenue once held by Latino and Italian businesses. Counts of mosques in the area run from seven in the City Limits piece to eight in a later 6sqft guide. Urban Areas describes residents from Bangladesh, the Dominican Republic, Guyana and Puerto Rico, and the Asian American Federation's East New York profile found that half of the Bangladeshi residents in its study area had limited English. That last point changes the handover more than the hardware: we name every camera in plain words, write the export steps on paper, and ask that the person who will actually use the recorder, often a parent or a grandparent who is home during the day, be there when we show how it works. Many City Line houses hold an owner's family on one floor and tenants on another, and a camera plan has to work for both.

The safety picture is mixed, and we would rather lay it out than lean on it. Hoodline describes the 75th Precinct, headquartered at 1000 Sutter Avenue and covering East New York and Cypress Hills, as one of the largest and busiest in the city, and reported 700 grand larceny complaints there by mid-October 2026, down 7 percent from 753 a year earlier. A precinct data site lists 17,972 reported crimes, 9,133 arrests and 36 shootings in the precinct for 2025. A private crime index gives vehicle theft as City Line's highest concern and burglary as its lowest risk; that is an estimate, not police data. On Liberty Avenue, NBC New York reported a trio filmed taking a sledgehammer to a jewelry store's front window, and CBS New York reported, in an older story from the Cypress Hills stretch, burglars filmed sliding under a security gate to get into a jewelry store. In the fall of 2026 News 12 reported an NYPD pattern of 19 commercial burglaries across Brooklyn between August 3 and September 10 in which two people got in by throwing something through a window, without naming precincts. None of this ranks a single block. What it shows is that cars at the curb and storefront glass are where cameras here are asked to do their work.

City Line is also changing. City Limits and Brownstoner report that the 2016 East New York rezoning, which covered almost 200 blocks and mapped mandatory affordable housing along Atlantic, Liberty, Fulton and Pitkin Avenues, has brought large new buildings to Atlantic Avenue, including a 13-story, 229-apartment building at 2683 Atlantic Avenue, the 275-apartment Chestnut Commons, and 400 affordable apartments planned by Phipps Houses on the former Chloe Foods site between Chestnut Street and Euclid Avenue. Smaller infill buildings have gone up too: StreetEasy shows 44 Euclid Avenue as a four-story, 12-unit condo built in 2020. City Limits also describes investors buying and renovating two- to four-family houses nearby, which means more houses changing hands with new drywall over old wiring and, sometimes, a camera left behind by the seller. Each of those situations needs a slightly different camera plan, and the sections below take them one at a time.

What City Line owners tend to ask for next. Once the stoop and rear cameras are working, owners of two-families often want the front door picture to reach a handset or tablet on each floor, or a keypad on the lower door so relatives stop copying keys. We leave a labeled spare port on the recorder and, where a door system is likely later, a spare cable to the stoop, so the basement ceiling only has to be opened once.

The Back of the Row

Attached on Both Sides: Getting a Camera to the Back of a City Line House

On a detached house the camera plan can walk around the building. On an attached City Line house it cannot. The front faces the sidewalk, the back faces a narrow yard, and the two side walls are shared with the houses next door, so there is no side alley to watch, no side door to cover and no outside wall to run cable along from front to back. That one difference explains most of what we do on these blocks. The front of the house is easy to reach and easy to see from the street, and it is where nearly everyone already has something, often a video doorbell. The back is the opposite: out of sight, reached from the street only by going through the house or over the yards of the neighbors, and usually the last place anyone puts a camera. Yet on a row of attached houses the yards tend to be divided by low fences, and someone who gets into one yard can often reach several. A camera plan that ends at the stoop leaves the most private side of the house unrecorded.

Getting a wired camera to the back without stapling cable across the outside is a matter of finding the house's own path. The most common one is the basement. On many City Line houses the basement ceiling is open joists, or at least has an unfinished utility side, running from the front foundation wall to the rear. A cable can travel along those joists and leave the house through a small opening drilled through a mortar joint in the rear wall, sloped slightly down toward the outside and sealed, then rise a short way to a camera above the yard door. Where the basement has been finished, often as a rented space, we look for a closet stack, a pipe chase or the gap beside a chimney breast that runs up through the floors. The third route is the roof edge: from the top floor, through the ceiling into the space under the roof, out at the rear parapet or cornice. We only use a roof route on the owner's own roof, never across the neighbor's, even when the roofs look like one continuous surface.

Wireless cameras are the tempting shortcut here, and on these houses they disappoint more often than not. The router usually sits in the front room where the internet line comes in. Between it and the back yard are interior walls, a stair, sometimes a masonry chimney, and then the rear brick wall itself. A wireless camera on the rear wall may connect on the day it is installed and then drop out on a damp evening, or record choppy video exactly when it matters. One battery camera at the back can be a reasonable stopgap for a renter who cannot drill, but for an owner who wants the yard recorded every night, a cable through the basement is the dependable answer. We say so plainly at the walk-through and price both if you want to compare.

The rear extension problem

Many attached houses carry a one-story addition at the back, a kitchen or a bedroom pushed into the yard, with a flat roof that sits just below a second-floor window. That roof is a step up for anyone who has already reached the yard. A camera on the main rear wall, aimed down and out, can cover the yard door and the extension roof at once if it is placed with that in mind, rather than pointed only at the ground. Where the extension itself has a back door, that door often deserves its own camera at head height.

The front: deterrence and detail are two cameras, not one

A camera high on the front wall makes the house look watched and records the street, but at that height it shows the tops of heads. A camera close to the stoop door shows faces but not much of the curb. On most City Line houses we recommend both, each set for its own job, rather than one camera asked to do two things badly. If an existing doorbell camera already covers the stoop well, we leave it in place and add the high camera.

Two Entrances

One Stoop, Two Front Doors: Planning Cameras for City Line's Converted Two-Families

The StreetEasy record for 452 Eldert Lane describes a house built in 1920 as a one-family and later converted into two units. That kind of conversion is common across City Line, and it usually leaves a recognizable front: steps up to the original door, which now leads to the upper apartment, and a second door tucked under the stoop or beside it, a few steps down, which leads to the lower apartment. Each household uses its own door. Packages arrive at both. Visitors sometimes knock at the wrong one. A single camera aimed from the top of the stoop records the upper door well and the lower door poorly, because the lower door sits in shadow under the steps and behind the stoop wall. Two small cameras, one for each door, almost always work better than one wide one trying to see both.

The lower door is the harder of the two to record. It is darker, closer to the ground and often framed by an iron gate or a short wall. A camera mounted on the underside of the stoop, or on the front wall just beside the lower door at about head height, looks straight at anyone standing there. In the evening the area under a stoop can be the darkest spot on the front of the house, so we check the existing light. If there is a fixture, we aim to use it rather than fight it; if there is none, a low-light camera or a small motion light added by the owner's electrician keeps the picture in color. Cable for the lower door is usually the shortest run on the whole job, since the basement wall is right behind it.

Then comes the question of who sees what. In a converted two-family the owner often lives on one floor and rents the other, or two branches of one family share the house. We set up separate accounts on the recorder before we leave. The owner sees every camera. Each household can be given its own door, the shared front garden and the stoop, and nothing of the other household's door if that is what everyone prefers. Where the back yard belongs to one unit, its camera goes on that unit's account and the owner's. These choices take ten minutes at the handover and save a great deal of friction later, so we ask about them at the start.

When the house goes from two units to three

Some owners in City Line add a third legal unit, often in a finished lower level. A third household means a third entrance to cover, a third account, and in most cases the first truly shared indoor space, a common hallway or stair. Common-area cameras should show the doors to the street and the stair, never the inside of an apartment when its door opens. If you are planning that conversion, tell us early, and we can pull spare cable while the contractor still has the walls open.

The recorder belongs to the person responsible for it

We put the recorder in the owner's space: a closet in the owner's apartment or a dry, lockable corner of the basement that the owner controls. Tenants get views through their own accounts on their own phones and never need to touch the box. If the owner lives elsewhere, we agree in writing who holds the main password and who gets alerts.

Attached brick two-families: brick, mortar and a painted front

The brick two-family is the house we expect most in City Line, and the outer wall decides how the mounts go on. We drill into mortar joints whenever the camera position allows, because a mortar joint can be repointed cleanly and a cracked face brick cannot be put back. Each mount uses masonry anchors sized for the camera and its bracket, set in a bed of exterior sealant so water does not track into the wall behind it. Many fronts on these blocks have been painted, and CityNeighborhoods.nyc mentions pastel-painted facades in particular. Paint on brick can flake at the edge of a new hole, so we score the paint around each anchor before drilling and leave the immediate area sealed and clean; a small touch-up is the owner's choice, and we tell you in advance if the paint looks likely to lift. Cornices are often pressed or formed metal over a wood frame rather than stone. They can carry a small, light camera if the frame behind them is sound, and the space behind a cornice sometimes gives cable a hidden path across the front. We open nothing on a cornice until we have looked at its condition from a ladder.

Houses from 1915 and houses from 1955 on the same block

Eldert Lane alone has houses recorded from 1915, 1920 and 1955, and the difference shows up when the drill goes in. The older houses usually have plaster on wood lath inside, open basement ceilings that have been rewired several times, and old bell, phone and coaxial wire from earlier owners. Their basements are generous for cable runs. The 1955 houses can have more solid interior construction and, in some cases, a basement that was finished early in its life, which closes off the easy route. We do not assume one from the street; we look in the basement first, then decide whether the run goes along the joists, up a closet or along the roof edge.

Renovated and flipped houses: new walls, old surprises

City Limits describes investors buying two- to four-family houses near Atlantic Avenue and redoing them, sometimes with a uniform modern look residents nicknamed "gentrification gray." A renovated house can look perfect inside and still have a camera on the front with no recorder anyone can find, a cable cut off flush in a new ceiling, or a doorbell registered to the previous owner's account. Before adding anything, we trace what is there, reset or remove devices that cannot be handed over cleanly, and route new cable through the basement and closets so the fresh drywall stays intact.

New buildings on Euclid and Atlantic Avenues: one plan, made early

The new condo and rental buildings have poured concrete or block walls, drywall corridors, lay-in ceilings in common areas and a riser closet that ties the floors together. Camera cable in a building like this belongs in the construction schedule, running above corridor ceilings and through one planned core per wall, not drilled in later through a dozen small holes. The views a new building needs are the street door from both sides, the mailboxes and package area, the elevator landing or stair at the lobby, any trash or side door, and parking if the building has it. In a condo like 44 Euclid Avenue the board decides about common areas; in a rental, the owner does. We work from the drawings with the developer or contractor, mark each camera and cable path, and leave a labeled port and pull string for anything added later.

Retail and community space in the new buildings from the rezoning, where the landlord's system and the tenant's system meet.

Mosques and Community Groups

Places of worship and nonprofits such as BACDYS, which City Limits and BACDYS describe as a lead community organization here.

Groceries and butcher counters on Liberty Avenue

A grocery's camera job is mostly about three places: the door, the register and the counter where goods are weighed or cut. The door camera sits just inside at about head height, framed so each customer coming in fills the picture for a moment. The register camera sits above and slightly in front of the cashier, aimed at hands, cash and the counter top rather than the card terminal's keypad. At a butcher or deli counter, a camera can be aimed so the scale's display and the item on it are both readable, which is useful when a customer questions a weight or staff want a record of what was handed over. Refrigerated cases and walk-ins bring condensation, so cameras near them are chosen and placed to avoid fogging, and nothing is mounted where it will be splashed during cleaning. The aisle overview comes from a back corner, high enough that tall shelving does not block it.

Jewelry stores: the window, the gate and the cases

The two Liberty Avenue jewelry incidents in the research happened in two different ways: a window broken with a sledgehammer, and burglars sliding under a security gate. A camera plan has to cover both. Inside the glass, a camera aimed at the window and the sidewalk beyond records the approach and the moment of impact, and because it is inside, it keeps recording after the glass breaks. A second view from the back of the store covers the cases and the path from the window to the cases. The bottom edge of the roll-down gate is a separate concern: a camera low and to one side inside the store, pointed along the floor toward the gate line, shows anyone coming under it. The recorder goes in a locked room or cabinet away from the sales floor, on battery backup, so cutting power at the front does not end the recording. Where an alarm is part of the store's protection, glass-break and door sensors on that alarm do a job cameras cannot, which is calling for help while it happens.

Clothing shops: the counter and the floor, never the changing area

Clothing shops on Liberty Avenue tend to have racks standing close to the window and a long, narrow floor. Cameras go at the door, over the counter and at each end of the floor, mounted high enough to see over the racks. Changing rooms and any curtained area where customers try on clothes are never on camera, and we check each view for mirrors that might reflect into one. If the shop wants a record of what goes toward the changing area, the camera stays on the open floor outside it.

Registering a store camera with the NYPD: what it does and does not mean

NYC 311 describes a program that lets any business with a security camera share live video with the NYPD during emergencies, at no cost. When the Mayor's Office announced the earlier retail-theft pilot, it drew a line between two steps: registering a camera, which tells the NYPD where cameras are, and integrating a camera, which gives the NYPD real-time access to its feed. A shop can decide about each step separately. We set up the recorder so its clock, camera names and export work properly either way, and if the shop decides to register or integrate, we help with the technical side the program asks for. That decision belongs to the business owner, not to us.

Rules and limits for business cameras in City Line

  • Private spaces stay off camera. Restrooms, changing areas and prayer or staff rooms are never recorded, and mirrors are checked.
  • Keypads and screens stay out of frame. Register cameras watch hands and cash, not PIN pads, receipts with card numbers or computer screens.
  • The landlord controls the outside. On a Liberty Avenue store-and-apartments building, the facade above the gate, the sign band and the residential door belong to the building owner, and we need written agreement before mounting there.
  • The residential door is separate. A shop's cameras cover the shop; the tenants' door beside it is the building owner's decision and, ideally, a separate system or account.
  • Places of worship decide their own views. At a mosque or church, cameras usually watch the doors, the shoe or coat area by the entrance and the sidewalk, and the congregation's leadership decides whether any camera covers the prayer space.
  • City property is off limits. Nothing goes on lampposts, signposts, bus shelters or sidewalk trees.
  • Pictures, not conversations. We set business cameras to record video without audio.
  • Fire-rated openings stay rated. Any hole through a fire-rated wall or floor between a store and the apartments above is sealed with rated firestop.
  • New power is an electrician's work. If a camera location needs a new outlet or circuit, a licensed electrician does that part, and the quote says so.
  • Paperwork on request. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431 with insurance in place, and we send proof to landlords and managers before the visit when asked.
Field Notes

Field Notes: What We Look at First in an Attached City Line House

On an attached house the walk-through runs in a straight line, and the order matters. I start on the sidewalk and look up: is there a cornice, is the brick painted, are there old brackets or a dead camera from an earlier owner, and where is the internet line coming in. Then the stoop: how many doors, where the bell or doorbell is, how dark it is under the steps, and how close the neighbor's stoop sits. Inside, the basement tells me most of what I need. If the ceiling is open from the front wall to the back wall, the job is straightforward and the quote reflects it. If the basement is finished and rented, I ask to see the closets on every floor and the space under the roof, because the route will be vertical instead of horizontal. At the back I look at the yard door, the extension roof, the fences on both sides and what the neighbors can see from their windows. Last, I ask who lives on each floor and who will actually use the recorder. That answer decides the accounts, where the recorder sits and who is standing next to me at the handover. Nothing gets ordered until all of that is written down.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in City Line: Questions Answered

Attached brick houses

Our row house is attached on both sides, so there is no side alley. How does cable from the recorder reach a camera on the back of the house?

Usually along the basement ceiling. Most attached houses here have a basement running the full depth of the house, and if its ceiling is open joists on at least one side, a cable can run from the recorder to the rear foundation or rear brick wall and leave through a small opening drilled in a mortar joint, angled slightly downward and sealed, a short distance below the camera. If the basement is finished, we look for a closet stack, a pipe chase or a chimney gap that runs up through the floors, or go through the top-floor ceiling to the rear roof edge. Running the cable across the outside of the brick from front to back is the last resort, and on an attached house it rarely works anyway, because the side walls are shared.

Our two-family has a pressed-metal cornice along the roof line. Can a camera be mounted under it, or does it have to go on the brick?

Often it can, if the cornice is sound. Most cornices on houses like these are formed metal over a wooden frame, not solid stone, so the camera's anchors have to reach that frame or a solid block behind it, not just the thin metal. Before deciding, we look at the cornice up close from a ladder for rust, loose seams or soft wood. If it is in good shape, the space behind it can also hide the cable as it crosses the front. If it is weak, the camera goes on the brick just below it, where it is still out of easy reach and partly sheltered from rain.

A previous owner painted our brick front a pastel color. What happens to that paint where the camera mounts go in?

Paint on brick can chip or flake around a new hole, especially if it is old or was put on over dirty brick. We score the paint around each anchor point before drilling so the edge stays clean, drill into the mortar joint where the camera position allows, and set each anchor in exterior sealant so no water gets behind the paint. If the paint looks likely to lift, we say so before starting. A small touch-up with the same color afterward is up to you; we leave the area sealed and tidy either way, and the camera color is chosen to sit quietly against the wall.

Can a camera on our flat roof watch the row of back yards, instead of putting one on the rear wall?

It can see farther, but it usually records less that you can use. A camera at the rear roof edge looks down steeply over the yard and can take in the fences on both sides, which is useful for seeing someone cross into your yard. What it cannot do well is show a face, because from that height it sees the top of a head. For most houses the better answer is a camera on the rear wall at about the height of the first-floor ceiling, aimed at the yard door and along the fence lines, with a roof-edge camera added only if you want the wider picture. Any roof-mounted camera goes on your own roof and nowhere on the neighbor's, and its cable comes through your own top-floor ceiling.

Two doors, two households, two neighbors

Our converted two-family has a door at the top of the stoop and a second door below it for the lower unit. Which entrance should get the better camera spot?

Both deserve their own camera, but if you must pick, the lower door usually needs the more careful placement. It sits in shadow under the stoop, close to the ground and partly hidden by the stoop wall, so anyone standing there is harder to see from the street and from the upper door. A small camera mounted beside it at about head height, or on the underside of the stoop looking at it, records a face and the steps down. The upper door is easier: it is in open light and many owners already have a doorbell camera there. Each camera is then assigned to the right household's account.

Behind our row, the yards are separated only by low fences. Which way should the yard camera face?

Toward your own yard door and along your own fence lines, not out across the neighbors' yards. Mount it on your rear wall a little to one side, so it looks diagonally across your yard toward the far corner. That angle shows anyone coming over either side fence and walking toward your door, and it keeps the picture filled with your own space. If a neighbor's yard or window still falls into the frame, we block that part out in the camera's settings. A camera aimed straight out from the house mostly records the back of the next row of houses, which is less useful to you and more likely to upset the people living there.

Our next-door neighbor says the camera on our rear extension looks into their fenced yard. Do we have to move it?

It is worth taking seriously. New York's Civil Rights Law §52-a gives a resident a civil claim against a neighbor who records them in a place where they reasonably expect privacy, and a fenced back yard behind attached houses may be treated that way. In practice the fix is usually small: we turn the camera a few degrees, change the lens setting so it covers less, or black out the part of the picture that shows their yard. Most cameras let us draw those masked areas exactly. We are glad to come out, look at the view together with you, and adjust it so it covers your door and fence and not theirs.

The Brooklyn-Queens line

Our block is cut by the Brooklyn-Queens line. Does it matter for the camera job which side of the line our house sits on?

It matters for a few practical things, so we check before quoting. Urban Omnibus notes that the border runs across streets and property lines here, and Forgotten NY traces it along Drew Street, Liberty Avenue, 75th Street, Dumont Avenue and 78th Street. Which side you are on determines the borough on the paperwork, the precinct an officer comes from when you report something and want to hand over footage, and the rate area on your quote, since our Queens pricing differs from our Brooklyn pricing. Liberty Avenue shops

Customers sometimes question the weight at our halal butcher counter. Can a camera show the scale display clearly enough to settle it?

Usually, yes, if the camera is chosen and placed for that one job. The scale's digits are small and often lit, so a wide camera over the whole counter will show a blur. A camera with a narrower lens, mounted above and slightly to the customer's side of the scale, can record the display and the meat on the scale in the same picture. We test it with the counter lights on, adjust the camera's exposure so the lit digits do not wash out, and keep it out of reach of splashes from cutting and cleaning. That camera records continuously and is named so staff can find it quickly when a question comes up.

NYPD reported a 2026 pattern of Brooklyn store break-ins where thieves threw something through the window. Should our Liberty Avenue shop add glass-break sensors as well as cameras?

Cameras and sensors do different jobs, and for a storefront the two together make sense. A camera inside the glass records who threw the object, when, and which way they went, and it keeps recording after the window breaks if it is mounted inside. What a camera does not do on its own is call for help at that moment. Glass-break and door sensors on an alarm system can trigger a siren and a notification the instant the glass goes. News 12's report on 19 such burglaries between August 3 and September 10 did not name precincts, so it says nothing about your block in particular, but it does describe the method. We can quote cameras alone or plan the camera system so sensors can be added later.

Who Decides

Owners, Boards, Landlords and Tenants: Who Signs Off on a City Line Camera

Most camera jobs on City Line houses need no city filing, and that makes it easy to skip the question that matters most: whose wall is it, and whose decision. Here is how it works out on the kinds of property in this neighborhood.

Owner-occupied two-families. The owner decides what goes on the front, the back, the roof and the shared stoop of the house. Where title is held by family members who do not live there, we ask for written approval from whoever holds title or manages it. Tenants are told what the outdoor cameras cover and given access to the views agreed on. A tenant can put a small camera inside their own apartment, aimed at their own door or rooms, without the owner's involvement.

Attached neighbors. The walls you share with the houses next door are not yours alone. We mount on your own front and back, never on a party wall's exposed edge or a shared parapet, and we never run cable across a neighbor's roof. If a neighbor wants cameras too, we are happy to quote separately, but each house's system stays its own.

Condos and co-ops. In a building like the 12-unit condo on Euclid Avenue, the board controls the lobby, halls and outside walls. Guzov LLC notes that a co-op's rules may even bar a shareholder from putting a video doorbell in a common area, and condo rules often work the same way. We ask for the board's written approval before planning common-area cameras, and we put the handover of logins to the board in writing.

Liberty Avenue buildings. The building owner decides the facade, the sign band and the residential door; the store tenant decides the inside of the store, subject to the lease. Where both want cameras, we plan two systems or one system with strictly separate accounts.

Public housing. At Cypress Hills Houses and the East New York City Line development, NYCHA controls the buildings and grounds under its own design guidelines and contracts. A resident there can discuss indoor cameras inside their own apartment, but anything on doors, halls or grounds is NYCHA's decision, not a private installer's.

Required or optional. One New York compliance guide notes that no general city law requires cameras in apartment buildings, and that holds for two-families too. Owners add them because they want a record, which is why we write down what each camera covers and how long it keeps footage.

Openings and seals. Every hole through brick is sloped and sealed against water. In a three-family or a mixed-use building, any opening through a fire-rated floor or wall is sealed with rated firestop before we leave.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in City Line

Quick answers from search tools, cost sites such as Angi and HomeAdvisor, and AI summaries tend to describe City Line either as a corner of generic East New York or as a public-housing district. These are claims we see repeated about cameras here, written as summaries rather than quotations, followed by what the sources and the work actually show.

"Wireless cameras are the simple choice for any Brooklyn house."

Wireless cameras are simple to put up, and on a detached frame house with the router nearby they can work well.

City Line's houses are mostly attached brick. The router sits at the front, and the rear camera has to reach it through interior walls, a stair and the back wall itself. That is where wireless video tends to stutter or drop, often on wet nights.

A single wireless camera can be a fair stopgap for a renter. For an owner who wants the back yard recorded every night, a cable through the basement is the dependable route.

"City Line is mostly public housing."

Forgotten NY does describe the area as known for public housing, and Cypress Hills Houses and the East New York City Line development are real and nearby.

But City-Data's housing figures show about 34.7 percent of units in two-unit structures and 11.8 percent in attached townhouses, and current listings are dominated by two-families, three-families and mixed-use buildings. Most camera jobs here are on private houses.

For NYCHA residents the rules are NYCHA's. For everyone else, the owner of the house decides, and the work is planned around brick, stoops and shared walls.

"Nobody needs a license to install cameras in New York."

Partly true. The Department of State's installer application says installing passive, non-detecting devices does not require licensure, and owners installing on their own property are not in the business of installing.

General Business Law §69-m, though, requires a license for anyone in the business of installing or servicing security systems, and some sources read that to cover any paid video surveillance installation. Sources disagree on where the line falls for a given camera.

The simple step is to ask any paid installer for their license number and check it. Ours is NYS license #12000287431.

"You need a Department of Buildings permit to put cameras on a house."

One installer's guide says low-voltage camera wiring in a single-family house in New York City generally does not require a DOB permit, and that matches our experience on ordinary house jobs.

What does change things is new line-voltage power, such as a new outlet at the rear of the house or a circuit for a storefront camera. That is electrician's work.

When a City Line job needs new power, the quote says so, and the electrician's part is listed separately.

"Registering a store camera means the police can watch it anytime."

Not necessarily. When the city announced its retail-theft camera pilot, it separated registration, which tells the NYPD where cameras are, from integration, which gives the NYPD real-time access.

NYC 311 describes sharing live feeds with the NYPD during emergencies, at no cost to the business. Each step is a choice the business makes.

A Liberty Avenue shop can register without integrating, do both, or do neither, and its recorder should work cleanly in every case.

"Old Brooklyn houses need Landmarks approval for cameras."

Inside a designated historic district or on a designated landmark, the Landmarks Preservation Commission's permit guidebook does cover installing security cameras.

The research found no historic district inside City Line. The nearest listed landmark it turned up, the former 75th Precinct station house at 484 Liberty Avenue, is in New Lots and on the National Register, outside City Line.

We still check each address before drilling, because it takes a minute, but on ordinary City Line houses Landmarks review is not expected.

"A camera on your own house can point wherever you want."

You can record what anyone could see from the street, including your stoop and the sidewalk.

Civil Rights Law §52-a gives neighbors a claim against cameras aimed where they expect privacy, and on attached blocks the neighbor's stoop is a few feet away and the neighbor's yard is just over a low fence.

We frame every view on your own doors, yard and frontage, and black out anything else, which also keeps the picture sharper where it counts.

Our Installation Process

Every City Line camera job, from one camera on a lower door to a full attached house, a new condo lobby or a Liberty Avenue grocery, follows the same eight steps. 1. Send the address, photos and who uses each door

Street and cross street, photos of the stoop, lower door, basement and back wall or the store's window and counter, and who owns, rents and watches. Requests are booked within 2 hours.

2. Confirm the borough, the owner and who signs off

Brooklyn or Queens side of the line, the title holder, a condo or co-op board, or a store's landlord, agreed in writing before the plan is set.

3. Walk the house front to back and top to bottom

Curb, stoop, lower door, basement ceiling, closets, rear wall, extension roof and roof edge, marking where each face, door and stretch of yard can be recorded.

Stoop door, lower door, rear yard, register, scale: one task per camera, named in words the whole family can read.

Each camera, mount, cable run, wall opening, recorder, drive, battery unit, screen and setup task listed before anything is ordered.

Basement joists, closets or the roof edge, small sloped openings in mortar joints, every opening sealed, and the recorder in a dry, locked spot inside.

7. Certify each run and test by day and night

Each cable tested, the clock synced to a time server, every view checked in daylight and after dark, unique passwords and two-step sign-in set.

8. Hand over accounts, a practice export and a camera map

Each household or staff member gets only their views, the owner exports a test clip while we watch, and a written map names every camera and cable.

Our house was built in 1915 and the one next door in 1955. Why might the camera work differ between two houses on the same block?
+

The fronts may look alike, but the insides often are not. Houses from around 1915 to 1920 usually have open basement ceilings and plaster walls, which give cable an easy path from front to back. A house from the 1950s may have a basement that was finished early or more solid interior construction, so the route has to go up through closets or along the roof edge instead. We look in the basement before quoting so the labor on each house reflects its own construction, not its neighbor's.

Our front garden gate is only a few steps from the stoop. Can one camera cover the gate and the door together?
+

Sometimes, if the gate and door line up and the camera can sit far enough back to see both at face height. On many City Line fronts the stoop climbs at an angle to the gate, so a camera at the door looks down on the gate and sees only the tops of heads there. In that case a camera beside the door for faces and a higher camera on the front wall for the gate and sidewalk do a better job than one camera stretched across both. We check the angle on site before deciding.

If we supply our own cameras, what does the warranty cover?
+

Our one-year parts-only warranty applies to products AESS supplies. Equipment you buy yourself is excluded, along with existing or customer wiring, power surges, physical damage or unplugging, acts of God, internet, router or phone changes, and camera readjustments after the job is complete. If you bring your own cameras, any coverage on them comes from the store or the maker. We will still install and set them up, and we will tell you up front if a kit is unlikely to hold up on the views you want.

The roof of our one-story rear extension sits right under a bedroom window. Should a camera watch it?
+

It is worth covering, because a flat extension roof is the easiest step from the yard to a second-floor window. Often the rear-wall camera can be aimed to take in both the yard door and the extension roof, so no extra camera is needed. If the extension is deep or the window is off to one side, a small camera at the edge of the main house looking along the roof does the job. Any opening through the extension ceiling or roof is sealed so no water gets in.

Will the holes for camera cable let water into our old brick wall?
+

Not when they are made properly. We drill through mortar joints rather than face brick where the camera position allows, angle each opening slightly downward toward the outside so water runs away from the wall, and seal the opening and every anchor with exterior sealant. The cable forms a small drip loop before it enters the wall. A mortar joint can also be repointed cleanly later if the camera is ever moved, which is not true of a cracked brick.

We are converting our two-family into a legal three-family. Does that change anything about the cameras?
+

No law requires cameras because of the change, but the plan usually grows. A third household means another entrance to cover and another account to set up, and the house often gains a shared hallway or stair for the first time. Common-area cameras should show the street door and the stair, never the inside of an apartment. Any opening through fire-rated floors or walls is sealed with rated firestop. If the contractor has walls open now, it is the cheapest moment to pull spare cable.

Our street runs east to west and the low winter sun sits at the end of it. Will it blind the front camera?
+

It can wash out a camera aimed straight down the street for an hour or so at the start or end of the day. The fix is mostly in the aim and the settings. We angle the front cameras toward the stoop and the sidewalk in front of the house rather than down the length of the street, tuck them under the cornice or a ledge so the lens is shaded, and turn on the camera's backlight compensation so faces stay readable against a bright background.

Our recorder will live in a closet in the owner's apartment. Will it be noisy?
+

A home recorder usually makes a quiet hum from its hard drive and a small fan, similar to a desktop computer, and most people stop noticing it. It does need some air around it, so we avoid sealed cabinets and leave space on the vented sides. If the only spot is a bedroom closet and quiet matters, tell us at the walk-through and we will look for another location, such as a hall closet or a dry corner of the basement the owner controls.

Should we post a sign on our house saying cameras are recording?
+

It is optional for a private house. We could not verify any city rule requiring a notice sign on a one- or two-family house, and claims online about a local notice law did not check out in our research. Some owners like a small sign because it discourages people from trying the door, and others prefer not to advertise. Either way, the cameras should be framed on your own doors, yard and frontage, which matters far more than a sign.

What does it cost to add a wall-mounted screen for the cameras in our front room?
+

Mounting a screen up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs starts at $209.99, with the bracket sold separately. Mounting on plaster adds $45, and on brick or stone adds $65. Hiding the power and cable inside the wall with a code-compliant kit is $169, or a paintable cord cover is $59. Removing an old TV first is $85. A screen set on its stand without a wall mount is $137.99. The camera feed to the screen is listed on the same quote.

Will the crew need to block the sidewalk in front of our house during the install?
+

Only briefly. Front cameras on an attached house are mounted from a ladder set on the stoop or the sidewalk, usually for a short time per camera, with a crew member at the base so neighbors can pass safely. Cable work happens inside the house and the basement, so most of the day nothing is outside at all. We do not leave tools or cable across the sidewalk, and we time the front work around school drop-off and pickup on your block when you tell us about it.
