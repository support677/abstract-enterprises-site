The Neighborhood

Why Flatbush Properties Need Security Camera Repair

Flatbush has never had official borders, but the blocks most people mean run from Parkside Avenue south toward Brooklyn College and the Junction, and from around Coney Island Avenue east toward Nostrand. What makes this part of Brooklyn different for camera work is the building stock. After the First World War a city tax exemption that ran until 1932 brought a wave of six-story elevator apartment houses, many of them Art Deco or Colonial Revival, along Ocean Avenue and the edges of the old village. Between them sit three-story walk-ups with a store on the ground floor, smaller brick apartment buildings, houses of worship of every size, and four long commercial corridors: Flatbush Avenue, Church Avenue, Nostrand Avenue and Rogers Avenue. Almost every one of those buildings already has cameras. The repair problem is that the people who rely on them are rarely the people who maintain them.

The numbers show why. The NYU Furman Center counts 147,911 people in the Flatbush and Midwood community district in 2024, with a homeownership rate of 24.4 percent and a rental vacancy rate of just 3.1 percent. An older Furman profile of the same district found that more than four out of five rental apartments were rent-regulated. In practice that means long-term tenants in big buildings run by owners and managing agents who may be in another borough. When a hallway camera dies in one of those buildings, the tenants notice first, the super may or may not pass it on, and the recorder in the cellar keeps quietly recording nothing. Brick Underground put the legal side plainly in its column on stolen packages and a broken lobby camera: no city law makes a rental building keep its cameras working. But attorney Jeffrey Reich told the column that a landlord on notice of crime may have to take reasonable precautions, and that "fixing the security camera would certainly be a reasonable action for the landlord to take in this instance."

A Flatbush building showed what happens next in July 2025. News 12 reported that tenants of a Flatbush apartment building shared video of a shirtless man walking up to a hallway camera on July 11 and deliberately covering its lens, then coming back the next day and doing it again. The clip the station aired came from a tenant's own Ring camera. Residents blamed broken entrance locks and said management answered with temporary fixes; one told News 12, "I don't feel safe in my apartment. I don't feel safe in this building." For camera repair, that story holds the whole Flatbush problem in one hallway: a building camera that could be reached and blinded, no sign that anyone was alerted when it went dark, and the useful video coming from a tenant's device instead of the system the building paid for.

The storefront side of Flatbush has its own pattern. The city's Avenue NYC study of the area, built from fieldwork in 2021 and 2022 with the local business improvement districts and merchant groups, counted 1,662 storefronts along Church, Flatbush, Nostrand and Rogers Avenues. Fifty-three percent of the merchants it surveyed wanted changes that would make the corridors safer, shoplifting came up as a steady worry, and so did poor street lighting at night. Late in 2025 Fox 5 New York reported a string of Brooklyn break-ins through store roofs, including one at 302 Church Avenue where about $7,500 was taken and a failed roof attempt at 1188 Flatbush Avenue on October 11; police released still images from the store cameras that recorded the successful ones. In May 2024 a masked man robbed a smoke shop at 866 Flatbush Avenue, just off Church Avenue, at gunpoint shortly before 11 p.m. In July 2025 News 12 aired camera video of a gunpoint robbery inside Z & H Five Star Salon in Flatbush at about 11 in the morning. Some of those stores had cameras that did their job. The repair calls we get come from the ones that did not: the camera aimed at the wrong door, the recorder that stopped saving weeks before, the night picture that turned into grey noise under a broken streetlight.

Then there is the power. On June 20, 2025, a manhole fire on Beverly Road left at least 1,700 Con Edison customers in Flatbush without power, with crews still working to restore it that night, and on the night of July 13, 2026, another manhole fire near 650 Ocean Avenue cut about 343 customers until early the next morning. A camera system does not mind the dark. It is hurt when the power drops while the recorder is writing to its drive, when the surge on the way back kills an adapter or a switch port, and when the recorder's clock and settings reset. After an outage on a Flatbush block we hear the same sentence over and over: the lights came back, the elevator came back, the cameras did not.

Flatbush is not short of cameras. A 2022 Patch report on Amnesty International's Decode Surveillance NYC map noted that the volunteers' count "documents a high number of them in Flatbush," far more than in neighboring Ditmas Park. Plenty of cameras is not the same as plenty of usable video. A repair here has three jobs: get the system saving again, make sure someone is told the next time it stops, and leave the owner, agent or congregation with the logins, labels and backup power to keep it running. Buildings that call about lobby cameras often ask us to look at the street door at the same time, because a working camera is worth far more beside an entrance that locks and an intercom that works.

The urgent calls we take from Flatbush tend to come in three shapes. If footage from a recent incident matters, the first rule is simple. Leave the recorder running, and do not let anyone restart it, unplug it, format it or "reset it to see if that helps." Most recorders write over their oldest video once the drive is full, so every hour of waiting costs some of the past. A restart can also hide the very error that explains the gap. If a detective from the 70th or 67th Precinct has already asked for a copy, mention it when you book; the export is made to USB in the recorder's own format with its original timestamps, and we note the difference between the recorder's clock and the real time so nobody argues about when it happened.

For everything else the route is the same. The $150 service call reserves a two-hour arrival window, the technician finds the cause, and you see a written price before any part is replaced. Requests are booked within 2 hours. We do not promise every repair on the same day, because some recorders, drives and cameras have to be ordered to match what you have, but most single-camera, power-supply and hard-drive faults are fixed on the first visit when the part is on the truck.

Six-story elevator apartment houses

The prewar buildings along Ocean Avenue and the edges of the old village, often built around light courts, with a recorder in the cellar or the super's office and long runs to the roof, the halls and the courtyard.

Three-story walk-ups over a store

A shop on the ground floor and apartments above, along Flatbush, Church, Nostrand and Rogers Avenues. Two camera systems often share one facade, one cellar and one electrical service.

Smaller brick apartment buildings

Four- to twenty-unit walk-ups on the side streets with no super living on site, where cameras cover the stoop, the vestibule, the stairs and the back yard, and nobody checks them between incidents.

Co-ops and condominiums

Apartment houses that converted to co-op ownership in the waves of the late 1980s, and newer condos, where a board or a managing agent controls the system and every shareholder has an opinion about where the cameras point.

Houses of worship and schools

Churches, temples, mosques and synagogues of every size, the small schools on the Erasmus Hall campus and programs in former storefronts, often run by volunteers who change from year to year.

New and converted mixed-use buildings

Recent construction and conversions such as the planned housing at the former Sears building at Beverley Road and Bedford Avenue, where cameras are newer, network-based and often still tied to the contractor who installed them.

Six-story apartment houses: one recorder, many hands

The classic Flatbush apartment house has six floors, an elevator, a lobby with mailboxes, one or two stairs, a cellar with the boiler and the laundry, and a roof with a bulkhead and often a light court cut into the middle of the building. A typical camera system has a dome in the vestibule, one in the lobby, one or two per hallway, a camera on the courtyard or rear yard, one on the roof door and sometimes one in the elevator, all wired back to a single recorder. When that recorder lives in the super's office or a locked cellar room, the system is only as healthy as the last person who looked at it.

Two things go wrong over and over in these buildings. First, the recorder stops saving and nobody notices, because the monitor in the super's office still shows live pictures; we cover how to spot that later on this page. Second, cameras drop out one at a time as contractors work in the building. Roofers, elevator mechanics, plumbers opening a chase and internet installers pulling new lines up the stairwell all work where camera cables run. When cameras on the upper floors fail in the same month as a roof job, we start by tracing those runs from the recorder up, not by replacing cameras.

Light courts deserve their own mention. Many Flatbush buildings from the 1920s were laid out with wings around courts so every apartment had light and air, which means a courtyard camera is often mounted on the far wing with its cable crossing the roof or running along a parapet. Those runs face sun, ice, foot traffic from roofers and the occasional satellite installer, and they are the first to fail. When we repair one, we look at whether the run can drop inside the building sooner, or travel in a protected path, so the courtyard view stops being the camera that goes first every winter.

Walk-ups over a store: two systems, one building

Along the shopping streets, a typical building is three stories of brick with a store below and apartments above, reached by a narrow street door beside the shop window. The store usually has its own camera system with a recorder in the back room. The landlord may have a separate camera on the apartment door and stairs, or none at all. Both depend on the same electrical service and often the same cellar, and they get confused for each other on the day of a repair. Before we start, we sort out which cameras belong to whom, which recorder each one feeds, and who is paying for which part of the work, so the store owner is not billed for the landlord's stairway or the reverse.

These buildings also share roofs. Many blocks of Flatbush Avenue and Church Avenue are rows of buildings whose roofs meet at low parapets, which is exactly what made the 2025 roof break-ins possible. A store camera system that watches only the front door and the register leaves the roof hatch, the skylight and the back of the store to chance. On a repair visit we can show you what each camera actually covers and where the gaps are, and price the change as part of the repair rather than as a new system.

Smaller walk-ups: the building that gets checked only after something happens

The four- to twenty-family brick walk-ups on the side streets usually have a few cameras at the stoop, the vestibule, the stairs and the back yard, wired to a recorder in the cellar or a locked closet under the stairs. With no super living on site, the system gets looked at when the owner visits or a tenant complains. That is why the most common walk-up call is not "a camera broke" but "we needed the video and found the camera had been dark for months."

So a walk-up repair has two halves: the fix, and alerts for lost video and drive errors sent to the owner or agent, so the next failure arrives as a message rather than a surprise in front of a detective.

Co-ops and condominiums: a board, a managing agent and a lot of opinions

Flatbush saw waves of co-op conversions in the late 1980s, and many of those buildings still run camera systems installed during or after conversion. In a co-op or condo, the board or the managing agent approves the repair, and the camera plan often becomes a building discussion: which hallways need coverage, whether a camera can see into apartment doors, who may view footage and who may export it. We work through the agent or the board's designated contact, put the condition of each camera in writing, and set up viewing permissions so a doorman or super can watch live without being able to delete or copy video.

Houses of worship and schools: volunteers and big doors

Flatbush's houses of worship range from the Flatbush Reformed Church on Flatbush Avenue, built in the 1790s, to small congregations in former storefronts and houses. In February 2024 three people forced their way through the back door of a house of worship at 26 Rugby Road at about 2:30 on a Sunday afternoon and took phones, belongings and cash while four monks were inside, according to News 12. That afternoon timing is a reminder that a camera system set to care only about the night is missing half the risk. Church and temple systems are often set up by a volunteer, held on one person's phone and forgotten when that person moves on. A repair usually means bringing the login under the institution's own name, checking every door camera in daylight and darkness, and making sure the back and side doors are recorded as carefully as the front.

New and converted buildings: newer cameras, different faults

Flatbush's newest buildings, and conversions such as the planned housing at the former Sears, Roebuck store at Beverley Road and Bedford Avenue, use network cameras that rarely fail the way older coax systems do. Their first problems are paperwork more than hardware: a contractor who still holds the passwords, firmware that was never updated, recorders set to keep only a few days, and cameras that were mounted but never added to the recorder. A repair in a new building often starts with an inventory of what is installed and who controls it.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Finding the Recorder Before Finding the Fault in a Flatbush Apartment House

These notes describe how our technicians work through a Flatbush apartment house or walk-up when several cameras are out and nobody in the building is sure why. They are our standard practice, not a story about one building.

Find the box first. In a six-story building the recorder can be in the super's office, a locked cellar room, a closet off the lobby, a cabinet in the managing agent's storage space, or even inside the super's own apartment. Before the visit we ask who has the key, because a technician who cannot reach the recorder cannot save footage or test anything.

Copy before touching. If anyone needs video from a recent night, the technician exports it before restarting, unplugging or opening anything, and writes down the recorder's clock against the real time.

Read the recorder, not just the monitor. A monitor full of live pictures says nothing about recording. The technician checks the drive's status, the recording schedule and the playback timeline for each channel, and notes the last day each camera actually saved video. That date often explains more than any camera does.

Follow the power. In the cellar the technician checks the outlet, the power strip, the power box or adapters, the PoE switch and any battery backup, because an outage on the block shows up there first. A surge-damaged adapter or a dead switch port explains many after-outage calls.

Trace, then label. Unlabeled cables are toned out and labeled as each one is identified, and the recorder's channel names are corrected to match the cameras on the walls. Buildings that have had three installers over twenty years almost always need this.

Walk the building. At each dead camera the technician tests power and signal at the lens, then checks the connector, bracket and run for water, building work and tampering.

Price first, then repair, then leave a record. You hear the cause and see a written price before any part is replaced. After the repair, every camera is checked in daylight and after dark, alerts for lost video and drive errors are turned on, and the owner or agent gets the logins and a list of what is connected where.

Where Footage Gets Lost

The Hallway, the Roof and the Recorder Shelf: Where Flatbush Camera Systems Break Down

When a Flatbush building or store needs video and does not have it, the cause almost always sits in one of three places. Knowing which one is the difference between a $150 visit and a new system.

The hallway camera within arm's reach

Hallway cameras in Flatbush apartment houses are usually mounted where the original installer could reach them from a stepladder, which also means anyone tall enough can reach them from the floor. The July 2025 case News 12 reported, where a man covered a hallway lens two days running, is the extreme version. The everyday version is a camera knocked sideways by a move-in, smeared by a cleaning crew, or turned toward the ceiling. Repairs here mean a higher mount where the ceiling allows it, a vandal-resistant dome in place of an exposed bullet, cameras placed so each one is in view of another, and a tamper or video-loss alert so the building knows within minutes, not weeks.

The roof and the cables that cross it

Roof cameras, courtyard cameras on the far wing of a light court, and any camera whose cable crosses the roof are exposed to sun, ice and every contractor who walks up there. On stores, the roof is also where some burglars start, as the 2025 break-ins through store roofs on Church Avenue and Fulton Street showed. Repairs include replacing roof runs in protected paths, re-sealing roof-level connectors, and in stores, checking whether a camera sees the hatch, the skylight or the ceiling under it.

The recorder shelf

Recorders in Flatbush cellars share space with boilers, laundry machines and dripping pipes; recorders in stores sit under counters, on top of coolers or in back rooms with stock piled against their vents. Heat and dust shorten drives, water ends them, and a recorder anyone can reach can be unplugged by someone who needed the outlet. Swann's support guide lists disabled recording settings, wrong motion settings, missing storage and faulty drives among the main reasons a recorder stops saving, and CCTV Camera World notes that drives built for desktop computers are not designed for round-the-clock recording. Moving the recorder, putting it on battery backup and fitting a surveillance-rated drive fixes more Flatbush systems than new cameras do.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Repair in Flatbush: Questions Answered

These are situations Flatbush tenants' groups, owners, managing agents, boards, store owners and congregations describe when they call. Each answer is written for the buildings and blocks here.

Apartment houses and their shared halls

A man covered our hallway camera two nights in a row, and the only clear video came from a neighbor's doorbell camera. When the building camera is fixed, can it be placed so whoever reaches for it gets recorded first?

Yes, and that is the most useful change a hallway repair can make. A camera that can be reached and covered should be in the view of another camera, so the person reaching for it is recorded face-on before the lens goes dark. In a Flatbush hallway that usually means pairing the camera at the elevator with the one at the stair door so each sees the other, raising the mount as high as the ceiling allows, and swapping an exposed bullet for a vandal-resistant dome that is harder to grip or turn. We also switch on the recorder's video-loss or tamper alert and send it to the super's or the agent's phone, so a covered lens is reported in minutes. A tenant's doorbell camera is a fine second witness, but it only sees one door, and the building should not have to depend on it.

Our building's wings wrap around a light court, and the camera that watches the court hangs on the far wing with its cable laid across the roof. Why does that one camera fail more often than the rest?

Because its cable has the hardest life in the building. A run laid across a roof or along a parapet bakes in summer, stiffens and cracks in winter, gets stepped on by roofers and pinched under new flashing, and often ends at a connector that was taped rather than sealed. Water works into the crack and the connector, and the picture fades, flickers and then disappears, usually after a freeze or a roof job. The repair is to replace that run, ideally dropping it into the building near the camera and carrying it down an interior path, or protecting it in a sleeve where it must cross the roof, with the connector in a sealed box. Once the run is sound, the courtyard camera usually lasts as long as the hallway ones.

Every time a roofer or the elevator company works up top, the cameras on our top two floors drop out. Is that a coincidence?

Almost certainly not. In many six-story Flatbush buildings the camera cables for the upper halls and the roof run up through the stairwell or a chase and meet near the bulkhead or the elevator machine room. Contractors working there move cables, unplug a power strip to run their own tools, pull a staple or set equipment on a run. If the cameras come back after the job, power was interrupted; if they stay dead, a cable or connector was damaged. On the repair we trace the upper-floor runs from the recorder up, fix the damage, label every cable where contractors work, and move any camera power supply off an outlet that a work crew is likely to borrow. A short note to the building's roofer and elevator company about which cables to leave alone helps too.

Stores, salons and offices on the shopping streets

Our store is in a row of one-story shops along Flatbush Avenue where every roof meets at a low wall. Should our cameras watch the shared roof even though only part of it is ours?

Your cameras should watch the parts of the roof that lead into your store: your hatch, your skylight and the stretch of roof right around them. The 2025 break-ins that reached 302 Church Avenue and 1188 Flatbush Avenue came through roofs, so a roof-level view of your own entry points is worth having. What you should avoid is aiming a camera across the neighbors' roofs or into windows of apartments above the row. In practice we mount the camera at your hatch or on your parapet, aimed back across your own roof, and if a neighbor wants their section covered too, they can add a camera on their side. Inside the store, a camera on the ceiling under the hatch or skylight records anyone who gets that far, even if the roof view is defeated.

We have an office above a store on Church Avenue, reached by a stair door at the street, and the camera at the bottom of the stairs also records everyone going up to the apartments. Is that a problem?

It is a reason to settle who controls that camera, not a reason to switch it off. A shared stair is a common area, and a camera there protects every tenant in the building, including you. The questions are whose system it belongs to, who may watch it, and who may copy footage of residents coming and going. If the landlord owns it, you can ask for live view of the stair from your office without export rights. If it is yours, it is fair to let the landlord and the residents know it is there and to keep its view to the stair and the street door rather than any apartment door. We can set up separate viewing accounts on the recorder so each party sees only what they should, and log who exports video.

Houses of worship, schools and community rooms

Thieves forced the back door of a temple on Rugby Road at 2:30 on a Sunday afternoon in 2024. Our congregation set its cameras to record only at night to save drive space. Should that change?

Yes. A night-only schedule assumes trouble waits for dark, and the Rugby Road robbery News 12 reported happened in broad daylight with people inside. Most recorders made in the last several years can hold weeks of round-the-clock video from a handful of cameras on a properly sized drive, especially if the cameras use efficient compression and record at full quality only when there is motion. On the repair we set every door camera to record around the clock, check how many days the drive actually keeps, and replace the drive with a larger surveillance-rated one if needed. We also make sure the back and side door cameras are aimed so someone forcing the door is recorded, not just the empty doorway after they are inside.

Four trustees at our church watch the cameras on their phones, and whenever one of them changes phones the app stops working for everyone. Why would one person's phone affect the others?

Because all four are probably signed in to one shared account. When that account is moved to a new phone, the app may log out the other devices, change the password or trigger a security reset, and everyone is locked out at once. It also means nobody knows who looked at or exported video. The fix is to make one administrator account in the church's own name and email, held by the office or the senior trustee, and to give each trustee a separate viewing account with only the permissions they need. Then a new phone affects only its owner, a departing trustee can be removed without disturbing anyone, and the recorder keeps a record of each person's activity.

Our congregation streams services from a camera in the sanctuary, and since a volunteer put that camera on the same recorder as the security cameras, everything freezes on Sunday mornings. Should they be separated again?

Usually yes. A streaming camera sends a large, constant video stream, and when it shares a recorder, a switch or an internet connection with the security cameras, the busiest hour of the week is exactly when they all compete. The recorder can run short of bandwidth or processing, the app stutters, and the streaming software can drag the network down with it. We separate the stream so it goes straight to the computer or encoder that broadcasts it, keep the security cameras on the recorder alone, and give each its own network segment if they share a switch. If the congregation wants the sanctuary camera recorded for security as well, it can be added as a lower-resolution second stream that does not crowd the others.

Footage and the block

Flatbush already has a lot of cameras, including public ones. If a street camera covers our corner, is it still worth repairing our own?

Yes. Amnesty International's volunteers mapped a high number of cameras in Flatbush, but a camera on a pole sees the street from far away and at an angle chosen for someone else. Your camera sees your door, your lobby, your counter and your back yard up close, and it records on a system you control. When something happens at your building, detectives ask you for that video, and you can hand it over the same day. A public camera may help reconstruct where someone went afterward, but it rarely shows a face at your entrance. Repairing your own system is also much cheaper than replacing it, and on our Brooklyn list most single faults cost far less than a new install.

Detectives asked several buildings on our block for video the same night. The neighbors' clips all showed the man on the sidewalk, but ours showed only our own door. Should a repair turn one camera toward the street?

If you have a camera that can cover your stoop or storefront and part of the sidewalk in front of it, angling it to include that stretch makes your system far more useful to the block without watching anyone's home. A camera aimed only at the door records who comes in; a camera that also sees the sidewalk records who walked past, which direction they went and when. On the repair we look at each outward-facing camera and widen or re-aim it to cover your frontage and the sidewalk, avoiding windows across the street. It is usually a re-aim or a lens adjustment within the service call, not a new camera. Keep the recorder's clock correct as well, because a block's clips are only useful together if their times line up.

Types of Security Cameras and Recorders We Repair

We repair whatever is on the building, whoever put it there. These are the kinds of equipment we find in Flatbush apartment houses, walk-ups, stores and houses of worship, and the faults each one tends to develop.

Coax and HD-over-coax cameras

Cameras with round BNC connectors, from old analog to HD models, still common in prewar apartment houses wired a decade or more ago. Typical faults: water in connectors, failed power boxes, worn infrared and long runs that lose picture quality.

Network (IP) cameras

Cameras powered and connected through one data cable, common in newer buildings, offices and recently upgraded stores. Typical faults: dead switch ports after a surge, address conflicts, firmware problems and lost passwords.

DVRs, NVRs and hybrid recorders

The box that holds the video, in a cellar room, a super's office, a closet or a back room. Typical faults: worn or desktop-grade drives, corrupted settings after an outage, overheating near a boiler or a cooler, and clocks that drift.

Vestibule and hallway domes

Domes in lobbies, vestibules and halls that get cracked, smeared, painted or turned. Covers and brackets can often be replaced separately from the camera, and varifocal models can be re-framed on site.

Pan-tilt and 360-degree cameras

Small pan-tilt domes in courtyards and corridors and fisheye cameras over store floors. Typical faults: motors that stick, presets that are lost, and fisheye views that need the right display settings after an update.

Video intercom door stations

Entrance stations with built-in cameras at apartment and walk-up doors. Typical faults: fogged or scratched lenses, failed night illumination and wiring to the recorder or apartment monitors that was never finished.

Wi-Fi and plug-in cameras

Indoor cubes and outdoor Wi-Fi cameras in package areas, offices and spots cable never reached. Typical faults: weak signal through brick and plaster, crowded Wi-Fi in big buildings, unplugged power and accounts tied to someone who has left.

PoE switches and power supplies

Switches, injectors, power boxes and adapters, often on a cellar shelf with no backup. Typical faults: dead ports or units after a surge, blown channel fuses and overloaded power budgets after cameras were added.

Battery backups, monitors and apps

UPS units, super's-office monitors and phone apps. Typical faults: backup batteries that died years ago, monitors showing live view while recording has stopped, and apps that lost the recorder after an internet change.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Flatbush

These claims show up in AI-written answers, repair-lead sites such as Angi and HomeAdvisor, and national how-to guides. Each is partly true somewhere, and each sends Flatbush owners, tenants and store owners toward the wrong repair or a lost clip.

"Apartment buildings in New York have to keep their security cameras working"

Quick answers often assume a city rule obliges landlords to maintain building cameras, so a tenant only has to report a dead one.

Brick Underground's experts said the opposite: no city law requires a rental building to keep cameras working. What can create a duty is notice of crime, and attorney Jeffrey Reich said a landlord on notice may need to take reasonable precautions, with fixing a broken camera an obvious one. What the Multiple Dwelling Law does require in many buildings is a self-locking street door and a working intercom.

For Flatbush tenants, that means written complaints that tie the dead camera to specific incidents carry more weight than a general request. For owners, it means a working, documented camera system is a sensible answer once incidents are on record.

"A camera in every hallway means the building is covered"

Answers about building security tend to count cameras: one per floor, one in the lobby, one at the door, and the job is done.

The July 2025 Flatbush case News 12 covered shows the gap. A man walked up to a hallway camera and covered it on two separate days, and the clear video came from a tenant's own device. Coverage is not the number of cameras; it is whether the cameras watch each other, whether they are out of easy reach, and whether anyone is told when one goes dark.

A repair that re-aims cameras to cover one another and turns on video-loss alerts adds more protection than a new camera mounted where the old one was.

"Most camera repairs cost $100 to $300"

Many cost answers quote a low national range built on suburban homes with one or two consumer cameras.

HomeAdvisor's Brooklyn page shows how wide local work runs: an average of $1,416 for surveillance camera installation or repair, a typical range of $680 to $2,145 and a full spread from $160 to $3,500. That figure mixes new systems with repairs. On our Brooklyn list the $150 service call finds the fault, and most single repairs in a Flatbush building or store fall between $150 and $700.

The honest way to budget is to find the fault first. A cracked connector on a roof run is a small repair; a dead recorder for a forty-camera apartment house is not, and no online average will tell you which one you have.

"Point your cameras at the front door, because that's how burglars get in"

Placement advice usually starts and ends with the entrance.

Flatbush shows other routes. Fox 5 reported 2025 break-ins that came through store roofs, including 302 Church Avenue and an attempt at 1188 Flatbush Avenue, and News 12 reported the 2024 Rugby Road robbery through a back door in the afternoon. On blocks where store roofs meet and buildings have rear yards and cellar doors, the front door is only one of several ways in.

A repair should include a walk-through of what each camera actually sees, including the roof hatch, the skylight, the back door and the cellar entrance, and a plain list of what is not covered.

"If the monitor shows live video, the system is recording"

Troubleshooting answers often stop once the picture is on the screen.

Live view and recording are separate. Swann's support guide lists recording switched off, wrong motion settings, no storage installed and failed drives among the causes of a recorder that shows pictures but saves nothing, and CCTV Camera World tells owners to check the drive's status and scroll back through playback channel by channel. In Flatbush apartment houses the monitor in the super's office is often the only thing anyone looks at, so a recorder can stop saving for weeks before an incident reveals it.

The real test is playback. On every repair we check the last date each camera saved video, the drive's health and the recording schedule, and we turn on alerts so a drive error reaches a phone.

"Swap the broken wired cameras for Wi-Fi ones and the problems go away"

Some answers suggest replacing a failing wired system with wireless cameras to end cable trouble for good.

Flatbush apartment houses are the hardest case for that advice. Prewar brick and plaster block signal between floors, a single hallway may sit among dozens of tenants' home networks, and every wireless camera still needs power from an outlet someone can unplug. Battery models need charging, and each camera depends on an app account somebody has to own.

Repairing the existing wired runs is usually cheaper and steadier. Wi-Fi cameras make sense for the odd spot cable cannot reach, set up on the owner's network with the account in the owner's name.

"Registering your cameras with the NYPD means police watch your store"

Answers about police camera programs often blur two different things.

The city's 2024 program described two levels: registration, which only tells the NYPD where a business's cameras are, and integration, which gives police live access to feeds, with the business controlling when that access applies. Both are free. The program began in the 109th Precinct in Flushing and was to expand to nine more precincts; whether a given Flatbush precinct is included is something to ask the precinct directly.

Either way, a registered camera that is not recording helps nobody. Fix the system first, then decide how much access you want to give.
