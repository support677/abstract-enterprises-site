The Neighborhood

Why New Dorp Homes Need Soundbar Installation

New Dorp takes its name from the Dutch Nieuw Dorp, the “new village”, and it has been a village with a centre ever since. Wikipedia dates the settlement to 1671, when lots along the South Shore were laid out beyond the older Oude Dorp, the place we now call Old Town. The modern neighbourhood is bounded roughly by Mill Road, Tysens Lane, Amboy and Richmond Roads and Bancroft Avenue, with Todt Hill rising behind it and Oakwood, Grant City, Dongan Hills and Midland Beach around it. Its ZIP code is 10306. What makes it different from most of Staten Island is that you can live here and walk to the shops, the library and a train.

The spine is New Dorp Lane. The Landmarks Preservation Commission’s 1988 report on the Lane Theater records that the Lane existed by the time of the Revolution, that development first came in the 1880s when James W. Hughes and William Ross bought up tracts here, and that by 1917 a commercial strip had grown along the Lane facing the railroad. In 2005 the City Council approved a rezoning for the Lane; CityLand’s summary says commercial-only buildings were capped at two floors, mixed-use buildings at 35 feet, and that residential units were barred from the ground floor. The practical result is the streetscape you see today: shops at street level, homes upstairs. In 2017 the Council created the New Dorp business improvement district — 146 properties and about 180 businesses along the Lane and the streets next to it between Hylan Boulevard and Richmond Road, according to CityLand.

The Lane is changing again. In July 2026 The City Reporter described Chinese-owned restaurants, pharmacies, dentist offices and clinics with Chinese-language signs clustered around New Dorp Lane, an Asian supermarket that opened on Hylan Boulevard in 2022, and a Staten Island Chinese School in New Dorp whose enrolment rose 45 percent in 2023. The 2020 census figures Wikipedia cites for the New Dorp–Midland Beach area show 29,083 residents, up 5.3 percent in ten years, with 15.5 percent of residents Asian. For a soundbar installer that means more households with two or three generations under one roof, more TVs fed by more than one box, and more shopkeepers who want the screen by the counter to be heard and nothing more.

Behind the Lane, most of New Dorp is houses, and many of them share walls. The Point2 summary of the 2019–2023 American Community Survey for New Dorp counts 41.4 percent of homes as detached single-family houses, 25.3 percent as attached single-family houses and another 17.7 percent in two-unit buildings. The median home was built in 1968, and the 1960s alone account for nearly a quarter of the stock. That is the era of the brick-fronted attached house with a garage at street level and the living room above it, of the semi-attached pair that mirrors its neighbour, and of the two-family house where parents live on one floor and grown children on the other. Those houses are why so many New Dorp jobs begin with the question “which wall is left?”

Then there is the shore. New Dorp Beach, between Midland Beach and Oakwood Beach, began as a resort of hotels, campgrounds and summer bungalows off Cedar Grove Avenue, and Wikipedia notes that most of its houses are converted beach houses, with many attached homes among them. Sandy flooded it badly in 2012. Residents asked the state to extend its buyout programme to their streets, as DNAinfo reported in December 2013, but in April 2014 the state said no more neighbourhoods would be added. So, unlike Oakwood Beach next door, New Dorp Beach mostly stayed, and rebuilt: some houses were repaired and raised, others were bought through the city’s acquisition programme and rebuilt under post-Sandy rules. Walk the streets near Cedar Grove Avenue today and a single block can hold an original bungalow, a renovated cottage and a new house standing a storey up on its foundation.

New Dorp by the numbers, for anyone planning a TV wall

  • 29,083 residents and 10,148 households in the New Dorp–Midland Beach area at the 2020 census, up from 27,623 in 2010; 31.8 percent of households include children under 18 (Wikipedia, citing NYC Planning).
  • 25.3 percent attached single-family houses and 17.7 percent two-unit buildings in Point2’s New Dorp area (ACS 2019–2023), against 41.4 percent detached — so more than four homes in ten share a wall or a building with another household.
  • Median year built 1968; 23.5 percent of homes date from the 1960s and 14.0 percent from the 1970s, while 13.7 percent are from 1939 or earlier (Point2). Point2 reports 69.3 percent owner-occupied; Wikipedia gives a much higher renter share for its wider area, so we treat tenure as mixed.
  • Mixed-use buildings no taller than 35 feet, and no homes at street level on the Lane under the 2005 rezoning (CityLand) — the reason so many New Dorp flats sit above a storefront.
  • 1860: the year New Dorp station opened on the Staten Island Railway. It sits in an open cut at New Dorp Lane, morning rush-hour expresses to St. George begin their express run here, and in September 2023 it became the first SIR station with elevators (MTA; Wikipedia).
  • No state buyout: New Dorp Beach asked to join the post-Sandy enhanced buyout programme and was turned down in April 2014, when Graham Beach became the last neighbourhood added (DNAinfo).

Put together, those figures describe a neighbourhood where the TV is rarely in the obvious place. In a flat over the Lane it might share a wall with the stair that climbs up from the street door; in an attached house it might hang over the stairs to the garage; in a raised house by the beach it sits a full storey above the street, and the cable service may come in near the ground. The questions we ask first are “which floor?” and “what is on the other side of that wall?”, and only then “which soundbar?”

Over the Lane

Because the 2005 zoning keeps homes off the ground floor, almost every flat on New Dorp Lane is reached by a door beside the shop and a stair that rises straight up between party walls. The living room usually faces the Lane, with one or two big front windows, and the TV goes on a side wall. Those side walls are frequently the oldest surfaces in the building: plaster on wood lath in the earlier structures, plaster over brick in some, and drywall on light steel studs where a landlord has refitted a flat between tenants. We bring the tools for all three. Below the floor is someone else’s business — often a commercial ceiling with lighting, ductwork and sometimes a suspended grid — so we never route a cable downward without the landlord’s agreement.

Between the semis

The inland streets are the New Dorp most people picture: short front yards, driveways, and pairs or rows of brick and siding houses. In a typical attached house the garage and a utility room take the street level, the living room sits half a flight or a full flight up, and the bedrooms are above that. One long side of the living room is the party wall; the other long side often carries the staircase. The front wall is a picture window and the back opens to the kitchen. That leaves very little blank wall, and the wall that does remain may sit directly above the garage door opening, where the framing has to carry a header. We find the studs, keep clear of the header, and talk about where the bass will go — because under that floor is a hollow garage, not soil.

Below Hylan

South of Hylan Boulevard the ground drops toward Miller Field and the bay, and the houses change. The surviving bungalows are small, low and often still on piers or short foundations, with board, panel or beadboard walls and floors that move underfoot. The rebuilt houses are the opposite: tall, stiff and new, with insulated walls, modern wiring and a living floor raised above the flood elevation. The city’s Department of Buildings, in a 2022 bulletin on flood-resistant construction, limits the enclosed space below that elevation to parking, storage and building access. For a TV system that settles one question straight away: the soundbar, subwoofer, receiver and power strip all live on the living floor, and nothing goes in the enclosure underneath.

None of these zones changes a price on this page; they change what the technician puts in the van and how long the first ten minutes take. Tell us which of the three you are in and we arrive with the right anchors, the right length of cable and a realistic idea of where the TV can go.

Building Specialists

Soundbar Installation for New Dorp’s Architecture

New Dorp was built in layers, and each layer left its own kind of wall. A nineteenth-century railroad village, a commercial strip from the early twentieth century, the post-war and 1960s housing that filled the farms, a summer colony by the water, and a wave of rebuilding after 2012. This is what each usually means for a soundbar.

Mixed-Use Buildings on the Lane

Two- and three-storey buildings with shops below and one or two flats above, held to 35 feet for mixed use since the 2005 rezoning. Older ones have plaster on lath or plaster on masonry; refitted flats often have drywall on light steel studs. The stair is narrow and the floor is the shop’s ceiling.

Attached Houses of the 1960s and 1970s

The largest single group by era in Point2’s figures. Brick or siding fronts, a garage at street level, the living room above, drywall over wood studs inside. Party walls are usually masonry or fire-rated framing; interior walls are ordinary studs at regular spacing.

Semi-Attached Pairs

Two houses sharing one wall, each a mirror of the other. Free walls on three sides make TV placement easier than in a row, but the shared wall still carries sound, and the TV often ends up on the same wall as the neighbour’s.

Two-Family Houses

Almost one home in five in Point2’s New Dorp area is in a two-unit building. Two kitchens, two meters, often one router or two. Each floor’s TV room is its own job, and the floor between them is the only thing separating one household’s bass from the other’s sleep.

Older Houses Toward Richmond Road

The 13.7 percent of homes from 1939 or earlier include frame houses on the older streets near the railway and up toward Richmond Road and New Dorp Heights. Expect plaster, irregular framing and outlets that may be older than the TV is used to.

Bungalows and Cottages at New Dorp Beach

Small converted summer houses, some on piers, with walls of boards, panelling or beadboard and low ceilings. Light framing, light floors and very little space behind the TV for anything except a slim bracket.

Houses Rebuilt After Sandy

Raised or rebuilt to post-2012 rules, with the living floor above the design flood elevation and only parking, storage and access below it. New drywall, deep insulated cavities, plenty of outlets — and sometimes a stair up from the street that is longer than the old house was tall.

Apartment Complexes

Larger rental complexes near Mill Road and Ebbitts Street, such as Tysens Park, with studios to two-bedroom flats. Management decides about drilling; we work within whatever the lease allows.

How we tell the walls apart before drilling

Knocking tells you a lot. Drywall on studs sounds hollow between studs and dull over them, and a magnet finds the screw heads in a straight line. Steel studs give the same hollow sound but the magnet clings along the whole stud, and they need toggles rated for metal or a backer board across two studs (the metal-stud add is $30). Plaster on lath sounds harder and gives a stud finder false readings; we drill one small test hole where the bracket will cover it and fix into the timber we find (the plaster add is $45). Masonry — the party wall in some attached houses, or the brick behind plaster on the Lane — sounds solid everywhere and takes masonry anchors set with a hammer drill ($65 for brick, $75 for block or concrete). Board and panel walls in bungalows are a category of their own, covered in the questions below.

What “post-Sandy construction” means behind a TV

A rebuilt house is easier for the drill and harder for the cable. The walls are straight, the studs are where they should be and the outlets are modern. But the cavities may be full — packed with insulation, crossed by fire blocking at mid-height, and sealed at top and bottom for air-tightness and energy rules. An in-wall kit ($169) still works in many of those walls; in others we route the cable down to a cabinet or use a painted cord cover ($59) rather than tear into a new wall. We tell you which, and why, before drilling.

Commercial Soundbar Installation

Business Soundbar Installation on New Dorp Lane and Hylan Boulevard

New Dorp has two commercial streets that work in very different ways. New Dorp Lane is a walking street of small, mostly family-owned shops — the BID counts about 180 businesses — while Hylan Boulevard is a strip of shopping centres and larger stores built for the car. Both have screens that need sound.

New Dorp Lane, Station End

Between the railway and Richmond Road: cafés, bakeries, salons and services within a few steps of the station house. Small rooms, short counters, TVs that play news or music video at low volume. A compact bar with a capped volume is usually right.

New Dorp Lane, Toward Hylan

Restaurants, pharmacies, dentists and clinics, including the Chinese-owned businesses The City Reporter described in 2026. Waiting rooms with chairs along two walls, where the bar has to be heard at the far chair without being loud at the near one.

Hylan Boulevard Shopping Centres

Wikipedia counts five 1960s shopping centres along Hylan in New Dorp, the largest being Hylan Plaza, opened in 1966. Larger tenants, metal-stud partitions, dropped ceilings and break rooms where a TV runs all day.

Medical and Care Offices

Clinics, dental practices and the adult day-care centres that The City Reporter noted serving Chinese-speaking residents. Captions on, volume steady, nothing a client can unpair by accident.

Schools and Community Rooms

New Dorp High School and Staten Island Technical High School both stand in the neighbourhood, and community and church rooms use TVs for meetings and events. Rolling carts and wall mounts both work; carts need the bar fixed to the cart.

Landlords Above the Shops

Owners of mixed-use buildings who let the flats upstairs: one visit can cover the shop’s screen and a tenant’s TV, each on its own line and each invoiced to whoever is paying for it.

Compliance notes for business rooms

  • Things that stick out. Where people walk past a screen — a clinic corridor, the aisle beside a counter — the accessibility rules in §307.2 of the 2010 ADA Standards cap anything fixed to the wall between 27 and 80 inches up at four inches of depth. A TV plus a bar beneath it can exceed that, so in those spots we lift the pair above 80 inches or move it out of the path.
  • Music after 10 PM. The city noise code’s commercial-music rules apply between 10 PM and 7 AM, including limits measured inside nearby homes. Flats sit directly above New Dorp Lane businesses, so a restaurant or bar should treat the soundbar’s night setting as part of the installation, not an afterthought.
  • Low-voltage work. Soundbar wiring is low-voltage and doesn’t need a separate DOB permit in our experience; new outlets do, and those are an electrician’s job.
  • Insurance certificates. Shopping-centre managers and landlords often ask for one before work starts. Ours is free; send us the wording they need.

Retailers on the Lane who already have cameras or a door entry system can add a soundbar to the same visit; our AV and sound team for Staten Island businesses handles rooms where ceiling speakers make more sense than a bar.

Your Questions Answered

Soundbar Installation in New Dorp: Questions Answered

These are the questions New Dorp households ask that we rarely hear elsewhere: living rooms over garages, flats over shops, floors that move and walls that are full. Attached and semi-attached houses

Our semi-attached house has the living room directly above the garage — will the subwoofer boom down through the garage ceiling?

It can, and it is one of the most common surprises in New Dorp’s raised-garage houses. A subwoofer standing on the floor pushes on that floor as much as on the air. Over a basement full of soil and concrete, that energy has nowhere much to go. Over a garage, the floor is a framed deck with a large, hollow, usually uninsulated space beneath it and a thin metal door at one end — very close to a drum. The result is bass that sounds bigger in the garage, in the utility room and sometimes in the house next door than it does on the sofa.

The fixes are mostly about position and setting, not equipment. We keep the subwoofer off the middle of the floor span, where the joists flex most, and put it near a wall or corner where the floor is supported, ideally a wall that doesn’t sit over the garage door header. A dense pad or a heavy rug under the sub helps a little; turning the sub’s level down a few steps and letting the bar’s room-correction routine set the crossover helps a lot more. If the bar offers a night or reduced-bass mode, we show you where it is.

If the living room is mostly over the garage and you watch late, a bar with a built-in woofer and no separate subwoofer can be the better buy. It will never shake the room the way a sub does, but it won’t shake the garage either. We would rather tell you that before you buy than after the sub is unboxed.

In our attached house the front wall is mostly window and door, the back opens to the kitchen and one side is shared — which wall is actually left for the TV?

Usually the long wall opposite the party wall, and usually only part of it, because that is where the staircase runs. That still works. In most of these houses the stair is open on the living-room side or enclosed by a framed partition; either way, the first few feet of that long wall nearest the front window are often clear. A TV of up to about 55 inches and a bar below it fit there, with the sofa against the party wall facing it. The geometry isn’t perfect — you may sit a little off-centre — but a soundbar copes with that far better than TV speakers, and its dialogue setting keeps voices clear from a slight angle.

The other options each have a catch. The party wall itself can carry the TV, and the shared-wall question on our borough page covers what that means for your neighbour. The front window wall can carry it only if the TV goes beside the window rather than in front of it, and screens facing a bright window wash out by day. The kitchen opening sometimes leaves a short return wall that suits a smaller TV and a compact bar, especially if the family watches from the dining table.

Whatever wall you choose, send us one photo taken from where you sit. We check the stud layout, the outlet position, the clearance to the stair rail and whether the cable can reach a cabinet or the boxes can sit under the TV. A figure for the room comes back with that check, the same working day.

Living over New Dorp Lane

Our apartment over a New Dorp Lane shop is reached by a steep, narrow side stair — can a long soundbar box and a ladder make it up?

Almost always. Full-width soundbar cartons run to about four feet long, but they are slim and light, and we carry them up on end. The awkward items are the subwoofer box, which is heavy but compact, and a stepladder, which we bring folded and carry up first. In the narrowest stairs we unbox on the landing or on the street so the cardboard doesn’t scrape your walls, and take every piece of packaging away afterwards (the box-removal line is $25 if you want that).

The Staten Island price list on this page has no stairs line for a flat over a shop. What does help is knowing in advance: the stair’s width at its narrowest point, whether there is a turn at the top, and whether the street door is shared with the shop or has its own key. If the shop opens before your appointment, tell the staff someone is coming up; if the street door is the shop’s door, make sure somebody will be there to open it inside our two-hour window.

Getting a TV up the same stair is a different matter. Screens of 75 inches and up arrive in very large, rigid boxes that don’t bend around a turn. If you are planning a new TV as well, measure the stair before you order, or ask the store about delivery to the room; we can then hang it and fit the bar below it in one visit, priced from the Staten Island TV lines further down.

The ceiling in our apartment over the stores is a dropped grid of tiles — can the soundbar cable run above it?

Sometimes, but it is rarely the best route, and it needs the landlord’s agreement first. A suspended ceiling in a flat often hides old plaster, pipes, ductwork and other people’s cables, and in mixed-use buildings it can be part of a fire-separation arrangement between the shop and the homes above. Cables must not be laid loose on the tiles, and anything run there should be supported on its own and rated for the space it passes through.

What usually makes more sense is shorter: the TV, the bar and the boxes on the same wall, with the HDMI and power dropping straight down behind the TV to a console. On plaster or steel studs, a slim painted cord cover ($59) does that neatly without opening anything. If the boxes must live across the room, the cable run line ($99, up to 25 feet) covers a route along the skirting or behind furniture.

If your landlord is happy with a ceiling route and the space above the grid is clean and accessible, we look at it on the day and tell you whether we would use it. We won’t lift tiles that are stained, sagging or held in with clips we can’t refit, and we won’t touch anything that belongs to the shop below.

We own a two-storey building on the Lane, with the store downstairs and our own apartment upstairs — can one visit handle both TVs, and is each priced the same way?

Yes on both counts. Each TV is priced from the same lines on this page, whether it hangs over a shop counter or a living-room console, so the shop TV and the home TV are simply two sets of rows on one quote. There is no separate commercial rate for a small shop. If you want the shop part on the business’s invoice and the apartment part on a personal one, say so when you book and we split the paperwork that way.

What changes between the two rooms is the setup, not the price. Downstairs we cap the bar’s maximum volume, turn off the features a customer could trigger from a phone (Bluetooth pairing and voice assistants), label the input, and leave the remote somewhere sensible. Upstairs we set the bar for evenings: dialogue lift, a night mode and the subwoofer level chosen with the shop closed and the street quiet.

One practical detail: if both TVs are on the same Wi-Fi, the soundbar apps will see each other. We name them clearly — “Shop” and “Living Room” — and, where the bars allow it, put them on separate networks or turn off grouping so a phone upstairs can never start music in the store.

Below Hylan Boulevard

Our converted New Dorp Beach bungalow sits on piers and the floor bounces when someone walks across — will the TV and bar shake, and where does the subwoofer go?

A wall-mounted TV and bar are the right answer in a bouncy room, because the wall moves far less than the floor. A TV on a stand or a bar on a console rocks every time someone crosses the room; the same pair on the wall stays still. We fix the bracket into the wall studs, which in a bungalow are often lighter and further apart than in a modern house, and if the studs are thin or the wall is boarded we spread the load with a plywood backer board ($85) across several of them.

The subwoofer is the harder part. On a light floor over a crawl space, a sub excites the whole floor, and you feel it in your feet as much as hear it. We place it on the stiffest part of the floor — near an outside wall or over a beam rather than mid-span — and turn it down until the floor stops buzzing. In a small bungalow living room, a bar with built-in bass may simply sound better than any separate sub.

We don’t route cables down through the floor into the crawl space. Parts of New Dorp Beach, between Marine Way and Tysens Lane, sit at or below sea level and flood regularly, and anything under the house is the first thing water reaches. Cables go along the wall or behind furniture instead, and every plug goes to one surge strip above the floor.

Our house was rebuilt after Sandy and the walls are filled with spray-foam insulation — can a cable still be fished inside them?

Not usually, and we won’t force it. Spray foam, especially the closed-cell kind, fills the stud bay completely and sets hard. A fish tape can’t pass through it, and cutting a channel means opening the wall and breaking the air and insulation seal the builder worked to achieve. Fibreglass batts are different: a cable can often be eased down beside them, so it is worth knowing which insulation your builder used.

There are good alternatives. If an interior wall carries the TV — a partition between rooms rather than an outside wall — it is often uninsulated or loosely filled, and the in-wall kit ($169) goes in normally. If the TV must be on an insulated outside wall, a painted cord cover ($59) from the bar down to a console is tidy and invisible from the sofa. And in a new build, the boxes can often live in a cabinet directly beneath the TV, so the only visible cable is a short one.

If you are still building or renovating, there is a better answer: ask the contractor to set an empty conduit or a recessed media box between the TV position and the cabinet before the foam goes in. That costs little at framing and leaves a clean route for every future TV. Our structured cabling team on Staten Island can plan it with your builder.

The desk in the teenager’s room

Our teenager wants one compact bar on the desk to play both the gaming PC and the console — can it switch between them?

Yes, provided the bar has the right inputs, and many compact bars do. The neatest arrangement uses the monitor as the hub: the PC and the console both plug into the monitor by HDMI or DisplayPort, the monitor’s audio output feeds the bar, and switching the monitor’s input switches the sound with it. That works when the monitor has a headphone or optical output, which most gaming monitors do.

The alternative is a bar with two inputs — say optical from the console and USB or Bluetooth from the PC — selected with the bar’s remote or app. It works, but somebody has to remember to switch, and Bluetooth adds a delay that gamers notice. For games we prefer a wired connection and turn on the bar’s low-latency or game mode, then check lip-sync with a test clip.

On a desk, a compact bar sits on a small stand or on the desk itself in front of the monitor foot; a wall mount is rarely needed. The console-placement line ($79) covers it, and the streaming device or games console line ($49 each) covers setting each source to the right input.

HDMI eARC, ARC and optical: what your TV offers decides the cable

Most TVs sold in the last few years have one HDMI port labelled eARC or ARC. Plug the bar in there, turn on HDMI-CEC in the TV menu, and the TV remote controls the bar’s volume and power. Older TVs without ARC still work through an optical cable, but then the bar usually needs its own remote or a learned code, and lossless formats are lost. Then the bar plays whatever the TV is showing, and nobody needs to change the bar’s input when they change the channel. New Dorp Beach

The Shore That Stayed: TV Rooms in New Dorp Beach After Sandy

New Dorp Beach is the part of the neighbourhood with the longest memory. Wikipedia records a first land grant here in 1671 and a resort that grew after the Civil War: campgrounds and seasonal bungalows on the gridded side streets off Cedar Grove Avenue, wooden piers and hotels on the waterfront, the New Dorp Beach Hotel and the Cedar Grove Beach Club with its row of summer cottages. The city took much of the old resort by eminent domain for Robert Moses’s Shore Front Drive, a highway that was never built, and the land behind the dunes became parkland. The streets inland of it kept their small houses, most of them converted from summer use, with many attached homes among them.

Sandy reached those streets in October 2012. In The Neighborhoods, Rob Stephenson writes that parts of New Dorp were left under about six feet of water. A year later, residents met at the New Dorp Beach Civic Association and signed letters asking Governor Cuomo to extend the state’s voluntary buyout programme to them; DNAinfo reported that the state wanted at least 80 percent of residents to sign before considering it. In April 2014 the state said no more neighbourhoods would be added. Oakwood Beach and Ocean Breeze were bought out; New Dorp Beach was not.

What happened instead was rebuilding, house by house. Some owners repaired and raised their homes; others sold through the city’s acquisition programme, which, unlike the buyouts, resold lots to be built on again. Stephenson describes the rebuilt houses as “suspended over their foundations”. The result, today, is a neighbourhood of three kinds of house standing side by side, and each needs a different approach to the TV.

The original bungalow

Small rooms, low ceilings, light framing and, often, a floor on piers. Walls may be boards, wood panelling or beadboard over studs spaced further apart than modern ones. A compact bar on a slim bracket, a backer board if the studs are light, cables along the wall rather than under the floor, and a subwoofer only if the floor can take it.

The renovated cottage

A bungalow brought up to date — new drywall, new wiring, sometimes a second storey added. Inside, it behaves much like a 1960s house: studs where you expect them, outlets behind the TV, an ordinary wall mount. The old floor structure may still be underneath, so the subwoofer question remains.

The raised or rebuilt house

Living space on the upper level, parking and storage in the enclosure below, a long stair from the street. New insulated walls that may be closed to cable, plenty of outlets, and often fibre or cable service that enters near the ground. We keep the router, the TV boxes and the soundbar system all on the living floor, run any cable from the service entry upward inside the house rather than along the lowest level, and give the whole TV system one surge-protected strip you can reach and switch off.

Whichever house you have, the same principle applies: the expensive parts of the system live as high in the house as the room allows, and nothing that matters is installed where water reaches first. That costs nothing extra; it is simply where we put things.

Can the soundbar be set to come on at the same moderate volume every time, whatever the last person left it at?
+

Many bars can, although each maker names the setting differently — look for a start-up volume, maximum volume or volume limit option in the app. Where the bar has it, we set a comfortable start-up level and, if you want one, a ceiling. Where it doesn't, the TV's own volume limit, or a universal remote programmed with a fixed volume step, can do a similar job.

Our soundbar spent two years in a storage unit while the house was rebuilt — is it safe to plug in again?
+

Usually, but check it first. Let it sit indoors for a day so any condensation dries, then look for corrosion on the connectors, rust on the screws, a cracked power cord or a musty smell. If the unit was stored somewhere that flooded or stayed damp, don't plug it in; we can test it on the day, and a bar showing signs of water damage shouldn't be mounted. Expect a large firmware update the first time it connects.

We sing karaoke at family parties — can microphones run through the soundbar without the voice lagging?
+

Not well, in most cases. A soundbar processes sound before playing it, and when a microphone signal passes through a TV or app and then the bar, the delay is enough to throw a singer off. The better arrangement is a karaoke machine or small mixer with its own speaker for the microphones, while the TV and bar play the music and lyrics. Some bars have a low-latency mode that narrows the gap; we can set it and connect the karaoke box, but we won't promise lag-free singing through a bar alone.

Our cats walk along the TV console — how do we stop them knocking the soundbar off?
+

Put the bar on the wall. A wall-mounted bar ($129) sits above the console, out of a cat's path, and its cables go into a cord cover or the wall so there is nothing to bat or chew. If the bar has to stay on the console, we set it back from the front edge, use removable non-slip pads under it and route the cables behind the furniture.
