  • Three landmark districts cover the outside of hundreds of buildings. The Prospect Lefferts Gardens Historic District was designated in 1979 (LP-01024, October 9, 1979), Ocean on the Park in 2009 and Chester Court in 2014, and the larger district joined the National Register in 2023. Fire Alarm Installation for Prospect Lefferts Gardens's Architecture

Six kinds of building make up nearly every fire alarm job in Prospect Lefferts Gardens. They differ in who lives inside, how the stairs run, what the walls are made of, and how much of the outside is protected. Those differences decide the plan well before any brand or model comes up.

The Manor single-family house

Two- and three-story brick or stone row houses, plus some freestanding houses, built under the Lefferts deed rules between 1895 and 1925 and still single-family by covenant.

The two-family house behind one stoop door

Row houses outside the Manor built for two households, where a tall stoop leads to a single entrance and one stair that both apartments use.

The house divided into apartments

Rowhouses such as those in Ocean on the Park, first owned by single families and divided into apartments by the 1930s. Some have been put back together since, and some have not.

The six-story apartment house

The large buildings of the 1920s through the 1940s on Ocean Avenue and the side streets, each holding dozens of apartments around a lobby, a stair and a cellar.

The tenement and frame-house blocks

Four-story tenements, frame houses and rowhouses on the streets running east from Rogers Avenue toward New York Avenue.

The store with apartments above, and the new building

Mixed-use buildings along Flatbush Avenue, plus new construction such as The Parkline and the mid-rises filed for Lefferts Avenue, Maple Street and Flatbush Avenue.

The Manor single-family house: one household, three or four floors, solid walls

A Lefferts Manor house is about as straightforward an occupancy as Brooklyn offers: one family, one front door, a cellar, a parlor floor, and one or two bedroom floors above. The challenge is the building itself. The deed rules required brick or stone, and the Historic Districts Council describes two- and three-story row houses, some with limestone fronts, along with freestanding houses in Romanesque, Colonial and Tudor Revival styles. Party walls are masonry, the interior walls are often plaster over lath or masonry, and the floors may carry decades of renovations layered on one another. That shapes three decisions. First, where each alarm goes: on each level, outside each sleeping area, inside each bedroom, and in the cellar where the boiler and the laundry usually are. Second, how the alarms are linked. Hardwired interconnection is the most dependable, but it means fishing cable through plaster and between floors. Wireless interconnection avoids that, as long as we confirm the signal reaches through the masonry on that particular house. Third, how the work is done without damage. We drill small, patch neatly, run along existing chases where they exist, and protect floors and stair runners. We plan cable routes from the inside out, so nothing ever needs to be run down the face of a landmarked front.

The two-family house behind one stoop door: two households, one way out

6sqft's neighborhood guide describes the two-family row houses here as having a tall stoop leading to a single entrance that serves both apartments. That means both households share one door, one vestibule and usually one stair. A fire on the lower floor can put smoke into the only route the upper household has. In practice, the plan treats the house as two homes with one shared spine. Each apartment gets its own smoke and CO alarms, in and outside every sleeping area, and the shared hall and stair get coverage too. The decision that matters most is how the alarms are linked. Linking everything throughout the house means a fire anywhere wakes everyone. The trade-off is that burnt toast downstairs also wakes the tenant upstairs. Keeping each apartment separate and linking only the shared areas cuts down on nuisance alarms, but one household may not hear an alarm in the other. We explain both choices, recommend one for your layout, and write down which alarms belong to which household and who tests them. In an owner-occupied two-family, the owner usually holds that responsibility. In a house with two tenants, it belongs to the landlord.

The house divided into apartments: plans that changed more than once

Google Arts & Culture's account of Ocean on the Park notes that its rowhouses were first owned by single families, but many had been divided into apartments by the 1930s. Houses like that have often changed hands, and layouts, several times since. A parlor became a studio. A rear room became a kitchen. A cellar became a bedroom, then storage, then a bedroom again. Each version may have left alarms behind, sometimes in rooms that no longer exist in the same form, while the rooms people sleep in now have none. Our survey in a building like this starts from the present: who lives on which floor today, where each household sleeps and cooks, and how each one gets out. Then we compare that with what is on the ceilings. Usually some alarms are right where they should be, some are in the wrong place for the current layout, and some are missing. If you are planning to put a house back to fewer apartments, or the reverse, tell us before the walls close, because the alarm plan should follow the new layout, not the old one. Where a change in the number of households affects what the code requires, that is a question for your architect or engineer, and we will say so.

The six-story apartment house: a lobby, a stair, a cellar and dozens of homes

The six-story apartment houses on Ocean Avenue, Lefferts Avenue, Lincoln Road, Clarkson Avenue and the streets between them are the neighborhood's largest occupancies apart from the new tower. 6sqft notes that their six-story height and large footprints grew out of the 1901 Tenement House Act, and that most were built from the 1920s to the 1940s. A building like 415 Lefferts Avenue, which city data lists at 96 units, has dozens of kitchens, at least one long stair, a lobby, a cellar holding the boiler and meters, and often a roof bulkhead and a space under the roof. Whatever the building has for detection, whether apartment alarms, hall devices, a panel or some mix, the job starts with an as-found survey. We find the panel if there is one, trace what reports to it, check the age of every device we can reach, test the standby batteries, and look for FDNY paperwork. Then we sort the work. ASM Integrators' summary of the rules draws the key line: replacing detectors, pull stations or control panels with identical parts and no design change is a repair, while moving or replacing the control panel requires new plans filed with the FDNY. In an occupied building, we work floor by floor so protection is never off where people are sleeping.

The tenement and frame-house blocks: narrow stairs, wood framing, mixed ownership

Living Places describes the streets running east from Rogers Avenue as a mix of frame houses, rowhouses and tenements, including four-story tenements. These buildings tend to be narrower than the Ocean Avenue apartment houses. They have tighter stairs, fewer apartments per floor, and sometimes wood framing where the Manor has masonry. The fire at 572 Rogers Avenue, in a three-story building where smoke from the first floor moved up the stairwell and trapped three people on the third floor, shows the main concern in buildings of this size. The stair is the only way out, and it is also where the smoke goes. Our plan in a small walk-up puts the household alarms where they belong inside each apartment, then looks at the stair and the public hall as one vertical space that needs its own warning. In a frame house, we pay extra attention to concealed spaces in the walls and under the roof, where wood framing gives a hidden fire something to travel along. We are low-voltage installers, not electricians. If we see old electrical service on the survey, we note it and recommend a licensed electrician look at it, since the reports from 80 Clarkson Avenue show residents raising exactly that concern.

The store with apartments above, and the new building with a full system

Along Flatbush Avenue, Living Places describes apartment buildings with commercial ground floors and two- and three-story commercial buildings. The Hawthorne Street fire happened in a four-story building with a deli and bodega downstairs. In a building like that, the store keeps different hours from the apartments. It may run a kitchen, coolers or charging equipment, and it is usually empty overnight while the families above are asleep. The plan has to make the ground floor warn the floors above, and it needs a written record of who owns, maintains and receives signals from each part. New buildings are a different job again. The Parkline, built to LEED Gold and Energy Star standards, has retail and community facility space and its own co-generation plant. The new six-story, eight-story and twelve-story buildings filed on Lefferts Avenue, Flatbush Avenue and Maple Street will open with fire alarm systems designed from the first drawing. Under FDNY Fire Code Chapter 9, fire alarm design and installation documents go to the FDNY for review and approval before installation. Our part in a new building is installation to the approved drawings, labeling, pre-testing and coordination with the design professional. We make that clear at the first meeting, so nobody mistakes us for the designer.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Questions a Technician Asks Inside a Prospect Lefferts Gardens House Before Touching a Ceiling

Who sleeps where, tonight. The first question is not about alarms. Which rooms do people sleep in now, including the cellar room a relative uses, the third-floor room a student rents in a two-family, or the parlor sofa during the holidays? In a house that has been renovated or divided over the years, the answer often differs from what the room was built for.

Follow the stair, top to bottom. In a row house the stair is the chimney. The technician walks it from the top floor down to the cellar, noting doors that close it off, open landings, a skylight or bulkhead at the top, and where smoke from the kitchen or the cellar would rise.

Look up, then look higher. At each floor we note ceiling height, plaster condition, cornices and medallions, beams, and any hatch to an attic or a roof space. The fires at 80 Clarkson Avenue and 203 Fenimore Street both reached the space under the roof, so the technician checks whether that space can be reached, and what it holds, before deciding what kind of detector it gets.

Listen from the farthest bedroom. With bedroom doors closed and any window air conditioner running, a second person sets off an existing alarm, or a test sounder, at the far end of the house. If the person in the top-floor back room cannot hear it clearly, the plan needs interconnection or an additional alarm.

The cellar last, closely. Boiler, water heater, flue connection, gas meter, laundry, storage, and anything being charged. Firehouse.com's 2026 report that the FDNY saw battery fires rise about 70 percent is why chargers and e-bikes go on the survey sheet in every cellar and hall.

What the Fires Here Show

Smoke Smelled by a Neighbor, a Horn on Hawthorne Street, a Fire Under the Roof: Reading the Local Fire Record

The fires below were all reported in Prospect Lefferts Gardens or placed there by the outlets that covered them. We describe them using only what those reports say. We do not claim to know how any of them was detected, whether any alarm worked, or what caused them unless an outlet reported it. We list them because each one points to a specific decision in a fire alarm plan.

35 Winthrop Street, early 2026: found by smell. News 12 Brooklyn reported that an 81-year-old woman died in an early-morning apartment fire, and that a neighbor noticed what he described as an electrical smoke smell and called for help. The Brooklyn Eagle reported that the fire was reported by neighbors who smelled smoke, and that five people were injured. The lesson for a plan is simple and hard. A person asleep cannot smell smoke, and an older person living alone may have nobody nearby to notice. Alarms belong inside the sleeping room as well as outside it, and every alarm in a home should sound when any one of them does.

292 Hawthorne Street, June 2023: woken by a horn. Brooklyn Paper reported that neighbors hailed a sanitation truck driver as a hero after he repeatedly blew his horn to wake residents of a four-story mixed-use building with a deli and bodega on the ground floor. Whatever happened inside, the warning that reached people came from the street. In a building with a store below and apartments above, the plan has to answer a specific question: if a fire starts in the closed store at night, what makes a sound on the floors above, and who hears it?

80 Clarkson Avenue, December 2022: trapped above the top floor. ABC7 reported that the flames were trapped between the roof and the building's top floor, that some residents suspected faulty wiring, and that a resident said the building was still using fuses. A fire in that void sits above every detector in the top-floor apartments, behind a ceiling. Whether a building system should reach into that space is a design question. But the survey should always note whether the space exists, how it is reached, and what runs through it.

130 Clarkson Avenue, September 2020: a fire high in a seven-story building. The UFA reported that firefighters found heavy fire and smoke billowing from the fifth floor of the seven-story building, and seven people were injured in what it called a fast-moving fire. In a taller apartment house, people on the floors above a fire are in the path of its smoke, which is why hall and stair detection, and the condition of the panel serving it, get as much attention on our surveys as the alarms inside apartments.

572 Rogers Avenue: a stairwell full of smoke. CBS New York reported that three people were trapped on the third floor of a three-story building, and that the FDNY described a heavy smoke condition on the first floor going up the stairwell. When there is one stair, early warning on the lower floors buys the upper floors time to get out before that stair fills.

203 Fenimore Street: from a bedroom floor to the attic. CBS New York reported that a fire broke out on the second floor, quickly spread to the attic, and that a woman was pronounced dead. An attic above living space is a place where fire can travel unseen, and a place nobody goes into. When we survey a house with an attic, we decide whether heat detection belongs up there, based on access, temperature swings and dust.

456 Brooklyn Avenue and the Ebbets Field Apartments. CBS New York reported that a lightning strike hit 456 Brooklyn Avenue and that some 220 firefighters battled the fire. ABC7 placed a December 2018 fire at the Ebbets Field Apartments in the neighborhood, though many sources put the complex in Crown Heights. In that fire, a unit on the 12th floor burned and smoke spread into the hallways because residents left their door open, and ABC7 noted that a 2008 fire in the same complex killed an FDNY lieutenant. One of those stories is about what a building system can withstand. The other is about what it cannot do on its own: an alarm warns people, but a closed door is what keeps the smoke in.

Compliance notes for Prospect Lefferts Gardens buildings and businesses

  • Plans come before installation. FDNY Fire Code Chapter 9 requires fire alarm design and installation documents to be submitted to the FDNY for review and approval before installation.
  • Repair and redesign are different things. According to ASM Integrators' summary, replacing detectors, pull stations or control panels with identical parts and no design change is a repair. Moving or replacing a control panel requires new plans filed with the FDNY.
  • Small buildings have a specific exception. The 2022 NYC Building Code, as published on UpCodes, does not require a manual fire alarm system in buildings of two stories or fewer, if each dwelling unit is separated by one-hour fire partitions and has a direct exit to a public way, exit court or yard. Whether a particular building qualifies is decided from its actual construction.
  • Height changes the system. SPARC Fire Protection's summary notes that one-way voice communication is required in buildings over 125 feet, measured from the lowest level of fire department vehicle access to the highest occupied floor. A tower like The Parkline is the kind of building where that applies. The six-story apartment houses are not.
  • The front is reviewed separately. In the three landmark districts, any exterior alteration needs Landmarks Preservation Commission approval before work begins. That approval is separate from any FDNY or Department of Buildings process.
  • Outages are limited. The Fire Code limits how long a system can be out of service in an occupied building, which is why we stage every apartment house upgrade so each section is restored before the crew leaves.
  • We say what our license covers. We are a New York State licensed low-voltage installer. On work that requires a filed design, a registered design professional designs and files, and we install, label and pre-test to the approved drawings.
Three Landmark Districts

Fire Alarms on Landmarked Blocks: Keeping the Work Indoors in Lefferts Manor, Ocean on the Park and Chester Court

Hundreds of houses in Prospect Lefferts Gardens are inside one of three historic districts. The Prospect Lefferts Gardens Historic District, designated in 1979, takes in the Manor streets: Sterling Street and Lefferts Avenue between Bedford and Nostrand Avenues, Lincoln Road from Flatbush to Rogers Avenue, and Maple, Midwood, Rutland and Fenimore Streets. Ocean on the Park, designated in 2009, covers 189 to 211 Ocean Avenue, including No. 193, where Charles Ebbets, owner of the Brooklyn Dodgers and developer of Ebbets Field, once lived. Chester Court, designated in 2014, is a cul-de-sac of eighteen Tudor Revival houses. Owners in these districts sometimes assume landmark status makes fire alarm work complicated. For almost every household job, it does not. We are installers, not preservation consultants or lawyers, and this is not legal advice. It is how we keep a landmark-district job from stalling.

Nearly all of a home fire alarm lives indoors. Smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, heat detectors, the cable that links them, any panel or power supply, and any sounder in a hall are interior items. The Lefferts Manor Association explains that the historic district status applies to the exteriors of buildings and that proposed alterations to the outside need Commission approval before work begins. A household fire alarm can usually be planned and installed entirely inside, with nothing for the Commission to review. The discipline we apply is to keep every cable path inside the building envelope. For example, we do not run a line out a window and down the front to reach the cellar, because that turns an interior job into an exterior one.

The exterior exceptions are specific, and we flag them early. The items that can cross the line are an exterior bell, horn or strobe; a box, keyed switch or annunciator beside an entrance; a monitoring antenna; and any visible conduit on the front of the house. We raise any of these at the survey, not after the plan is drawn. For a single-family house there is rarely a reason to put fire alarm equipment outside at all. For an apartment house or a store in a district, the design professional may be asked whether an interior location meets the same purpose, before anything exterior goes on a drawing.

Ordinary upkeep is a different matter from new hardware. The LPC has published an updated Rowhouse Manual that sets out which work counts as ordinary maintenance and which needs a permit. Indoor work such as replacing a failed alarm, changing batteries, repairing a cable fault, or swapping a like-for-like device stays indoors and stays simple. Adding, moving or removing anything on the outside is an exterior change and should be checked first.

Check the address before assuming. The districts do not cover the whole neighborhood. Many apartment houses, the streets east of Rogers Avenue and the commercial avenues include buildings outside any district. The LPC's map is the place to confirm a specific address. One website gives a 2015 designation date for the Prospect Lefferts Gardens district, which conflicts with the LPC map's 1979 date. We go by the LPC's own map.

Manor houses and the way they are built

Does living under the Lefferts Manor single-family covenant mean our house will never need a building fire alarm system?

As long as it stays a single-family home, it almost certainly stays on the household path of smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, not a filed building system with a panel. The covenant itself is a private deed restriction enforced by the Lefferts Manor Association. It is not a fire code rule. What decides the fire alarm requirement is how the building is actually used and built. A one-family house is the simplest residential occupancy, and the protection it needs is good alarms in the right places, linked together. That changes if the use changes. If a house is converted to more households, or part of it becomes a business, or it is rebuilt in a way the code treats differently, the question has to be asked again by your architect or engineer. The covenant does not make your house automatically safe either. It keeps the use simple, so the plan can focus on what matters in a single-family house: an alarm in each bedroom, one outside each sleeping area, one on every level including the cellar, carbon monoxide alarms near sleeping areas and near the boiler, and all of them sounding together.

Our row house has one open staircase rising from the parlor floor to the top floor, with no doors on it anywhere. Why does that change where alarms belong?

Because an open stair turns the house into a chimney, and that works in two directions. Smoke from a fire in the parlor or the kitchen will rise straight up the stair and reach the top floor fast, often faster than it spreads sideways across the floor where the fire started. That is good for detection at the top of the stair and bad for anyone sleeping up there. So we always put an alarm at or near the top of an open stair, where rising smoke collects first. We put one at each landing level outside the bedrooms, and one inside each bedroom, so people behind a closed door get warned even if the stair is filling. The open stair is also the household's way out, which is why interconnection matters so much in a house like this. A fire on the parlor floor must sound the alarm in the top-floor bedroom right away, before the stair becomes impassable. If the stair continues down to the cellar, the cellar alarm becomes the first warning of a boiler-room fire. It should be linked as well, so the warning goes up the house as fast as the smoke would.

Our parlor floor is a double room that pocket doors can close into two. Is it one space or two for smoke detection?

Plan it for the way the doors are actually used. If the pocket doors stay open nearly all the time, the double parlor behaves like one long room, and one well-placed alarm may cover it, depending on its size and ceiling. If you close the doors at night, for warmth in winter, for privacy when guests stay, or to make the back half into a bedroom, then smoke from a fire in the front half has to get past a closed door before it reaches an alarm in the back half, and the reverse. In that case each half deserves its own alarm, linked to the rest of the house. Use matters even more than layout. If anyone ever sleeps in either half, that half needs an alarm inside it. We ask how the doors are used on the survey, look at the ceiling height and any beams or plaster detail, and place alarms where they stay clear of the corners where air does not move and away from cornices and medallions. Usually that means two alarms in a double parlor, even if one would cover it while the doors are open.

Will wireless interconnected alarms actually reach each other through the brick and stone of a Manor row house?

Often yes, but we never assume it. We test it in your house before we rely on it. Wireless interconnected alarms use radio signals to tell each other when one is sounding. Masonry walls, plaster on metal lath, ductwork and floors with old repairs all weaken that signal, and the Manor's brick and stone construction is exactly the kind that can. Each manufacturer publishes a range, but that figure assumes conditions much kinder than a 1909 row house. So on the survey we place the units, or a test pair, in their intended positions: cellar, parlor, each bedroom floor and the top of the stair. Then we trigger each one and confirm that every other one sounds, with doors closed. If one location will not link reliably, we move it, add a unit between, or hardwire that part of the house. Hardwired interconnection is the most dependable choice when it is practical, and a renovation with open walls is the best time to do it. Wireless linked systems on our Brooklyn list run $600 to $1,200, and a custom hardwired system through masonry runs $2,000 to $6,000. We price whichever one your walls will actually allow.

Apartment houses, complexes and new buildings

Our complex has several apartment buildings around shared grounds. Should each building have its own panel, or one panel for all of them?

That is a design decision that depends on how the buildings are built and connected, and on what the system has to do. A registered design professional makes it on any filed work. Some complexes share a cellar, a boiler plant or underground passages, and some are separate buildings that only share a courtyard. Separate buildings often have separate systems, each with its own panel, because firefighters arriving at one entrance need to know what is happening in that building, not somewhere else on the grounds. Connected buildings may be served by one system or by networked panels that report to one place. Our part starts with an as-found survey of each building. We document what panel serves which building, how they are connected, where the central station connection is, and whether anyone has records. That survey is what the design professional works from. On the maintenance side, whatever the arrangement, the management office should have one written map showing which device is in which building and which panel it reports to. We leave that map behind, building by building, after any work.

Our building has a co-generation plant in the cellar that makes its own electricity and heat. Does that change carbon monoxide detection in the apartments above?

It means carbon monoxide deserves careful attention in the plant room and near anything that shares air or a flue with it. The design of that detection belongs to the building's engineers and the plant's maintenance contractor. A co-generation plant burns fuel to make electricity and recovers the heat, so it is a combustion appliance that runs for long hours. The Parkline at 626 Flatbush Avenue is the neighborhood's example of a building with one; we do not describe its systems and do not present it as a customer. In general, CO detection for combustion equipment starts in the room where it runs, and how that detection reports, to a building panel, to building staff or to a ventilation control, is set by the building's design. In the apartments above, the usual rule still applies: CO alarms near sleeping areas. Residents should not assume the plant room's detection protects them. If you manage a building like this and want to know whether the apartment-level alarms and the plant-room detection are both in place and working, we can survey both. Where the plant-room side is part of an engineered system, we will refer it to the people responsible for that system.

A school uses the community space at the base of our apartment building. How are fire alarm tests scheduled around children and teachers?

Around the school's calendar, agreed in writing ahead of time, with the school's staff in the loop. When a school occupies part of a residential building, the building fire alarm tests and the school's own fire drills both affect the same people. A test that sets off horns and strobes in a room full of young children with no warning causes panic, and it can also undermine the drills the school runs to teach children what the alarm means. The Maple Street School, which uses the community space at The Parkline, is the local example of this arrangement; we do not describe that building's procedures. In general, we ask the building manager to bring the school's director into the scheduling. We test the school's area before or after school hours, or during a planned drill if the school prefers. We put the date and time in writing to both the building and the school. If any device in the school's space has to sound during the school day, we make sure the staff knows exactly when. The same care applies to quieter tests on residential floors, so that families are not caught off guard either.

Our new building is sealed tight for energy efficiency. Does an airtight building change how smoke reaches the alarms?

It can change how air moves inside apartments, so placement should account for the ventilation design, not just the floor plan. Buildings built to standards like LEED and Energy Star, as The Parkline was, are designed to leak far less air than older buildings. Fresh air comes in through mechanical ventilation rather than gaps around windows, and the supply and exhaust points create steady air currents. Those currents can push smoke away from an alarm placed in their path, or draw cooking steam toward an alarm near the kitchen and cause nuisance alarms. In a new building, the apartment alarms are often part of the original design, and we would not move them without the building's approval. Where we are adding or replacing alarms, we look at where the supply and exhaust grilles are and keep alarms out of their direct airflow, following the manufacturer's spacing guidance. In an older building that has had its windows replaced and its gaps sealed, the same thinking applies on a smaller scale. A tighter apartment also holds cooking smoke longer, which makes choosing the right alarm near the kitchen more important.

Kitchens, candles, chimneys and storms

Our restaurant kitchen cooks over charcoal and very hot ovens: jerk drums, a tandoor, a wood-fired oven. What detection works, and do we need carbon monoxide detection as well?

Heat detection suited to the cook line, not smoke detection, near the hottest equipment, and yes, think about carbon monoxide wherever charcoal or wood burns indoors. Charcoal and wood produce smoke and a lot of heat as a normal part of cooking. A smoke detector anywhere near the line will sound constantly, and staff will end up covering it or pulling it down, which leaves the kitchen with nothing. A heat detector rated for the kitchen's normal temperatures warns about a real fire without reacting to service. Smoke detection belongs farther away, in the dining room, storage and back of house, where smoke means something is wrong. Charcoal also produces carbon monoxide, and if the exhaust is not capturing it, CO can build up in the kitchen and drift toward the dining room or the apartments above. That makes CO detection a sensible addition wherever solid fuel burns indoors. All of this has to work with the hood, its suppression system and its exhaust, which are separate trades. So we coordinate with the hood contractor on what signal goes where. If the building has apartments above, the plan also decides how a kitchen fire after closing reaches the people upstairs.

Our wine bar puts a lit candle on every table. Does open flame in the dining room change what goes on the ceiling?

It changes the choice and placement of detection less than you might think, and it changes the closing routine more. A candle burning normally gives off very little smoke, and a well-placed photoelectric smoke detector in a dining room with a reasonable ceiling height usually ignores it. What sets off alarms is the burst of smoke when many candles are blown out at once at closing, or a candle left smoldering under a menu or a napkin. So we keep smoke detectors in the dining room away from directly above where candles usually sit, especially low-hanging fixtures that hold heat and smoke. We may also suggest blowing out candles one table at a time, or switching to a snuffer. The bigger fire risk with candles is after hours. A candle missed at closing, near paper or fabric, in a room that empties at two in the morning under apartments, is exactly the kind of fire detection should catch while it is small. That is why a bar on Rogers Avenue or Flatbush Avenue with homes above should have detection that reports to someone when the room is empty, whether that is monitoring through our partners or alarms the tenants above can hear.

Our boiler vents through an old masonry chimney that runs up past the upstairs bedrooms. Where does carbon monoxide detection belong?

Near the boiler, and near every sleeping area the chimney passes, on every floor. Carbon monoxide from a boiler normally goes up the flue and out of the house. But an old masonry chimney can develop cracks, gaps between the flue and the bricks, or a partial blockage. When that happens, CO can escape into the house anywhere along the chimney's run, not only in the cellar. In a row house, the chimney usually rises through the middle or the party wall, past bedrooms on the upper floors. So a CO alarm in the cellar near the boiler is the first essential, and CO alarms on each upper floor near the sleeping rooms, especially rooms that share a wall with the chimney, are the second. We follow the manufacturer's instructions on height and on distance from the appliance, and we use combination smoke and CO alarms where they fit the location so there are fewer devices on the ceiling. An alarm is not a fix for the chimney. If you suspect a flue problem, have the chimney and the boiler venting inspected by a qualified contractor, and treat any CO alarm as a reason to leave the house and call for help.

Lightning hit 456 Brooklyn Avenue and started a fire that some 220 firefighters fought. Can lightning or a power surge knock out a fire alarm panel, and what protects it?

It can, and protection starts with proper installation: surge protection where the panel's power and outside connections enter, good grounding, and cable routes that do not invite a surge. A building fire alarm panel has several paths for a surge to get in: its electrical supply, the phone, cellular or network line it uses to reach the monitoring center, and any cable that leaves the building or runs near something that does. A nearby strike, or a surge on the utility lines, can damage the panel's electronics or one of its circuits. You may find out only when the panel shows trouble, or when it should have reported and did not. We install surge protection on the panel's power and communication connections where the panel and the design call for it, check the grounding, and keep fire alarm cable away from routes that run outside the building. After a storm, a trouble light or loss of communication should be checked promptly, not reset and forgotten. Damage from power surges is excluded from our product warranty, which is all the more reason to install protection from the start.

Events and visitors

Visitors walk through our home during the Lefferts Manor House Tour. Should anything be done with the alarms before we open the doors?

Yes, and the steps are simple. Test the alarms a few days before, keep them all working throughout, and plan the day so nothing in the house starts a fire. The annual Lefferts Manor House Tour is one of the neighborhood's best-known events, and opening a house to visitors changes how it is used for a day. More people are on every floor and stair, rooms are dressed with candles, flowers and lamps, and the kitchen may be serving refreshments. A few days ahead, press each alarm's test button and confirm every interconnected alarm sounds. Replace any alarm past its date, and check that none has been taken down for painting or decorating and left off. On the day, keep candles in places where visitors cannot knock them into curtains or flowers, or use battery candles. Do not disable any alarm because the kitchen might set it off; choose quieter refreshments instead. Make sure the route out is clear, since visitors do not know your stair or your back door. If anything in the house is unusual, such as an attic room open to visitors, a cellar that is part of the tour, or a fireplace that will be lit, tell us when you book a check, and we will look at those spaces too.

FAQ

Prospect Lefferts Gardens Fire Alarm Installation FAQ

Can a smoke alarm be hidden inside a decorative cover so it does not stand out on our plaster ceiling?
+

We do not put alarms inside covers, boxes or decorative housings unless the manufacturer lists that cover for the alarm, because anything around the vents can keep smoke out and delay the warning. What we can do is choose a low-profile model, place it where it reads as part of the ceiling rather than in the middle of a medallion, and keep it clear of cornices. Never paint an alarm to match the ceiling, since paint can clog the openings.

Our dog and cats spend the workday alone in the house. How would anyone learn about a fire while we are out?
+

Alarms that only sound inside the house will not reach you, and pets cannot call for help. The answer is detection that reports somewhere: central station monitoring through our partners, listed at $19.99 a month, which alerts a monitoring center that can dispatch help and call you. We place smoke and heat detection on the floors where the animals stay and near the likeliest starting points, and we give the monitoring center a note that pets are inside.

Our basement office has a closet full of networking equipment and battery backup units. Does that closet need its own detection?
+

A closet that holds equipment running all day, with battery backup units and their batteries, is a sensible place for a detector, especially with the door closed. Smoke from a failing power supply or battery can fill a small closet before it reaches an alarm in the next room. We usually put a smoke or heat detector inside the closet, linked to the rest of the house, and recommend leaving air space around the equipment and replacing old backup batteries on the maker's schedule.

Our house east of Rogers Avenue is wood frame, with brick buildings on either side. Does a frame house need a different detection plan?
+

The basic layout of alarms is the same: every level, inside and outside every bedroom, and carbon monoxide near sleeping areas and fuel-burning equipment. What changes is how carefully we look at concealed spaces. Wood framing can let a hidden fire travel inside walls and under the roof, so we check attic and roof space access and consider heat detection there. Cable runs are usually easier in a frame house, which often makes hardwired linking practical and affordable.

When our linked alarms go off, every one sounds at once and we cannot tell which floor started it. Can that be fixed?
+

Yes. Many interconnected alarm lines include a feature that makes the alarm that first sensed smoke flash or sound differently from the others, and some models announce the room by voice. If your current alarms lack it, we can replace them with a compatible set that has it, and label each one by room. Then, when the house goes off at night, a quick look at which alarm is flashing tells you whether the problem is in the cellar or the kitchen.
