The Neighborhood

Why Sheepshead Bay Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Sheepshead Bay is a neighborhood of houses with driveways and back doors, of postwar brick co-ops by the dozen, and of low streets close to the water, and every one of those things changes where a camera does its work. The sources do not agree on exactly where it begins and ends. Wikipedia draws it from Ocean Parkway on the west to Nostrand and Gerritsen avenues on the east, and from Kings Highway on the north down to the water; another neighborhood guide draws a tighter box, from Coney Island Avenue to Nostrand Avenue and from Avenue U down to Emmons Avenue and the inlet. Either way, the heart of it is the same: the bay and its piers along Emmons Avenue, the commercial strip of Sheepshead Bay Road, the Avenue U shopping street, and the house and co-op blocks in between. NYC Parks records that a large farm here was subdivided in the 1870s, with political boss John McKane guiding the development, and that more than 400 houses had gone up by the end of the century. The racetrack that once stood here gave way to the Sheepshead Speedway and then, in 1919, to housing, according to the Brooklyn Eagle, and the city widened and paved Emmons Avenue in the 1930s. In the 1950s brick apartment buildings replaced many of the wooden houses, and by 1960, NYC Parks says, Sheepshead Bay was the fastest growing community in Brooklyn. That history is still visible on the street: a wood-frame bungalow on a walkway, a 1950s brick co-op on the next block, and a detached house with a driveway around the corner.

The building stock is what decides how a camera job runs here. One co-op directory lists 41 co-op buildings in Sheepshead Bay, 24 of them elevator buildings and 17 walk-ups. Most of the elevator buildings are six-story postwar blocks, such as 2620 and 2650 East 13th Street with 192 apartments each, or 1516 Voorhies Avenue, built in 1961 with 95 apartments and a live-in super. Some are older: 2950 Ocean Avenue went up in 1930, four floors and 63 units, while 3060 Ocean Avenue dates from 1956 with 149 units and a full-time doorman. On Emmons Avenue, 1625 Emmons was built in 1962 with 148 units, an evening doorman and a ground-floor apartment with its own private street entrance, and the Atlantis at 3235 Emmons has seven floors and 172 units. Two NYCHA developments, the Sheepshead Bay Houses and the Nostrand Houses, both completed in 1950, sit on the east side of the neighborhood. Then there are the houses: detached homes, semi-attached brick houses, townhouses on Voorhies Avenue, narrow lots off East 22nd Street, and the bungalows near Emmons Avenue, some on streets and some on pedestrian walkways, a few of them raised onto new pilings since Hurricane Sandy.

ABC7 reported that in September 2026 residents were trying to identify a man who damaged more than 20 cars, and that a resident's home security footage showed a person jumping onto a vehicle and shattering the windshield. News 12 reported that in March 2026 a home on East 9th Street was broken into and a car stolen around 2 a.m., and that two days later, a few blocks away in Gravesend, two people entered a home and took two car keys; Shmira Public Safety stepped up its patrols after both. CBS New York reported an earlier burglary, captured on surveillance video, near East 12th Street and Avenue X around 2 p.m. on February 27, where the suspects escaped through the back door. In a Brooklyn commercial burglary spree covered by CBS New York, one burglar at Harvest Grocery in Sheepshead Bay used a broom to try to cover a security camera, and the owner of Lucky 7 Smoke Shop arrived to find the ATM knocked off and the cameras broken. We found no source that ranks Sheepshead Bay blocks by crime, and we do not offer one.

The ground is the other half of the story. Urban Omnibus describes the bungalow courts north of Emmons Avenue as surrounded by higher ground on all sides, like bathtubs. The Department of City Planning's Sheepshead Bay summary report says many homeowners here have basements below the design flood elevation and subject to flooding. City Limits reported that roughly 85 percent of businesses on Emmons Avenue suffered some form of damage in Sandy. And in February 2026 a water main break left some basements and garages with up to 20 inches of water, according to the Brooklyn Eagle. A camera on an outside wall usually sits well above any of that. The recorder that holds every clip, the network switch that powers the cameras and the battery unit that keeps them running are a different matter, and on too many systems they sit on a basement floor or a garage shelf a foot off the slab. The Water Line

Cameras High, Recorder Higher: Keeping a Sheepshead Bay System Recording When the Ground Takes Water

A camera system is only as good as the box that stores what it sees. In most of Brooklyn, nobody thinks twice about putting that box in the basement next to the internet modem. In Sheepshead Bay that habit can cost an owner the whole record at the moment it matters most. The planning department's summary report says many homeowners here have basements below the design flood elevation. The bungalow courts off Emmons Avenue, as Urban Omnibus put it, sit like bathtubs below the ground around them. And flooding here does not only come from storms: the February 2026 water main break the Brooklyn Eagle covered put up to 20 inches of water into some basements and garages. A recorder sitting on the floor, or a power strip on the slab feeding the switch that powers every camera, goes out with the first inch.

So on a Sheepshead Bay job, choosing where the equipment lives comes before choosing cameras. The outdoor cameras themselves are rarely the problem; they are rated for weather and sit on walls well above the ground. What needs protecting is the recorder and its drive, the network switch if there is one, the battery backup unit, the internet modem and router the remote viewing depends on, and the outlet all of it plugs into. Each of those should sit above the highest water the property has seen, in a spot that stays dry and does not cook in summer, with enough air around it to stay cool. The cable can still travel through the basement or crawl space; cable on the joists is fine. It is the electronics and the power that move up.

Power that stays on, and comes back on

Every recorder we leave in Sheepshead Bay sits on a battery backup unit, and the internet modem and router go on the same unit when they are close enough, so a short outage does not leave a gap in the recording or cut off the phone app. A battery unit covers a flicker and a short outage, not a day without power, and we say so plainly. What matters just as much is what happens when the power returns: the recorder should restart on its own, find every camera and resume recording without anyone pressing a button. We test that during the handover by cutting power at the unit and watching the system come back. If a new outlet is needed at a higher spot, a licensed electrician installs it, and that is a line on the quote.

Openings that keep water out

Every hole a camera cable passes through in an outside wall is a place water could follow it. We drill the opening sloped slightly down toward the outside, leave a drip loop in the cable before it enters, seal the opening with an exterior-grade sealant around a grommet or sleeve, and keep junction boxes and connectors above any level that splashes or floods. On a raised bungalow, where cable has to climb from a camera under the house to a recorder on the living floor, it rides in conduit up a post or wall rather than lying loose where water and debris can pull at it.

A copy of what matters, somewhere else

A recorder on a high shelf is safe from water, but not from a burglar who finds it, and the CBS-reported spree showed people here going after cameras deliberately. For the clips that matter, we show you how to export them to your phone or a USB stick the same day, and where it suits the property we set up a cloud copy of selected cameras' alert clips, so a drowned, stolen or smashed recorder does not take the only copy with it. We name the cloud service on the quote and say plainly whether it carries a subscription; a recorder you own carries no monthly fee from us.

Curbs, Cars and Back Doors

The Windshield, the Car Keys and the Back Door: Building a Sheepshead Bay House Plan Around What Actually Happened

Most of the camera-related stories from Sheepshead Bay in 2026 start or end at a car. The September vandalism was a string of smashed windshields on parked cars, and the useful footage came from a camera on a house. The March break-in on East 9th Street ended with a car being driven away, and the Gravesend case two days later was a home entry to take car keys. A house plan here therefore starts at the curb and the driveway, not at the front door, and asks what a camera would need to see to turn a bad night into something a neighbor, a patrol or a detective can act on.

The answer is rarely "the whole block." A camera framed to take in every car on both sides of the street turns each person into a few pixels, and at night under streetlights it records shapes, not people. The camera that helps is one aimed along your own frontage, at your own car or driveway, low enough and close enough that a person standing at a car door or climbing onto a hood fills a useful part of the frame. If several houses on a block each do that, together they record the whole street in usable detail, which is very close to what happened in September: one resident's footage, plus photos a City Council member shared, pointed to the same person across many cars.

The curb camera

We mount the curb camera on the front of the house or the porch post, usually between eight and ten feet up, aimed so its frame runs from the front of your parking space to the back of it, with the sidewalk in the near edge of the picture. A varifocal lens lets us zoom so the car fills the frame instead of the street. We set a faster shutter at night so a person moving quickly does not smear across the picture, and we record continuously rather than only on motion, because a person approaching a car slowly may never trip a motion setting, and because the seconds before the damage are often the ones that show the face. Alerts are a separate setting: we draw the alert zone around the car itself, so passers-by on the sidewalk do not ring your phone all night.

The driveway and the keys

On a detached house with a side driveway, the driveway camera goes where it sees a face, not a roof: at the house end of the driveway looking toward the street, so anyone walking up the driveway is coming toward the lens. A second, wider view from the street end can show which way a car left. Inside the house, the East 9th Street and Gravesend cases are a reminder that keys hanging by the door are part of the risk; where an owner wants it, an indoor camera can watch the entry hall and key hook while the household is out, set to switch its recording off when the family is home so it never records daily life.

The back door and the yard

The Avenue X burglars left through the back door in the middle of the afternoon. That is a common pattern on houses with yards: the front faces the street and the neighbors, the back faces fences and garages, and the back door is where someone can work unseen. A camera on the rear wall, placed to look down the yard toward the back door and the gate rather than straight down from the eave, records faces at close range on the way in and on the way out. On semi-attached houses, where the side yard is the only path from the street to the back, a camera at the house corner covering that path does double duty. Each yard camera is framed to stop at your own fence, because Civil Rights Law §52-a gives a neighbor a claim against someone who records their backyard where they reasonably expect privacy, and because nobody wants to be the house that films the whole row of yards.

Detached and semi-attached houses: the basement or the attic, never the face of the house

Most houses here give us a choice of two clean routes. Where the basement has an open ceiling, cable for the curb, door, driveway and back-door cameras runs along the joists and comes up through small openings at each camera location, so nothing is stapled across the brick or siding. Where the basement is finished, or where it has taken water and the owner would rather keep the system out of it entirely, we come from the attic instead and drop cable down to the eaves and porch. On brick, the mounts go into mortar joints, which are easy to patch if a camera ever moves; on siding, they go through a mounting block into the sheathing and framing behind it, so the camera does not work loose or crack the siding. On semi-attached houses, where the shared wall is off limits, the side and rear cameras sit on the walls that belong to the house alone. The recorder comes up out of the basement into a closet on a living floor, on a battery unit, with the modem beside it if possible.

Bungalows: small walls, short runs, careful power

The bungalows near Emmons Avenue are often only a room or two wide, with low eaves and little attic. The runs are short, which helps, but there are fewer places to hide cable and fewer high spots to put a camera out of reach. We use compact cameras in hard housings on the corners of the eave where there is one, run cable inside small paintable covers along the trim where there is no attic, and keep every connection inside the house. On a court that sits below the street, the camera that matters most is the one that sees the walkway or steps down into the court, aimed up toward the approach so it catches faces against the brighter street rather than the tops of heads. On houses lifted onto pilings since Sandy, cameras for the open space below and the stair go on the structure, and the cable climbs in conduit to the living floor, where the recorder lives.

Postwar co-ops: risers, cellars and a board decision

The six-story co-ops that make up so much of the neighborhood were built with cellars that run under most of the building, central stairs and an elevator, and they usually have a live-in super or a doorman for part of the day. Cable routes are generally good: through cellar corridors and up existing risers or pipe chases to the floors, with every opening through a fire-rated wall or floor sealed with rated firestop. Field Notes

Field Notes: How We Walk a Sheepshead Bay House, Curb to Back Fence and Basement to Attic

This is how we work on these properties, not a story about one job. We start at the curb, standing where the car parks, because the first camera question here is usually about a car. From there we look back at the house to find the height and corner from which a camera would see a person at the car door large in the frame. We walk up the driveway or the path, stand at the front door and at the porch steps, and look for where a doorbell sees a face and where a second camera would catch someone leaving with a box. Then we go down the side of the house to the back door and out to the rear fence, and look back, which shows where a rear camera sees the whole yard and which neighbors' windows and yards it must stop short of. Inside, we go down to the basement and look at the walls for water lines before we look at anything else, then at the ceiling for the clearest cable route, any old wire we will need to trace, and where the internet line comes in. If the basement has taken water, we go up to the attic or to a closet on a living floor and decide where the recorder, switch and battery unit will live. By the end we know how many cameras the property needs, where each goes, how long every cable is and where the equipment stays dry, and that is what the written quote is built from.

Houses, curbs and yards

A neighbor's home camera helped point to the man who smashed windshields on more than 20 cars in September 2026. Should our camera watch our own car or try to cover the whole row along the block?

Your own car and your own frontage, framed tightly. On most Sheepshead Bay houses with a yard, yes. The front already faces the street, the neighbors and often a doorbell camera; the back faces fences and garages, and it is where someone can work at a door without being seen. There is. Most Sheepshead Bay houses are well served by a handful of cameras, each with one clearly named job: curb, front door, porch, driveway, side path, back door, yard, garage. Both together, because they see different moments. Brooklyn Paper reported daylight porch thefts in Sheepshead Bay and the surrounding area, with Bedford Avenue named, so both cameras should record continuously in daylight and alert on a person at the door. Telling couriers where to leave boxes, out of view of the sidewalk, helps as much as either camera.

Permission, Privacy and Licenses

Owner, Board, Tenant or Neighbor: The Rules Behind a Sheepshead Bay Camera Job

Sheepshead Bay has only one city landmark, so the rules that shape most camera jobs here are not about facades. They are about who owns which part of a building, who is allowed to record where, and who may do the installing. Here is how each plays out on the houses, co-ops, rentals and shops in the neighborhood.

Who may install. New York's General Business Law Article 6-D, as Kirschenbaum & Kirschenbaum summarize it, requires a security and fire alarm installer license for the installation, maintenance or servicing of a CCTV system used, full-time or part-time, for detecting or monitoring intrusion. Home and business owners putting a system on their own property are not in the business of installing and are not required to be licensed, but a contractor or handyman doing it for pay is. AESS holds NYS license #12000287431 and sends a copy to owners, boards and landlords who ask.

Co-op hallways and lobbies. As Connextivity puts it, in a co-op the hallway is a common area under the board's control, so a shareholder cannot install hardware there on their own. An attorney answering on Justia notes that landlords and co-op boards in New York generally have the right to install cameras in common areas such as hallways, lobbies, stairwells and entrances. With 41 co-op buildings in one local directory, that means most Sheepshead Bay building jobs start with a board vote and a written policy on who may view and export.

Renters. Brick Underground notes that a renter needs the landlord's permission to install any equipment in the common areas of a building, including the hallway outside their own apartment. In a two-family house, the owner decides about the outside, the yard and any shared hall; in a rental building, the landlord does. Inside their own apartment, a renter can generally place a camera aimed at their own door and rooms.

Neighbors. Civil Rights Law §52-a lets a resident sue a neighbor who records them in their backyard where they reasonably expect privacy. On blocks of semi-attached houses with yards side by side, and in bungalow courts where cottages face each other across a walkway, every outdoor camera we install is framed on your own curb, door, path, yard and fence, and anything else that falls in the frame is masked out in the camera settings.

City property. The city's DOT, in a case CBS New York reported, said it does not allow private equipment on city property. Cameras go on your building, your porch or your fence, never on a lamppost, street tree or sign pole.

Public housing. The Sheepshead Bay and Nostrand Houses are NYCHA developments, and their cameras belong to NYCHA. New York YIMBY reported that financing closed in October 2026 for a $607 million modernization of the Nostrand Houses at 2241 Batchelder Street that includes new access control, expanded CCTV and an on-site security guard. A NYCHA tenant's own options are inside their apartment, and anything on a door or in a hallway is a question for NYCHA management.

Walls and openings. Every opening through brick, block or siding is sloped outward and sealed against water. Every opening through a fire-rated wall or floor in a co-op or apartment building is sealed with rated firestop before we leave.
