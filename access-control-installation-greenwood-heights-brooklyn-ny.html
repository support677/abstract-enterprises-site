The Neighborhood

Why Greenwood Heights Properties Need Access Control

Greenwood Heights is a neighborhood of small doors. Wikipedia describes a district of one- and two-family homes on the slope west of Green-Wood Cemetery, roughly between the Prospect Expressway to the north, the Gowanus Canal and the bay to the west, Eighth Avenue to the east and the cemetery's southern edge at 39th Street, all in ZIP code 11232 and Brooklyn Community District 7. There are no towers with a security desk here and very few lobbies with a doorman. What there is, block after block, is a front door shared by two or three households, a garden door under a stoop, a store door with apartments above it, and, west of Fourth Avenue, a steel door beside a roll-up or drive-in garage. Each of those doors is opened every day by more people than the owner can easily keep track of, and that, more than any crime figure, is why owners here call us.

Start with the houses. The city's planners described the rowhouses of the area as two to four stories, built in the late 1800s and early 1900s as worker housing, and Brownstoner's architecture writers have long noted that Greenwood Heights is "better known for its wood framed houses, both restored and covered with aluminum siding," with brick and brownstone rows mixed in. Many of those houses have been in the same families for decades, and Wikipedia mentions older Polish and Italian households alongside newer arrivals. A house that has rented its upper floor or its garden apartment for thirty or forty years has also cut a key for every tenant in that time. Nobody knows how many are still in drawers. Changing the cylinder helps until the next tenant moves in; giving each person a code or fob that can be turned off on the day they leave solves the problem for good.

Then the newer buildings. The South Park Slope rezoning approved by the City Council on November 16, 2005, mapped R8A along Fourth Avenue with inclusionary housing and kept the midblocks low, and the buildings that followed are a mixed set. Compass lists 639 Fourth Avenue as an 11-story condo of 68 apartments, built in 2005, with a garage and a part-time doorman. CityRealty lists Greenwood on 4th, at 683 Fourth Avenue, as a nine-story building from 2008 with "a package room for convenient deliveries," a bike room and no doorman; DNAinfo reported in 2014 that it held 16 rentals with balconies over the cemetery. On the side streets the new buildings are smaller still: StreetEasy lists the 218 23rd Street Condominium, built in 2026, as four stories and four units with a doorman described only as "Virtual." In buildings like these, the front door, the package room, the garage and the storage cages are run by a small board or a single owner, often with no staff on site at all.

West of Fourth Avenue the doors get bigger. The 2005 rezoning described the Fourth Avenue edge as large lots with one-story warehouse and commercial buildings, and the commercial listings for the blocks between Third and Fourth show what is still there: a 1920 two-story building at 154 26th Street with "two oversized drive-in garages with approximately 13-foot clear heights," zoned M1-2D for manufacturing, contractor, storage and showroom uses, and a 10,000-square-foot brick multi-tenant warehouse at 149 29th Street "between 3rd and 4th Avenue," with drive-in and grade-level doors, four blocks from the 25th Street station. New York YIMBY reported in September 2026 that permits were filed for a three-story building at 156 21st Street with manufacturing space and four enclosed parking spaces. Doors like these are opened by crews who start early, by drivers who return late and by several businesses that share one building, and they are almost never on the same system as anything else.

The cemetery shapes the timetable. Green-Wood's published hours for November 2026 through mid-March 2027 open the main entrance at Fifth Avenue and 25th Street "8am–5pm to vehicles and pedestrians," and the Sunset Park entrance at Fourth Avenue and 35th Street to pedestrians on the same hours, with a note that gates lock on the hour. On the Fifth Avenue blocks that face the cemetery fence, the street has buildings on one side only, and in winter it empties early. Shops, offices and community rooms along that edge, and the houses on the side streets that end at the cemetery, are good candidates for door schedules that tighten in the evening rather than doors that are simply locked or unlocked all day.

The precinct numbers argue for steady attention rather than alarm. The NYPD's CompStat report for the 72nd Precinct, which polices Greenwood Heights from its station house at 830 Fourth Avenue along with Sunset Park and other blocks nearby, showed 89 burglaries in 2026 through November 8, against 90 in the same period of 2025, and grand larceny at 303 against 308. Those figures cover a much larger area than Greenwood Heights, and they do not say how anyone got in. We cite them only to make the point that the risk here is ordinary and constant. For most owners the reason to install access control is not a crime wave. It is knowing, on any given night, exactly who can open the door.

What the materials mean for the install

Wood-frame houses are the easiest buildings in Brooklyn to wire and the easiest to get wrong. Behind the siding or the clapboard there is usually a hollow stud wall, so a cable can drop from the door frame to the cellar without opening a finished room, and a strike can be mortised into a solid wood jamb. The risk is the jamb itself. A frame that has carried a door for more than a century has often been shimmed, patched and re-bored, and the screws for a new strike need sound wood to bite into, so on the visit we probe the jamb and sometimes splice in a new section of hardwood before any electric hardware goes in. Where siding covers the outside, nothing is fixed through it that would let water in behind; readers and stations go on the door casing or on a mounting block that is sealed and flashed.

Brick and brownstone rows are the opposite case: the walls are solid, so the route matters more than the wall. Cable usually comes up from the cellar inside the stoop or through the vestibule floor rather than through the masonry, and devices are fixed in mortar joints so that a later change leaves little to repair. In the newer buildings on Fourth Avenue and the side streets the challenge is aluminum and glass. Storefront-style doors have narrow, hollow stiles that will not take a strike in the edge, so the lock usually goes into the frame header, the panic device or a concealed electrified lock. In the industrial buildings below Fourth the frames are steel, often grouted solid with concrete, and the door may be dented from years of hand trucks; strikes are cut into the frame with the right tools, and a door that no longer sits square in its frame is fixed or replaced before it is electrified.

One Front Door, Two Households and Forty Years of Keys

The commonest Greenwood Heights call goes like this. A family has owned a frame house on one of the side streets for a long time. The owner, or a parent, lives on one floor; a tenant lives on the other; sometimes a garden apartment is rented too. The street door has had the same lock, or a lock rekeyed now and then, through every tenancy. Each new tenant was handed a key, and so was the occasional contractor, the cousin who checked on the house over a winter, the home attendant, the dog walker and the plumber who needed to get in on a Saturday. Some of those keys came back. Most were never asked for. The owner cannot say who can open the house tonight, and the tenant cannot either.

Changing the cylinder resets the count to zero, and then it starts climbing again with the next set of copies. Access control changes the arithmetic. Each person gets their own credential: a code for the parent who would rather not carry anything, a fob for the tenant and each adult in the tenant's household, a phone key for the owner's adult children who visit, a code that works only on Tuesday mornings for the cleaner and only until the end of the month for a contractor. When a tenant moves out, their credentials are switched off on the last day of the lease. When a fob is lost, it is cancelled from a phone in a minute, and the house is as secure as it was the day before. The key cylinder stays on the door as a backup, but only the owners hold its keys.

Two-family houses have one advantage over larger buildings: the city's smart-access rules are written for multiple dwellings. HPD's summary of the Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, applies to owners of multiple dwellings that use smart access, which in New York means buildings with three or more households. In a two-family house the owner is not bound by its paperwork, but its principles are still good practice: tell the tenant what the system records, keep only what you need, delete old entry records, and never make a phone app the only way into anyone's home. In a three-family house the law does apply, and we hand the owner a written privacy and retention policy with the system.

How we set up a house with a tenant

The owner holds the administrator account, and the tenant gets a household account that can add and remove the tenant's own people, inside limits the owner sets: for example, up to four fobs and two temporary codes, none valid for more than thirty days without the owner's approval. The street door's entry log is visible to the owner, and the tenant can see only their own household's entries. Where the tenant's apartment has its own entrance door inside the house, that door keeps an ordinary mechanical lock unless the tenant asks for something else, because an owner should not be able to open a tenant's apartment with an app. If the garden apartment has its own door to the street, it goes on the same controller with a separate door list, so the garden tenant's code opens only the garden door and the owner keeps a way in for a burst pipe through a code held in reserve.

Many long-time owners are older, and many of them do not use a smartphone. That is not a problem. A code on a keypad with large, lit buttons, or a fob on the key ring they already carry, works without an app, and the station's call button can ring a landline or an indoor screen. The family member who manages the account can do it from their own phone across town, so the parent never has to touch the software at all.

Small Condos and the “Virtual Doorman” Line in the Listing

Real estate listings for Greenwood Heights have been using the phrase for years. Brick Underground's 2018 look at a new condo at 236 17th Street "in Greenwood" listed a "virtual doorman" among its features, and StreetEasy describes the doorman at the four-unit 218 23rd Street Condominium, finished in 2026, simply as "Virtual." The phrase covers several quite different things. Sometimes it means a remote attendant service that answers the door station by video and releases the door for couriers and guests, for a monthly fee. Sometimes it means a cloud-connected panel that calls residents' phones. Sometimes it means little more than an intercom with an app. Buyers and new boards often discover which one they have only when the first bill arrives or the first resident moves.

For a board of four, eight or sixteen owners, the right question is which parts of the door should belong to the building and which should be a service. Our advice is that the hardware, the controller and the list of credentials should belong to the building, sit in the building and keep working without anyone's cloud, and that a remote attendant should be an optional layer on top. A small building with a package room, like the one CityRealty describes at 683 Fourth Avenue, can let couriers into the package room and nowhere else with a time-limited code or a video call answered by any resident on their phone. Whether a paid remote attendant is worth it then becomes a choice the board can make on its merits, not something the door depends on.

The same thinking applies to the larger Fourth Avenue buildings. A condo with a part-time doorman, like the 68-unit building Compass lists at 639 Fourth Avenue, needs a lobby that works in two modes: when the desk is staffed, the doorman releases the door for visitors and sees every entry; when the desk is closed, the door opens only for residents' credentials and visitors reach residents through the station. Behind the lobby, the garage, the package room, the bike room and the storage cages each need their own rules. Who should open the storage room? Every owner with a cage, but not the dog walker. Who should open the garage? Owners with a parking space, at any hour, and a vendor who services the gate, only by appointment. Those rules take an afternoon to write down and five minutes to change later, provided the board owns the system.

What a new board should ask for at handover

  • Its own administrator account, held by the board or managing agent, not by the developer's sales office or the original installer.
  • A list of every enrolled credential, so codes and fobs issued during construction and sales can be cancelled.
  • The model numbers of the readers and controller, and whether they work with credentials from more than one supplier.
  • Any service contract for a remote attendant or cloud platform, with its term, its notice period and what happens to the doors if it ends.
  • The door schedule and the fire-alarm release wiring, so the board knows which doors unlock on their own and which release when the alarm sounds.

Drive-In Doors, Roll-Ups and the Small Steel Door Beside Them

The side streets between Third and Fourth Avenues still hold the kind of building the 2005 rezoning described: one- and two-story brick and masonry structures with an overhead door for vehicles and a steel door for people. The Crexi listing for 154 26th Street, built in 1920, advertises "two oversized drive-in garages with approximately 13-foot clear heights" and "Multiple Points of Access for Efficient Operations"; the CommercialCafe listing for 149 29th Street describes a 10,000-square-foot multi-tenant brick building with drive-in and grade-level doors. Contractors, fabricators, storage and distribution businesses, set builders and small manufacturers use buildings like these, and their doors are opened by crews and drivers at hours when nobody from the office is around.

The door that matters most is usually the small one. A roll-up or sectional overhead door is heavy, motorized or chained, and in most buildings it is only opened from inside; the steel personnel door beside it is the one people use dozens of times a day, the one a key gets copied for, and the one that is left on the latch while a van is loaded. That door gets the reader first. A fob or code per person, a schedule that lets the first crew in at the hour they start, an alert when the door is held open for more than a few minutes, and a log that shows who came in at 5:40 a.m. on a Saturday are what the owner is really buying.

The overhead door can join the system too, with care. A modern commercial door operator usually has an input for an external opener, so a reader or keypad can tell it to open, and many owners want drivers to be able to open it from the cab with a phone. What a reader must never do is take away the operator's safety devices: the photo eyes or edge sensors that stop and reverse a closing door when something is in the way. If an operator has no working safety devices, it needs them before anything is connected to it. We also set the system so that a credential can open the door but closing is left to the operator's own controls or a timer the operator supports, and we label which crews may raise it at which hours. Hand-chained doors and old operators without an input stay manual, and the personnel door does the work.

Buildings with several businesses

A multi-tenant building on a side street often has one shared personnel door, one shared loading door and several units inside, each rented to a different business. The landlord controls the shared doors; each tenant controls its own unit. One controller can hold all of it in separate partitions: each tenant administers its own people and its own unit door, the shared door opens for anyone with a valid credential from any tenant during that tenant's hours, and the landlord sees shared-door events and nothing else. When a tenant leaves, its whole partition is switched off in one step, and the next tenant starts with a clean list. Industry City, a few blocks south between Second and Third Avenues, describes the same idea at a much larger scale in its visitor FAQ: building hours, "tenant key card access after hours," and a security team on patrol. A two-tenant building on 29th Street does not need the patrol, but it benefits from the same structure.

Fail-safe or fail-secure on a workshop door

Every electric lock has to be one or the other. As Kintronics puts it, a fail-secure lock means "the door stays locked if there is a power failure," while a fail-safe lock means "the door is unlocked if there is a power failure." A workshop's personnel door is almost always fail-secure, because a door that unlocks in a blackout leaves the tools and stock open to the street; the inside lever still opens it by hand, so nobody is ever trapped. Where a door is fire-rated, for example between a workshop and a stair, Kintronics also notes that "electric strikes on fire doors must be fail-secure, so the door is positively-latched if there is a fire." Magnetic locks, by contrast, are fail-safe by nature, and the NYC Building Code sets strict conditions for them on exit doors. We choose the lock per door and write the reason on the plan.

When Green-Wood Locks Its Gates: Door Schedules That Follow the Block

Few Brooklyn neighborhoods run on a clock as visible as this one. Green-Wood publishes its gate hours by season, and from November 1, 2026 to March 14, 2027 the main entrance at Fifth Avenue and 25th Street is open "8am–5pm to vehicles and pedestrians," while the Sunset Park entrance at Fourth Avenue and 35th Street opens to pedestrians on the same hours and to vehicles until 4 p.m. The cemetery asks visitors to leave at least ten minutes early because the gates lock on the hour. In April 2026 the city and the cemetery opened The Green-House at Green-Wood, the restored 1895 Weir Greenhouse and a new visitor building across from the main arch, open Thursday through Monday, which has given the Fifth Avenue corner more daytime visitors. After the gates close, the cemetery side of Fifth Avenue is a long iron fence with no doors on it.

For a business on that edge, a fixed weekly schedule is usually the wrong tool. A café, an office, a shop or a community room across from the fence has a busy daytime street and a quiet evening one, and the change comes an hour or more earlier in winter than in summer. A modern controller can hold more than one schedule for the same door and switch between them by date, so the customer door can stay unlocked through the daytime hours of the busy season, switch to buzz-in at an earlier hour from November to March, and lock to staff credentials only after closing. Holidays and special events go in as dated exceptions on top of the regular schedule. The point is not that the evening is dangerous; it is that a door set for July is wrong for January, and nobody remembers to change it by hand.

Houses on the side streets that end at the cemetery have a simpler version of the same need. A street door that is fine on the latch during a busy Saturday afternoon is better locked by the time the block goes quiet. Many owners set the street door to relock automatically a few seconds after every opening at all hours, and use schedules only for the people who need them: a contractor whose code works on weekdays from 8 a.m. to 5 p.m., a tenant's child whose code works from the end of the school day, a cleaner on one morning a week.

Daylight saving time and the door

A schedule is only as good as the controller's clock. Systems managed through the cloud set their time from the internet and change for daylight saving time on their own; standalone keypads and older controllers often do not, and a shop door can start unlocking an hour early or late every spring and fall until someone notices. We set the time zone and the clock source on every controller we install, and on systems we take over we check them on the first visit.

The Rules Behind a Greenwood Heights Door

An entry system in a New York City building touches several sets of rules at once, and which ones apply depends mainly on how many households live in the building and whether the door is an exit. None of them make access control difficult to install. All of them shape how it should be set up.

Every home still needs a key

amNY's 2019 report on keyless entry, written as tenants in several city buildings objected to app-only and face-recognition systems, noted that the city's Department of Buildings requires a traditional key-based way in, and that fobs, cards and metal keys all count as keys under its protocols. The report also recorded tenants' worries about privacy, about losing access when a phone or a company fails, and about Sabbath observance for residents who do not use electricity on Saturdays. The NYCLU's policy director told the paper, "It's fundamentally inappropriate for anyone to be told that a condition of living somewhere is giving up their data." Every system we install keeps a mechanical key at each controlled door and at least one non-phone credential for every resident.

Three or more households: the Tenant Data Privacy Law

HPD's summary of Local Law 63 of 2021 says owners of multiple dwellings who use smart access, which includes fobs, key cards, apps and biometrics, must get tenants' consent, give them a written privacy and data-retention policy, collect only the minimum data, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, never sell it, and may not require smart access for a tenant to enter their own apartment. A three-family house in Greenwood Heights is a multiple dwelling for this purpose; a two-family house is not. We set retention to the shortest period the owner is comfortable with, and we provide the policy text for the owner to hand out.

Rent-stabilized apartments

Few Greenwood Heights houses are rent-stabilized, but some of the older walk-ups on the avenues are. Brick Underground reported in 2018 that state housing regulators had ruled that fobs must be provided free to all lawful occupants, with replacement fees for lost fobs capped, and that owners could not use a fob rollout to collect new identification. amNY noted the following year that changes like these in rent-regulated buildings need the state agency's approval. In a stabilized building we plan the rollout around those rules before the first fob is ordered.

Street doors, intercoms and exits

Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a requires self-closing, self-locking entrance doors in class A multiple dwellings built or converted after 1968, and an intercom with voice and a remote door release in buildings of eight or more apartments; older buildings must install them when a majority of tenants ask in writing. Roof doors must be self-closing but must not be self-locking. On the exit side, the NYC Building Code allows electrically locked egress doors only on conditions: a sensor on the inside, unlocking when power fails and when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate, and a manual PUSH TO EXIT button near the door that unlocks it for at least 30 seconds. A magnetic lock needs release hardware in the door itself that works with one hand. A wood house door whose inside knob or lever always opens it mechanically needs none of that, which is one more reason fail-secure strikes are the usual choice on Greenwood Heights houses.

Who may do the work

The city's electrical code defines low-voltage work as signaling, alarm, communication and data wiring under 50 volts, and its permit requirements do not apply to that work; the 120-volt outlet a power supply plugs into is ordinary electrical work for a licensed electrician. New York State licenses the installation of security and fire alarm systems through the Department of State. AESS does the low-voltage side under New York State license #12000287431, and where an outlet is needed we say so on the plan so your electrician can add it before installation day.

Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Greenwood Heights: Questions Answered

Houses on the side streets

We are having a new wood front door milled to match the original on our frame house. What should the carpenter do now so it can take an electric lock later?

Ask for three things while the door is still on the bench. First, a solid hardwood lock stile, at least where the lockset and the strike side meet, with no finger joints or glued-up softwood in the area where hardware will be mortised. Second, a lockset prep that suits an electrified mortise lock or a standard latch that works with an electric strike; tell the carpenter which, or send us a photo of the frame and we will say which fits. Third, if there is any chance the lock will be in the door rather than the frame, a channel drilled from the hinge edge to the lock pocket before the panels are glued up, so a cable can pass through later without boring a finished door.

The frame matters as much as the door. If the jamb is being replaced or rebuilt at the same time, have it made from solid stock on the strike side, deep enough for a strike body, and leave a pull string from the strike location down through the wall into the cellar. That one string saves most of the labor on installation day. It costs a carpenter minutes during the restoration and costs far more to recreate once the siding or clapboard is back on.

Our frame house still runs on an old fuse box in the cellar. Can the power supply for a door lock be connected to it?

Usually, yes, because an access system draws very little power: a controller, a reader and a strike together typically need less than a single light bulb. The power supply plugs into an ordinary outlet near the controller, and it carries a backup battery, so the door keeps working for hours if a fuse blows or the block loses power. What it needs is a dedicated outlet that will not be switched off with the cellar lights or overloaded by a dehumidifier.

Adding or moving an outlet on an old fuse panel is work for a licensed electrician, not for us, and an old panel is a good reason to have an electrician look at the house anyway. We mark on the plan exactly where the outlet should go and what it will feed, so your electrician can price and add it before we arrive. If the house is being rewired as part of a restoration, ask the electrician for that outlet at the same time.

Burglary numbers in the 72nd Precinct are about the same this year as last. Is that a reason to wait?

It is not a reason either way. The 72nd Precinct's CompStat report for the week ending November 8, 2026 showed 89 burglaries so far in 2026 against 90 in the same period of 2025, across a precinct much larger than Greenwood Heights. A flat number means the risk is steady, not that it has gone away, and it says nothing about your door. The better test is a question you can answer yourself: can you say tonight exactly who has a key to your street door?

If the answer is no, because tenants, contractors or relatives have held keys over the years, access control solves a problem you already have. If the answer is yes and nothing about the household is changing, it can reasonably wait. Owners often time it to a moment when they would be changing locks anyway: a new tenant, a renovation, the death or move of a parent, or the replacement of the front door.

Fourth Avenue and the newer condos

Our four-unit condo was sold with a monthly virtual doorman service. Do four owners really need it, or can we run the front door ourselves?

Four owners can run their own door very comfortably. A video station at the street door that calls each owner's phone and indoor screen, fobs and codes for each household, a time-limited courier code for the package area and a door that relocks itself cover what most small buildings need. Remote attendant services earn their fee mainly where many deliveries arrive while nobody is home, or where owners do not want to answer the door from work. In a four-unit building, that is a choice, not a requirement.

Before cancelling anything, find out what the service actually controls. If the door station and the lock are tied to the provider's cloud, cancelling may switch the door off or leave it on a basic mode, and the building may have to replace the station. If the station is a standard device and only the attendant layer is a subscription, the building can keep the hardware and drop the service at the end of its term. We survey the equipment, read the contract with the board and give a written option for both paths.

Our Fourth Avenue building's school, clinic or program on the ground floor wants a button that locks every outside door at once. Can that be done without trapping anyone?

Yes, and it is one of the simplest things an access system does. A lockdown command, from a button at the front desk, a wall switch in an office or an app on a staff phone, tells every controlled exterior door to stop accepting ordinary credentials and stop following its unlock schedule. Doors that were open on a daytime schedule lock immediately. Staff credentials can still be allowed, or not, depending on the plan.

What lockdown never does is stop anyone leaving. Every door still opens from inside by its lever, panic bar or exit button, and doors that must release on fire alarm still do. The command is reversed from the same places, and every activation is logged. For a school or program sharing a building with apartments, the lockdown covers only the program's doors, so residents' entry is not affected.

Workshops and warehouses between Third and Fourth

If our drive-in door goes up with a fob, what stops it from coming down on a van or a person?

The door operator's own safety devices, which must stay in charge. A commercial operator closes only when its photo eyes across the opening, its pressure edge along the bottom, or both, show the way is clear, and it reverses if something breaks the beam while it is moving. A reader or keypad connected to the operator only sends an open command, the same as the wall button; it does not override those devices, and we never wire around them. If an operator's safety devices are missing or disconnected, they are restored by the door company before we connect anything.

Closing is best left to the operator: either the close button inside, or a timer-to-close function if the operator has one and the door company has set it up with working safety devices. We can add a held-open alert, so that if the overhead door is still up thirty minutes after the last crew left, the owner hears about it on their phone. Who may open it, and at what hours, is set per crew in the access system.

Our only door for people is a small door cut into the big overhead door. Can that take a lock and a reader?

Sometimes, and it takes more care than an ordinary door. A pass door, or wicket door, in a sectional or rolling door moves with the big door, so any cable feeding a lock on it has to survive the big door opening and closing; that usually means a coiled cord or a door-loop at the hinge and a design in which the electric lock is on the small door's own frame. Many manufacturers also fit an interlock so the big door cannot open while the pass door is open. We work with the door's manufacturer or the door company on what may be mounted on it.

Often the better answer is a separate personnel door. If the building wall has room, a steel door in its own frame beside the overhead door is easier to secure, easier to wire and safer as an exit. Where that is not possible, a keypad lock that needs no cable on the pass door itself, combined with a reader on the overhead door's operator, is a practical compromise. We price both before recommending one.

Three businesses share the loading door in our 29th Street building. Who should be able to open it, and how do we know it was closed again?

Each business should open it with its own people's credentials, during its own hours, and the landlord should control the overall rules. One controller can hold a partition for each tenant: every tenant manages its own staff, the loading door accepts any valid credential from any tenant during that tenant's hours, and the record shows which company's person opened it. Nobody shares a code with the business next door.

A door position contact answers the second half of the question. It tells the system whether the door is actually shut, so a door that has been held open longer than a set time sends an alert, and the alert goes to whichever tenant opened it last as well as to the landlord. Disputes about who left the building open over a weekend usually end once the log shows it.

Our shop's back door is also the second way out. Is a lock that unlocks in a power cut harder to fit, or more expensive, than one that stays locked?

Neither is generally harder or dearer to fit as hardware; the difference is in what the door needs around it. A fail-secure electric strike stays locked when power is lost, and it is the usual choice for a back door because the inside lever or panic bar always lets people out mechanically, power or no power. That keeps the exit legal without unlocking the shop in a blackout. A fail-safe lock, such as a magnetic lock, unlocks when power is lost, and on an exit door the Building Code then requires release hardware in the door, an inside sensor and a push-to-exit button, which add parts and labor.

So on most Greenwood Heights shop and workshop back doors, fail-secure is both the simpler and the cheaper path. The exception is a door whose hardware cannot be changed and has no mechanical way out other than a key, which has to be fixed first: an exit door must always open from inside without a key.

Fifth Avenue and the cemetery side

Yes, and a controller can do it without anyone remembering. The schedule changes on its own each season, the system's clock moves for daylight saving time, and you can override it from your phone on any day that turns out different.

Practical ones, yes. If three or four owners on one block are ready at the same time, we can schedule the site visits on the same morning and the installations on consecutive days, which makes it easier for everyone to plan around a door being worked on. Each house is still its own job, with its own written price from the Brooklyn list, its own controller and its own accounts; nobody's door depends on a neighbor's system.

That is easy to set up, house by house, with each owner's permission, and it is a better use of the system than sharing codes. Field Notes

Field Notes: Ask Who Locks Up Last

This describes how we approach a Greenwood Heights job, not a particular customer. Before we look at readers, we ask one question: on a normal night, who is the last person through this door, and what do they do when they leave? In a two-family house the answer is often "whoever gets home last, and sometimes nobody locks the deadbolt." In a workshop it is often "the last driver, who pulls the overhead door down and goes out the side door, which locks itself, we think." Then we watch the door do it. We close it from inside the way that person would and see whether it latches; we pull the overhead door down and see whether the personnel door really locked behind us.

That two-minute test decides more of the plan than any product sheet. A door that does not latch on its own gets fixed first, because a reader on an unlatched door only records who walked through an open building. A routine that depends on one careful person gets a schedule and an alert instead. The best access system in Greenwood Heights is the one that makes the last person's job automatic.

FAQ

Greenwood Heights Access Control FAQ

When the clocks change in March and November, do the door schedules move with them?
+

On the systems we install, yes. Controllers managed through the cloud take their time from the internet and change for daylight saving time on their own, and we set the time zone on every controller before handover. Standalone keypads and some older controllers keep their own clocks and may not change, so a door can start unlocking an hour early or late. On systems we take over, we check the clock and the time source on the first visit.

Our upstairs tenant may be handing her code out to friends. Can the system show that, and what can we do about it?
+

The log shows when her code was used, so a code used at hours or on days that do not match the household's routine is easy to spot. A code cannot prove who typed it, though, so the practical fix is to give each adult in her household a personal code or fob and to offer her temporary codes for guests, which she can create inside limits you set and which expire on their own. Shared codes then become unnecessary, and any one credential can be cancelled without affecting the others.

Can a keypad or reader go on the iron railing at the top of our stoop instead of on the house?
+

Sometimes. A railing post can hold a small reader or keypad on a clamp or a welded plate, which keeps drilling off the house, but the cable then has to reach the door from the railing, usually down the post and under the stoop into the cellar, and that route has to be protected from feet, water and snow shovels. On most side-street houses the door casing is the simpler, sturdier place. We look at both on the visit and price whichever route is cleaner.

Is our old wood front door thick enough to take an electrified lock?
+

Most original house doors are, but the lock may not be the best choice anyway. An electrified mortise lock needs a deep, solid pocket in the lock stile, and doors that have been bored for several locks over a century often do not have enough wood left. In those cases we leave the door alone and put a fail-secure electric strike in the jamb instead, which needs nothing from the door except a latch that works. We check the stile and the jamb on the visit before recommending either.

Can the system tell us when someone used the mechanical key instead of a code or fob?
+

Indirectly, yes. With a door position contact fitted, the controller knows when the door opened, and it knows whether a valid credential, an exit sensor or a release button opened it. An opening with none of those is recorded as a forced door, and in practice that is usually the key. Owners who want to know every time the backup key is used can have that event sent to their phone, which also shows whether a key you thought was gone is still in use.

Will a reader on a door that gets the full afternoon sun overheat in summer?
+

A reader or station rated for outdoor use is built for it, but a dark housing in hours of direct summer sun does get hot, and the screen of a touchscreen panel can be hard to read in glare. We check which way the door faces on the visit, choose a device rated for the temperature range, and where we can we mount it in the shade of the door reveal or a small hood rather than on the brightest part of the wall.

How big is the controller box, and where does it go in a house with no spare closet?
+

For one or two doors the controller and its power supply with backup battery usually fit in a metal cabinet about the size of a small medicine cabinet. In most Greenwood Heights houses it goes on a dry cellar wall near the stair, off the floor and on the owner's side if the cellar is shared with a tenant. It needs an outlet nearby, and it should not sit next to the boiler or a dehumidifier's drain.

Can only our crew leads open the tool room while everyone else opens the shop door?
+

Yes. Each door has its own list of people and hours, so the tool room's reader can accept only crew leads' credentials while the personnel door accepts the whole crew during working hours. Every opening of the tool room is logged by name. If a lead is off sick, you can give someone else access for one day from your phone and it ends on its own.

Can the street door unlock by itself as we walk up with our hands full?
+

Some readers support phone keys that work from a pocket at short range, so the door releases as you reach it without taking the phone out. We set the range short and require the door to be approached, so it does not unlock while you are still on the sidewalk or upstairs. For people who prefer not to rely on a phone, a fob on the key ring tapped against the reader is almost as quick.

For a condo, the name of the board member or managing agent who will hold the administrator account. For a workshop, the make of the overhead door operator if you know it.
