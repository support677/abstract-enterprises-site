Ocean Parkway is not an ordinary street with houses on it. It is a 210-foot-wide corridor, designated a city scenic landmark in 1975, with a main roadway in the middle, a planted mall and path on each side, a one-way service road beyond each mall, and then a sidewalk. Behind the sidewalk, every lot that fronts the parkway has to keep a 30-foot front yard, open and unbuilt. So a front door here sits a long way from anyone who might notice it: across the yard, across the sidewalk, across the service road and across the mall. This page is about designing an alarm for that distance, for the six-story apartment houses that line most of the parkway, and for the houses that stand behind the same thirty feet of lawn.

The Neighborhood

Why Ocean Parkway Properties Need Alarm Installation

Start with what the parkway is. The Landmarks Preservation Commission’s designation report of January 28, 1975, describes it as “the first road of its kind built in the United States,” worked out by Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux from an idea in their 1866 report on Prospect Park and named in their 1868 report, where they coined the word “parkway.” Construction began in 1874, reached Kings Highway in 1875, and the extension to Coney Island opened on November 11, 1876. The report gives the cross-section that still governs daily life on the parkway: 210 feet in all, made of a central roadway 70 feet wide, two malls 20 feet wide, two side roads 25 feet wide, and two sidewalks 15 feet wide. Wikipedia puts the full length at 5.5 miles, from the circle at the southwest corner of Prospect Park to the Brighton Beach end near the ocean.

Then look at what sits behind the sidewalk. The same report records that the Brooklyn Park Commissioners originally controlled an extra 30 feet on each side of the parkway, where no buildings were allowed and the space was to be “used for courtyards only.” That idea survives in today’s zoning. Section 113-12 of the New York City Zoning Resolution requires a 30-foot front yard on any zoning lot that fronts Ocean Parkway, and says that no obstructions, “including porches either open or enclosed, canopies or stairs,” are permitted within it. Balconies that may project into front yards elsewhere may not do so along the parkway, driveways must run straight in, and Section 113-21 requires accessory parking for new developments fronting the parkway to be completely enclosed. The result is a long, open, planted space in front of almost every building, which is lovely to look at and which puts every front door a good distance from the public.

Then the buildings. Wikipedia’s article describes the parkway as zoned mostly for mid- and high-rise apartment buildings, with co-ops and condos of six to seven stories in the Gravesend stretch, taller rental and co-op buildings near Kensington, and single- and two-family houses and some mansions in between, many of them expanded or combined; it adds that many apartment buildings became co-ops in the late twentieth century. Building records give the pattern a face. 1601 Ocean Parkway, between Avenue P and the blocks south of it, is a six-story rental of 59 apartments built in 1929. 1171 Ocean Parkway, between Avenue K and Avenue L, is a six-story co-op of 64 apartments built in 1939, with an elevator, an intercom and a live-in super, and no doorman listed. 2727 Ocean Parkway, near the southern end, is a six-story co-op of 174 apartments built in 1956, with a private courtyard and a live-in super. Six stories, an elevator, a super who lives in the building and no one at a front desk is the most common arrangement we see along the parkway.

Then what goes wrong. The Ocean Parkway items that police blotters have published over the years are short, but they line up. In September 2018, according to the 60th Precinct blotter in Brooklyn Paper, someone broke the lock on a bedroom window and got into an Ocean Parkway apartment near Brighton Beach Avenue at about 2 p.m. In November 2018 the 61st Precinct reported two people coming through an unlocked window into an Ocean Parkway apartment near Avenue P at about 9:20 at night, taking cash and headphones belonging to three people. In April 2021 the 60th Precinct reported a vacant Ocean Parkway apartment between Avenue Z and Murdock Court entered through an open kitchen window, with a diamond ring and a bracelet among losses of more than $18,000. In August 2011 a Midwood blotter described an Ocean Parkway apartment between Quentin Road and Kings Highway burgled during the working day, with no sign of how the thief got in. Windows of apartments, in daylight or early evening, are the thread.

What we will not claim is just as important. We found no published burglary rate for “Ocean Parkway” as a place, because the parkway is a road that crosses many neighborhoods and several police precincts, and the city does not report crime by road. The blotter items above are individual reports from different years and different stretches, not a trend. So this page does not tell you the parkway is dangerous. It explains how the 30-foot yard, the six-story apartment house and the service road change where an alarm should look, and it uses those police reports only as examples of openings that were actually used.

Six facts about the parkway that shape an alarm

  • 210 feet from building line to building line, before the yards. Roadway 70 feet, malls 20 feet each, side roads 25 feet each, sidewalks 15 feet each (LPC designation report, 1975).
  • A 30-foot front yard on every lot that fronts the parkway. No porches, canopies or stairs inside it; no balconies projecting over it; driveways perpendicular to the street (Zoning Resolution § 113-12).
  • Enclosed parking for new buildings. Accessory off-street parking for new developments fronting the parkway must be completely enclosed (Zoning Resolution § 113-21).
  • One-way service roads on both sides. A one-lane, one-way service road runs on each side of the main roadway, which carries traffic lights; commercial vehicles are barred from the parkway (Eastern roads survey; Wikipedia).
  • The landmark ends at the curb. The scenic landmark runs from Church Avenue to Seabreeze Avenue and is bounded by the outer curb lines of the two side roads, so the sidewalks, yards and buildings are outside it (LPC designation report).
  • The oldest bike path in the country, rebuilt recently. Wikipedia calls the western mall path the first bike path in the United States, dedicated in 1895; NYC Parks rebuilt the path between Avenue R and Avenue X, with construction starting in March 2023 and the project recorded as complete in November 2024.

Which stretch this page means. Ocean Parkway passes through Windsor Terrace, Kensington, Midwood, Homecrest, Gravesend and Sheepshead Bay before it reaches Brighton Beach and Coney Island. Those neighborhoods have their own pages, and their side streets have their own character. This page is for properties that face the parkway itself, on either service road, and for the first buildings in on the side streets, whose rear windows look onto the same courtyards. If you are several blocks in, our Kensington, Midwood, Gravesend or Homecrest pages may fit you better; the prices are identical.

Most parkway apartment owners who ask about an alarm also ask about seeing who is at the apartment door or in the lobby. A doorbell-style camera on the apartment door or an intercom upgrade for the building is handled on the same visit; our Ocean Parkway security camera installation and Ocean Parkway intercom installation pages cover those sides.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Ocean Parkway’s Architecture

The parkway was built in the 1870s, but most of what lines it went up over the following century under one rule that never changed: thirty feet of open ground in front. Five kinds of property cover nearly everything we are asked to protect.

The prewar six-story apartment house

Brick buildings of the late 1920s and 1930s, like 1601 Ocean Parkway (1929) and 1171 Ocean Parkway (1939): six floors, an elevator, a lobby behind the front yard, apartments on every side and a rear court. The ground and second floors carry the risk; the upper floors mostly need the door.

The postwar co-op

Larger postwar buildings, such as 2727 Ocean Parkway (1956, 174 apartments, private courtyard). More apartments per floor, longer corridors, a courtyard the residents use, and sometimes replacement windows installed building-wide by the co-op.

The house behind the lawn

Detached and semi-detached houses on parkway frontage, many enlarged over the years. Windows on several sides, a side drive running straight in from the service road, a garage, and a front door that is only visible to someone standing in the yard.

The newer condominium

Six- and seven-story condos, which Wikipedia notes in the Gravesend stretch, with the enclosed parking the zoning requires, a lobby with a camera, and larger windows. Unit owners usually want only their own door and windows on an alarm.

The side-street two-family

Brick and frame two-families on the blocks that cross the parkway, whose rear yards share the light and air of the big buildings’ courtyards. Not subject to the parkway front-yard rule, but part of the same view from behind.

The apartment house: start at the second floor and look down

In a six-story parkway building, the question for each apartment is simple: which of its windows can a person reach without a ladder? On the front, the ground-floor windows open onto the planted yard, and the second-floor windows sit above it. Zoning keeps canopies and stairs out of the front yard, so there is rarely a structure out there to climb, but the building’s entrance surround, a ground-floor bay or a low wall can bring a second-floor sill within reach. At the back, the court is where risk collects: ground-floor windows a few feet above the paving, a garbage enclosure or a shed roof under a second-floor window, a fire escape where one exists, and the roof of a one-story extension. Our technician stands in the court, looks up at your windows, and lists each one as reachable or not before he ever enters the apartment.

Inside, the plan is usually short. The apartment door gets a contact. Every reachable window gets a contact on the moving sash, and in rooms with several windows on the court side a glass-break detector covers all the glass at once. One motion detector goes in the foyer or hall where the bedrooms, the living room and the kitchen meet, so that anyone who gets in through any window has to cross it. The panel sits in a closet, the keypad by the door, and a small indoor siren goes where you will hear it from the bedrooms. Nothing goes on the facade, the window exteriors or the hallway, which keeps the co-op board or the landlord comfortable.

The house behind the lawn: the front is the quiet side

On most Brooklyn streets the front of a house is the watched side: neighbors across the road can see the door, and people walk past it within a few feet. On the parkway that reverses. A house on the service road has its front door thirty feet back, a sidewalk and a service road in front of the yard, and a mall and the main roadway beyond, so the nearest house facing yours is across the full width of the parkway. Front plantings, which the special district was written to encourage, screen the lower windows. The sides of the house, along the perpendicular drive and next to the neighbor, are often more overlooked than the front. So we do not assume the front is safe. Front windows behind shrubs get glass-break or contacts like any rear window, and the front door is treated as a door nobody may see you at.

Larger houses add their own list: a garage door and the door from the garage into the house, a side door off the drive, rear doors to a deck or yard, windows above a garage roof or an extension, and sometimes a separate entrance to a lower floor. Those are ordinary alarm problems, and the survey simply makes sure none of them is skipped because the house looks so open from the parkway.

The postwar co-op and the condo: what the board owns

In larger co-ops and condos, the windows, the facade and often the apartment entry door belong to the corporation or the condominium, not to you. That shapes the method more than the risk does. Contacts are fixed with adhesive or small screws on the interior face of the sash and frame, wireless so no wire is run through walls the board controls, and every part comes out cleanly if you sell. Where a co-op has installed new windows across the building, we fit contacts made for those frames rather than older surface types, and we keep the paperwork the board asks for: certificate of insurance, a description of what is mounted where, and confirmation that nothing touches building systems.

Parkway apartment owners who replace an alarm often add keyless entry at the apartment door at the same time; our Ocean Parkway access control page explains smart locks and fob entry for buildings.

Thirty Feet Back

Reading the Distance Between the Parkway and Your Door

Everything that makes the parkway pleasant also changes who can see what. By day the malls are busy: the LPC report says the benches, playing tables and bicycle path are “heavily used by nearby residents,” and for many people in Brooklyn the parkway is the only large green space close by. The service roads carry residents’ cars, delivery vans going to individual buildings and buses on parts of the parkway. At night the picture changes. The mall paths empty, the service road quiets, and the front yards, which are deep and planted, become the least watched ground in front of the buildings.

Diagram drawn by AESS from the dimensions in the 1975 LPC designation report and Zoning Resolution § 113-12. The landmark arrow is drawn to the outer curbs of the service roads; sidewalks and yards lie outside it. Real facades may sit further back than thirty feet.

Add the 30-foot yards on both sides to the 210-foot corridor and the facades facing each other are about 270 feet apart. A neighbor across the parkway cannot see your front door in any useful way. The person most likely to notice someone at your door is a neighbor in your own building or the house next door, and the device most likely to notice is the one you install.

That leads to four design rules we use on the parkway. First, protect the front as a hidden side, not a watched one. Second, put the earliest detection where it cannot see the public: on the door, on the windows, along your own walk, never toward the mall. Third, get the alert to a person quickly, because no passer-by will raise it for you; a siren alone, ringing behind a deep yard and a closed window, may be heard by nobody outside. Fourth, give the monitoring station or whoever is on your call list directions that work on a one-way service road, including which side of the parkway you are on and which cross avenue to turn in from.

The houses on the parkway have rules of their own. Part of the special district is a Subdistrict whose stated purpose, in Section 113-501 of the Zoning Resolution, is to encourage large single- or two-family detached and semi-detached homes, with multiple dwellings in appropriate locations. Its floor area ratio tops out at 1.50, or 2.00 on qualifying sites, and open parking is generally kept out of the front yard. In practice that produces big houses with deep yards, garages and long side drives, many of them rebuilt or enlarged. For an alarm, size is less important than shape: more doors at the side and back, more windows above garage and extension roofs, and a front that faces a planted yard rather than a sidewalk. We survey those houses side by side and back first, then the front, so the parts nobody overlooks are counted before the part everybody admires.

The precinct map adds one more reason to write down good directions. The parkway is a single road, but the police reports on its addresses come from different commands: the blotter items above came from the 60th Precinct at the Brighton Beach end, the 61st Precinct near Avenue P, and a 66th Precinct report in August 2025 about someone who entered an apartment building near Ocean Parkway and Avenue M at about 4:40 a.m. and pried open a resident’s mailbox. Which precinct answers your alarm depends on your address, not on the parkway. Monitoring stations dispatch through the city by address, and the clearer that address and its approach are on file, the less time is lost.

Commercial Alarms

Business Alarm Installation on Ocean Parkway

Ocean Parkway itself is almost entirely residential and institutional, and commercial vehicles are barred from it. Business alarms on the parkway are therefore of three kinds: professional offices on the ground floors of apartment buildings and in a few small office buildings, the large community buildings the parkway is known for, and the shops on the commercial avenues that cross it.

Ground-floor offices and practices

Professional offices in ground-floor suites of parkway buildings and in small office buildings. A News 12 report listed a commercial office at 3049 Ocean Parkway, near the Brighton Beach end, among four burglaries blamed on one man, with a laptop, electronics and about $3,000 in cash taken across the series.

Synagogues, schools and nursing homes

Wikipedia cites a 2009 estimate of more than 40 synagogues on and around the parkway, alongside yeshivas, schools and nursing homes; it names Mir Yeshiva at Avenue R and Abraham Lincoln High School at Shore Parkway. These buildings need intrusion alarms separate from their fire alarm systems.

Church Avenue and the Kensington end

At the northern end the parkway meets Church Avenue and the Prospect Expressway. Shops on the avenue near the parkway corner have the usual storefront needs: gate, glass, rear door and cellar.

Kings Highway and Avenue U

Two shopping streets that cross the parkway in its middle stretch. Stores within a block of the parkway corner share the corner’s foot traffic by day and its quiet by night.

Neptune Avenue and Brighton Beach Avenue

Near the southern end, close to the Ocean Parkway station on the Q, and to South Brooklyn Health near Avenue Z, which Wikipedia says has had a hospital on its site since 1909. Offices, pharmacies and small stores cluster here.

An office alarm differs from a store alarm in what it guards. A store’s risk is the gate, the glass and the register. An office’s risk is the record room or file cabinets, the computers, the medication or sample cabinet in a medical practice, and the after-hours cleaning or maintenance access that the building controls. We give an office its own panel and its own cellular communicator, independent of the building, with a contact on the suite door, motion inside, a contact or vibration sensor on the room or cabinet that matters most, a panic button at reception, and opening and closing reports so the practice owner knows who armed and disarmed and when.

Compliance notes for parkway businesses and buildings

  • Siren limits. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires an audible burglar alarm on a building to be able to stop, and to stop automatically, within fifteen minutes of activation. In a mixed building with apartments over an office, we set the cutoff well inside that limit.
  • Licensing. Alarm installation businesses in New York are licensed by the Department of State under Article 6-D of the General Business Law. Ours is #12000287431; ask any installer for theirs.
  • Fire alarms are separate. A burglar alarm is not a fire alarm system. Community buildings, schools and larger offices need code fire alarm work, which we handle through Ocean Parkway fire alarm installation.
  • Detectors in rental housing. HPD requires smoke detectors in each unit of a multiple dwelling and sets carbon monoxide rules for owners and tenants; adding monitored smoke and CO devices to an apartment alarm does not replace the owner’s duties under those rules.
Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation on Ocean Parkway: Questions Answered

These are the questions that come from the parkway’s particular layout: the deep front yard, the six-story apartment house, the one-way service road and the offices at the southern end. Each is answered in full here, and none of them repeats the FAQ further down.

The thirty-foot front yard

Our building stands behind the 30-foot front yard that the parkway zoning requires. Should the alarm react when someone crosses that yard, or only when a door or window opens?

For most apartments, only when a door or window opens; for houses, often both. In an apartment house the front yard belongs to the building and is crossed all day by residents, visitors and delivery drivers, so an apartment alarm has no business watching it. For a house, the yard is yours, and thirty feet of lawn is enough distance for an early warning to be useful. We do that with a narrow beam or a curtain-pattern outdoor sensor set low along the front walk or across the planting bed under the front windows, so it sees a person on your own ground and nothing beyond it. That sensor should send a chime or a phone notification, not sound the siren, because a mail carrier or a neighbor’s child will cross it in daylight. The siren belongs to the door and window sensors, which only trip when someone actually tries to get in.

Hedges and shrubs planted along the front of our building hide the ground-floor windows from the service road. What does that change?

It means those windows should be treated as if they were at the back. The special district’s stated purpose is to strengthen the scenic landmark “by requiring landscaping along Ocean Parkway,” and many buildings have planted their yards generously, which is good for the parkway and gives cover to anyone standing at a ground-floor window. Behind a hedge, a person working on a sash lock is invisible from the sidewalk and the service road. So every ground-floor window behind planting gets a contact on its moving sash, and rooms with several such windows get a glass-break detector as well, so that a broken pane is reported even if nobody climbs in. If the shrubs are the building’s, we also mention to the super or the board that keeping them below sill height helps everyone on the ground floor.

People use the mall benches and the bike path in front of us late on summer nights. If we put a detector outside, how is it kept from reacting to them?

By never aiming it at them. The malls, the service road and the sidewalk are public, they are busy, and a detector that can see any of them will send alerts all night. Outdoor detection on the parkway is aimed along the face of your house or down your own walk, angled back toward the building, with its range set short enough that it ends before the sidewalk. Beam sensors, which only trip when something crosses a straight line between two points, suit the parkway better than wide motion sensors for exactly this reason. If a camera is part of the plan, its detection zone is drawn to cover your yard and door and to exclude the public space, which also keeps the recording focused on what matters to you.

Apartments in the six-story buildings

A 2018 police report described an Ocean Parkway apartment near Brighton Beach Avenue entered at about 2 p.m. through a bedroom window whose lock had been broken. If a window lock can be forced, what does a sensor add?

Time and a witness. A lock decides how hard the window is to open; a sensor decides whether anyone finds out that it was opened. In that report the lock was broken and the intruder was inside in the middle of the afternoon, when the apartment was presumably empty, and nothing told anyone. A contact on the sash reports the moment the window moves more than a small gap, whether the lock held or not, and sends an alert to your phone and, if you have monitoring, to the central station. A glass-break detector in the same room reports a broken pane. Neither stops a determined person from opening the window, but both turn a quiet afternoon break-in into a noisy one that is reported within seconds, and that is usually what makes people leave.

We live on the second floor. Can someone reach our windows from the front yard or the rear court, and do they need sensors?

Often some of them can, and those need sensors; the rest may not. Second-floor windows on the parkway front usually sit too high to reach from the yard, because zoning keeps stairs, canopies and porches out of it, but an entrance surround, a ground-floor bay or a low garden wall can change that for the windows directly above. At the back, look for anything that makes a step: a shed or garbage-enclosure roof, the roof of a one-story extension or a neighboring garage, an air-conditioner cage, a fire escape. Our technician checks each of your windows from outside and marks it reachable or not. Reachable windows get contacts, and the motion detector in your foyer covers the rest of the apartment, so you are not paying for sensors on windows nobody can get to.

Our apartment faces the parkway on one side and the rear court on the other. Which side should the motion detector watch?

Neither side directly; it should watch the place where the two sides meet. In most prewar parkway layouts that is the foyer or the inside hall, which anyone coming from a front room, a rear bedroom or the kitchen must pass through to get anywhere else. A detector there, mounted high in a corner, catches movement from either side with one device and does not stare at a window where curtains move in the breeze or sunlight sweeps across the floor. If the apartment is laid out as two separate wings with no shared hall, we use two detectors, one per wing, and still keep them aimed away from the windows themselves.

Our co-op’s rules do not allow anything fixed to the outside of the windows or the facade. Can every sensor go on the inside?

Yes, and that is how we fit them anyway. Window contacts go on the interior face of the moving sash and the frame. Glass-break detectors sit on an interior wall or ceiling. The panel and siren sit in a closet, the keypad beside the apartment door. The cellular communicator lives inside the panel, so no antenna goes on the building. Nothing passes through the window frame, the exterior wall or the public hallway. We closed on an apartment that will sit empty for months while the kitchen and bathrooms are redone. Should the alarm go in before the contractors start or after they finish?

Usually a simple version before, and the finished version after. An empty apartment under renovation is exactly the kind of place the blotters describe: in April 2021 a vacant Ocean Parkway apartment near Avenue Z was entered through an open kitchen window. During the work, we fit the panel, a door contact, contacts on the reachable windows and one motion detector, give the contractor a code that works only during agreed working hours, and send you an alert whenever the apartment is disarmed or left open overnight. When the walls are closed and the windows are final, we reposition anything the work disturbed, add the sensors for the new kitchen and bathrooms, and hand over the system for living in. You pay for the equipment once.

Houses on the parkway frontage

Our house has been enlarged over the years and now has more than thirty windows on four sides. How do you decide which of them go on the alarm?

By asking of each one whether a person can reach it and whether anyone would see them doing it. Ground-floor windows on any side are reachable and get a contact or glass-break coverage. Upper windows get a sensor only if something outside makes them reachable: a garage roof, an extension, a porch roof on an older house, a deck. Windows facing the parkway are not assumed safe just because the parkway is busy, for the reasons explained above. Windows facing a neighbor’s wall across a narrow side yard are judged on what is below them. The written price lists every window and the reason it is or is not on the alarm, so you can change any line before work starts. Glass-break detectors often replace several contacts in a room with many panes, which keeps the count and the cost reasonable.

A house cleaner took more than $4,000 in cash from an Ocean Parkway home in 2021 while the owner was there. Can an alarm do anything about theft by someone who has been let in?

Less than it can about a break-in, and we would rather say so plainly. In that 60th Precinct report the owner heard the dog bark and saw the cleaner running down the hall; no alarm would have been armed while someone was home and working. What an alarm can add is narrower: a separate zone on one closet, cabinet or room that stays armed even when the rest of the house is not, with a phone alert the moment it opens; individual codes for everyone who works in the house, so the event log shows who disarmed and when; and an indoor camera covering a hallway or the door of that room if you want a record. Cash and valuables are best kept in a safe that is bolted down. The alarm cannot replace careful hiring or locking things away.

Getting help to the right door

Our address is on one of the one-way service roads. What should the monitoring company have on file so police come in from the correct end?

The side of the parkway, the direction the service road runs, the cross avenue to turn in from, and how to find the door from the curb. Each service road is a single lane in one direction, so a patrol car that turns onto the wrong one, or onto the right one past your building, has to go around the block. On file with the monitoring station we put the building number, “east service road” or “west service road,” the nearest cross avenues on either side, whether the entrance is on the parkway or around the corner on the side street, and for apartment houses the floor and how the lobby door opens. Those notes are read to the dispatcher with the alarm. It takes five minutes to write them, and we do it on install day.

Offices and practices

Our medical practice is on the ground floor of an apartment building on the parkway. Can our alarm be completely separate from the building’s systems?

Yes, and it should be. The practice gets its own panel, its own cellular communicator and its own codes, with no connection to the building’s intercom, cameras, fire alarm or network. That way the building’s staff cannot disarm the practice and the practice’s alarm never depends on the building’s phone or internet service. The only shared matters are practical: agreeing with the landlord where the panel and siren may be mounted, how the cleaning or maintenance staff get in after hours, and where the suite’s exterior door is in relation to the building entrance. If the practice already has a sign-in system for staff, the alarm’s user codes can mirror it, so each person’s arrivals and departures appear in one log.

A burglar blamed for a series of break-ins near Brighton Beach also got into a commercial office at 3049 Ocean Parkway. What should an office alarm cover that a store alarm does not?

The rooms and furniture that hold what an office actually loses. That series, reported by News 12, took a laptop, other electronics and cash. A store alarm concentrates on the gate, the front glass and the register; an office alarm needs a contact on the suite door, motion in the corridor and in any room with computers, a contact or shock sensor on the cabinet or room that holds records, cash or medication, and a panic button at the front desk. Opening and closing reports matter more in an office than in most stores, because offices are often left by the last person out at an irregular hour, and the owner wants to know it was armed.

“Busy streets with lots of people around are safer, so the front of the house needs less protection.”

The parkway is busy, but its traffic is in the middle, on the mall paths and the main roadway, not at your door. A front door thirty feet back behind planting is out of sight of almost everyone using the parkway. The neighbors who could see it from across the street are about 270 feet away, behind their own yards.

The busy part of the parkway does matter in one way: it is the reason outdoor detection has to be aimed carefully. A sensor that sees the mall will alert all night.

“Apartments above the ground floor don’t need alarms.”

Many upper-floor apartments need little beyond a door contact and a motion detector, and we say so. But the blanket claim ignores how parkway buildings are built: rear courts with shed roofs and extensions, entrance surrounds and bays at the front, fire escapes where they exist.

The Ocean Parkway police reports we found involved apartment windows, not ground-floor storefronts. Whether your window is reachable is a question for someone standing in your court, not a rule of thumb.

“Landmark rules mean you can’t install security equipment.”

Generic advice often assumes any landmark means a permit for anything visible. Field Notes

Field Notes: Stand in the Court Before You Stand in the Apartment

This describes how our technicians survey a parkway building, not a story about a particular job. The survey starts outside. At the front, the technician stands on the sidewalk and then in the yard, looking at which ground-floor windows are hidden by planting and whether anything around the entrance brings a second-floor sill within reach. Then he goes to the back, by the building’s side passage or through the basement with the super’s permission, and stands in the rear court looking up: shed roofs, extensions, garbage enclosures, fire escapes, the neighbor’s garage. Each of the customer’s windows is marked on a sketch as reachable or not, with the reason.

Only then does he go up to the apartment, try every window and the door by hand, and choose the motion detector’s corner by walking the routes from each reachable window to the bedrooms. The written price follows that sketch line by line. On houses the same order applies: the yard, the drive, the back, then inside. The survey takes longer than looking at a floor plan, and it is the reason the system has the right number of sensors.

Ocean Parkway Alarm Installation FAQ

What does an alarm for a one-bedroom apartment in a six-story Ocean Parkway building cost on your Brooklyn list?
+

Most land on our basic residential system, from $600: a panel with a cellular communicator, four to six door and window sensors, one motion detector, a keypad and monitoring setup. Ocean Parkway is a scenic landmark. Is the front wall of our building part of that landmark?
+

No. The 1975 designation defines the landmark as the property between the outer curb lines of the two side roads, from Church Avenue to Seabreeze Avenue. Sidewalks, front yards and buildings lie outside it. Your building could still be landmarked on its own or governed by co-op, condo or lease rules, and an apartment alarm mounted entirely inside raises none of those issues.

Buses run along parts of the parkway right outside our front windows. Will a glass-break detector mistake them for breaking glass?
+

Not if it is chosen and set properly. Current acoustic detectors listen for the particular pattern of breaking glass, a low thud followed by high-frequency shatter, rather than for loudness. We mount them facing the windows, test them with a simulator that reproduces breaking glass, and adjust sensitivity for the room. Rumbling engines and brakes do not match that pattern.

Our co-op replaced every window in the building with new frames. Do contacts fit them?
+

Yes. Modern replacement frames take slim surface contacts on the interior face of the sash and frame, and many accept small recessed contacts if the board allows drilling. We bring both types and choose after seeing the frames, so nothing is fixed to the outside or through the window’s seal.

Do you cover both ends of the parkway, from the Kensington end near Church Avenue to the Brighton Beach end, at the same price?
+

Yes. Every address along the parkway, on either service road, and on the side streets around it pays the same Brooklyn list price, with no travel charge for any stretch. Our crews come from our Troy Avenue office in Brooklyn.

We own a two-family house on a side street just off the parkway. Does the parkway’s front-yard zoning apply to us?
+

The 30-foot front-yard rule in Zoning Resolution section 113-12 applies to zoning lots that front Ocean Parkway, so a house facing a side street is normally governed by its own district’s rules instead. For the alarm it makes little difference: we protect the doors and reachable windows you have, and partitions let the owner and the tenant arm their own floors.

Our in-unit washer and dryer sit in a closet off the kitchen. Can the alarm warn us about a leak before it reaches the apartment below?
+

Yes. A wireless leak sensor on the floor beside the washer, and another under the kitchen sink, report to the same panel and send a phone alert the moment water touches them. In a building where your floor is your neighbor’s ceiling, those few minutes matter. The sensors are battery powered and need no wiring.

Can the panel and siren go inside a closet so nothing shows in our living room?
+

The panel, yes, and that is our usual choice in parkway apartments. The siren can go in the same closet if the door is louvered or the closet is near the bedrooms; otherwise we put a small sounder high in the hall. Only the keypad by the door and the slim sensors on windows are visible, and the keypad can be replaced by the phone app if you prefer.

Is a hardwired alarm possible in a co-op apartment with plaster walls, or is wireless the only practical choice?
+

Hardwiring is possible but rarely worth it in an occupied apartment, because fishing wire through old plaster means opening and patching walls that the co-op may also regulate. Wireless sensors use the same panel, report as reliably, and their batteries usually last years. During a gut renovation, when walls are open, hardwiring is the better choice.

My mother lives alone in an apartment on the parkway. Can her alarm include a button she can press if she falls or feels unwell?
+

Yes. A wireless panic pendant or wristband, and a fixed button by the bed, can report to the same panel. With central station monitoring, a press sends an operator call to her and then to the people on her list, and notes on file tell responders which floor she lives on and how to get through the lobby.
