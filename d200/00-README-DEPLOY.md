## Distinct theses

**Tremont** — a prewar facade does not mean prewar wiring. Much of this stock was gutted and rebuilt from the late 1970s onward, so a 1924 building may carry a 1989 entry system. The page argues that establishing the generation before diagnosing is the basic discipline here, that faults concentrate at the boundary where new work met surviving old, and that condemning thirty-five-year-old wiring for being in a hundred-year-old building costs owners real money.

**University Heights** — in a walk-up the riser lives in the public stairwell, because these buildings were never given a service shaft. That cuts both ways: the run can be inspected end to end without opening a wall, and it has been painted around, moved during stair repairs and knocked by furniture for seventy years. Also covers why top-floor apartments fail first and get reported last.

**Van Nest** — houses held in the same families for decades, running systems that outlived their manufacturers. The core argument is that "the brand is discontinued" and "this part is unobtainable" are different questions, and only the second one matters. Most of an entry system — transformer, wiring, strike, button — is generic and always was.
