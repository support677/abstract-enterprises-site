Cameras the Landmarks Rules Can Live With · Faces at the Stoop and Garden Door · Cable Kept Off the Front

Security camera installation in Brooklyn Heights begins with a rule most of Brooklyn never has to think about. The whole neighborhood sits inside the city's first historic district, designated by the Landmarks Preservation Commission on November 23, 1965, and the Commission's own permit guidebook treats a camera on a building as health-and-safety equipment that its staff can approve only when it is small, discreet, kept off decorative stone and ironwork and, wherever another option exists, kept off the primary facade. Behind that rule stand the roughly 1,284 buildings of the original designation, at least 684 of them older than the Civil War: Federal houses of wood and brick in the north Heights, Greek Revival and Italianate row houses of brick and brownstone, carriage houses on mews such as Grace Court Alley, pre-war co-ops along Pierrepont and Hicks streets, the converted Hotel St. George, and newer condominiums down on Furman Street. The local record points cameras at three places: the stoop and the garden-level door under it, the rear garden and the windows that face it, and the lobby and package shelf of an apartment building. Past reports of Heights break-ins describe weekday, daytime entries through unlocked doors and open windows, and in one case a spare key left in a mailbox. So we give each camera one job, route cable through cellars, rear walls and interior chases instead of across the front, keep the recorder locked and on battery backup, and put every camera, cable run and approval on a written quote before anything is ordered.

The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Heights Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Brooklyn Heights grew up as a commuter village above the East River, and almost all of that village is still standing, which is exactly what makes a camera here a careful job rather than a quick one. Robert Fulton's steam ferry began running to Manhattan in 1814, and residential building picked up through the 1820s and continued for the rest of the century. The landowners whose names are still on the street signs, among them Pierrepont, Middagh and Hicks, laid out uniform house lots, and the earliest row houses, built up to the 1850s, were of wood, two or three stories over a basement. Brick and brownstone took over from there. When the Brooklyn Bridge opened in 1883 and the subway arrived in 1908, many of the big houses were divided into apartments and boarding houses, and after 1929 more became rooming houses. The Brooklyn-Queens Expressway was cut along the western edge in the 1940s and 1950s, and the Promenade was laid over it. Then, in 1965, the neighborhood became New York City's first historic district. The designation report counted 1,284 buildings, at least 684 of them from before the Civil War, including about 60 Federal, 405 Greek Revival, 47 Gothic Revival and 201 Anglo-Italianate houses, plus 61 carriage houses on the mews.

That history leaves several very different kinds of building on the same few blocks, each with its own decision-maker. The Brooklyn Heights Association describes the neighborhood as overwhelmingly low-rise brick and brownstone row houses with a smattering of apartment buildings, and it reports that 57 percent of housing units are rented and 40 percent owner-occupied. City property data for the historic district, as that dataset draws its lines, shows 1,247 tax lots holding 9,519 homes, with 253 one-family houses, 228 two-family houses, and 189 lots of co-operative apartments holding 3,732 units. Some of those co-ops are tall pre-war buildings, such as the 30-story St. George Tower at 111 Hicks Street, built in 1929, designed by Emery Roth and converted to a co-op in 1984; some are 13-story buildings on Pierrepont Street from the 1920s; and some are single row houses divided into a handful of shares, run by the people who live in them. A row-house owner decides about their own walls, but still answers to the Landmarks Commission for the outside. A co-op shareholder answers to a board. A floor-through tenant answers to the owner. A shop on Montague Street answers to its landlord and, for the storefront, to the Commission. Sorting that out comes first on every camera job here.

The incidents in the public record are a guide to where a camera earns its keep. In January 2017, DNAinfo reported seven burglaries in Brooklyn Heights in the week of January 2 to 8, according to CompStat, and thirteen between December 5 and January 1. Patch described forced entries in some homes and an unlocked window, possibly reached from a fire escape, in others, mostly on weekdays during working hours; one burglar used a spare key that had been left in a mailbox near Hicks and State streets. Brooklyn Paper reported that most entries in a string of home burglaries along Willow, Joralemon and Montague streets came through open windows that someone climbed to. Back in March 2011, Brownstoner carried a Brooklyn Heights Association warning about five daytime break-ins over four days, all through unlocked doors. In apartment buildings, the Brooklyn Heights Blog has shown a package thief caught on camera at 90 Pierrepont Street, with the building posting the footage in its lobby, and Brooklyn Paper reported a woman's iMac snatched just as she reached her Cranberry Street apartment building. None of those cases is recent, and the overall trend is down: a Brooklyn real-estate team's summary of NYPD figures for the 84th Precinct, which covers Brooklyn Heights along with Boerum Hill, DUMBO, Vinegar Hill and part of Downtown Brooklyn, shows the seven major crimes down 18.5 percent through October 11, 2026, compared with 2025. We found no source that ranks Heights blocks by crime and we do not offer one.

What those reports share is the time of day and the route. Daytime, weekday entries through a back window, a garden door or a door someone forgot to lock happen when the house is empty and the street is quiet, at the back of the house where nobody on the sidewalk can see. So the camera plan for most Heights houses runs front to back: something at the stoop and the garden-level door that shows a face at close range, something at the rear that covers the garden, the rear doors and the lower windows, and in apartment buildings, something at the lobby door and the package shelf. Public cameras do not do that job. City Council Member Lincoln Restler's office set aside $90,000 for surveillance cameras at two entrances to the Brooklyn Heights Promenade, the Brooklyn Eagle reported in June 2025, but those cameras are for the Promenade, not for anyone's garden door, and nobody outside the city can pull their footage. A private system covers your own doors, keeps the recording in your hands, and stays framed on your own property, which on a row of attached houses with gardens side by side matters as much as the cameras themselves.

Brooklyn Heights facts that shape a camera plan

  • The whole neighborhood is landmarked: the Brooklyn Heights Historic District was designated on November 23, 1965, the first in the city, so anything fixed to the outside of a building goes through Landmarks Preservation Commission rules.
  • Cameras are a staff-level item: the Commission's 2018 rule changes listed security cameras among equipment on facades and areaways that its staff can approve, provided the installation meets set criteria in Rules Section 2-20.
  • Lots of very old houses: city property data puts 617 of the district's lots in buildings estimated to date from before 1860, which means plaster on lath, brick party walls and cellars that were never planned for cable.
  • Many households per house: 228 two-family houses and dozens of small rental and co-op row houses mean one camera system often serves several households that share a stoop and a garden.
  • The Heights files a lot of entrance work: LPC permit data shows 91 Brooklyn Heights filings since 2019 that include the work type for light fixtures and intercom panels, the most of any historic district in Brooklyn, a sign of how much doorway equipment goes through the Commission here.
  • The front is busy, the back is not: the 2011 and 2017 break-in reports describe weekday, daytime entries through unlocked doors and open rear windows, out of sight of the street.

What Brooklyn Heights owners often add next. Once the stoop, the garden door and the rear garden are covered, owners of divided houses tend to ask about a video panel at the street door that each floor can answer from its own phone, and co-op boards ask whether the package area could log who opens the door. We leave a labeled spare port on the recorder and, where an intercom upgrade is likely, a spare cable to the vestibule, so the plaster only has to be opened once.

The Landmarks Line

Stoop, Areaway, Band Course or Rear Wall: Where a Camera Can Sit on a Brooklyn Heights Row House

Every Brooklyn Heights camera plan starts with a sketch of the front of the house and a second sketch of the back, because the Landmarks Preservation Commission looks at the two very differently, and so do we. Chapter 11 of the Commission's permit guidebook covers health, safety and utility equipment, and security cameras are in it alongside light fixtures, intercom panels, doorbells and mail boxes. The guidebook says equipment of this kind cannot go on or in front of a primary facade unless no other option exists, and that it belongs in discreet places: beneath a band course, under the stoop, or behind an areaway wall. It asks for attachment at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, never through cast iron or decorative surfaces, for conduit to be concealed, and where some has to show, for no more than about ten inches of it, painted to match. It asks for equipment that is small and blends in. And for smaller buildings, as we read the guidebook's criteria, it limits the number of cameras by the width of the frontage. Ordinary maintenance and repair of equipment that is already approved does not need a permit at all.

Those rules happen to line up well with good camera work. A camera tucked under the stoop at the garden-level door is closer to eye level than one high on the parlor floor, so it records faces rather than the tops of heads. A camera behind the areaway wall sees the gate and the steps down, which is where anyone heading for the garden door has to pass. A rear camera on the back wall covers the place where the reported Heights break-ins actually happened. What the rules rule out, mainly a row of cameras across the brownstone face or a cable stapled up beside the parlor door, are things we would talk an owner out of anyway. Where the rules and the camera's needs part ways is usually at the very top of the house, where a long view down the block would be useful but the cornice is decorative, or at the parlor door, where the doorway itself is the most protected part of the front.

Why "not visible from the street" is not the test

Some installers say a camera only needs Landmarks approval if it can be seen from the street. The Commission's own material is broader than that: work that affects the exterior of a building in a historic district, including its rear facade and yard, is the Commission's business, and a NoHo district guide puts it plainly that a permit is still needed even when the work affects only the part of the property out of public view. In Brooklyn Heights the point is sharper than usual, because the backs of the Columbia Heights houses are in full view of everyone walking the Promenade. In practice, rear cameras are usually the easier conversation with Commission staff, but easier is not the same as exempt, and we plan them as exterior work.

What a camera looks like when the Commission is happy with it

The cases that go badly are well documented. At a 2015 Commission hearing about a Bedford-Stuyvesant house, the Historic Districts Council called cameras put up without approval on a well-preserved facade "sore thumbs," and suggested black cameras placed in the cornice or other spots where they would not detract from the building. The lesson we take is about size, color and count rather than any single spot. A small turret or dome in a dark finish, tucked under the stoop or into the shadow of the areaway, with no exposed cable, reads as nothing much. A large white bullet camera on a bracket beside the parlor door, with a loop of cable down the brownstone, is exactly what the guidebook is written to prevent. We choose housings and colors with the elevation in front of us, and we keep the front of the house to the fewest cameras that do the job.

Inside the house, the Commission mostly steps back

Cameras in the vestibule behind the outer doors, in a hall, on a stair or in a garden-floor room do not change the outside of the building, and one installer's guidance is that interior work is generally allowed without Commission approval, though a co-op or condo may still have its own rules. The Commission does issue permits for interior work when that work affects the exterior, and it offers an expedited certificate for certain kinds of interior work in historic-district buildings, which it says can be issued within three days. For most camera jobs, the interior side is about protecting plaster, mouldings and original woodwork rather than paperwork, and we deal with that in the building section below.

Daytime, Back of the House

Planning a Brooklyn Heights House Camera System Around How Past Break-Ins Happened

The burglary reports from 2011 and 2017 describe a pattern that most row-house owners in the Heights will recognize from their own blocks: someone comes during the working day, on a weekday, when the house is empty and the street is quiet, and gets in at the back or through a door nobody locked. That pattern tells us more about where cameras should go than any crime map. It means the rear of the house matters as much as the front, that daytime alerts matter as much as night vision, and that a camera's real job is to give a clear record of the person and the route, not to look busy on the facade.

It also tells us what to leave out. A Heights row house is narrow, often eighteen to twenty-five feet wide, with a stoop, an areaway, a front door and a garden door at the street and one or two doors and a run of windows at the back. Four well-placed cameras cover most of that. A camera in every window does not help anyone and gives the Commission, the neighbors and the household a lot to dislike.

The stoop and the garden-level door

The front of a row house has two entrances one above the other, the parlor door at the top of the stoop and the garden-level door below and behind the areaway gate, and on many divided houses each one serves a different household. The camera that does the most work at the front is the one under the stoop, because everyone who uses the garden door, leaves a package below the stoop or tries the areaway gate passes within a few feet of it, roughly at face height. The parlor door is better served by a doorbell or intercom camera within the door opening than by a second camera on the facade, since that is where the guidebook expects entry equipment to sit and where a visitor stands still and faces the lens. Between them, those two record nearly everyone who approaches the house from the street.

The rear garden, the rear doors and the lower windows

Behind the house, the garden is usually enclosed by masonry walls or fences shared with neighbors on three sides, and the rear facade has a garden door, sometimes a parlor-level door to a deck or iron stair, and windows on every floor. We mount the rear camera high on the back wall, often just under the parlor-floor windows, looking down the garden toward the back fence, so anyone crossing the garden walks toward it and the lower windows and doors are in the bottom of the frame. If the parlor floor has its own rear door or a stair down to the garden, a second, closer camera covers that door. Because the reported Heights break-ins came through open windows that someone climbed to, we set the rear camera's alert zone over the garden wall tops and the lower windows rather than the whole garden, so a cat on the lawn does not ring the phone but a person on the wall does.

Keeping the gardens next door out of the picture

The back of a row in the Heights is a patchwork of gardens separated by low walls, and a careless rear camera can easily record three neighbors' patios. New York's Civil Rights Law section 52-a lets a resident sue over a camera that records them in their backyard where they reasonably expect privacy. We stop the frame at the owner's own walls and mask anything beyond them in the camera settings, so the recording shows the owner's garden and doors and nothing of the people next door. On the Columbia Heights side, where the gardens look out toward the Promenade, we also keep the frame off the Promenade itself, which keeps the recording focused and keeps passers-by out of it.

Daytime alerts and the empty house

Because the pattern here is daytime and weekday, the recorder's schedule matters. We set every camera to record continuously, because motion triggers miss people who move slowly, and set alerts separately: the rear garden and garden door send alerts whenever the household marks itself away, the stoop alerts only on a person stopping at the door, and the parlor door's doorbell handles visitors. An alert at two in the afternoon on a Tuesday, when nobody should be in the garden, is the most useful message the system will ever send.

Federal houses: the oldest walls on the job

The north Heights still has houses from the 1820s, some of them wood-frame, and they are the most fragile walls we work on anywhere in Brooklyn. On a frame house, a camera screwed straight into clapboard will loosen and split the board, so the camera goes on a small mounting block fixed through the siding into the framing behind it, sealed around its edges, with the cable entering through the center of the block and hidden behind the camera. On early brick, the brick itself is often soft and irregular, so we drill only into mortar joints, with bits sized to the joint, and never into the face of a brick. Inside, these houses rarely have a clear chase from cellar to upper floors, so we look for a route behind built-in cupboards, beside an old flue, or along the attic, and we would rather add a few feet of cable than open a historic plaster wall.

Greek Revival and Italianate row houses: the cellar is the highway

The brick and brownstone row houses almost always have a cellar running the full depth of the house, and that is where the cable goes. The run for the under-stoop camera comes through the garden-level front wall at a mortar joint, a few inches from where it enters the house, and travels along the cellar joists. The rear camera's run goes the length of the cellar and up through the back wall, or comes up inside the house and out through the rear facade under the parlor windows. Nothing crosses the front. The brownstone face of an Italianate house is a soft sandstone that chips and stains easily, and it is the part of the front the Commission most wants left alone, so we treat it as off limits for mounts; plain brick at the side of an areaway or the underside of the stoop does the job instead. The recorder belongs on a living floor rather than in a damp cellar, on a battery unit, beside the internet router if possible, and the party walls with the houses next door are never used for anything.

Carriage houses: big doors and a lane that isn't a street

The carriage houses on the mews were built as stables for the mansions on the streets around them. CityLore describes Grace Court Alley as the Remsen family's stable lane, its two- and three-story carriage houses later becoming garages, chauffeurs' quarters and then homes, and almost all of them keep their hay cranes. On these houses the ground floor is often still a garage, so the most useful camera is usually inside the garage looking out through the door, or on the plain brick beside the big doors, rather than on the facade above. We keep mounts off the original doors, the arched openings and any hay-crane beam or bracket, which are exactly the features that make a mews protected. Cable is simple, since the camera is usually a few feet from the inside of the house. The view is planned for the lane immediately in front of the doors, not the length of the mews, which belongs to several owners.

Pre-war co-ops: one recorder, many floors, rated walls

The apartment buildings on Pierrepont, Hicks and Clark streets put cameras in lobbies, package and mail rooms, laundry rooms, service entrances and elevator cabs, and the work is mostly about getting cable from a cellar equipment room up through the building without damaging a finished lobby. We use existing risers and conduit where they have room, run new cable in conduit through the cellar, and seal every opening through a fire-rated floor or wall with rated firestop before we leave. Lobby cameras sit where they see faces coming in, not the backs of heads; the package-room camera looks at the shelves from the side, so a face and the box are in the same frame; and the recorder goes in a locked, ventilated room with its own battery backup. Before any of that, the board or managing agent gets a scope letter and our certificate of insurance.

Converted hotels and newer condos: concrete and someone else's systems

The Hotel St. George was built in stages from 1885 to 1930 and was once the largest hotel in the city; part of it became the St. George Tower co-op in 1984, and other parts became student housing from the late 1990s. The Hotel Bossert at 98 Montague Street, built in 1909, is being converted into 61 condominiums with completion targeted for 2028. The 1928 warehouse at 360 Furman Street became One Brooklyn Bridge Park in 2008, and Pierhouse at 90 Furman Street is a 10-story condo by the park. Buildings like these have concrete slabs, block walls, fire-rated corridors and their own network and security equipment, so camera work means sleeves, core drilling at planned points, cable trays, and coordination with whoever runs the building's systems. It also means asking early, because a building in mid-conversion is the cheapest time to put cable in.

A Montague Street shop: door, counter, floor, back room

A shop on Montague Street rarely needs more than four or five cameras if each one has a clear job. The first is inside the front door at about head height, facing the door, so everyone who comes in walks toward it. The second covers the counter from slightly above and in front, showing hands, goods and cash while keeping card terminals and screens out of view so no one's PIN is recorded. The third covers the floor or main aisle from a high corner. The fourth watches the back room or rear door from inside, and if the shop has a cellar reached by a stair or sidewalk doors, that entrance gets one too. In January 2017 DNAinfo reported a bagel shop on Montague Street whose front door was broken at about 1:40 in the morning, and that is the case for a camera inside the door that sees the glass, plus a recorder in a locked cabinet away from the register, where someone who gets in cannot carry the evidence out with the cash.

The storefront itself is a Landmarks question

Because every storefront in the Heights is inside the historic district, a camera on the outside of a shop goes through the Landmarks Commission like a camera on a house. The Commission runs a service called LPC Business Express for storefront businesses, covering health, safety and utility equipment such as security cameras and light fixtures, with applications made through its Portico portal. Most shops are better served by an inside camera aimed out through the glass, which needs no exterior change, plus one small exterior camera where the sign band or the side of the door opening allows it. The facade, the sign band and the residential door beside the shop usually belong to the landlord, so the landlord's written approval comes first.

Offices and practices near Borough Hall

Offices on the Court Street side usually sit inside buildings with lobby staff and their own cameras. An office's own system covers its suite door, reception, any records or equipment room and the back of the suite, and stays out of private offices and meeting rooms where clients talk. Alerts are scheduled for the hours the office is closed and go to the people who actually respond. If the building's managing agent has rules about contractors or about equipment in shared corridors, we follow them and send insurance certificates in advance.

Churches, schools and rented halls

Several Heights congregations lease space to schools, offices and performers; the Church of St. Ann and the Holy Trinity on Montague Street, for example, leases school, office, rehearsal and concert space. Buildings like that have many key holders and long empty stretches, so the cameras that matter are at the doors outsiders use, the office and any room where money or equipment is kept, with sanctuaries and classrooms left off camera unless there is a clear reason. Each tenant group can be given access to the door views for its own hours and nothing more, and the building's own staff keep the full archive.

Field Notes

Field Notes: How We Walk a Brooklyn Heights Row House, From the Areaway Gate to the Back Garden Wall

This is the order we work in on these houses, not a story about one job. We start on the sidewalk, across the street if we can, and photograph the whole front, because the Landmarks conversation, if there is one, starts with that picture. We note the stoop, the areaway, the railings, the band course, the door hood and the cornice, and mark which surfaces are plain brick and which are brownstone or decorative. Then we open the areaway gate, stand at the garden-level door and look up at the underside of the stoop, which is usually where the first camera wants to be, and check whether there is a mortar joint nearby where cable can enter the house. We ring the parlor-door bell and look at what any existing doorbell or intercom camera shows on the owner's phone. Inside, we ask who lives on which floor, who uses which door, and who should see which views, because that decides how many accounts the recorder needs. Then we go down to the cellar, look along the joists for a clear path front to back, find where the internet line enters, check how damp it is, and trace any old wire we find. If the cellar is damp, the recorder goes up to a closet on a living floor. We go out the back to the garden, stand against the rear wall and then at the far wall looking back, which shows where a rear camera covers the garden, the lower windows and the doors, and which neighboring gardens and windows it has to stop short of. Last, we check the roof hatch or bulkhead and any rear iron stair. By the time we leave, we know the camera count, every cable length, the recorder spot, the accounts and whether a Landmarks filing is needed, and the written quote is built from that.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Brooklyn Heights: Questions Answered

Landmarks and the outside of the building

What usually makes Landmarks staff turn down a proposed camera on a Brooklyn Heights row house front?

From the guidebook's own criteria, the common problems are placement on the primary facade when a discreet spot exists, fixing through decorative stone, cast iron or other ornament, exposed cable or conduit beyond a short painted stretch, equipment that is large or calls attention to itself, and too many cameras for the width of the house. A proposal that puts one small dark camera under the stoop, one behind the areaway wall, a doorbell camera within the door opening and the rest at the rear, with cable entering at mortar joints right behind each camera, answers all of those points before staff have to raise them. We draw it that way from the start, and if staff ask for a change, we redraw rather than argue.

We run a shop on Montague Street inside the historic district. Is LPC Business Express the route for a camera over our door?

It is designed for exactly that. The Commission describes LPC Business Express as a service for storefront businesses, and health, safety and utility equipment such as security cameras and light fixtures is one of the categories it covers, with applications going through the Portico portal. Before filing, get your landlord's written approval, because the storefront and sign band are usually the owner's. We supply photos of the storefront, a marked drawing of where each camera goes, and the manufacturer's sheets for the camera and mount, which is the kind of material the Commission asks for. Cameras inside the shop, including one aimed out through the window, do not need the filing.

Can a camera hang from the old hay-crane beam over our carriage-house doors on Grace Court Alley?

We would not put one there. The hay cranes, beams and brackets are part of what makes the mews carriage houses historic, CityLore notes that almost all the Grace Court Alley houses keep theirs, and the guidebook steers equipment away from decorative and historic features. A camera on plain brick beside the doors, under the soffit, or inside the garage looking out through the door glass or a small opening gives the same view of the lane in front of the house without touching the beam. Inside the garage is often the best spot of all, because the camera is protected and the cable is only a few feet long.

Our house is one of the 1820s wood-frame Federal houses near Middagh Street. Does a frame house in the district change where the cameras go?

It changes how they are fixed more than where they go. On wood siding we mount each camera to a small block fastened through the siding into the framing, sealed around the edges, so nothing pulls at the clapboard, and the cable enters through the block where it is hidden. The Landmarks criteria still apply, so the front gets as little as possible and the rear does most of the work. Old frame houses are also drier inside than masonry ones and often have attic space, which can make cable routes easier, but their original trim, doors and windows are irreplaceable, and we plan around them rather than through them.

Public cameras and the police

The city is paying for cameras at two Promenade entrances. Could those cameras see into our garden or rear windows on Columbia Heights, and who would we ask?

The Brooklyn Eagle reported in June 2025 that Council Member Lincoln Restler's office had set aside $90,000 for surveillance cameras at two entrances to the Promenade. The reports we found do not describe exactly where the cameras will point, so we cannot tell you what they will see. If you are concerned, the Council member's office is the place to ask, since it secured the funding, and it can direct you to the city agency installing them. Your own rear camera is a separate matter: we frame it on your garden and doors and keep the Promenade out of the picture, which protects walkers' privacy and keeps your recording focused on what matters to you.

The 84th Precinct offers free security surveys. Should we book one before we ask for a camera quote, or after?

Before is usually more useful. The NYPD says its precinct crime prevention officers carry out security surveys free of charge and look at locks, doors, lighting, windows, landscaping and alarm systems, and they can be booked through 311 or by asking the precinct for its crime prevention officer. An officer's view of your doors and windows is a good starting list, and we can then plan cameras around the weak points they found. If you already have our quote, the survey is still worth doing, because a better lock on the garden door or a latch on a rear window may change which cameras matter most.

In the 2017 Heights burglaries, one burglar used a spare key from a mailbox. Our dog walker and cleaner need to get in. Can cameras keep a record of every arrival instead of a hidden key?

Yes, and that is one of the most practical uses for a camera in a row house. A camera inside the vestibule facing the front door records everyone who comes through it, by key or code, and its alert can be set to notify you whenever the door opens while you are marked away. Paired with a lock that gives each helper their own code, the camera clip and the lock's log tell you who came in and when, without anyone needing a key under a mat or in a mailbox. If you keep a spare key outside anyway, at least keep it where the under-stoop camera can see whoever goes to it.

Co-ops, condos and apartment buildings

Our building lobby once posted a package thief's footage, the way 90 Pierrepont Street did. Is posting a still in the lobby a good idea, and what should the board think about first?

It is a decision for the board, and worth thinking through before the next time. The Brooklyn Heights Blog showed footage from 90 Pierrepont Street posted in that building's lobby. A posted still can warn residents and sometimes brings a tip, but it can also point at the wrong person, and the original clip matters more than the poster. We would suggest the board first export and lock the full clip so it cannot be overwritten, report it to the 84th Precinct with the time and camera, and agree a written rule on who may export footage and what may be posted. We are not lawyers, and boards with doubts should ask their own counsel.

Our condo at One Brooklyn Bridge Park sits on a ground lease. Who has a say over adding cameras in the garage or at the Furman Street door?

Start with the condo board and managing agent. CityRealty and brokerage research describe the building as a 1928 warehouse converted to condos in 2008, with the land conveyed to the Brooklyn Bridge Park development corporation under a 99-year ground lease. How that lease, the condo's offering plan and its rules divide responsibility for common-area equipment is something only the board and its documents can answer, and we have not seen them. In practice, common-area cameras are planned and approved through the board, and a unit owner's options are inside their own unit. We work through whoever the board names and send insurance certificates in advance.

We live above the triple cantilever on Columbia Heights. Should a camera system we install now be planned around the BQE rebuild that starts in 2030?

It is worth planning for, without waiting for it. The city's plan, reported in August 2026, puts a temporary roadway on Furman Street from 2030, triple-cantilever repairs from 2032 and a Promenade rebuilt in sections through about 2040. For a house or building on the western streets, that likely means years of construction lighting, crews and changing views behind the gardens. We would install the system now, set rear alert zones tightly on your own walls and doors so construction does not trigger constant alerts, mount the rear cameras where scaffolding on the public side will not block them, and leave a spare port and cable so a camera can be added or moved later without reopening plaster.

Permission, Privacy and Licenses

Landmarks, Boards, Landlords and Neighbors: The Rules Behind a Brooklyn Heights Camera Job

In most of Brooklyn the rules that shape a camera job are about ownership and privacy. In Brooklyn Heights there is a third layer, the historic district, sitting on top of the other two. This is how all three play out here. It is a plain-English summary, not legal advice.

Landmarks. Everything fixed to the outside of a building in the historic district, front or rear, falls under the Landmarks Preservation Commission. Since its 2018 rule changes, security cameras on facades and areaways are among the items its staff can approve at staff level when the installation meets the criteria in Rules Section 2-20. Ordinary repair of approved equipment needs no permit. Interior work generally does not involve the Commission unless it affects the exterior. Where an application is needed, the owner files, with our photos, drawings and equipment sheets, and the Commission's staff are at 347-934-8335 or info@lpc.nyc.gov.

Who may install. New York General Business Law section 69-m requires a Department of State license for anyone in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security systems. An owner putting a camera on their own house is not in that business; a contractor or handyman doing it for pay is. AESS holds NYS license #12000287431 and sends a copy to anyone who asks.

Boards and managing agents. Co-op and condo boards control common areas and the building's exterior, and building-wide camera work usually needs board approval and a contractor's insurance certificate before anyone starts. Shareholders and unit owners generally decide only about their own apartments, within the house rules.

Tenants and floor-throughs. With 57 percent of Heights homes rented, according to the Brooklyn Heights Association, many people asking about cameras are tenants. A tenant generally needs the landlord's permission for anything on the outside of the building or in shared halls, stoops and gardens. Inside their own apartment, a camera aimed at their own rooms and the inside of their own door is usually their own business. A camera on the outside of an apartment door facing a shared hallway is a question for the landlord and the lease.

Notices to residents. One installer's website says a New York City law requires residential buildings with common-area cameras to notify residents. We could not confirm that law from an official source, so we do not state it as a rule. Many boards and landlords tell residents about cameras anyway, and we think that is good practice.

Neighbors. Civil Rights Law section 52-a gives a resident a claim against a neighbor who records them in their backyard where they reasonably expect privacy. On a row of attached houses with walled gardens side by side, every outdoor camera we install is framed on the owner's own stoop, areaway, doors and garden, and anything else in the frame is masked out.

Police and footage. NYC 311 says owners can voluntarily register camera locations with the NYPD so detectives know whom to ask after an incident. Registering a location does not hand over recordings; you decide whether to export a clip when asked.

Walls and openings. Every opening through brick, brownstone or siding is made at a mortar joint or through a mounting block and sealed against water. Every opening through a fire-rated wall or floor is sealed with rated firestop before we leave.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Brooklyn Heights

We could not view Google's AI Overview for this topic from our build tools, so these are claims we see repeated in quick answers, installer blogs and national cost sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, written as summaries rather than quotations, followed by what the sources and the work show for Brooklyn Heights.

"You can't put cameras on a landmarked brownstone."

It is easy to see where this comes from. Brooklyn Heights is the city's first historic district, and the Landmarks Commission is strict about the fronts of these houses.

But the Commission's own rules treat cameras as ordinary health-and-safety equipment. Its 2018 rule changes moved approval for security cameras on facades and areaways to staff level, and the permit guidebook spells out where they can go: under the stoop, behind an areaway wall, beneath a band course, fixed at mortar joints, small and discreet.

The real limit is on where and how, not whether. A well-drawn proposal with a few small cameras in the guidebook's discreet places is a routine staff matter.

"If the camera can't be seen from the street, Landmarks doesn't care."

Some installers say this, and for rear cameras it is often how the work feels in practice, because rear proposals are simpler.

The Commission's material covers work that affects the exterior of a building in a historic district, including rear facades and yards, and a district guide published in NoHo says a permit is needed even when the work is out of public view. In the Heights, the backs of the Columbia Heights houses are in plain sight from the Promenade anyway.

So we plan rear cameras as exterior work, keep them small and well placed, and treat the paperwork as part of the job rather than something to hope nobody notices.

"Wireless cameras are the easy answer for an old house."

Wireless cameras do avoid drilling, which sounds ideal on a landmark.

In a Heights row house, the router is usually in the front parlor and the garden is behind solid brick walls and floors, and installers who work here report that Wi-Fi does not reliably reach through that masonry. Battery cameras also need charging, often from a ladder over an areaway.

One cable through a mortar joint at the back of the house, or a wired access point at the rear, usually ends the dropouts for good, and the hole is hidden behind the camera.

"Brooklyn Heights is safe, so cameras are a waste of money."

The trend supports part of this: a Brooklyn real-estate team's summary of NYPD figures shows the 84th Precinct's seven major crimes down 18.5 percent through October 11, 2026, compared with 2025.

Safe neighborhoods still have daytime break-ins and package theft. The reported Heights cases, from the 2011 unlocked-door break-ins to the 2017 window entries and the package thief at 90 Pierrepont Street, all happened in what people considered a safe place.

A camera is not a bet on crime rates; it is a clear record on the one day something happens to your house. Low crime is a reason to buy a small, well-placed system, not a large one.

"The city's new Promenade cameras will cover the neighborhood."

The funding is real: the Brooklyn Eagle reported $90,000 from Council Member Lincoln Restler's office for cameras at two Promenade entrances.

Those cameras are for the Promenade. They are not aimed at anyone's stoop, garden door, lobby or shop, and private owners cannot pull their footage.

Your own doors and garden are still yours to cover, and your own recorder is the only footage you control.

"Any handyman can mount a few cameras."

Mounting a camera is not complicated, and plenty of homeowners do their own.

A contractor paid to do it is a different matter: New York General Business Law section 69-m requires a Department of State license for anyone in the business of installing security systems. In the Heights, a careless job also costs money later, because holes drilled through brownstone or ornament are permanent and may have to be explained to the Commission.

Ask any installer for a state license number and a plan that keeps the front clear. Ours is NYS license #12000287431.

"A video doorbell covers a brownstone."

A doorbell at the parlor door is a good start, and it sits exactly where the Landmarks criteria expect entry equipment.

It does not see the garden-level door below and behind the areaway, the packages left under the stoop, the rear garden, the lower rear windows or the back door, and it mostly records people from the front as they arrive.

Most Heights houses need the doorbell plus an under-stoop camera and a rear camera, all recording to one recorder the owner controls.

FAQ

Brooklyn Heights Security Camera FAQ

Does a Brooklyn Heights address cost more than the rest of the borough for camera work?
+

No. Brooklyn Heights is priced on our standard Brooklyn rate, the same as any other Brooklyn neighborhood, with no surcharge for the historic district. What can make a Heights job longer is the work itself: finding mortar joints for cable at the front, routing through old cellars and plaster, firestopping in co-op risers, and preparing photos and equipment sheets for a Landmarks filing. Each of those appears as its own line on the written quote, so you can see where the time goes. Estimates are free and requests are booked within 2 hours.

Can AESS file the Landmarks application for our cameras, or does the owner have to?
+

The application belongs to the owner, or the architect if one is already working on the house, because it is the owner's building. What we do is prepare the material it needs: photographs of the facade and each proposed location, a marked drawing showing where every camera and any visible conduit goes, and the manufacturer's sheets for each camera and mount. Most owners find that is the hard part. If Commission staff have questions about the equipment, we can explain the choices to the owner or architect so they can answer.

If Landmarks staff ask us to move a proposed camera after we file, does the quote change?
+

Only if the new position needs different work. Moving a camera a short distance along the underside of the stoop usually changes nothing. Moving it from the front to the rear, or to a spot that needs a longer cable run or a different mount, can change the labor or the parts, and we send a revised written quote before doing anything. We draw the first proposal to fit the guidebook's discreet locations so that changes are less likely, and nothing is ordered until the approval is in hand.

Masons are restoring the brownstone on our front this spring. Should the front cameras go up before they finish or after?
+

After the masonry work, but planned before it. If we know where the under-stoop and areaway cameras will go, the masons can leave a clean mortar joint at each point and we can pull the cable into the house while access is easy, then mount the cameras once the stone and joints are finished and cured. That avoids drilling into new repairs and avoids cameras being knocked or covered in dust during the work. The rear and interior cameras do not have to wait and can go in at any time.

If we take a camera down off our brownstone in ten years, will it leave permanent marks?
+

Not if it was mounted properly in the first place. We fix mounts and cable entries at mortar joints rather than through the brownstone or brick face, and mortar can be raked out and repointed so the old holes disappear. A camera drilled straight into the face of brownstone leaves holes that need a stone patch, which rarely matches perfectly. That reversibility is one reason the Landmarks guidebook asks for attachment at joints and plain masonry, and it is how we work on every front.

Our router is in the front parlor and the garden is behind two brick walls. Should we run a cable to the back or add a wireless access point?
+

For cameras, a cable is almost always the better answer. A wired camera gets its power and picture over one cable, does not depend on Wi-Fi getting through masonry, and records continuously without dropping. The cable usually runs along the cellar from front to back and comes out through the rear wall behind the camera. If you also want better Wi-Fi in the garden for phones, a wired access point at the back of the house can share the same route, so one run serves both.

If your board or managing agent needs names, a certificate of insurance or a scope letter before the visit, we send them in advance.

Yelp reviewers praise installers who leave no wire showing. On a landmark row house, how much cable has to show at all?
+

Usually none at the front, and at most a few inches. Where we can, the cable enters the wall at a mortar joint directly behind the camera, so it is hidden by the camera itself. Where that is not possible, the Landmarks guidebook allows only a short stretch of visible conduit, about ten inches, painted to match the wall, and we treat that as the outer limit rather than the target. Inside the house, cable runs through the cellar, closets and chases rather than across plaster or woodwork.

I rent a floor-through in a Hicks Street brownstone. What can I put up without asking the owner?
+

Generally, cameras inside your own apartment aimed at your own rooms and the inside of your own door. The stoop, the vestibule, the shared stairs, the garden and the outside of the building belong to the owner, and anything on the exterior also falls under the Landmarks rules, so those need the owner's written permission. Many owners are open to a shared system with a view of the street door for each tenant, so it is worth asking. We can set up a battery or plug-in camera inside your apartment that leaves no marks when you move.

St. George Tower became a co-op in 1984 inside a hotel built in stages. Why are cable routes in buildings like that so hard to predict?
+

Because the building was put together over decades for a different use. The Hotel St. George was built in sections from 1885 to 1930, and its parts became a co-op, student housing and other uses at different times, so risers, shafts and ceilings were added, closed off or reused along the way. Original plans may not match what is in the walls. We trace existing pathways before quoting, work with the building's staff and records, and price any opening of finished ceilings or walls as its own line on the written quote.
