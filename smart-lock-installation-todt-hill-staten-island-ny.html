The Neighborhood

Why Todt Hill Homes Need Smart Lock Installation Planned From the Gate Inward

Todt Hill is not a place where one smart deadbolt on the front door makes a house “keyless.” The Landmarks Preservation Commission's designation report for a house on Circle Road describes the hill as the highest of the serpentine hills running through Staten Island, with a summit of 409.8 feet — said to be the highest natural point on the eastern seaboard between Maine and Florida. The report also records how the hill was settled: wealthy families assembled large estates here, among them the Cromwells and the architect Ernest Flagg, and those estates were later broken up into building lots. What sits on those lots today is mostly large detached houses, reached by private or semi-private roads, many behind a vehicle gate.

The roads themselves set the tone. Writing about the neighbourhood for Brick Underground, a local broker noted that most streets on Todt Hill have neither sidewalks nor street lights, and described many properties sitting behind “gargantuan cast iron gates.” In practice that means nearly everyone who comes to a Todt Hill house — family, guests, staff, contractors, couriers — arrives by car, at a gate or the top of a driveway, often after dark on an unlit road. The first lock decision on these properties is therefore not the front door. It's how a person gets from the road to the house at all.

Then there is the land. Section 105-941 of the New York City Zoning Resolution places Emerson Hill, Dongan Hills, Todt Hill and Lighthouse Hill in Special Natural Area District-1, describing the “central, serpentine, hilly spine of Staten Island” as “richly endowed with steep slopes, rock outcrops, erratic boulders and ponds” and many trees. Lots are big, steep and wooded. Houses step down the slope, so a garage may sit a full storey below the front door, a walk-out lower level may open onto a terrace, and a carriage house or pool house may be a hundred feet from the router. Trenching a cable across that ground to a gate is not a casual decision, and a radio signal crossing it has trees, rock and grade in the way.

The houses are as varied as the slope. Ernest Flagg's fieldstone mansion, Stone Court, and the small stone cottages he built on its grounds are individual city landmarks along Flagg Place. A Greek Revival house built in Massachusetts in the 1840s was moved here in 1931 and is now a landmark on Circle Road. Around them are pre-war Colonial and Tudor-style houses, mid-century houses on curving private roads, and newer custom houses with tall, heavy entry doors. The doors on those houses are where most of the technical problems sit: solid hardwood slabs thicker than most smart deadbolts are listed for, mortise locksets where one handle works both the latch and the bolt, multipoint locks on French doors, pivot doors with no conventional deadbolt at all.

And the people are many. A large household on the hill may include parents, children, grandparents, a housekeeper, a nanny, a landscaping crew, a pool company, a property manager, a dog walker and a private patrol — each needing a different door at a different time. The question that decides a Todt Hill smart lock plan is less “which lock?” than “who gets through which gate and which door, when, and how do we take it back?” We plan that list first and choose hardware to fit it.

Security pressure is real here too, and we'd rather describe it accurately than dramatise it. CBS New York reported a burglary pattern across Todt Hill, Emerson Hill and Grymes Hill in October 2011. In February 2026 the Borough President's office warned of a pattern in Todt Hill and Dongan Hills in which suspects were said to use crowbars on windows to get into homes and vehicles. None of that is stopped by a keypad on its own. What the locks can do is make sure no door is ever left unlocked by accident, tell you within seconds which door opened and with whose code, and refuse to be the weak point when the rest of the house is protected.

Mid-Century Houses on Private Roads

Ranches, split-levels and colonials on curving private roads, often with a two-car garage below the living floor, a mudroom door and sliding or French doors to a deck. Standard bores on many doors, but long distances between them.

New Custom Houses

Large recent builds with tall entry doors, pivot hinges, multipoint locks on terrace doors, oversized garages and pre-wired doorbells. Hardware here is often European or custom; we confirm what is behind the trim before recommending anything.

Houses Stepping Down the Slope

On steep lots in the natural area district, houses are built into the grade: the front door at road level, bedrooms below, a walk-out terrace at the back and a garage tucked under. Each level has its own door, its own weather and its own signal problem.

Gated Properties

Iron vehicle gates on masonry pillars, sometimes with a separate walk gate. Older installations often have a wired keypad or intercom on a post at the road. We work out what powers the gate and how it is told to open before connecting anything to it.

Outbuildings and Guest Quarters

Pool houses, cabanas, carriage houses, detached garages and guest suites over garages. Used by guests, staff and contractors, often far from the house and its Wi-Fi.

Door and hardware details we check on every Todt Hill property

  • Door thickness. Smart deadbolts are listed for a range of door thicknesses: Schlage's Encode Plus is listed for doors 1-3/8 to 1-3/4 inches thick, while Yale's Assure Lock 2 specification sheet covers doors from 1-3/8 to 2-1/4 inches. A custom hardwood entry door can sit outside the first range and inside the second, so we measure with callipers rather than guessing from the photo.
  • Lock body type. A bored cylindrical deadbolt, a mortise lock with a single body in the door edge, or a multipoint system with bolts or hooks top and bottom each need a different smart solution. We remove the inside trim to see which one is really there.
  • Backset and bore. Standard smart deadbolts expect a 2-3/8 or 2-3/4 inch backset and a 2-1/8 inch cross bore. Doors that have had several generations of hardware may have oversized or doubled bores that need filling or a reinforcing plate.
  • Weight and hinges. Heavy doors sag. A bolt that drags on the strike drains batteries and stalls motors, so hinge screws go into the framing and the strike is set so the bolt throws freely with no hand on the door.
  • Glass and sidelights. Leaded glass, sidelights and French-door glazing near the inside thumb-turn change where the lock can sit and how it should be protected.
  • Exposure. On the upper slopes doors can face wind-driven rain with no shelter. We check overhangs, which way the door faces and whether the keypad will sit in running water.

Your Questions Answered

Smart Lock Installation in Todt Hill: Questions Answered

These are the questions that come up on walkthroughs on the hill and that the generic smart-lock guides skip. They are grouped by the part of the property they really concern: the gate and the drive, the heavy doors, the reach of the network, the people who work at the house, and the break-in pattern residents have been warned about.

The gate and the driveway

Can a guest's code open the driveway gate and the front door, or do they need two?

It can be either, and the choice is worth making on purpose. If the gate keypad and the house locks come from systems that can share users — or if the gate keypad can be enrolled in the same app as the locks — one personal code can be set to work at both. That is convenient for grandparents and close family who arrive often. Where the gate keypad is a stand-alone unit wired to the gate operator, it keeps its own code list and the house keeps another.

For most households we recommend a middle path. Family and long-term staff get one code that works at the gate and on the specific house doors they use. Couriers, the pool company and one-off contractors get a gate-only code, or a code that works at the gate and on one outbuilding, never on the house. A guest staying the weekend gets a time-limited code on the gate and the guest door that expires when they leave.

Whatever we set up, it goes onto the written access list we leave with you, so years from now you can still see at a glance which codes open the gate, which open the house, and which do both.

When nobody is home, how should a courier or guest get from the closed gate to the front door?

Decide first whether they should get past the gate at all. For parcels, many households here prefer a locked parcel box or a delivery shelf at the walk gate, so nobody needs a code; the drive stays shut and the house is never involved. For a guest arriving before you, a time-limited code at the gate plus a code on one house door — usually the guest or service door, not the front door — is the cleanest arrangement.

The route matters on a dark, wooded lot. Most Todt Hill roads have no street lights, so a backlit gate keypad and some path lighting between the gate and the door make a bigger practical difference than any lock feature. If the door is at the top of a flight of stone steps, the keypad goes where someone carrying bags can reach it without leaning over a drop.

Where you want to see who is at the gate before letting them in, a camera or video intercom at the gate lets you open it remotely for a face you recognise. That is a gate and camera job rather than a door lock job, and we plan the two together so the codes and the remote-open permissions match.

If the power fails, does an electric gate stay locked or swing free, and how does the car get in?

That depends on how the gate is locked and powered, which is why we ask before connecting a keypad to it. A swing or slide gate operator usually holds the gate closed mechanically; many have a battery backup that allows a few cycles in an outage, and all should have a manual release for the owner. A walk gate held by an electric strike or magnetic lock is either fail-safe (it releases when power is lost) or fail-secure (it stays locked), and the choice is made when the hardware is chosen.

For a private house gate, fail-secure with a keyed or mechanical override is usually what owners want: the gate stays shut in a storm outage, and the family has a key or a manual release to get in. Anything that is also an escape route — a pool-area gate, a gate people leave a yard through — must still open from the inside with no power, no code and no key.

We write the outage plan into the handover: where the manual release is, who holds the override key, how long the backup battery lasts, and what the keypad does when power returns.

Heavy, unusual and high-value doors

Our front door has a mortise lockset where one handle works both the latch and the deadbolt — can a motor drive that?

Sometimes, and it depends entirely on what is inside the door. A mortise lock is a single box in the door edge; on many residential mortise handlesets the thumb-turn or key throws the deadbolt and the lever works the latch, and on some the inside lever retracts both at once. Most consumer smart deadbolts are made for bored cylindrical deadbolts and won't drive a mortise body directly.

The options are three. A retrofit motor that fits over the inside thumb-turn can work where the mortise deadbolt has a compatible spindle — August, for example, sold a mortise adapter kit for this. A purpose-built smart or electrified mortise lock can replace the body entirely, which keeps the look of a mortise handleset and puts the electronics inside the door. Or the mortise set stays as it is and the smart lock goes on a different, busier door, which is often the honest recommendation for a formal front door that is rarely used.

We take the inside trim off at the walkthrough to see the case, the backset and the spindle before anyone orders anything, because the right answer changes from one door to the next.

We have a tall pivot front door with no room for a standard deadbolt — what goes on it?

Pivot doors hang on pins top and bottom instead of side hinges, and many use long pull handles with a concealed or flush lock rather than a conventional deadbolt. A standard smart deadbolt usually doesn't fit because the stile is narrow, the lock sits low or in the floor or head, or the door's maker supplies its own lock body.

The best options usually come from the door's maker or from hardware designed for that door family: a motorised version of its own lock, a keypad or fingerprint reader built into the pull handle, or an electrified lock in the frame controlled by a separate keypad or reader. Where none exists, an electric strike or a concealed magnetic lock with a separate exterior reader can work, provided free exit on the inside stays one simple action.

Before quoting we look for the door's manufacturer label or paperwork, because fitting third-party hardware to a premium pivot door can affect the door's own warranty.

Can a smart lock take a high-security key cylinder so the override key can't be copied at a hardware counter?

Some can. The keyed override is the part of a smart lock most people forget about, yet anyone holding that key can open the door with no code and no log. On a large property where keys have passed through builders, previous owners and staff, a restricted or high-security key on the override is a sensible upgrade.

Some smart deadbolts accept their maker's restricted or interchangeable-core cylinders as an option; others take only their standard cylinder. We check the specific model before you buy, and where a lock can't take a better cylinder we may suggest one that can, or a model with no exterior key at all and a reliable battery-backup method instead.

Either way, the override keys go on the written access list: how many were cut, who holds them and where the spare is kept. A smart lock with five unaccounted-for override keys is not a controlled door.

Reaching every door on a large property

Our mesh Wi-Fi uses one network name for both bands — why won't the lock join, and what fixes it?

Many Wi-Fi smart locks and lock bridges connect only on 2.4 GHz — Schlage, for example, states that its Wi-Fi deadbolts require a 2.4 GHz network — while modern mesh systems often combine 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz under one network name and steer each device between them. During setup a phone sitting on 5 GHz may hand the lock a network it can't reach, and the pairing fails or the lock drops off later.

The fixes are routine. Most mesh systems let you create a separate 2.4 GHz-only network, often labelled for smart-home or IoT devices, which also keeps the locks apart from the household's laptops. Some let you pause band steering while devices pair. And on a big house, the more common real problem is distance: a lock at the far end of the house may be at the edge of a 2.4 GHz signal even when the phone shows full bars.

We set the locks up on whichever network suits your system, write down which one they're on, and test each lock from mobile data outside the house so we know remote access works when you're not on the Wi-Fi.

How many locks can one bridge or Thread network carry in a house with eight or nine doors?

The limit is usually distance rather than count. A Wi-Fi bridge for a Bluetooth lock is designed to sit close to its lock — August asks for its Connect bridge to be within 10 to 15 feet — so a house with locks at both ends and on outbuildings often needs more than one. Built-in Wi-Fi locks each talk to the router directly and simply need signal where they stand.

Thread works differently. It is a low-power, self-healing mesh, and Matter-over-Thread locks join it through a border router such as a recent smart speaker or hub. Yale's guidance is to keep the border router within about 50 feet of the lock, or 25 feet if there are problems. Mains-powered Thread devices around the house can strengthen the mesh, which helps on a long floor plan.

So on a large property we plan the network as a map: which locks are Wi-Fi, which use a bridge, which join Thread, where each bridge or border router lives, and which outbuildings need a long-range radio or an offline keypad lock. That map goes into the handover pack.

Should a voice assistant ever be allowed to unlock our doors?

Locking by voice is harmless and handy — “lock the front door” from the bedroom is one of the nicest features of a connected house. Unlocking by voice is a different matter, because a speaker can't tell who is talking, and in a large house with staff, guests and children the person giving the command may not be one you would hand a key.

The main assistants recognise this. Eufy's own setup guide notes that Google Assistant makes a four-digit security code mandatory for every voice unlock, and that Alexa may ask for a voice PIN before unlocking. That helps, but a spoken PIN can be overheard. Our default is to leave voice unlock switched off and use voice only for locking and status.

If you do want it, we enable it on as few doors as possible — never the gate or the front door — with a PIN only the adults know, and we check that no speaker sits where it can be heard from outside a window or door.

Staff, service companies and the alarm

Should staff use the service door rather than the front door — and can the locks enforce that?

On most large houses, yes, and the locks make it easy. Each code can be enrolled only on the doors that person should use, so the housekeeper's code opens the mudroom door and the gate but not the front door, the terrace doors or the carriage house. If a code is tried on a door it isn't enrolled on, it simply doesn't work.

This does more than keep the formal entrance formal. It means the busy door is the one with the best lock, the clearest log and the auto-lock, and the front door can be locked and left alone for days. It also makes the history easy to read: one door, a predictable set of people, at predictable times.

We agree the door-by-person plan with you on the walkthrough and write it into the access list, so when someone new joins the household staff, adding them is one decision rather than a reconsideration of the whole house.

Our private patrol checks the house while we travel — should the patrol company hold a code?

Only if you want them to go inside, and only on terms you set. Many households want a patrol to check the grounds and the outside of the doors, not to enter, in which case a gate code — or a gate code that works only during the patrol's hours — is all they need. If the patrol is expected to respond inside on an alarm, they need a code for one specific door, with its use reported to you every time.

Give the company its own named code rather than sharing a family code, so every entry shows as theirs. Ask how they store codes and who in the company can see them, and change the code when your contract ends or the company changes staff on your route.

We set the patrol code with whatever schedule you agree, enable notifications for it, and make sure it can be switched off from your phone in seconds when you are home again.

Should unlocking the mudroom door also disarm the alarm?

It can, and for the family it is convenient. Some alarm systems link to compatible Z-Wave locks so that a code entered at the lock also disarms the system; Ring's alarm, for example, does this with compatible locks, but only codes created in the Ring app work for it, and the feature needs a subscription.

The trade-off is control. If every staff code also disarms the alarm, the alarm stops telling you anything when staff come in, and a code that leaks becomes both a key and an alarm code. Our usual recommendation is to link disarming only to the family's codes, and let staff and service codes open the door while the alarm expects its own code or is handled on a schedule.

We check your alarm panel and provider before promising any link, because not every panel speaks to every lock, and some links can only be set up by the alarm company.

After the break-in warnings

Thieves on the hill have pried at back doors with crowbars — will a smart lock warn us if a door is forced?

A lock alone often won't. A smart deadbolt knows when it was locked or unlocked and by which code, but if someone pries a door open with the bolt still thrown, the lock may report nothing more than “locked”. What catches forced entry is a door-position sensor — built into some locks, or as a separate contact on the frame — that reports the door opening without a matching unlock, ideally tied into an alarm.

The physical side matters as much. The February 2026 warnings describe crowbars used on windows and a back door. A deadbolt is only as strong as the strike and frame it throws into, so we fit reinforced strikes with long screws into the framing, check that the door fits tightly in its frame, and look at the glass beside the lock.

If the walkthrough shows that the weak point on a door is the glass or the frame rather than the lock, we say so plainly and point you to the right fix — sensors, glazing, lighting or cameras — rather than selling a lock as the answer.

Where should the car key fobs live now that break-ins here have targeted them?

Not in a bowl by the door. Hoodline's account of the February 2026 burglaries describes a Range Rover key fob being taken from a house and the car then stolen; the Borough President's office urged residents the same week to take keys out of vehicles and lock them. A fob within reach of the busiest entrance is a gift to anyone who gets a door open for thirty seconds.

Keep fobs well inside the house, away from outside walls and doors, and consider a signal-blocking pouch or box if your cars use keyless entry. Make sure the garage-to-house door locks itself, so a car or garage left open doesn't become an open route into the house.

The smart locks help indirectly: with auto-lock on the service and garage doors and an alert when a door is left open, the window in which someone could walk in and pick up a fob gets much shorter.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Smart Lock Installation in Todt Hill

We couldn't capture Google's AI Overview text from our build environment, so the claims below are the ones national cost sites and manufacturers publish and AI summaries tend to repeat. Each is set against what we find on properties up on the ridge.

“Smart lock installation runs about $300”

The figure comes from national cost guides. Fixr's page, last updated in January 2025, gives $250 to $400 nationally with an average of $300 for a Wi-Fi lock and one accessory; Homewyse's per-lock estimate is lower still. These are honest averages for one lock on one ordinary door.

A Todt Hill job is almost never that. It is usually a gate keypad, four to eight house doors of different construction, one or two outbuildings, a network plan to reach them, and a list of a dozen people with different rights. The front door alone may be a mortise or pivot door that no $300 average contemplates.

So we price the property, not the lock: one flat quote after a free walkthrough of the gate, the house and the outbuildings. Where AESS publishes figures — keypad or reader with electric strike, strike work, setup-only visits — they are in the pricing table below at the Staten Island rate.

“Any smart deadbolt fits any standard door”

Manufacturers publish the doors their locks fit, and the ranges differ. Schlage lists its Encode Plus for doors 1-3/8 to 1-3/4 inches thick; Yale's Assure Lock 2 sheet runs to 2-1/4 inches. Both expect a bored deadbolt with a standard backset.

Custom and period doors on the hill regularly fall outside the first range, and many aren't bored for a deadbolt at all: they have mortise bodies, multipoint gear or pivot hardware. “Standard” is the exception on a hand-built front door, not the rule.

We measure thickness, backset and lock type at the walkthrough and recommend only locks whose published door range includes your door — or tell you it needs a different kind of hardware altogether.

“Your Wi-Fi will reach the lock”

Setup guides assume a router within sensible range. August asks for its bridge to sit 10 to 15 feet from the lock and to show full Wi-Fi bars where it's placed; Yale suggests a Thread border router within about 50 feet. Those numbers are easy in a compact house.

They're harder in a large house built into a slope, with stone walls, a garage a floor below, and a pool house across a wooded yard. Stone, grade and trees all stand between the router and the far doors, and the lock that keeps dropping offline is almost always the one at the edge of the signal.

We test every door and outbuilding with a phone before choosing how each lock connects, and we place bridges, border routers or mesh points where the test says they're needed rather than where the box suggests.

“A smart lock keeps burglars out”

A smart lock is a good lock that never gets left open by accident and that records who used it. That's genuinely valuable. It isn't armour, and the burglary reports from the hill show why.

The February 2026 warnings for Todt Hill and Dongan Hills describe crowbars used on windows and a back door; a 2022 report described a stone thrown through back-door glass. In both cases the lock wasn't the part of the house that was attacked.

We fit locks with reinforced strikes, recommend door-position sensors so a forced door is reported, and say plainly when a door's real weakness is glass, the frame or the dark — problems for sensors, glazing, lighting and cameras, not for a lock.

“A gate keypad is just a bigger door lock”

Consumer guides often treat the gate as one more lock to add to the app. On a real driveway it's a separate system: a gate operator with its own controls, power, safety devices and manual release, or a walk gate with a strike or magnetic lock and its own power supply.

That means a gate keypad has to be compatible with the operator or strike it controls, must behave predictably in a power cut, and needs to be weather-rated, lit and mounted where a driver can reach it without getting out of the car. If it's also a way out of a yard, it must open from the inside without power or a code.

We price gate work from AESS's published single-door keypad-and-strike figure rather than as a residential lock, and we check the operator's paperwork before connecting anything to it.

“Landmarked houses can't have smart locks”

Owners of landmark houses are sometimes told the hardware can't be touched. The city's own guidance is more nuanced: owners of a landmark need a Landmarks Preservation Commission permit before doing work, a Permit for Minor Work covers changes to significant protected features such as replacing doors, and simple repairs and maintenance don't need review at all.

For an inside-only retrofit, the door's protected exterior doesn't change; for a new exterior keypad or replacement hardware on a protected facade, the LPC is the authority to ask, and its decision — not ours — is the one that counts.

On a landmark house we start with the least visible option, help you describe the work if you choose to apply, and don't book exterior changes until you've confirmed what the Commission requires.

“Unlock by voice for hands-free convenience”

Smart-lock marketing often shows a homeowner unlocking the door by asking a speaker. The assistants themselves are more cautious: Google requires a security code for every voice unlock, and Alexa may ask for a voice PIN.

In a house with staff, visiting contractors and children, a spoken unlock PIN is a shared secret said out loud. It's also a route around the careful per-person, per-door code plan that makes a large-house system worth having.

We set voice up for locking and status by default, and enable voice unlock only on doors and for people you specifically choose, never on the gate or the main entrance.

Landmark Doors

Smart Locks on Landmarked and Historic Houses on the Hill

Todt Hill has an unusual number of individually designated houses for a neighbourhood of private homes. Ernest Flagg's Stone Court on Flagg Place, with its fieldstone mansion, gatehouse and gate, is one; several of the small stone cottages Flagg built on the estate, such as Wallcot at 285 Flagg Place, are others. The Gillett-Tyler House at 103 Circle Road joined the list in 2007. Further down the ridge, the Vanderbilt mausoleum and parts of the Moravian Cemetery were designated in 2016.

For an owner, designation changes the order of operations on a front door. New York City's business portal summarises the rule: owners of a landmark or of a building in a historic district need a Landmarks Preservation Commission permit before doing any work. Work that changes significant protected features without needing a Department of Buildings permit — replacing windows or doors is the example given — falls under a Permit for Minor Work, which has no fee; interior work that needs a DOB permit gets a Certificate of No Effect; and simple repairs and maintenance, like repainting a door the same colour, need no review.

That gives a clear way to plan. We start with what changes nothing outside: an inside-only retrofit motor on an existing single-cylinder deadbolt, a smart lock on a rear or service door that isn't part of the protected facade, or a keypad at the gate rather than on the historic door. Where an owner wants new exterior hardware on a protected door, that is a question for the Commission, and we don't book it until the owner has its answer.

How we work on period doors

  • Keep the original hardware. Anything we remove from a historic door is labelled and returned to the owner, not discarded, so it can go back on later.
  • No new holes without a reason. We reuse existing bores and mortises wherever the new hardware allows, and fill old ones with matched wood rather than plates where they show.
  • Protect the finish. Painted and stained surfaces are masked before drilling, and screws go into sound wood, not old repairs.
  • Respect the stone. Where a bridge or keypad must be fixed to masonry, we use mortar joints rather than drilling the stone face.
  • Write it down. The handover pack notes what was changed on each historic door, which is useful if the Commission or a future owner asks.
The Ridge

Gate Keypads on the Serpentine Ridge: Cable, Slope and the Natural Area District

A gate keypad needs power and, unless it is wireless, a cable back to the gate operator or the house. On a flat suburban lot that is a short trench beside the drive. On Todt Hill it may mean crossing a steep, wooded slope with rock just under the surface, and the land itself is regulated.

Zoning Resolution §105-941 maps Emerson Hill, Dongan Hills, Todt Hill and Lighthouse Hill into Special Natural Area District-1, and the Department of City Planning explains that the district, created in 1975, protects rock outcrops, trees and forests, wetlands and water features, and steep slopes, with City Planning Commission authorisation needed when an applicant seeks to modify a significant natural feature. A cable trench is small work, and we are not the authority on whether a given trench needs anything; but it is exactly the kind of question an owner on the hill should settle before anyone digs near a mature tree or an outcrop.

So we design gate work to disturb as little as possible. Where the gate operator already has power at the pillar, the keypad usually wires to it there with no new trench at all. Where a separate keypad post is needed, we look for an existing conduit, a path already cut for lighting or irrigation, or a wireless keypad that talks to a receiver at the operator. And where a new run is unavoidable, we route it along the drive's edge, clear of tree roots and rock, and tell the owner beforehand so they can check with their architect, landscape designer or the Department of City Planning if the lot is subject to an authorisation.

What we check at the gate

  • Operator and power. The make of the gate operator, its control inputs, whether it has battery backup and where its manual release is.
  • Driver reach. The keypad height and set-back from the drive, so a driver can reach it from the car window without getting out on a slope.
  • Light and weather. A backlit keypad, a hood or shelter where rain runs down the pillar, and lighting on a road without street lights.
  • Free exit. For walk gates and any gate people leave through, release from the inside with no code, key or power.
  • Ground. Existing conduits, trees, rock and grade along any route a cable would take.

Field Notes

Field Notes on Doors Along the Ridge

How our installers approach a large property on the hill — general working practice, not a story about any particular customer or house.

We park at the gate, not at the door. The first thing we want to know is how a person who isn't family gets onto the property: what the gate keypad does, who knows its code, whether anybody remembers who installed it. On older properties the honest answer is often that nobody does, and the gate code has been the same through two owners.

Then we walk the house the way the household lives in it, not the way the architect drew it. The formal front door is often the quietest door on the property. The door by the garage, where children, staff and shopping come in, is the one with the worn threshold, and it is the door that most needs the best lock. We take the inside trim off the front door anyway, because a heavy entry door can hide a mortise case, a multipoint gearbox or a pivot lock, and that discovery changes the whole hardware list.

Last, we stand at the far doors with a phone: the terrace door on the lower level, the pool house across the lawn, the carriage house down the drive. Stone, slope and trees eat signal, and a lock that only works when you're standing beside it isn't a smart lock. Whatever we find, the plan we leave has three parts on paper — the people, the doors and the network — and on every door, getting out still takes one turn of the hand.

FAQ

Todt Hill Smart Lock Installation FAQ

Does a big house on the hill cost more per door than a smaller house elsewhere on the island?
+

Not per se. Todt Hill is priced at the same Staten Island rate as the rest of the borough, with no neighbourhood surcharge. What changes the quote is the doors themselves: a mortise, multipoint, pivot or extra-thick door takes more hardware and time than a standard bored door, and far-away doors may need a bridge or border router. Several similar doors done on one visit are usually cheaper per door than one door done alone.

How far ahead should we book when the property has eight or more doors and a gate?
+

The walkthrough can usually happen within a few days. The installation date depends on the hardware: standard smart deadbolts are often available quickly, while mortise, multipoint, pivot-door or gate hardware may need to be ordered for your exact doors. We give you a realistic date with the written quote, and we can phase the work so the busiest doors go keyless first.

Our road is private and gated — what do you need from us so the van reaches the house?
+

Just a way through the gate on the day and a note of any road or association rules about contractor vehicles and hours. A temporary gate code that expires at the end of the visit is ideal, and it doubles as a first test of how the gate keypad handles time-limited codes. If someone has to meet us at the gate instead, let us know when we book.

Can the pool house or cabana get a keypad so guests can use it without going through the main house?
+

Yes. A keypad lock on the pool house lets guests reach the changing room and bathroom with their own code while the main house stays locked. Because pool houses are often far from the router, we choose a lock that keeps its codes offline or connects by bridge or long-range radio after a signal test, and we can schedule the codes for the swimming season only.

Can our children unlock with a fingerprint instead of remembering a code?
+

On locks with a fingerprint reader, yes. We enrol two fingers per child where the lock allows, so a cut or a cold hand doesn't lock them out, and we give each child a backup code as well. Small fingers and growing hands can read less reliably, so we test each enrolment at the door with the child before we leave and show you how to re-enrol later.

Is there a way to pause staff and service codes while we are away and turn them back on when we return?
+

Most lock apps let you disable or schedule individual codes, and some have a holiday or privacy mode that suspends all codes except the owners'. We set the household up so pausing every non-family code takes a few taps, and leave the patrol or house-sitter code active if you want someone checking the property while you travel.

Will fitting a lock mark or scratch a stained hardwood door?
+

Not if the work is done carefully. We mask the face of the door around the work area, drill from both sides so the wood doesn't splinter, and protect the finish under the lock's escutcheons. Where old hardware leaves a mark or a hole the new lock doesn't cover, we tell you before we start and can fill and touch up wood, or suggest a finisher for a full refinish.

Can you take down an old keypad or intercom box that a previous owner left on the gate pillar?
+

Yes. We first work out what it still controls, because an old keypad is sometimes wired into the gate operator's open input. Then we either remove it and make the wiring safe, or replace it with a new keypad on the same cable. Either way, any codes it held stop working, which is often the main reason owners ask.

What do you leave behind so a house manager can run the system without calling us?
+

A written handover pack: the access list by person and door, the network map showing how each lock connects, the model and battery type of every lock, the outage plan for the gate, and short instructions for adding, scheduling and removing codes. We set the house manager up with the permissions you choose, never owner-level control unless you want that, and walk them through each task once.
