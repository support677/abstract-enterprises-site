Garden apartments and small buildings

How the method changes from house to house

Vinyl and aluminum siding. On a house clad in vinyl or aluminum siding, a camera screwed straight through siding pulls the panel out of line and gives water a path behind it, so outdoor cameras go on a mounting block or a junction box that sits flat on the siding and is fastened through to the sheathing. Cable enters behind the block, so the hole in the wall is covered by the mount itself.

Brick fronts and shared walls. Brick-front semi-attached houses get their exterior holes in a mortar joint, angled slightly down to the outside and sealed. The party wall shared with the neighbor is never drilled. On attached rows we route cable through the attic to the front and rear eaves rather than along the facade, because a run of surface cable across a shared front is the first thing a neighbor objects to.

Flood elevation. New York City’s flood-resistant construction rules define a design flood elevation for houses in mapped flood zones — the base flood elevation plus one or two feet of extra height, depending on the building’s category, under an emergency rule the Department of Buildings issued on January 31, 2013 — and require a house’s electrical equipment to sit at or above it. We use the same line for the recorder, the PoE switch and any battery backup. If you do not know your elevation, the city’s flood maps and your elevation certificate (if you have one) tell us; if neither exists, we go by where the water reached last time and stay well above it.

Attics and garages. Many Staten Island houses have an unfinished attic over the top floor and a garage ceiling under the bottom one. Those two spaces are where camera cable should travel: out of sight, protected, and easy to reach later. We avoid stapling cable across a basement ceiling in a flood zone when an attic route exists.

Who may be recorded. Inside a two-family house, the cameras watch the shared entrance, stairs and the owner’s own spaces, not the tenant’s unit. Outside, they watch your property. New York’s unlawful-surveillance law (Penal Law §250.45) and General Business Law §395-b cover cameras aimed at places where people expect privacy, and we design well clear of both.

Rules Staten Island shops and landlords should keep in view

  • Licensing. A contractor who puts in cameras for paying customers needs a state security-installer license under General Business Law §69-m. A shop owner whose own staff wire the owner’s own store does not.
  • Permits. NYC Administrative Code §28-105.4.7 exempts low-voltage signaling and data wiring under 50 volts from the electrical permit requirement when a licensed master electrician, special electrician or qualified person does the work. New outlets for the recorder are regular electrical work, and certain hazardous locations, including some areas inside commercial garages, are excluded from the exemption.
  • Back rooms. Under Labor Law §203-c no camera may record employees in a staff toilet, a locker room or a changing room; we stop coverage at those doors.
  • Face data. NYC Administrative Code §22-1202 requires stores and restaurants that collect customers’ biometric identifier information to post a sign at every customer entrance. Standard Uniview recording does not do that; face-recognition features change the answer, and we leave them off unless you decide otherwise with that rule in mind.
  • Flood zones. A shop in a mapped flood zone falls under Building Code Appendix G too; the Electrical Code lets non-residential buildings keep service equipment below the flood elevation only where approved dry floodproofing is provided, so in practice the recorder still goes up.
  • Grants and contracts. If a federal grant or a government contract is paying for the system, read the camera clause in that paperwork with whoever advises you before picking a brand. A store paying with its own money has no such clause to satisfy.

This list is a working checklist, not legal advice; when a lease or a lawyer says otherwise, the lease or the lawyer wins.

Uniview Camera Installation in Staten Island: Questions Answered

Floods, Raised Houses and Where the Recorder Lives

Does the city’s flood code say anything about where camera equipment can go in a flood-zone house?

Not by name, but the rule for a house’s own electrical gear points the same way. Appendix G of the New York City Building Code requires mechanical, plumbing and electrical systems in a flood hazard area to be elevated to or above the design flood elevation, with narrow exceptions, and the city’s Electrical Code places panelboards, meters and service disconnects at or above that line as well. A camera recorder, its PoE switch and its battery backup are not listed in those sections. They are, however, exactly the kind of equipment those rules are written to keep dry, and we apply the same line to them on every flood-zone job.

In practice that means a hall closet, a high shelf in a spare bedroom, or the top of a utility room on the main floor. Cables from the cameras come up through the attic or a wall cavity instead of all collecting in the basement. When the only workable cable path runs through the cellar, the cable can pass through it — a wet cable can be replaced in an afternoon — but the recorder does not stay there.

If the basement floods anyway, is the footage on the recorder simply gone?

If the recorder itself goes under water, assume the drive is lost. That is the strongest reason to move it, but there are two more layers worth having on a flood-zone block. First, many Uniview cameras accept their own memory card — the EC-T4F28DL turret on our price list takes a microSD card of up to 512 GB according to its datasheet — so each camera can keep a short local copy that does not depend on the recorder. Second, clips you know you will need, such as a car being tried at night, should be exported to a phone or a USB stick as soon as you see them, not weeks later.

What we do not promise is an off-site copy of everything. The EZCloud account behind the Uniview apps is what lets your phone reach the recorder and receive alerts; we do not design a Staten Island system on the assumption that continuous video is stored anywhere but the recorder. If a client needs a full off-site copy, we discuss a second recorder at another address or a separate storage service, and price it as its own line.

Our house was raised under Build It Back — how do the cameras cover the stairs and the open space under the house?

Plan for three views instead of one. A raised house usually has a long exterior stair or ramp to the front door, an open or enclosed area under the living floor, and a driveway beside or beneath it. One camera at the top of the stair, aimed down it, gives a clean face at the door; a second under the house, mounted to the underside of the floor framing, watches the open space and anything stored there; a third covers the driveway from the eave. The city’s Build It Back program served 1,982 Staten Island homeowners with construction, so this layout is common along the East and South Shore.

The details that change on a raised house are the cable path and the mounting surface. Cable to the under-house camera runs inside the floor framing, not draped down a pile where it can be snagged or washed. Anything mounted below the living floor is treated as if it will see water again: the camera is sealed, its connector is above the beam line, and its cable rises to the recorder rather than running along the ground.

Two-Family Houses, Shared Yards and the People Next Door

Each unit of our two-family has its own front door — does every door need its own camera?

Yes, one camera per entrance, and each household is alerted only about its own door. On many two-family houses the ground-floor unit and the upper unit have separate doors, often side by side on the stoop or one at the front and one down the side of the house. A single wide camera covering both doors shows that someone came up the steps but not which bell they rang or who answered. Two small turrets, or a video doorbell at each door, give each door its own record.

Which camera goes at which door depends on the door. Where there is a wired bell button and a frame wide enough, a video doorbell gives a face-height view and lets the visitor be answered; where the doors sit under a shared porch roof, a small turret above each one, angled down at the top step, does the same job without new bell wiring. Both feed the one recorder in the owner’s unit, so a single timeline shows who came to either door and when.

Our tenant upstairs wants a copy of yesterday’s clip after a package went missing — how do we share it without handing over the recorder?

Export the clip yourself and send the file; never share the admin login. On a Uniview recorder the owner finds the time on the playback bar, marks the start and end of the few minutes that matter, and saves them to a USB stick or straight to the phone through the app. That file can be texted, emailed or handed to the precinct, and it contains only those minutes.

Two habits keep this simple in a two-family house. First, decide with the tenant up front which camera views they may ask for — usually the shared front door and the stoop — so a request is routine, not a negotiation. Second, keep a short note of what was exported, when and for whom. If a dispute ever reaches a landlord-tenant court or an insurer, a dated note beside a clean export is worth more than a screenshot of a phone.

Can one recorder serve both halves of a semi-attached pair if the two owners split the cost?

It can, but we usually recommend two small recorders instead of one shared one. A shared recorder has to live in one of the two houses, one owner holds its admin password, and every disagreement about footage becomes a disagreement between neighbors. Two four-channel recorders, one per house, cost little more and leave each owner in charge of their own video.

If the owners still want one system — for a shared driveway between the two halves, for example — we put the agreement in writing first: who holds the admin password, which cameras each owner sees, and how a clip is shared when one of them needs it. The recorder then goes in the house with the drier, more accessible closet, and the other owner gets a viewing account.

Our house sits well above the street with a long flight of outdoor steps — how do cameras cover the steps and the sidewalk below?

Use two cameras at two heights. A camera at the top of the steps, under the porch roof or on the wall beside the door, looks down the flight and catches each person’s face as they climb toward it. A second camera lower down — on the retaining wall, the garage or a post near the street — covers the sidewalk and the parked cars that the top camera sees only as rooftops.

Steep lots also change the cable path. The lower camera usually needs its cable brought up inside the garage or along the retaining wall in outdoor conduit rather than straight up the face of the slope. We plan that route at the walk-through, because it decides whether the lower camera is a one-hour job or a half day.

Driveways, Curbs and Cars Left Outside

Should a driveway camera keep its white light on all night, or stay dark until something moves?

Dark until something moves, in almost every case on Staten Island. Dual-light Uniview cameras such as the EC-T4F28DL can record in infrared, which nobody sees, and switch to their warm white light when they detect a person, so the picture turns to color exactly when it matters. A light that burns all night on a semi-attached block shines into the bedroom of the house ten feet away and gets the camera turned around by an unhappy neighbor.

The switch-on also works as a warning. Someone walking up a driveway at night who is suddenly lit up knows they are being recorded. With car thefts on the island up roughly 47% through July 2026, according to the Staten Island Advance, and ABC7 New York showing a car taken from a driveway here in under fifteen seconds, a light that comes on before the door handle is touched is the most useful few seconds the system provides. We set the trigger zone on the driveway itself, not the street, so passing cars do not set it off.

Where should a driveway camera go on a high-ranch with the garage under the living room?

Under the eave above the garage door, offset to one side and aimed down the driveway toward the street, with a second, narrower view at the front door. On a high-ranch the eave is high enough to put the camera out of reach but low enough that a 4 MP camera still resolves a person at the bottom of the drive. Mounting right above the garage door centre puts the door opener’s light and any headlights straight into the lens, so we move it off-centre.

Inside, the garage is tempting for the recorder because power and the network are often there. On a low-lying lot it is the wrong place for the same reason the basement is: it is the first floor to take water. We run the driveway cable through the garage ceiling and up to the recorder in the living space above.

We park at the curb, not in a driveway — can a camera on the house really cover the car out past the sidewalk?

Yes, if the camera is aimed down at a narrow strip rather than out at the street. Mounted under the front eave, a camera can take in the sidewalk and the one parking spot in front of your house; whatever falls into the top of the frame across the street is blacked out with a privacy mask. You get a close view of your own car and the sidewalk beside it.

Curb parking changes the lens choice. A 2.8 mm lens shows a wide area but makes a car twenty feet away small; for a single curb spot a slightly longer lens, or an 8 MP camera like the IPC3618LB-ADF28K-DL on our price list, keeps more detail. We take a test frame from the actual mounting spot before tightening anything, so you see the curb view before we drill the second hole.

A recorder sitting on the shelf under the register goes out the door with the cash drawer, and with it the only record of the night.

The better places in a small shop are a locked office or stock-room cabinet, a ceiling space above a drop tile, or a metal lock box fixed to the wall of a back room, with its own outlet and a small battery backup. The cameras stay where they need to be — inside the front door, over the register and outside on the sidewalk — and only their cables lead to the hidden box. Phone alerts for the hours the store is closed mean the owner often learns about a break-in within minutes, while the cameras are still recording.

Should the register camera look down at the counter or out at the customer?

Both, from two cameras, if the budget allows; if it allows one, look out at the customer from behind and above the cashier’s shoulder. A camera straight above the counter records cash drawers and hands well, which answers till disputes, but it sees only the top of a customer’s head. A camera behind the cashier, slightly above head height, records the face of everyone who comes to the counter, which is what the police ask for.

New York Labor Law §203-c forbids employers from video recording employees in restrooms, locker rooms or rooms set aside for changing, so the back-of-house cameras stop at the stock room and office doors. Inside the selling floor and at the register there is no such limit, but tell your staff where the cameras are; employees who know the register is recorded rarely end up as the subject of a dispute.

Does New York City require an electrical permit to wire cameras into a Staten Island house?

Not for the camera cabling itself, provided the right person installs it. Section 28-105.4.7 of the city’s Administrative Code exempts electrical wiring that operates at less than 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data circuits from the electrical permit requirement, as long as the work is done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person as the Electrical Code defines it. Separately, New York State requires anyone in the business of installing security systems to be licensed by the Department of State; our license number is 12000287431.

A new 120-volt outlet is a different matter. If the closet chosen for the recorder has no outlet, adding one is regular electrical work that needs a licensed electrician and, in most cases, a permit. AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Uniview Camera Installation in Staten Island

AI summaries are stitched together from cost guides, dealer pages and forum posts. Our research tools could not record the actual AI Overview Google shows for these searches, so we went to those underlying pages instead, pulled out seven claims they repeat, and set each one against what a Staten Island house or shop actually needs.

Claim: installation costs about the same everywhere

National cost pages state averages with confidence. Angi says the national average for security camera installation in 2026 is $1,289, with most homeowners paying between roughly $590 and $2,040; other pages quote $80 to $200 per camera for labor alone. Those figures are real averages of real jobs, but they describe a mix of single doorbells, DIY kits with a technician’s afternoon, and full systems, all over the country.

On Staten Island the number that matters is what a specific house needs. Our published list puts a complete four-camera Uniview system, with the recorder, a 4 TB drive, mounting and up to 100 feet of cable per camera, at $2,524 installed. A two-family house with a camera at each unit’s door, the driveway, the yard and the stair usually lands on eight cameras at $4,440.

The gap between the national average and a Staten Island price is not markup; it is scope. An average that includes a single doorbell is not a price for a house with two entrances, a long driveway and a basement that cannot hold the recorder.

Claim: any weatherproof camera is fine in a flood zone

Product pages and AI summaries treat the IP67 label as if it answered the flood question. IP67 on a camera like the EC-T4F28DL means the housing is sealed against dust and protected against temporary immersion under test conditions. It says nothing about the recorder, the power supply, the PoE switch or the battery backup, none of which carry that rating.

On a Midland Beach or Oakwood block, the camera on the eave is rarely the part that fails in a flood. The part that fails is the box on the basement floor that all the cables run to. That is why every Staten Island quote we write names the room the recorder will live in.

The fix costs little: a closet on the first living floor, a cable path through the attic, and a dedicated outlet. It is far cheaper than replacing a recorder and losing the footage of the night the water came in.

Claim: Uniview is banned in the US

Some comparison pages state flatly that Uniview hardware is banned under the defense authorization act, alongside Hikvision and Dahua. The statute does not say that. Section 889 names Huawei, ZTE, Hytera, Hikvision and Dahua; Uniview is not among them, and as of the FCC’s October 16, 2026 update Uniview does not appear on the FCC Covered List either.

The real federal action is narrower and points the other way. Since December 11, 2024, American suppliers need a license to ship US-origin items to Zhejiang Uniview Technologies, because Commerce put the company on its Entity List. Nothing in that rule touches a Staten Island household or shop that buys a Uniview camera and screws it to the eave.

Rules can change, and some buyers — anyone spending federal money, for one — should check their own contracts. We put the status on every quote with the date we checked it, and we tell you if a different brand fits your situation better.

Claim: the default password is fine if the camera is on your own network

Many tutorials repeat that Uniview devices ship with the user name admin and the password 123456, and Uniview’s own guide confirms that most of its products do. Some go on to suggest that the default is harmless if the recorder sits behind a home router.

It is not harmless. Uniview has published security notices for its recorders and cameras, and security researchers have documented botnets probing older Uniview hardware for known weaknesses. A default password, old firmware and an open port on the router are the combination those attacks look for.

Every system we install gets a new admin password set by the owner, current firmware, and phone access through Uniview’s cloud service rather than open router ports. When we take over an existing system, those three checks are the first hour of the visit.

Claim: wireless cameras are easier for a house like this

AI answers often steer homeowners toward Wi-Fi cameras because there is no cable to run. For a single doorbell that can be true. For a Staten Island house with a driveway, a side yard and a detached garage, wireless brings its own problems: batteries to charge, Wi-Fi that does not reach the end of the yard through aluminum siding, and cameras that drop offline exactly when a storm knocks the router out.

A Uniview PoE camera takes one Cat6 cable that carries both power and video. In a typical house that cable runs through the attic or the garage ceiling, invisible from outside, and the camera keeps working as long as the recorder has power.

The labor of running cable is real, and it is in the price. It is also the reason the system still works in five years without anyone climbing a ladder to change a battery.

Claim: one camera covering the whole driveway is enough

Summaries tend to recommend a single wide-angle camera for a driveway. A wide view is good at answering whether something happened. It is poor at answering who did it, because a person at the far end of a wide frame is a few dozen pixels tall.

The local car-theft pattern makes the difference concrete. When a car is taken in under fifteen seconds, there is one moment worth having on video: the person standing at the driver’s door. A second camera at about head height, aimed at that spot, captures it.

That is why our Staten Island driveway layouts almost always use two cameras: one overview, one face camera. The second camera adds $449 on our list. It is the one that matters on the morning after.

Claim: the Uniview app you download today will be the app you use for years

Search results still point owners to EZView, Uniview’s long-standing phone app. Uniview announced in March 2024 that its new apps, UNV-Link for end users and UNV-Link Pro for installers, will replace the EZView service, and that EZView is expected to stop its service in 2027.

For someone who checks the cameras on the ferry every morning, that is not trivia. A system set up on EZView today will need its phones moved to UNV-Link before then, and the move goes smoothly only if the cloud account is in the owner’s name and the owner knows its password.

We set new systems up on UNV-Link from the start, with the account registered to you. When we take over an older system, moving the phones to the current app is part of the visit.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Carry the Recorder Upstairs

The easiest place to put a camera recorder in a Staten Island house is almost always the basement. The internet comes in there, the electrical panel is there, the cables from the front and the back of the house meet there, and nobody sees the box. On many East and South Shore blocks it is also the room where water stood in October 2012, and where a failed sump or a summer downpour still leaves a puddle.

So the planning question we ask at every walk-through is not which camera goes where. It is: where did the water reach? If the answer is anywhere near the basement floor, the recorder goes up a floor — into a hall closet, onto a high shelf in a spare room, into a cabinet in the living area — and the cables follow it through the attic or a wall cavity. It costs a little more cable and sometimes an outlet. It means the one box that holds every hour of recorded video is not the first thing to fail when the next storm comes in.

The cameras outside do not mind the weather. The recorder does. Everything else about a Staten Island install follows from keeping those two facts straight.

FAQ

Staten Island Uniview Camera Installation FAQ

Do both the upstairs and the downstairs households need to be home on installation day?
+

Not both, but we need a key or a person for every space a cable passes through. On a two-family house that usually means the basement, the stair hall and whichever unit holds the front-door wiring. We map the route at the walk-through and tell each household which rooms we need, for roughly how long, so the tenant upstairs is not waiting at home all day for a ten-minute visit to a closet.

How much dust and mess does the work leave inside the house?
+

Very little, because most of the drilling happens outside or in the attic. Where we do drill indoors, a vacuum is held at the bit and drop cloths go down under the ladder. Ceiling and wall openings are kept to the size of the cable, and any access hole we make in drywall is patched before we leave; painting a patch is the one thing we ask the owner to finish. At the end of the day the boxes, offcuts and packaging leave with us.

What should we do with the recorder when an evacuation order goes out for our zone?
+

Leave the cameras alone and deal with the recorder. If it already sits above the flood line, keep it running on its battery backup so you can watch the house from wherever you go. If it is still in a basement, unplug it and carry it upstairs with its power supply; the drive holds everything recorded so far. Export any footage you already know you will need to a USB stick before you leave, and take the stick with you.

After installation, can small changes such as a new login or a schedule be made without a visit?
+

Often, yes. If you have agreed to it, a technician can make small changes with you over the phone while you are logged in to the recorder: adding or removing a household member’s login, changing when alerts arrive, or redrawing a privacy mask. Anything that needs a ladder, a cable or a new camera needs a visit. We never keep standing remote access to your recorder; each session starts with you.

Will drilling through brick on our semi-attached house disturb the neighbor's side of the wall?
+

Not if the hole goes where it should. We do not drill into the shared party wall at all; exterior entries go through your own front, rear or side wall, in a mortar joint, angled slightly down toward the outside and sealed. Mounting screws for cameras near the property line go into your side of the facade only. If the only sensible cable route crosses into the neighbor's half, we stop and you talk to them first.

Can a camera keep an eye on a boat or trailer parked in the side yard?
+

Yes, and it is one of the easier views to get right because the boat does not move. A turret under the eave aimed down the side yard records anyone at the hitch or climbing the ladder, and a line-crossing rule drawn across the yard can alert your phone when someone steps past the gate at night. Point it along your yard, not across the fence into the house next door.

Do you need to log in to our home router, and what do you change there?
+

Usually not. The Uniview recorder runs its own camera network on its built-in PoE ports, so the cameras never touch your home Wi-Fi. The recorder needs one cable to your router for phone viewing through Uniview's cloud service, and that works without opening ports. If you want remote viewing without the cloud, we go through the router settings with you and write down exactly what was changed.

Can a camera watch the sump pump or the basement floor for water?
+

A camera can show you the basement floor on your phone, but it is not a flood alarm. Uniview's motion and intrusion rules are built for people and vehicles, not a slowly rising puddle. Pair the camera with a simple water sensor by the sump that sends its own alert, and use the camera to look and decide whether to come home. Keep that camera's cable and power above where the water reached last time.

Do outdoor camera lenses need cleaning, and how often?
+

A few times a year is enough for most houses. Spider webs are the usual problem: they catch the infrared light at night and fill the picture with moving white strands, which also sets off false motion alerts. Dust, pollen and road grime build up more slowly. A soft cloth and plain water are all a lens needs; avoid glass cleaner on the dome cover. If a camera is too high to reach safely, a cleaning visit is billed at the service rate.

Can a camera be mounted on a fence post at the back of the yard instead of on the house?
+

Yes, as long as the post is solid and a cable can reach it. A Uniview PoE camera gets power and video through one Cat6 cable, so no outlet is needed at the fence; the cable runs from the house along the fence line in outdoor-rated conduit or, on a longer run, in a shallow trench. A wobbly fence post makes a wobbly picture, so we fix the camera to a steel post or a set wooden post rather than to a fence panel.
