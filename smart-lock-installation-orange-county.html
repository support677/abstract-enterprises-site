Why Orange County Properties Need Smart Lock Installation at Every Handover

In Orange County the dangerous moment for a lock is not the burglary you read about. It is the handover nobody tracks. This is a county that keeps adding people and houses. USAFacts' reading of Census estimates shows Orange County growing by about 2,600 people between 2024 and 2025 and by 11.1 percent over the decade from 2015, with the population rising in 23 of the last 25 years. Census Reporter's profile of the latest American Community Survey puts the county at 411,767 residents. Every one of those new households moved into a door that somebody else held keys to the week before — a builder, a seller, a landlord, a property manager, an Army housing office. A smart lock job here starts with a list of everyone who could still open the door, and ends with that list reduced to the people who should.

New construction is the clearest case. The largest project in the county, Tuxedo Reserve, is described by the Orange County Partnership as a community of more than 1,600 units on roughly 1,200 acres between Harriman State Park and Sterling Forest, with Lennar and Related breaking ground, home sales from 2025 and rental leasing in 2026. Smaller builder communities are scattered across the county: Ridgebury Estates at Slate Hill, Grandview Estates at New Hampton, Palisades Ridge at Woodbury Junction in Central Valley, Blackburne Homes in Campbell Hall. During construction, a new house is opened by framers, electricians, painters, flooring crews, inspectors, the sales office and the punch-list team. Builders handle that with construction keys and, increasingly, keypad deadbolts. What matters to the buyer is whether those keys and codes were actually shut off at closing — and most buyers never find out.

The second group is military. The United States Military Academy at West Point sits on the county's eastern edge, and Stewart International Airport, in the Towns of Newburgh and New Windsor, is home to the New York Air National Guard's 105th Airlift Wing. Army families on post live in West Point Family Homes, which is managed by Balfour Beatty Communities and has written rules about locks: the housing office's community guidelines say residents may ask to alter or add locks only through a Request for Alterations, that an approved replacement lock "must be from the same manufacturer as the existing lock," and that Community Management must receive a copy of the new key. Off post, in Highland Falls, Cornwall, New Windsor and Washingtonville, those same families rent houses for a tour of a few years and then move on, which means the lock on the door is changing hands on an Army schedule.

The third group is rental property in the county's cities. Census Reporter's American Community Survey figures show that 65 percent of occupied homes in the City of Newburgh are renter-occupied and 64 percent of its housing is in multi-unit buildings — the brick two- and three-family houses and rowhouses that fill the East End Historic District, a district of 445 acres and 2,217 buildings listed on the National Register in 1985. Middletown is roughly half and half: 52 percent owner-occupied, 56 percent single-unit. Every lease that ends is a handover, and in New York a landlord who changes a tenant's entrance lock without supplying the occupant a key is breaking a statewide law, not just a city one. Keyless entry can make turnovers cleaner for owners and safer for the next tenant, but only if it is set up with the law in mind.

Finally, Orange County is a commuter county. Census Reporter shows a mean travel time to work of 35.3 minutes, above the state figure, and that average hides the households that drive Route 17 and the Thruway before dawn or catch the Port Jervis Line at Harriman, which Wikipedia's history of the line calls its busiest station. The Town of Newburgh Police Department's own burglary-prevention advice points out that a house that looks vacant is a target "during the work day when everyone may be at work or school." In a commuter household the door is often left to children coming off the school bus, a parent who lives alone during the week, or a contractor who needs to get in while nobody is home. Those are access decisions, and a smart lock lets you make them one person at a time.

Door and hardware details we check on every Orange County job

Builder hardware. New houses usually arrive with a matched set of entry hardware chosen for price. The deadbolt bore and strike are almost always standard, which makes a smart deadbolt a straightforward swap, but builder strikes are frequently fixed with short screws into the jamb alone. A motorised bolt that hits a strike held by short screws will loosen it within a season. We replace those screws with long ones that reach the framing, and we check the strike again after the house has been through its first heating season, when new framing dries and doors move most.

Settling and the first winter. A newly built house moves. Lumber dries, the foundation takes up load, and exterior doors that latched perfectly at the final walkthrough can start to bind or rub by February. A hand-turned deadbolt hides that; a smart deadbolt reports it as a jam and drains its batteries trying. On new construction we set the strike so the bolt clears it with room to spare, and we show owners what a misalignment looks like in the app so it gets fixed before it becomes a lockout.

Sliding patio doors. Ranches and new builds alike open onto a deck through a sliding door, and that door is where most smart lock plans have a gap. Most sliding-door locks are built into the handle and are not motorised. The Town of Newburgh police's vacation checklist still recommends making sure a "Charley bar" is secure on sliding doors before you leave. We fit a door contact or an extra sliding-door lock where the owner wants one, but we tell you plainly that the smart deadbolt on the front door does not protect the deck.

Rentals and borrowed doors. On an off-post rental, a landlord's house or anything on post, the door belongs to somebody else. We remove the existing cylinder or deadbolt intact, label it, and leave it with the tenant so it can go back in on move-out. Where the landlord or housing office hasn't agreed in writing, we don't drill, and on post we don't touch the door unless the alteration has been approved.

Landlords with a shared front hall usually ask about the street door next. Compliance callouts for Orange County business doors

  • Leaving never takes a credential. Codes, fobs and cards are for getting in. On any door people use to get out, the inside face carries only a lever, a panic bar, a push-to-exit plate or a motion sensor, so nobody has to remember anything in an emergency.
  • Decide what happens in a power cut. Every electrified door is set to release or to hold when the supply fails. A magnetic lock on an exit route must release; a strike on a stock-room door may reasonably hold. We write the choice into the quote for each door rather than leaving it to the factory default.
  • Line-voltage work is an electrician's. Orange County licenses electricians at county level; its 2024 local laws restate the county's Electrical Licensing Board, and the county's application says anyone wishing to work as an electrician in the county must hold a county-issued licence. Our low-voltage work stops at the power supply; a new 120-volt circuit or receptacle for it is done by a county-licensed electrician.
  • Written contracts. The county's consumer-law help manual for home improvements sets out what a written contract must contain on residential work above its dollar threshold, including a description of the work, the makes and models of materials, dates and a payment schedule. Our written quotes and service agreement are built to that standard.
  • Residents upstairs. Where a business shares a building with apartments, the residents' entrance follows landlord-tenant law, including the statewide rule against changing a tenant's entrance lock without supplying a key. Business credentials never go on the residents' door.

New houses and the builder's keys

Could the builder's construction keys still open the front door of our new house in Chester or Monroe?

Possibly, and you can't tell by looking. On many new homes the locks are set up so that one construction key opens every house on the street while it is being built. The intended practice is that the homeowner's key permanently disables the construction key the first time it is used, or that the builder swaps a temporary construction core for a permanent one at closing. A homeowner on Reddit's r/homeowners put the expectation simply: "New builds should be using construction cores and swapping out to an owners core that only you get the copies for." Whether that happened on your house depends on the builder, the lock brand and whoever handled the punch list.

Keypad deadbolts add a second layer of the same problem. A builder that fits keypads as standard has usually programmed them during construction — a code for the trades, a code for the sales office, sometimes a code for the realtor. Some of those may have been deleted at closing; some may not. Schlage's own support article on factory-resetting its Encode lock warns that a factory default reset "will remove all codes and virtual keys that have been programmed to your lock," which is exactly why a reset is the cleanest starting point for a new owner when the maker allows one.

On a closing-day handover we test every exterior door with any keys the builder gave you, reset or replace the keypads, rebuild the user list from your household only, and either rekey the mechanical cylinders or replace them with new ones. You get a written list of every door, what was on it when we arrived and what is on it when we leave. Garage doors from the house and sliding doors to the deck are on that list too, because they are the doors that get forgotten.

The house is still open framing — is anything worth running to the front door before the drywall goes up?

Yes, and it costs a fraction of what it costs later. A battery smart deadbolt doesn't need any wiring, so the lock itself can wait. What can't easily wait is the cable for the things that tend to follow it: a low-voltage pair or a network cable to the door for a wired video doorbell or a video intercom, a door-position contact in the hinge-side or head jamb so an alarm or hub knows whether the door is open or shut, and a cable path from the front door to wherever the router and network panel will live.

The decisions are simple to make on a framing walkthrough. Where will the doorbell camera mount, given the door, the sidelight and the porch light? Is there a chance a wired keypad and electric strike will ever go on a side or garage entry, for instance for a home business or a caregiver? Will the router be in a basement utility room two floors away from a townhome's front door? Each answer is a cable, pulled and labelled now, with a blank cover plate at the door.

Talk to your builder's site superintendent first: most builders allow owner-contracted low-voltage work during a defined window, and some insist their own contractor does it. Our structured cabling installation in Orange County handles the pre-wire, and the door hardware goes on after closing.

Will swapping the builder's lock for a smart lock affect our new-home warranty?

Read your builder's limited warranty, because that document is what sets the details. Underneath it, New York's General Business Law section 777-a gives buyers of new homes a housing merchant implied warranty that survives the closing: one year against defects from poor workmanship or defective materials, two years for the plumbing, electrical, heating, cooling and ventilation systems, and six years against material defects.

A deadbolt is door hardware. Replacing it doesn't alter the structure, the electrical system or the door slab, and on a pre-bored door a smart deadbolt goes into the same holes. Where a builder could reasonably push back is damage caused by the swap — an enlarged bore, a chiselled-out jamb, a cracked door skin — or a claim about the door itself that the builder blames on the new hardware. That's why we use the existing bore and strike pocket wherever the lock allows and photograph the door before and after.

Keep the original builder lockset, its keys and its packaging in the house. If a door warranty issue comes up during the first year, the builder's hardware can go back on for their technician, and if you ever sell, the buyer can choose.

Army households at West Point and Stewart

We live in on-post family housing at West Point — are we allowed to put a smart lock on our quarters?

Only through the housing office, and only on its terms. West Point Family Homes' community guidelines say residents "can request permission to alter existing locks or install additional locks by submitting a Request for Alterations form to Community Management." If the request is approved, "the replacement lock must be from the same manufacturer as the existing lock and the Community Management Office must receive a copy of the new key." In practice that rules out simply ordering any smart lock you like and fitting it; it means asking first, finding out which manufacturer's hardware is on the door, and choosing a smart model from that maker if one fits.

The same guidelines explain why the office wants a key: lost keys are reported to maintenance, the lock is changed and a $50 replacement fee is charged, and residents who get locked out can ask for help — the first business-hours lockout free, a second $25, and after-hours or weekend lockouts $50, with a resident on the lease present to show identification. A smart lock that maintenance can't open with a key breaks that whole system, which is why approval comes with conditions. The guidelines add that exterior cameras, including doorbell cameras, may only be aimed at the resident's own entry and yard, not at common areas or neighbours' homes.

We don't install on post without the approved alteration in hand. If you have it, send us the approval and a photo of the existing lock and its brand stamp, and we'll tell you whether a matching smart model exists and fits your door. If it doesn't, a doorbell camera aimed at your own entry and a keypad on a door the approval covers are often the better route.

Our off-post landlord in Cornwall won't allow any drilling — is there still a keyless option?

Usually, yes. The no-drill route is a retrofit unit: it replaces only the inside thumb-turn of the existing single-cylinder deadbolt, using the deadbolt's own mounting screws, and leaves the outside cylinder and the landlord's key exactly as they are. From the outside nothing changes; from the inside the bolt can be thrown by the motor when you use the app, an auto-lock timer or, with an add-on keypad, a code. When the tour ends, the original thumb-turn goes back on and the door is as you found it.

Two checks come first. The deadbolt has to be a single-cylinder type, with a thumb-turn inside rather than a key on both sides, and it has to throw smoothly by hand, because a motor can't turn a bolt that a person struggles with. And you still need the landlord's agreement that you may swap the thumb-turn, because the lease decides what tenants may change; a written yes by email is enough for most.

If even that is refused, the alternatives are things that don't touch the door at all: a doorbell camera on a no-drill mount where the landlord allows it, a key safe the landlord approves for a family member, and a plain conversation about whether the landlord would rather fit a keypad deadbolt themselves and keep it when you leave.

We'll only be at Stewart or West Point for two or three years — is a smart lock worth buying for such a short stay?

It depends less on the length of the stay than on how often the door changes hands while you're there. A household that has a sitter, a dog walker, family visiting for weeks at a time and a spouse away for training gets value from individual codes in the first month. A household of two people who both carry keys and are rarely away may not.

If you do buy one, buy it to move with you. A retrofit unit or a standard-bore smart deadbolt can be removed at move-out, the landlord's hardware put back, and the lock fitted again at the next duty station, provided that door takes the same type of deadbolt. Keep the box, the manual and the mounting parts together, and reset the lock before you pack it, so the next house starts with a clean user list.

On post the calculation is different, because the housing office decides what is allowed and wants a key. There, a doorbell camera aimed at your own entry, which the guidelines permit, may give you more of what you want than a lock you would have to get approved and then remove.

Selling and renting

We're selling our house in Warwick — should showings go through the realtor's lockbox or a temporary code on our smart lock?

Ask your listing agent first, because many agents and the multiple listing service have their own rules for how agents get access and how showings are recorded. Within those rules, a temporary code on a smart lock has real advantages for a seller. You can give the listing agent a code that only works between set hours, give each inspector or appraiser their own code for the day of their visit, and see in the app exactly when the door was opened and locked. When the house goes under contract, or the day after closing, the codes expire on their own.

A lockbox holds a physical key, and once a copy of that key exists you can't easily know where it goes. The weakness of a code is different: codes get shared. The answer is to keep them individual, time-limited and logged, and to look at the activity log after each showing. If the agent prefers the lockbox, the two can coexist — the lockbox for the agent, timed codes for everyone else.

Don't forget the handover at the end. Before closing, factory-reset the lock or delete every code and your own app account, leave the buyer the mechanical keys and the manual, and tell them the lock is reset. A buyer who inherits a smart lock with the seller's account still attached is the next owner who has to call us.

We own a dozen rental houses spread from Port Jervis to Newburgh — can one app run all the locks?

Yes, if you choose the hardware for it. Many consumer smart lock apps support several homes on one account, so a landlord can see every lock in one list, issue and cancel codes remotely and check battery levels from anywhere. Standardise on one brand and one model where the doors allow, connect each lock through Wi-Fi or a bridge so it can be managed remotely, and keep a spreadsheet of which code belongs to which tenant, contractor and cleaner at which address. That's what turns a turnover from a locksmith visit into a few minutes on a phone.

The law matters more than the app. New York's Real Property Actions and Proceedings Law section 768, added statewide by the Housing Stability and Tenant Protection Act of 2019, makes it unlawful to evict or try to evict an occupant who has lived in a unit for 30 consecutive days or longer, or who has a lease, by means that include "changing the lock on such entrance door without supplying the occupant with a key." The Attorney General's guidance to police treats unlawful eviction as a class A misdemeanor, and the section adds civil penalties of $1,000 to $10,000 per violation. Deleting a sitting tenant's code to force them out is the digital version of changing the lock. Codes come off when a tenant has actually moved out, or when a court says so.

Give each tenant both a code and a physical key, write into the lease how codes are issued, changed and removed, and tell tenants in writing what the lock records. We set the locks up that way across a portfolio, one visit per house, and leave you the register of what is on every door.

Businesses

Truck drivers check in at our Montgomery distribution building — how do we let them into the drivers' room without letting them into the warehouse?

Treat the drivers' room as its own small building with its own door. Many distribution buildings along I-84 and Route 17K give drivers a separate entrance to a room with a check-in window, a restroom and a waiting area, with a second door from that room into the warehouse. The outside door can be open during receiving hours or released by a keypad code that changes daily or weekly; the inner door into the warehouse takes a staff credential only, so a code that drivers know never opens the floor.

Use a reader or keypad with an electric strike on the inner door, not a standalone battery lock, so the door can be tied to the building's schedule and its alarm, and so a lost staff fob can be cancelled from a computer. Put a camera on both doors and keep the inner door's events in a log someone actually looks at. The exit side of both doors stays free — a lever or panic bar — because a drivers' room is still part of the building's way out.

If you're in a new speculative building, raise this with the landlord's fit-out team before the walls go up. A cable to each door and a power supply location agreed in advance is far cheaper than adding them to finished block walls.

Forklifts keep clipping the keypad beside our Wallkill dock door — where should it go instead?

Out of the path of the equipment, first of all. A keypad mounted on the wall right beside a loading-dock personnel door sits exactly where pallet jacks, forklifts and swinging pallets pass, and it gets knocked, cracked and pulled off its box. In the large new buildings along I-84 and Route 17K, we look at the traffic pattern before choosing the spot: which side of the door the trucks and lifts approach from, where pallets are staged, and where a person on foot naturally stands to use the door.

The fixes are simple once you see the path. Move the keypad or reader to the frame on the protected side of the door, or recess it into the wall so nothing projects; put it behind the line of an existing bollard or guard rail, or ask the building owner to add one; and choose a metal-housed, impact-resistant reader instead of a plastic keypad. A fob or card reader is easier than a keypad for staff wearing gloves, and it can't be read off a worn button.

Run the cable inside conduit from the reader to the controller or power supply, with the conduit clear of the dock area, and put the door's exit hardware where it can't be fouled either. A door event log is only useful if the reader that writes it is still on the wall.

Our Goshen office sees clients by appointment only — can staff let a visitor in from their desk?

Yes. The usual set-up for a small professional office is a video intercom or video doorbell at the street or building door and an electric strike in that door, so the person at the front desk sees the visitor, speaks to them and releases the door with a button or an app without leaving the desk. Staff and partners use their own codes or fobs at the same door, so nobody has to be buzzed in to get to work.

In an older village building the main design question is where the cables go. Strikes need a low-voltage run from the door frame to a power supply, and the intercom needs a run to the desk or a network connection. In a building with plaster walls or a shared hallway, we plan the route at the walkthrough, use surface raceway where we have to, and tell the landlord exactly what will be visible before we start.

Keep the door's exit side simple, set the strike to the right fail mode for that door, and decide in advance what happens after hours — whether the intercom rings through to a phone or simply goes unanswered. Those three decisions make the difference between a front door that works for clients and one that frustrates them.

“A new house is the one place you don't need to change the locks”

The logic sounds right: nobody has lived there, so nobody has a key. But a new house has usually been opened by more people than a resale — framers, electricians, plumbers, painters, flooring and cabinet crews, inspectors, the sales office and the punch-list team — over months of construction.

Construction keying and temporary cores are designed to shut those keys out at closing, and builder keypads can be reset. Both depend on someone actually doing it. One locksmith's public post describes rekeying a home in a new-construction neighbourhood that still had master pins in its locks.

On every new build in Tuxedo, Chester or Slate Hill we test the doors, reset or replace the keypads and rekey or replace the cylinders on closing day, then give the owner the list.

“Smart lock installation costs $250 to $400”

That is Fixr's national range for a single smart lock installed, updated in January 2025, with a $300 average. It is a fair figure for one lock on one pre-bored door with no complications, and it isn't an AESS price.

The Orange County jobs that cost more are usually the handovers: three or four exterior doors to clear at once, keypads to reset, cylinders to rekey, a strike to reset after the first winter, a portfolio of rentals, or a commercial door with a strike and a power supply.

“Any smart lock can go on any rented home”

Retrofit units that fit over the inside of an existing deadbolt are often marketed as renter-friendly, and on many off-post rentals they are. But a rental is still somebody else's door, and the lease decides what can change.

On post at West Point the rule is explicit: changes only through a Request for Alterations, a replacement from the same manufacturer as the existing lock, and a copy of the key to Community Management.

Off post, get the landlord's agreement in writing, keep the original hardware, and put it back when you leave. We work on that basis on every rental door.

“Landlords can just switch tenants to app-only entry”

Keyless entry can make turnovers easier, and articles aimed at landlords tend to stop there. In New York, the 2019 statewide unlawful-eviction law, RPAPL section 768, names changing the lock on an occupant's entrance door without supplying a key as one of the acts it prohibits.

That makes a lock change for a sitting tenant something to plan with care: supply a working credential, explain it in writing, and keep a physical key available. Using a lock to push someone out is a crime, not a technology choice.

We set up landlords' locks so every tenant gets a code and a key at move-in, and so codes are only removed after someone has actually left.

“The smart lock on the front door secures the house”

It secures the front door. Orange County houses typically have three or four other ways in: a door from the attached garage, a side or mudroom door, a sliding patio door to the deck and sometimes a basement walkout.

Sliding doors are the common gap. Their locks are built into the handle and rarely motorised, which is why the Town of Newburgh police still tell residents to set a Charley bar on sliding doors before a trip.

We include every exterior door in the walkthrough and recommend a contact or a bar where a smart lock isn't the right answer.

“You can install it yourself in thirty minutes”

On a well-hung pre-bored door, many people can. The thirty-minute version leaves out what makes a smart lock last: a strike fixed with long screws into the framing, a bolt that clears the strike after the house has moved through a winter, and a door that latches without a shove.

It also leaves out the parts of an Orange County handover that aren't about the lock at all: finding out what old keys and codes still work, resetting a builder's keypad, getting a landlord's or a housing office's approval.

Many of our calls are DIY installs that work at the counter and fail at the door. We fix the door, then the lock.

“Any handyman can wire a keypad door for a business”

A battery keypad deadbolt on an office door is a simple job. A keypad or reader with an electric strike, a power supply and a request-to-exit device is a small access-control system that has to suit the building's exits.

It also has a licensing edge in Orange County. The county licenses electricians, and any new 120-volt circuit or receptacle to feed a power supply is an electrician's work, separate from the low-voltage installation.

The order never changes: old access is found and closed before new access is handed out. That holds for a single townhome door in Tuxedo and for a landlord's houses from Port Jervis to the river.

Household members, tenants or staff set their own codes and try them while we watch; two people get admin rights; the owner keeps the signed register of what came off each door and what went on.

Field Notes

Field Notes on Orange County Handovers

A standing routine, written down so customers know what to expect on handover day — not an account of any particular house

We start with the keys on the kitchen counter. Whatever the builder, seller, landlord or housing office handed over goes in a row, and we ask how many copies exist and who else might have one. Nobody ever knows the full answer, which is the point of the visit.

Then every exterior door, in order: front, garage entry, side or mudroom, slider, basement walkout. Each one gets tried with every key in the row. A key that opens a door it shouldn't goes on the list. Each keypad gets checked for a model number and whether it can be reset completely.

Then the strikes. On a new house we pull the strike screws and replace short ones with long ones into the framing, because that is the thing that fails in February. On an older house we look for wood that is soft or split behind the plate.

Last, the people. Each person tries their own code or fob while we watch, the old keys are tried again and must fail, and the owner gets the written register. Then we open every door from inside, by hand, before we leave.

Glossary

Handover and Lock Terms, in Plain English

  • Construction keying: a set-up in which one key opens many houses during a build and is meant to stop working once the owner's key is used or the core is changed.
  • Temporary or construction core: a removable lock core used during the build and swapped for a permanent owner's core at handover.
  • Rekeying: changing the pins inside a mechanical cylinder so old keys no longer work and a new key does, without replacing the lock.
  • Factory reset: returning a smart lock to its out-of-box state; on many models this removes every code and virtual key that was programmed to it.
  • Bore and backset: the round holes through the door face and edge that a deadbolt fits into, and the distance from the door edge to the centre of the face hole.
  • Electric strike: a strike plate in the door frame that releases the latch electrically when a code, fob or button is used.
  • Fail-safe and fail-secure: whether an electrified lock unlocks or stays locked when its power is cut.
  • Request-to-exit: a button or motion sensor on the inside of an electrified door that releases it for people leaving.
  • Request for Alterations: the West Point Family Homes form residents use to ask permission for changes such as a different lock.
