The Neighborhood

Why Morris Heights Properties Need Access Control

The ground is the first thing anyone notices here. Wikipedia places Morris Heights in the West Bronx between West Burnside Avenue to the north, Jerome Avenue to the east, the Cross Bronx Expressway to the south and the Harlem River to the west, with University Avenue as its main thoroughfare, and describes the terrain as elevated and full of hills, with step streets connecting blocks at different heights. Rob Stephenson’s May 2025 portrait puts it more vividly: the neighborhood “rises steeply from the banks of the Harlem River,” past Roberto Clemente State Park and River Park Towers, then slopes down toward Jerome Avenue, where muffler and body shops line the street under the elevated 4 train. Sources do not agree on every edge. One neighborhood guide puts the northern boundary at West 179th Street, and Bronx Community Board 5 says the Cross Bronx Expressway bisects the neighborhood rather than bounding it. If your building sits on University Avenue, Sedgwick, Undercliff, Popham, Andrews, Harrison, Davidson or Montgomery Avenues, Macombs Road or Featherbed Lane, between the river and Jerome Avenue, this page is written for you.

That terrain shapes doors more than most people expect. A five-story walk-up on a slope often has its lobby at grade on one street and its cellar, boiler room or rear yard at grade on the street below. The stair walker Michael Cairl counted 71 steps on the West 176th Street stairs from Sedgwick Avenue up to Undercliff Avenue and 95 more from Undercliff to Popham Avenue, with further flights at West 175th and West 174th Streets. Buildings that front on those flights have doors that face a public stair rather than a sidewalk. Every one of those lower and side entrances is a way into the building, and each needs the same thought as the front door.

The second thing that shapes the work is who owns the buildings. By the 1920s, according to the same Substack portrait, University Avenue was lined with new five- and six-story apartment houses. In the 1970s, Wikipedia records, waves of arson left many of them badly damaged or destroyed; from the late 1970s the city rehabilitated abandoned tenements as low-income housing and built subsidized townhouses and apartment buildings on the vacant lots. Wikipedia lists ten NYCHA developments in the neighborhood, most of them rehabilitated five- and six-story walk-ups grouped under names like University Avenue Rehab and Morris Heights Rehab, alongside the seven 14- and 16-story buildings of Sedgwick Houses. Many of the entry systems in those rehabbed buildings date from the rehab itself, and many are reaching the end of their life.

The housing numbers explain why access control here is almost always a landlord’s or a board’s decision rather than a homeowner’s. The NYU Furman Center’s profile of Bronx Community District 5, which covers Morris Heights together with Fordham and University Heights and was last updated May 6, 2026, puts the district’s homeownership rate at 7.5 percent in 2024, against 32.7 percent citywide. The same profile counts 7,527 units under the federal Low Income Housing Tax Credit, 6,385 public housing units, 2,636 Mitchell-Lama units and 2,187 units with HUD project-based rental assistance. A door system in a building like that is bought by an owner who reports to an agency, a lender or an investor, and who will be asked to explain what it does with tenants’ information.

The district is also building again: Furman counts 910 units authorized by new building permits in 2025, against three in 2006. In 2026 New York YIMBY reported a lottery for Starhill Phase II, 169 apartments at 51 Featherbed Lane; permits for a 12-story building of 53 residences at 1884 University Avenue; and approval for a ten-story building of 97 affordable homes for older adults at 105 West Tremont Avenue.

The biggest buildings in the borough are here too. Wikipedia describes River Park Towers, completed in 1975, as two 38-story and two 44-story towers, still the tallest buildings in the Bronx. Its manager, Paths Management Services, lists 1,654 below-market Mitchell-Lama rentals. What changed there after 2013, and what did not, is a useful public lesson we return to below.

The law here is the same as anywhere in the Bronx. What differs is the number of doors per building, the number of owners who answer to someone else, and the number of residents who have lived through more than one change of landlord.

Morris Heights in a few lines, from the sources we read

  • Edges: West Burnside Avenue, Jerome Avenue, the Cross Bronx Expressway and the Harlem River (Wikipedia); one guide uses West 179th Street as the northern line, and the community board treats the expressway as running through it.
  • Size: about half a square mile, 0.502 square miles, with 36,779 residents in the 2011 figure Wikipedia cites; ZIP codes 10452 and 10453.
  • Civic map: Bronx Community Board 5; the NYPD 46th Precinct at 2120 Ryer Avenue; NYCHA property patrolled by Police Service Area 7.
  • Who owns the homes: fewer than one household in thirteen in Community District 5 owned its home in 2024 (7.5 percent, per the NYU Furman Center), so nearly every door decision is a landlord’s.
  • Regulated housing in the district: 7,527 tax-credit units, 6,385 public housing units and 2,636 Mitchell-Lama units (NYU Furman Center, 2025 figures).
  • Tallest buildings: River Park Towers, two 38-story and two 44-story towers completed in 1975 (Wikipedia).
  • Transit: the 4 train along Jerome Avenue, the B and D along the Grand Concourse, buses on University Avenue, Macombs Road and the Tremont–Burnside corridor, and the Morris Heights station on Metro-North’s Hudson Line beside the river.

The failures we see most here have more to do with use than with age. A closer worn out by hundreds of trips a day, so the street door drifts and never quite latches. A strike knocked out of line because the door has dropped on its hinges. A panel button pushed into the faceplate. A power supply in the cellar that died in a heat wave. A lower-street door that someone wedged open with a brick because it was the shortest route to the bus. Anything that keeps people from getting out is made safe before anything else.

Tenants should report a broken street door or intercom to the landlord or managing agent first, and to 311 if it is not fixed; we work on a building with the owner’s authorization.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Morris Heights’s Architecture

These are the building types we plan for in Morris Heights, described from the sources on this page and from what anyone can see from the sidewalk. Buildings and developments named here are examples of a type, not AESS clients.

Prewar walk-ups on University Avenue

The five- and six-story apartment houses of the 1920s that the Substack portrait describes lining the main avenue. Brick bearing walls, a single lobby with an inner vestibule door, a stair as the only vertical route, and, on a slope, a cellar that often reaches daylight on the downhill side. We usually find the original entrance doors replaced with steel.

City-rehabbed tenements

Buildings gutted and rebuilt as low-income housing from the late 1970s on, some kept by NYCHA as rehab groups and others passed to private and nonprofit owners. Inside the old shells are newer stairs, steel doors and frames, and intercom wiring run during the rehab, sometimes in conduit and sometimes not.

Mid-century public housing towers

Sedgwick Houses, seven buildings of 14 and 16 stories, is the clearest example in the neighborhood; the NYPL’s Sedgwick branch first opened inside it in 1951. Elevator buildings on open grounds, with several entrances per building. Named as a landmark only; NYCHA buildings are managed by NYCHA, and AESS does not work on them.

High-rise Mitchell-Lama towers

River Park Towers, 38 and 44 stories above the Major Deegan, completed in 1975. Elevator banks, long corridors, garages and a shared promenade, where one entrance policy has to work for about five thousand residents, the figure Reliant Safety gives, across four towers.

New affordable mid-rises

Six- to twelve-story buildings like Starhill Phase II on Featherbed Lane, the 1884 University Avenue filing and the planned senior building on West Tremont Avenue: aluminum storefront lobbies, hollow-metal doors, elevators, package rooms, and owners bound by regulatory agreements and lease-up schedules.

Older multi-unit houses and subsidized townhouses

Wikipedia notes older multi-unit homes and newer subsidized attached townhouses built on vacant lots. Small buildings with a stoop door, a basement door and sometimes a yard gate, where a single reader or a coded lock is usually enough.

How the slope and the materials change the method

Brick bearing walls. In prewar walk-ups the cable route from the street door to the cellar is the hard part. We look for the old intercom riser, a pipe chase or a closet stack before we touch a wall. Where nothing exists, the route goes down through the vestibule floor to the cellar ceiling, never in surface raceway up the outside of the building, and every penetration through the masonry is sealed.

Rehab steel doors in old openings. The rehabbed buildings usually have steel doors in steel frames set into brick openings that are not quite square. Before a strike or electrified lock goes in, the door has to hang true and latch on its own.

Aluminum storefront lobbies. New affordable buildings tend to use narrow-stile aluminum and glass entrances. They take electrified exit devices, electric latch retraction or narrow strikes, but only some readers fit the mullion, and the cable has to pass through a hinge or a transfer, not along the glass.

Doors that face downhill or onto stairs. A door at the bottom of a slope collects water at the threshold and ice in winter, and a door onto a public stair is used in the dark. We choose outdoor-rated readers, put drip edges over them, keep cable entries on the underside, and add light at the door wherever the owner can power it.

On the Slope

Every Level Is a Front Door: Planning Entrances on a Hill

The single most common mistake we see in Morris Heights buildings is a well-equipped lobby and an ignored lower door. The front door gets the new panel, the camera and the fobs; the door a floor below, onto the downhill street, keeps a worn key cylinder and a closer that gave up years ago. Residents use the lower door because it is closer to the bus or the station, and so does everyone else. The table below is how we sort the doors of a hillside building before we price anything.

Two principles run through every row. First, the whole envelope goes on the system at once: if the lobby is controlled and the lower door is not, the lower door becomes the entrance. Second, cable stays inside. On a slope the shortest line between two doors is often down the outside of the building, and that is exactly where a cable gets cut, soaked or stolen. We route through the cellar, up an interior chase or along a corridor ceiling, even when it takes a little longer, and the drawing we leave behind shows where it went.

AESS sample, not a recommendation for any particular building: each owner sets the hours, and the controller simply enforces them, while every door still lets people out freely.

Hills also change how doors behave. A building with entrances on two levels acts like a chimney: in winter warm air rises and pulls the lower door shut harder and pushes the upper one open; in summer the effect reverses. Tall towers feel it most, but even a six-story walk-up does. A door that swings shut cleanly on a calm day may hang a few inches open on a cold night. Before we put an electric lock on any door here, we check that its closer can latch it under both conditions, because an electric strike cannot hold a door that never reaches the frame.

Streets of apartment buildings and ground-floor shops toward the Concourse side, served by the Bx18 along Macombs Road, where the Starhill Phase II building has added 169 apartments with an on-site superintendent and intercoms.

Ground-floor community rooms and nonprofit offices inside residential buildings, and health providers such as the Morris Heights Health Center, which Wikipedia lists among local services. Their doors work best on their own schedule and administrator, kept apart from the residential side.

The rules a Morris Heights entry system has to meet

Planning summaries, not legal advice. The Department of Buildings, FDNY, HPD and, for regulated buildings, the supervising agency have the final word.

  • The street door and the call system. For apartment buildings put up or converted since the start of 1968, state law (Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a) wants an entrance that pulls itself shut and locks itself, staying locked unless someone is posted at it, and, once a building reaches eight apartments, a way for visitors to speak to each apartment and be let in from upstairs. The roof door is the exception: it must swing shut but never lock behind someone.
  • Nobody trapped inside. If a door on a way out is held by electricity, the city’s building code (§1010.1.9.8 and §1010.1.9.9) expects it to let go when the power fails or the fire alarm or sprinklers go off, and to open from inside with one hand, through a button or handle that cuts the lock’s power directly where the code calls for one.
  • The apartment door is separate. Housing Maintenance Code §27-2043 still requires a keyed lock, a heavy-duty latch set, a heavy-duty deadbolt and a chain guard on each apartment entrance door in a class A building, whatever happens in the lobby.
  • What happens to residents’ information. As soon as a building of three or more apartments switches to keyless entry, Local Law 63 of 2021 follows it: residents get a written policy and give consent, the owner keeps only a short list of facts, records of each entry are erased within 90 days unless anonymized, and nothing collected may be sold or turned against a tenant.
  • Fobs in rent-stabilized buildings. As Brick Underground summarized state housing agency rulings in 2018, fobs replacing keys go free to every lawful occupant, extra fobs are free, replacement of a lost fob can carry only a small capped charge, and no new identification may be demanded.
  • Mitchell-Lama and other regulated buildings. Buildings supervised under HPD’s Mitchell-Lama program or financed with tax credits answer to their supervising agency and their funders as well as to the codes. We do not make that agency’s decisions for an owner; we supply the scope, specifications and pricing the owner’s submission needs.
Lessons From the Neighborhood

What River Park Towers and 1520 Sedgwick Avenue Teach About Doors

Two Morris Heights buildings have unusually public records, and both are worth reading before spending money on a door. We have no role in either building, and nothing here describes their current equipment.

River Park Towers: cameras changed reporting, not the number of incidents. According to a case study published by Reliant Safety, the security company that took over the complex in January 2013 when its parent bought it, only about thirty exterior cameras were in place at the start; two years later there were 1,600, covering every non-residential space. The study says the cameras went in building by building, and that each time a building was covered, drug dealing moved to a building that had none. It reports that occupancy rose from about 1,100 to 1,600 units and that the kind of incident changed, but it also says plainly that the number of reported incidents stayed about the same, and that reported crime went up because tenants felt safer reporting it.

Two lessons follow for any owner here. Do every entrance, or every building of a campus, in one phase if you can, because problems move to the gap. And treat cameras as the record and the doors as the control.

River Park Towers again: tenant data is not an enforcement tool. In May 2026 THE CITY reported that the Legal Aid Society had sued the complex’s owners and its manager, alleging that nearly 500 eviction cases had been brought since July 2024 over tenants’ annual income recertifications required under the Low Income Housing Tax Credit. That dispute is about paperwork, not doors. But it shows how high the stakes around residency records are in regulated buildings, and why we set up entry systems so that door logs are kept briefly, seen by few people and never quietly folded into compliance files. The Tenant Data Privacy Law already forbids using entry data to harass or evict; a well-configured system makes that easy to honor.

1520 Sedgwick Avenue: the door is part of the building’s condition. The building where, on August 11, 1973, Cindy Campbell held the back-to-school party at which her brother DJ Kool Herc played in the ground-floor community room, is a 102-unit apartment building on the western edge of the neighborhood. Workforce Housing Group’s history says an investor bought it in 2008 planning to raise rents, could not keep up with the mortgage after the housing crash, and left tenants with broken elevators, a leaking roof and months without gas or heat, until the tenants enlisted Kool Herc and the Urban Homesteading Assistance Board and Workforce Housing Group took ownership in 2011. The Community Preservation Corporation’s 2013 release puts the rehabilitation at about $16.8 million and lists new building doors and a closed-circuit security system alongside a new roof and new elevator cabs.

The point for other owners is that entry doors decline along with everything else when a building is neglected, and that a rescue rehab treats them as structure, not decoration. When we survey a building that has changed hands under strain, we start with the doors’ physical condition, the closer, the hinges, the frame and the strike, before talking about readers at all. A fob reader on a door that does not latch is a light that tells everyone the door is open.

Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Morris Heights: Questions Answered

These are the questions owners, managing agents, tenant leaders and shopkeepers in Morris Heights bring to us, grouped by the part of the neighborhood they come from: the slope, the rehabbed walk-ups, the big regulated buildings and the commercial streets. Answers that depend on law are planning summaries, not legal advice.

Doors on the Slope

Our lobby faces the uphill street and our back door opens a full story lower onto the street behind. How does one controller serve both doors without a cable run on the outside of the building?

By going through the building instead of around it. The controller sits where both cable runs are shortest and driest, usually in the cellar near the lower door or in a closet off the lobby stair. From the lobby, the reader, lock and position switch cables drop through the vestibule floor or follow the old intercom riser down to the cellar ceiling; from the lower door, they run a short distance across the cellar. In most prewar walk-ups there is already a chase or an abandoned riser we can use, and finding it is the first job of the survey.

What we avoid is the tempting shortcut of a raceway down the facade or a cable through the yard. Outside, cable is exposed to water, ice, vandals and the next facade contractor, and on a slope it often passes right beside the door it is meant to protect. Where an interior route truly does not exist, such as a detached yard gate, we use buried conduit or a battery-powered networked lock and accept its limits, rather than hang a cable on the wall.

The two doors then run as one system with different rules, and the drawing we leave shows the route so the next electrician does not cut it.

A public step street runs along the side of our building, and our side door opens onto one of its landings. What does a door on a public stair need that the lobby door does not?

Less on its face and more around it. A door onto a step street faces people walking past in the dark and sometimes people sitting on the steps beside it, so the outside should give nobody anything to press, pry or ask about: no call buttons, a reader recessed into the wall or a vandal-rated one flush with the frame, a lock with a latch guard, and hinges that cannot be lifted from outside. Visitors should be sent to the main entrance, not invited to ring at the side.

Light matters as much as hardware. A lit door is used confidently by residents and avoided by people who prefer not to be seen; an unlit one is the opposite. Where the owner can power a fixture at the door, we put it on the same circuit plan as the reader. A camera, if there is one, should look at the door and its approach only; it should not record the public stair or into neighbors’ windows.

Inside, it needs free exit and a position switch. Whether it is residents-only, or closed at night, is a choice the owner makes, and the system simply carries it out.

AESS schematic, not any particular address: a door that faces a public stair gives passers-by nothing to press or pry, and its camera looks at the door, not at the stair or the neighbors.

Our entrance sits at the top of a long flight of outdoor steps above the sidewalk. Should the call panel be at the door or down at the sidewalk gate?

Usually at the door, with the gate left as a gate. A panel at the sidewalk means visitors are buzzed into a stair and a landing that nobody can see from the lobby, and it means cable has to travel down the steps outdoors. A panel at the door lets residents see who is actually standing at their building, and keeps the cable inside.

There are exceptions. A fenced forecourt closed at night can have a gate lock at the bottom released together with the door, and a building with a step-free entrance should put its panel there. Couriers who will not climb with heavy parcels are a real problem on a hill: a delivery code that opens a package room at the top, or a parcel locker, solves it better than moving the panel down.

Our boiler room opens straight onto the lower street because of the slope. Who should be able to open it, and how?

The super, the boiler contractor, utility workers and the fire department, each in a different way. Staff and regular contractors get their own credentials, scheduled to their working hours and deleted the day a contract ends, so a copied key from five years ago no longer opens the room. A position switch reports when the door is left open, which matters both for security and because a boiler room door propped in winter can freeze pipes.

For emergency access, the door must remain usable by firefighters: we do not lock it in a way that defeats the department’s entry, and where a key box is required or advisable the owner arranges it with the fire department rather than with us. From inside, the door opens freely at all times, without a key, because it may be someone’s way out.

Buildings the City Rebuilt

The apartment intercom handsets from our building’s rehab are no longer made. Must every handset be replaced when the lobby panel is?

Not always, but usually in the end. Many rehab-era systems are two-wire or multi-wire audio intercoms, where the lobby panel, the riser and the handsets were sold as a set. A new panel from the same family may still drive the old handsets, which lets an owner replace the panel now and handsets as they fail. A different system usually cannot talk to them at all.

There are then three routes, compared in the diagram below: new stations in every apartment, a panel that calls residents’ own phones and landlines, or a mix of the two. Calling phones is cheapest because no work is done inside apartments; stations feel most familiar; the mix suits buildings with many older residents.

Before deciding, we test the riser; one with shorts on several floors decides the question for you. Whatever is chosen, visitors must still be able to call every apartment and residents to release the door.

Our super looks after several walk-ups on different blocks. How can one person keep track of every front door without walking the route?

Put the buildings on one management account, with each building’s doors grouped under its own name and alerts sent to the super’s phone. A held-open alert from a lower-street door at 11 p.m., a lock that has lost power, a panel that has gone offline: each arrives with the building and the door named, so the super knows which block to walk to and what to bring.

The same account handles enrollments and deletions from the office. Each building’s controller keeps working on its own if the internet connection drops, so a dead router on one block does not lock anyone out; it only stops the alerts until it is fixed.

We took over a building after a distressed sale, and the door system’s accounts are still registered to the previous owner. How do we get control back?

A local controller can usually be reset to factory settings and the credentials re-enrolled. A cloud-managed system belongs to whoever holds the account, and the manufacturer or its dealer can usually transfer it to a new owner on proof of ownership, such as the deed and a letter on the new owner’s letterhead. We handle that request with the manufacturer.

Whichever route, treat every existing credential as suspect. Former managers, porters, contractors and even former tenants may still hold working fobs. We export the current list if we can, delete everyone, and re-issue credentials to verified current residents, which is also the moment to give them the written privacy policy and consent form that the Tenant Data Privacy Law calls for, and to collect no more information than it allows.

If the manufacturer will not cooperate, replacement is often quicker than recovery. Either way, the new owner should end the week holding the passwords, not a vendor.

Large Complexes and Regulated Buildings

Our complex has several towers that share one campus. If we upgrade one tower’s doors at a time, won’t trouble just move next door?

Very likely, and there is local evidence for it. Reliant Safety’s published account of River Park Towers says that as cameras went in building by building, dealing moved to whichever building had none. Doors behave the same way: a tower with readers and self-locking doors pushes unwanted visitors toward the tower whose lobby still opens with a push.

The answer is to phase by type of door rather than by building: every main lobby on the same day, then rear, garage and service doors, then interior doors, with the software set up campus-wide from the start. No building is left as the easy one.

We can afford new cameras or new door hardware this year, but not both. Which should come first?

In most residential buildings, the doors. A camera records who came in through a door that did not stop them; a lock that latches and a reader that refuses an old fob stop them in the first place. The River Park case study is candid that thousands of cameras changed what kinds of incidents were reported, not how many happened; the doors decide who is inside the building to begin with.

Cameras come first where the doors already lock properly but nobody can see who residents are letting in, or where an insurer or regulator has asked for recording.

Often the right first step is cheaper than either: closers, strikes and hinges put right on every exterior door.

Many of our older residents cannot hear the lobby call or read a small screen. What can an entry panel do for them?

Quite a lot, if the panel and the apartment end are chosen for them rather than for a brochure. At the door: large, well-lit buttons or a simple numeric directory instead of a scrolling touchscreen, high-contrast lettering, a speaker loud enough over traffic, and a release that holds the door unlocked long enough for someone with a walker or a cart. At the apartment: a station with a flashing light as well as a chime, adjustable volume, or a call routed to the resident’s existing landline or hearing-aid-compatible phone.

Credentials matter too. Some older residents prefer a card to a small fob; others do better with a short code. A good system allows both on the same door. Nobody should be forced to download an app to get home.

The new senior building planned at 105 West Tremont Avenue, as YIMBY describes it, will have a lobby with 24-hour reception. There the desk becomes part of the entry system, and residents who cannot answer are still let in by a person who knows them.

AESS schematic: on the same door, each household answers the way it can, and nobody needs an app to let a visitor in.

Part of our building is under a vacate order while repairs go on. How do we keep the empty section sealed and still let contractors and inspectors in?

Treat the vacated section as a separate building for the duration. In February 2024 ABC7 reported that tenants of a partially collapsed building on Billingsley Terrace, uprooted by a vacate order, had sued their landlords over repair delays; repairs of that kind can take a long time. Their doors need to stay shut against squatters, scrap thieves and curious children while letting workers through.

The doors into and out of the vacated part go on a temporary group: contractors get credentials that expire with their scope, inspectors get day codes, every opening is logged. Nothing may block residents’ exits or the fire department, and fire-rated doors are touched only with the repair engineer’s approval.

When the order is lifted, the temporary group is deleted and returning tenants are re-enrolled.

Shops on University and Jerome Avenues

A deli on University Avenue had its steel back door pried open with a crowbar overnight. What makes a back door hold, and what should happen when someone tries?

A steel door is only as strong as the place where its latch meets the frame. Pry attacks work by bending the frame away from the door until the bolt slips out. What resists them is a latch or bolt with a long throw, a steel latch guard over the gap, a frame reinforced or anchored to the masonry, hinge pins that cannot be removed from outside, and, on doors that open outward, hinge-side security studs. An electric strike adds control, not strength, so it is always paired with that hardware.

The second half of the answer is time. The ABC7 report described thieves who got in on a Sunday night and were not discovered until the owner arrived on Monday. A door position switch tied to an alarm or to the owner’s phone turns a forced back door into an alert within seconds of the frame giving way, while the burglars are still inside. Add a camera that sees the door from inside, and the alert comes with a picture.

Finally, take the prize out of the room. The ABC7 report describes cash left unsecured in a back office; that turns a nuisance break-in into a large loss. A separate lock on the office door, on its own code known to fewer people, and a safe for overnight cash do more than any single device on the back door.

AESS schematic: the reader decides who opens the door normally; the guard, frame, hinges and bolt decide how long a crowbar takes, and the position switch decides how soon someone knows.

Customers’ car keys hang on a pegboard in our Jerome Avenue body shop. What is a better way to control who takes them?

A locked electronic key cabinet, on the same credentials as the shop’s doors. Each key or set sits in its own locked slot; a staff member opens the cabinet with a code or a fob and can remove only the keys they are allowed to, and the cabinet records who took which key and when it came back. When a customer asks why their car was moved, the answer is in the log.

The cabinet belongs inside the office, behind a door that locks on its own schedule, not on the service floor where anyone walking in from the sidewalk can reach it. After hours, the cabinet and the office door both stay locked, and the bay doors and the side door report if they are opened. For smaller shops, a heavy lock box with a coded padlock is a reasonable start, though it keeps no record of who opened it.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Morris Heights

We could not open Google’s AI Overview or similar tools from our build environment for this page, so we do not quote any. These are claims we hear repeated by owners after they have searched, set against what the record and the work show in Morris Heights.

“Cameras are the security upgrade that matters most.”

Cameras are useful, but they record events rather than prevent entries. The most detailed public account of security in a Morris Heights complex, Reliant Safety’s case study of River Park Towers, describes going from about thirty cameras to 1,600 in two years and says that reported incidents stayed roughly level while their character changed and reporting rose.

That is a reasonable result for cameras, but it is not control. A door that latches and a reader that rejects a lost fob change who gets in; a camera tells you afterwards who did.

Judge any camera bundle, including the national alarm brands’, by one question: will this stop someone, or only show them?

“Affordable buildings don’t need modern access control.”

The law says otherwise for most of them. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires self-locking entrances and, at eight or more apartments, an intercom with a door release, whatever the rent. In a district where, by Furman Center figures, more than seven thousand units carry tax credits and thousands more are public housing or Mitchell-Lama, much of the housing stock is exactly the kind of building those rules were written for.

The more useful question is what “modern” should mean on a tight budget. It means encrypted credentials that cannot be copied at a kiosk, a controller that keeps working without the internet, a panel that rings landlines, and strong, plain hardware. It does not mean facial recognition or features that collect more tenant data than the city’s privacy law allows.

For a Mitchell-Lama or tax-credit owner, clear specifications and a test record also make the agency’s review easier.

“Terrain makes no difference to an entry system.”

On flat ground that is nearly true. In Morris Heights, where Wikipedia describes the land as elevated and hilly and step streets join blocks at different heights, the slope decides how many exterior doors a building has, where water collects at them, how air pressure pushes them, and which cable routes are safe.

A building with a lobby on the uphill street and a cellar door on the downhill street has two front doors as far as an intruder is concerned. A door on a step-street landing faces a public stair. A gate at the bottom of a yard stands in standing water after rain.

None of that appears in a generic price per door; it appears on our survey sheet.

“Door logs are a neat way to prove who lives in an apartment.”

This one is risky as well as wrong. In New York City, the Tenant Data Privacy Law limits what an owner may collect through a smart access system, requires authentication records to be destroyed within 90 days unless anonymized, and forbids using the data to harass or evict. Brick Underground’s summary of state housing agency rulings notes that fob records have been offered as evidence that a tenant did not live in an apartment, and treated as weak on their own.

In regulated buildings, where annual income and household certifications are part of tenancy, it can be tempting to treat the door system as a residency tracker. THE CITY’s 2026 reporting on recertification evictions at River Park Towers shows how contentious residency paperwork already is without door data being added to it.

We set systems up so that entry logs exist to answer security questions, are kept briefly and are seen by a short list of named people. That protects tenants, and it protects owners from building a record they are not allowed to use.

“A plug-in kit from the internet does what an installed system does.”

For a single apartment door, a good consumer smart lock can be a fine choice. For a building, kits usually fall short in the same places: they depend on Wi-Fi that does not reach the cellar, on batteries that die in the cold at a lower-street door, and on an app account held by whoever set it up.

The bigger gap is compliance. A building door has to release on loss of power and on fire alarm where required, has to give a voice call and remote release in a building of eight or more apartments, and has to sit on hardware that survives hundreds of uses a day. Cost guides from national lead sites often quote low per-door prices that assume none of that.

Our published Bronx prices are higher than a kit for that reason; what they buy is a door that still works, and still lets people out, after the first winter.

“Older residents won’t use fobs, so keep the keys.”

Some will not, and nobody should be forced to. But keeping metal keys at the street door keeps the main weakness of metal keys: copies made years ago by people who have since moved, been evicted or worked for a previous owner still open the building.

The better approach is choice at the same door. A card for residents who find a fob fiddly, a short code for those who prefer numbers, a panel that calls a landline, a desk or a neighbor for those who use none of them. In our experience, once the door reliably closes behind them, older residents are often the strongest supporters of the change.

What needs care is the rollout: several days and evenings of lobby enrollment, in the languages residents read.

“Once it is in, an access system looks after itself.”

The electronics are fairly durable. The doors are not. Closers lose tension, hinges sag, strikes drift out of line, and a panel faceplate takes knocks from carts and strollers. In a building where the street door opens hundreds of times a day, the mechanical parts set the system’s real lifespan.

Software needs upkeep too: departed users deleted, passwords changed when staff leave, backups taken.

We hand over a simple schedule: a monthly look at every door’s latching, a quarterly purge of old credentials, a yearly service visit for closers, strikes and batteries. It is cheaper than the emergency calls it prevents.

How It Works

Our Installation Process

The same seven steps apply to a six-apartment walk-up and to a campus of towers; on a small building some of them take an hour.

1. Envelope survey, top to bottom

We walk the outside of the building from the highest entrance to the lowest, list every exterior door, gate and bulkhead, note which street or stair each opens onto, and test whether each one closes and latches on its own.

2. Door repairs first

Closers, hinges, frames and latches are put right before any electronics go on. A door that does not latch is fixed or replaced, because a reader cannot secure an opening that never closes.

3. Routes and the head-end

We find the interior cable routes, usually an old riser or the cellar ceiling, and pick a dry, raised spot for the controller and power supplies, then write up the plan with each door’s lock, schedule and alerts.

4. Installation in working hours

Cable is pulled and labeled with the door number at both ends, and locks, readers and panels are fitted one door at a time so the building is never left without a working entrance overnight.

5. Exit, power and alarm tests

At every electrified door we test free exit, cut the power to prove the battery and the lock’s behavior, check the fire-alarm release where one applies, and record the results door by door.

6. Privacy paperwork and enrollment

The owner gets a Tenant Data Privacy Law policy and consent form to issue, and we enroll residents in the lobby over several days and evenings, with cards, fobs, codes or landline calling as each household prefers.

7. Handover and the first-month visit

The owner receives the door list, the route drawing, the test record and administrator access in its own name. We come back after about a month of use to adjust closers and clear what early use has shown.

What the owner keeps after we leave

A short folder, on paper and in a shared drive, that lets any competent installer support the system: the numbered list of every exterior and controlled door with its lock type and schedule, the route drawing showing where each cable runs between levels, the door-by-door test record, a backup of the controller database, two administrator logins in the owner’s name, the privacy policy and consent form, and a maintenance checklist taped inside the equipment cabinet.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Start the Survey at the Bottom of the Hill

This is a note on how we survey, not an account of any particular building. In flat neighborhoods a survey begins at the front door, because that is where the problem usually is. In Morris Heights we start at the lowest point of the lot and work up. We stand on the downhill street and look at the back of the building: a cellar door, a boiler room door, a yard gate, a window grille that has been cut. Then we climb, by the street or by the step street beside the building, and note every opening on the way to the lobby.

The reason is simple. People who should not be in a building rarely choose the brightest, busiest door. They choose the one at the bottom of the slope that nobody watches, the one propped with a brick so a resident could get to the bus, the one whose key was copied by a contractor years ago. The owner who called us usually wants to talk about the lobby; the building’s weak point is usually below it.

So our written survey always lists the doors from the bottom up, with each one marked as latching or not, controlled or not, and lit or not. The lobby is on the list too, but it is rarely first.

FAQ

Morris Heights Access Control FAQ

Can the lobby panel call residents who have only a landline and no smartphone?
+

Yes, if the panel is chosen for it. Several current video entry panels can place an ordinary phone call to a landline or a basic cell phone, and the resident presses a number on the keypad to release the door, the same way older telephone-entry systems worked. Residents with smartphones can answer by app with video at the same door. We confirm which residents need landline calling during enrollment and set each apartment up the way that household prefers, so nobody is cut off from their visitors because they do not use apps.

Can our existing electric strike and door closer stay if only the lobby panel is replaced?
+

Often, provided they still work properly. A strike that releases cleanly at the right voltage and a closer that pulls the door shut and latches it every time can be wired to a new panel, which keeps the cost down. We test both before quoting: we check the strike's voltage and whether it is fail-safe or fail-secure, and we watch the door close from fully open several times. A worn closer or a strike that only works when someone pulls the door is replaced, because the new panel will be blamed for every failure the old hardware causes.

How often should a busy lobby door's closer, strike and reader be serviced?
+

For a street door that opens hundreds of times a day, we suggest a quick check by the super every month and a service visit once a year. The monthly check takes a minute: open the door fully, let it go, and confirm it closes and latches without help, then try a deleted credential to make sure it is refused. The yearly visit adjusts or replaces the closer, realigns the strike, tightens hinge screws, cleans the reader and panel faceplate, and tests the backup battery under load. Buildings that skip it usually call us after the door has been hanging open for a week.

Can a building get temporary entry control while a replacement door is being made?
+

Yes. Custom steel doors and frames for older openings can take weeks to arrive. In the meantime we can fit a temporary electrified lock or a battery-powered networked lock to the existing door, enroll residents on credentials that will carry over to the new door, and add a position switch so the super is alerted if the old door is left open. When the new door is hung, the permanent reader and lock go on it and the same credentials keep working, so residents are enrolled only once.

Can we buy extra fobs from AESS for a system another company installed, or are we tied to that company?
+

That depends on the credential format the system uses. Many readers accept standard formats that any supplier can provide, and we can order and program compatible fobs or cards once we know the reader model and the format. Some systems use proprietary credentials sold only through one dealer network, which is one reason we ask about this before recommending any platform. If your building is tied to a single source and the price or service has become a problem, replacing the readers with ones that accept open formats is sometimes cheaper over a few years than staying.

Can the entry panel show a building notice, such as a heat or water shutdown, to residents as they come in?
+

Some touchscreen panels can display a short message or image on their screen, and some management apps can push a notice to residents' phones; simpler button panels cannot. If posting notices matters to your building, tell us before we choose the panel. Even where the feature exists, a paper notice inside the lobby is still the reliable way to reach residents who do not use the app, and any notice should avoid naming individual tenants or apartments.

Will someone show our super how to add and delete credentials, and what happens if he leaves?
+

Yes. At handover we spend time with the super or the manager on the everyday tasks: enrolling a new resident, deleting a departed one, giving a contractor a credential that expires, checking which door is reporting open, and what to do when a reader stops responding. We leave a one-page guide in the equipment cabinet. Administrator rights belong to the owner, so if the super leaves, the owner or manager deletes his login and gives the next super a new one; nothing about the system depends on one person's password.

Does AESS patch and paint the hallway walls after running cable in a finished building?
+

We keep openings small and close them neatly: holes for cable are patched and sealed, and surface raceway, where it cannot be avoided, is fitted straight and can be painted to match. Full plaster restoration and repainting of a finished lobby or corridor is usually left to the building's painter, so the finish matches the rest of the wall. We say in the written quote exactly which repairs are ours and which are not, so there is no surprise at the end of the job.
