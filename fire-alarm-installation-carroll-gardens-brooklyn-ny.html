The Neighborhood

Why Carroll Gardens Properties Need Fire Alarm Installation

Carroll Gardens is a neighborhood of houses, and that shapes fire detection more than anything else. Brick Underground, writing about the neighborhood's housing in 2023, reported that about 95 percent of it is single-family brownstones or brownstones converted into small condos and co-ops, and quoted the broker Steiker-Epstein: "It's not a neighborhood of big buildings." In a brownstone there is no super and no lobby panel, only the people who live there. Whether a fire is noticed in time depends on alarms on each floor, on whether those alarms are linked so one sets off the rest, and on whether anyone can hear them through a closed bedroom door three flights away.

The houses are also old, and their layout works against detection. The 134 rowhouses of the Carroll Gardens Historic District, the rectangle bounded by Carroll, President, Smith and Hoyt Streets, were built between about 1869 and 1884 and designated a city landmark in 1973. Most brownstones here follow the same vertical plan: a cellar with the boiler and meters, a garden floor half below the sidewalk with its own door under the stoop, a parlor floor above, and two or three floors of bedrooms. One open stair ties them together. Smoke from the garden-floor kitchen rises up that stair to the bedrooms, and a fire in a top-floor bedroom can go unnoticed on the parlor floor. Linked alarms answer both problems; getting them into lath, plaster and brick without ruining the house is most of the skill.

Many of those houses are no longer one household. Over the decades they have been split into two- and three-family homes, carved into condos, or turned back into single-family houses after years as apartments. Every one of those changes moves the alarm requirements, from HPD's rental rules to the hardwired alarms a filed renovation brings. We plan for the building as it is used today, which is not always what the Certificate of Occupancy says.

Then there is the calendar. HPD requires natural gas detectors in multiple dwellings and in one- and two-family homes that the owner does not occupy, wherever there is gas piping, starting January 1, 2027. When this page was reviewed that date was seven weeks away. Gas ranges, gas boilers and gas water heaters are standard in Carroll Gardens brownstones, so most rented apartments in the neighborhood fall under the rule. For many owners, the gas deadline is the reason to look at every alarm in the house now, while a technician is already inside each apartment, instead of paying for two rounds of visits and two rounds of tenant notices.

  • Most homes are brownstones. Brick Underground put the share of single-family brownstones and converted brownstone condos and co-ops at about 95 percent of the neighborhood's housing.   • A landmarked core. The Carroll Gardens Historic District holds 134 two- and three-story rowhouses in neo-Grec and late Italianate styles, set back behind the front gardens that give the neighborhood its name. The city designated it on September 25, 1973, and it joined the National Register in 1983.   • Small buildings with many owners. Building Specialists

Fire Alarm Installation for Carroll Gardens' Architecture

Six kinds of building account for almost every fire alarm job we see in Carroll Gardens. They differ in who owns them, how many households they hold, how the walls and floors are built and which city rules reach them, and those differences decide the plan well before any device is picked.

Landmarked rowhouses

The 1869 to 1884 brownstones of the historic district and the blocks around it: cellar, garden floor, parlor floor and two or three bedroom floors on one open stair, built of brick behind brownstone fronts with lath and plaster inside.

Brownstones with rented floors

Houses where the owner lives on some floors and rents the garden apartment or the top floors, or lives elsewhere and rents them all, each apartment with its own kitchen, usually on gas.

Brownstone condos and small co-ops

Houses split into three or four condos, and turn-of-the-century walk-ups of six to eight apartments run by a volunteer board, with a shared stair and a cellar that belongs to everyone.

Limestones and early walk-ups

Four-story buildings of the 1900s to 1920s on Court, Clinton and Luquer Streets, with eight or so apartments over a stair hall and, on the avenues, a store on the ground floor.

Newer condo buildings

Buildings from 2000 onward, from six units to seventeen, with engineered fire alarm systems, a panel that was filed and inspected, and a managing agent who holds the paperwork.

Shops, restaurants and churches

Storefronts on Court and Smith Streets with apartments above, restaurants with kitchens in rear extensions, and large institutions such as St. Mary Star of the Sea on Court Street.

Landmarked rowhouses: one stair, five levels, and walls you do not want to open

A Carroll Gardens brownstone is tall and narrow. Boiler and meters in the cellar, kitchen on the garden floor, tall ceilings on the parlor floor, bedrooms above, one stair through it all: good for living, bad for hearing. A smoke alarm in the garden-floor kitchen is two or three floors and several closed doors away from the bedrooms, and a fire in a top-floor bedroom may not be noticed by anyone on the parlor floor until the smoke comes down the stair.

So linking is the heart of almost every brownstone job here. We place smoke alarms inside each bedroom and outside each sleeping area, at the head of the stair on each floor, on the garden floor away from the stove, and in the cellar, plus CO alarms near the bedrooms and on the level with the boiler. Then the question is how the alarms talk to each other. Open walls in a renovation favor hardwired interconnect cable; finished plaster favors wireless interconnected alarms, with the signal tested through the brick and floors first.

Brownstones with rented floors: two sets of rules in one house

The most common Carroll Gardens arrangement we see is an owner on two or three floors and a tenant in the garden apartment or on the top floors. HPD's carbon monoxide rule covers one- and two-family homes the owner does not occupy where there are fossil-fuel appliances, and its natural gas rule, from January 1, 2027, reaches the same homes wherever there is gas piping. A house with three or more apartments is a multiple dwelling, and HPD's smoke detector rule applies to it as well.

The owner also picks up paperwork. HPD asks owners to give a notice to at least one adult in each unit when detectors are installed, to keep the installation date and the end of each device's useful life on record, and to replace devices that are missing or dead before a new tenant moves in. We plan the rented apartment and the owner's floors together, so a fire in either warns the other.

Brownstone condos and small co-ops: shared stair, separate owners

When a brownstone becomes three condos, or a 1900s walk-up becomes an eight-unit co-op, the stair and the cellar belong to everyone and the apartments belong to each owner. That split is where detection falls through the cracks, especially in the cellar with the boiler and meters. A building of three or more apartments is a multiple dwelling under HPD's rules, so the building has the owner's duties for smoke, CO and gas detection, whoever the owner is on paper.

We work apartment by apartment on the board's schedule, with one dated device list for the whole building. If the board wants stair and cellar detection that sounds on every floor, we explain first whether linked household devices can do it or whether it becomes a panel-based system needing a design professional and an FDNY filing.

Limestones and early walk-ups: masonry, a stair hall, and sometimes a store

The four-story limestones and walk-ups of the early 1900s on Court, Clinton and Luquer Streets hold six to eight apartments on a central stair hall, often with a store on the ground floor on the commercial streets. They are heavier than brownstones, with masonry partitions in places, so cable is routed through closets, chases and the stair.

Before pricing we check each apartment's smoke, CO and 2027 gas needs, whether the stair and cellar have any detection, and whether the store would warn anyone upstairs after closing.

Newer condo buildings: engineered systems and someone else's records

The buildings that went up in Carroll Gardens from 2000 onward, such as 162 Huntington Street, 14 President Street and 480 Degraw Street, are of a different kind. Buildings of that size were designed with engineered fire alarm systems that went through plan review and inspection, and the system belongs to the condo, run by the managing agent and serviced by the company that holds its contract.

For unit owners in these buildings, we do the work inside the apartment that belongs to the owner, and only after the managing agent confirms what is part of the building system. If a renovation moves walls, any building devices that have to move are coordinated with the building's fire alarm company and design professional, not done on our own.

Shops, restaurants and churches: the business below the families

The commercial buildings here are mostly mixed-use: a shop or restaurant on the ground floor and the cellar, apartments above. A fire in the back of a closed store, or in a restaurant kitchen after the staff have gone home, is seen by nobody. If the store has no detection that warns the apartments, the first sign upstairs may be smoke in the stair. So for a shop under apartments, we plan detection that sounds where the families can hear it or reports to a monitoring center that can send help, and we choose devices that will not false-alarm from cooking, steam, flour or dust.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Count the Households, Find the Boiler, Then Climb the Stair With the Doors Shut

Start at the doorbells and the meters. In a Carroll Gardens brownstone the first count is the number of doorbells, and the second is the number of gas and electric meters in the cellar. If the house has three doorbells and three meters, there are probably three households, whatever the owner thinks of as "the house". That count decides which HPD rules apply and which alarms should be linked.

Follow the gas. From the meters, the technician follows the gas to every appliance it feeds: boiler, water heater, dryer, each kitchen range, and anything added later, such as a gas fireplace insert in the parlor or a range moved during a kitchen renovation. Each appliance goes on the survey sheet by room, because gas and CO planning both start there.

Look up on the parlor floor. Parlor ceilings in these houses are high and often carry plaster medallions, cornices and ornament. Each device goes on plain plaster, clear of ornament and the corners where air stalls, at a height someone can reach to test it.

Close every door and listen. Last, with every bedroom door on the top floor shut and any window unit or white-noise machine running, a second person sounds an alarm in the garden-floor kitchen. If it cannot be heard clearly in the farthest bedroom, the plan needs linking or another device, whatever the code minimum says. In a five-level brownstone, that test changes the plan more often than not.

January 1, 2027

Seven Weeks to the Gas Detector Deadline: What Carroll Gardens Landlords and Small Co-ops Should Do Now

HPD's page on smoke, carbon monoxide and natural gas detectors says natural gas detectors are required starting January 1, 2027 in multiple dwellings and in one- and two-family homes the owner does not occupy, wherever there is gas piping. The detector goes where the gas-burning device is, and inside a sleeping room only if a gas appliance is in that room. Here that usually means each apartment's kitchen and the cellar.

Who it reaches here. The rule covers most of the neighborhood's rentals: brownstones with three or more apartments, small co-ops and condos, the walk-ups and limestones on the commercial streets, and two-family houses where the owner lives elsewhere. A brownstone with a rented garden apartment and the owner upstairs is a two-family the owner occupies, which on HPD's wording is outside the rule for the owner's own unit. We still recommend gas detection in that garden kitchen and the cellar, because the risk does not change with the lease. An owner-occupied single-family brownstone is outside the rule altogether, though nothing stops the owner from installing gas detection anyway.

What it costs the tenant. HPD lets owners of Class A multiple dwellings recover $25 for a single-function detector, $50 for a two-function device and $75 for a three-function device, with the tenant given a year to pay. In one- and two-family homes the figure is $25 per CO or gas detector. Class B buildings recover nothing. A combined smoke, CO and gas device counts toward the gas requirement only if gas detection is one of its listed functions, and it has to be mounted where each of its functions is required.

What to keep on file. HPD asks owners to keep the date the notice was posted in a multiple dwelling, the date each device was installed in each unit or common area, and the date each device reaches the end of its useful life. Our device list gives you all three for each apartment, by room, on the day we finish. HPD also gives owners 30 days to replace a device that fails from a defect within its first year, and the list shows which are still in that window.

Why one visit beats two. The slow part of detector work in a rented brownstone is getting into each apartment at a time the tenant can be there. If a smoke alarm is past its date or the CO alarm is missing, fixing it in the same visit as the gas detector means one notice, one appointment and one updated record. We price smoke, CO and gas work together, apartment by apartment, at our per-unit landlord rate of $400 to $800 per unit, and we can usually tell from photos which apartments need what before anyone visits.

Compliance notes for Carroll Gardens shops and buildings

  • Filed systems start with a design professional. The FDNY's fire alarm tips guide says a New York State registered design professional holding a Professional Engineer or Registered Architect license completes the filing documents, which go online through FDNY Business with the TM-1 application and a valid Certificate of Occupancy or an approved Schedule A.
  • Who installs a filed system. The same guide says that after plans are approved, the installation is done by a company holding an FDNY-issued Fire Alarm Company Certificate. We are a New York State licensed installer, license #12000287431. When a Carroll Gardens job turns out to need a filed system, we tell you so in writing, explain who has to hold which certificate, and work with the design professional and the certified company on the parts that belong to us rather than presenting the whole job as ours.
  • Plan for the calendar. The FDNY guide says initial plan review can take up to 8 weeks, and estimates about twenty to twenty-four weeks from plan submission to final inspection and approval. It also says there is no new filing fee if plans are resubmitted within six months of an objection.
  • Monitoring before inspection. The guide says the central station monitoring connection has to be active before the inspection can be scheduled, the installation has to be complete, and the panel has to be free of alarms, supervisory signals and trouble conditions, with an S97 or S98 Certificate of Fitness holder present at the inspection.
  • Landmarks for anything on the front. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's permit guidebook says equipment "cannot be on or in front of a primary facade unless no other option exists," that most equipment "must be as small as possible and painted to blend with surrounding building material," and that you must "demonstrate the need to install HVAC, life safety, or utility equipment." An exterior bell, strobe or communicator box on a designated building is an LPC question first.
Your Questions Answered

Fire Alarm Installation in Carroll Gardens: Questions Answered

These are the situations Carroll Gardens homeowners, landlords, co-op boards and shop owners bring to us, several of them raised on Brownstoner's forums by brownstone owners in the middle of renovations. Each is answered here in full and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Renovations, conversions and odd floors

Our architect's drawings for a brownstone gut renovation show fifteen smoke and CO devices, and the electrician says one interconnect run can only carry so many. How is that handled?

This exact problem came up on Brownstoner, where an owner turning a brownstone back into a single-family house had drawings showing fifteen smoke and CO devices, a permitted job that called for a hardwired interconnected system, and a limit of twelve interconnected units that the owner understood to come from NFPA 72. Replies suggested a relay on a second circuit so that one group of alarms triggers the other. That is the usual answer. Most hardwired alarm lines publish a maximum number of devices per interconnect line and sell relay modules that let one group set off another. We check the limit for the exact model on your drawings, split the house into lower-floor and upper-floor groups, and join them with the manufacturer's relay so a fire in the cellar still sounds in the top-floor bedrooms. We also look again at the count. The same thread had a reply pointing out that "a hallway detector does NOT cover the bedroom. You need both," which is why a five-level brownstone reaches fourteen or fifteen devices so easily. Combination smoke and CO devices can bring that down without losing coverage. We test the whole set together before the electrician closes up, and the device list shows which group each alarm belongs to.

We are turning our three-family brownstone back into one house. What happens to the alarms that were in the old apartments?

They stop being three separate sets and become one. In a three-family, each apartment had its own smoke and CO devices, usually not linked to the others, each apartment's kitchen had its own range, and the stair belonged to nobody in particular. As a single-family house, the goal changes: a fire anywhere should warn everyone in the house, so the alarms on every floor should be linked into one system. We survey what is up, keep the devices that are in date and compatible, and replace the rest. Old kitchens that become bedrooms need their alarms moved, since a device placed for cooking is in the wrong spot for sleeping, and the remaining gas appliances decide where CO goes. If the de-conversion is a filed alteration, the architect's drawings show the layout the Department of Buildings expects, and we work from those. One tip: if you may rent the garden floor again someday, give it its own devices and a link to the rest of the house now, which is cheaper than reworking it later.

Our garden floor sits partly below the sidewalk, tucked under the stoop. For the detector rules, is that a basement, a cellar or just another floor?

For placing alarms, what matters is how the floor is used, not what it is called. A Carroll Gardens garden floor is usually half below the sidewalk, with windows into the front areaway and a door under the stoop, and in most houses it is a full living floor with a kitchen, a dining or family room and sometimes a bedroom. The cellar is the level below it, with the boiler, the meters and the storage. The FDNY's smoke and CO brochure says basements need smoke alarms too, and asks for CO alarms on each level of a home, especially levels with fuel-burning appliances and outside sleeping areas, so both levels get detection whatever you call them. The garden floor is planned like any living floor, and the cellar gets smoke or heat detection plus CO by the boiler, all linked to the bedrooms upstairs. Whether a garden-floor room can lawfully be used for sleeping is a Certificate of Occupancy question; if someone sleeps there anyway, we still put detection in and tell you plainly that the room is the issue.

The house has two front doors: the parlor door at the top of the stoop and the garden-floor door beneath it. Which one should a monitoring account list as the way in?

List both, and say which one gets firefighters closest to the stair and the bedrooms. In most Carroll Gardens brownstones the parlor door at the top of the stoop opens into the main hall and the stair, while the garden door under the stoop opens into the kitchen level and may be behind a gate in the front garden fence. If the house has a monitored alarm, the account at the central station holds the address, the contacts and the notes that go out with an alarm. We suggest a short note: which door reaches the stair, whether the garden gate is locked, whether the garden floor is a separate apartment, and who can be reached with keys. If a tenant lives in the garden apartment, their contact belongs on the account too, so the center knows two households may be inside. Central station monitoring through our partners is $30 a month with a keypad or $45 a month with the app.

Gas, boilers and carbon monoxide

We converted the parlor fireplace to a gas insert. Does that room now need gas or carbon monoxide detection?

If the parlor floor is part of a rented apartment, yes for gas from January 1, 2027, and CO is wise either way. HPD's rule puts natural gas detection where a gas-burning device is located, and a gas fireplace insert is a gas-burning device in that room, fed by the building's gas piping. So in a rented apartment with a gas insert, the parlor gets a gas detector as well as the kitchen. Carbon monoxide comes from burning fuel, and a gas insert burns fuel in a room where people sit for hours with the doors shut on winter evenings. The FDNY's brochure suggests CO alarms on every level with fuel-burning appliances, and a parlor with a gas insert is exactly that. In an owner-occupied single-family brownstone HPD requires none of this, but a CO alarm on the parlor floor is a small price for a room with a flame in it. We place the gas detector high, because natural gas rises, and away from the insert itself so the normal start-up does not set it off, and we keep the smoke alarm for that floor out of the path of the warm air coming off the fireplace.

Our new high-efficiency boiler vents through the back wall into the yard, right beside the garden apartment's bedroom window. Where does CO detection matter most now?

In that bedroom and on the cellar level where the boiler sits. Many Carroll Gardens brownstones replaced old boilers that vented up the masonry chimney with high-efficiency units that vent sideways through the rear wall, often into the yard at garden-floor height. That puts the exhaust outlet close to the windows of the garden floor, which is often a bedroom or a rented apartment. An open window on a still night, an outlet blocked by snow or a boiler fault can bring exhaust inside. So the garden-floor CO alarm belongs close to that bedroom, as HPD already requires in a rented apartment, with a second CO alarm by the boiler and both linked upstairs. Ask the boiler installer to confirm vent clearances to windows, since that is their trade, not ours. If a CO alarm ever sounds, get out and call 911 first; replacing or moving the alarm comes after the FDNY or the utility has checked the boiler.

Every unit in our three-unit brownstone condo is lived in by its owner. Does that take the building out of HPD's detector rules?

No. The owner-occupied exception in HPD's carbon monoxide and gas rules is written for one- and two-family homes. A building with three or more apartments is a multiple dwelling, and HPD's smoke detector rule covers all multiple dwellings, Class A and Class B, whoever owns the units. The CO and gas rules for multiple dwellings apply wherever there are fossil-fuel appliances and gas piping. So a three-condo brownstone on President Street with gas ranges in each unit and a gas boiler in the cellar has the full set: smoke detection near each bedroom, CO in each apartment, and natural gas detection where the gas appliances are from January 1, 2027, plus the notice in the entrance area and the installation and expiration records. The condo declaration and bylaws usually decide whether the association or each owner handles devices inside the units; the association almost always handles the stair and cellar. Doing the whole building at once gives the association one dated list for every unit and the common areas.

Building systems, shops and schedules

Our 1910 co-op has eight apartments over four floors. At what point would a building like ours need a building fire alarm system, and who decides?

A design professional decides, by reading the building code against how the building is actually built and used. We can tell you what to ask them. Summaries of the code tables for residential apartment buildings, such as the one published by the engineering firm NY Engineers, list fire alarm requirements where dwelling units sit at least three stories above the lowest level of exit discharge, where units sit more than one story below the highest exit discharge, and in buildings with at least sixteen dwelling units, with New York City adding requirements of its own, including for high-rises. A four-story walk-up has apartments on the top floor that may be three stories above the street door, so the first threshold is the one to ask about, and the answer depends on the details of your stair and exits. Age matters too: existing buildings are not automatically held to every rule for new construction, while a major renovation or change of use can bring new requirements. In practice an eight-unit co-op here starts with what is clearly required, HPD smoke, CO and gas detection in each apartment, dated and recorded. If the board wants a full building system, or a renovation triggers one, the engineer designs and files it, and our apartment work can be planned so nothing is torn out later.

Our lease on a Court Street storefront starts in January and we hope to open in the spring. If the space needs a filed fire alarm system, is that realistic?

It is tight, and it depends on starting the design almost at once. The FDNY's own guide estimates about twenty to twenty-four weeks from plan submission to final inspection and approval, with the initial plan review alone taking up to eight weeks. A lease that starts in January with a design submitted in February points to an approval in summer, not spring, if everything goes smoothly. Objections from the plan examiner send the drawings back for changes, and although resubmitting within six months avoids a new fee, each round adds time. The monitoring connection also has to be active and the installation complete before the inspection can be scheduled. So first find out whether your space needs a filed system at all; many small Court Street shops need only detection suited to the space and a way to warn the families upstairs, which goes in within days. If your design professional says it does, ask them to file at once, line up the fire alarm company and the monitoring contract early, and we can survey during review so installation starts the week plans are approved.

Our house will be under scaffolding for months while masons restore the stoop and cornice. Should anything change with the alarms inside while it is up?

The alarms should stay exactly as they are, working, and two things deserve attention. The St. Mary Star of the Sea fire on Court Street in October 2024 happened on the outside of a building with scaffolding up for facade repairs, and while the cause was not reported, it is a reminder that construction on the outside of a building brings its own risks: tools, power cords, debris and sometimes heat or flame from the work. Keep every alarm in service, and ask masons not to cover or remove the ones near their windows, or to put them back the same day. Make sure the top-floor alarms, the ones closest to the cornice and roof, are working and linked to the floors below. If the house is monitored, tell the monitoring center about the work schedule.

Our dryer is in a closet on the garden floor. After the Columbia Street dryer fire, is a heat detector or a smoke alarm better next to the laundry?

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Fire Alarm Installation in Carroll Gardens

Generic answers from AI tools and national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr are written for an average American house on one or two floors. These are the shortcuts that mislead Carroll Gardens owners most often.

"A smoke alarm on each floor is enough for a house"

One per floor is a common rule of thumb in generic advice, and in a brownstone it falls short. The FDNY's brochure places smoke alarms within fifteen feet of the entrance to each sleeping room, inside each sleeping room in newer buildings, and in basements too.

A Brownstoner reply put it bluntly for a renovation: "A hallway detector does NOT cover the bedroom. You need both." A top floor with three bedrooms off a short hall needs more than one device.

Count it out for a five-level house with four or five bedrooms and CO near the boiler and the bedrooms, and twelve to fifteen devices is normal. That count is what makes linking and labeling worth the effort.

"Battery alarms and hardwired alarms are interchangeable"

For an existing house, sealed ten-year battery alarms are often the right choice, and New York's 2019 sales rule requires new alarms sold in the state to have a sealed battery of at least ten years or be hardwired.

But the same Department of State announcement said installation is still governed by the fire and building codes, and a renovation filed with the Department of Buildings typically brings hardwired, interconnected alarms onto the drawings, as the Brownstoner owner with fifteen devices found.

So the question is not which kind is better in general. It is what your job requires and what your walls allow. Open walls in a gut renovation favor hardwired; finished plaster in a house you live in often favors sealed-battery alarms with wireless linking.

"Landmarked houses can't have modern fire alarms"

Living in the Carroll Gardens Historic District does not stop you from protecting the inside of your house. Smoke, CO and gas detection mounted inside the rooms does not change the facade the district protects.

Where landmarks do come in is the outside. The LPC's guidebook says equipment cannot go on or in front of a primary facade unless no other option exists, must be as small as possible and painted to blend in, and that the need for life safety equipment has to be shown.

So an exterior bell, strobe or antenna box on a brownstone front is an LPC question, and we raise it before anything is ordered. The detection that actually saves lives is almost always indoors.

"A smoke and CO combination alarm covers gas leaks too"

It does not. Carbon monoxide comes from burning fuel. Natural gas is the fuel itself, leaking before anything burns, and a CO sensor is not built to detect it.

HPD's natural gas requirement from January 1, 2027 is a separate duty, with its own placement where the gas-burning device is located. A combination device counts toward it only if gas detection is one of the device's listed functions.

HPD does allow combined devices and caps the Class A tenant's charge for a three-function smoke, CO and gas device at $75. Read the box, not the marketing, before buying for a rented apartment.

"Owner-occupied brownstones are exempt from the city's detector rules"

Only partly, and only for one- and two-family houses. HPD's CO and gas rules reach one- and two-family homes the owner does not occupy, so an owner living in a two-family with a rented garden apartment is in a gray zone worth thinking about.

A brownstone with three or more apartments is a multiple dwelling, and HPD's smoke detector rule covers every multiple dwelling, whether or not the owner lives there and whether the units are condos, co-op apartments or rentals.

Many Carroll Gardens houses have been split and rejoined over the years, so it is worth checking how many households your house actually holds today. That number, not the name on the deed, decides which rules apply.

"Fire alarm approval in New York takes a few weeks"

For a filed system, it takes months. The FDNY's own guide says initial plan review can take up to eight weeks and estimates about twenty to twenty-four weeks from submission to final approval.

The central station connection must be live and the installation complete before the inspection can be scheduled, and objections send plans back for another review, though a resubmission within six months avoids a new fee.

Household alarms in a brownstone need none of that and can go in within days. Knowing which kind of job you have is the first thing a good installer should tell you, before quoting either.

"Landlords can recover whatever the detectors cost"

HPD sets the limits: $25 for a single-function detector, $50 for a two-function device and $75 for a three-function device for Class A tenants, who have a year from installation to pay.

Class B buildings recover nothing, and in one- and two-family homes the figure is $25 per CO or natural gas detector. Labor, travel and the owner's time are not something the rules let an owner pass on.

The owner also stays responsible for having working devices in place whether or not the tenant pays. Plan the cost of the 2027 gas detectors as an owner's expense, with the small allowed charge as a partial offset.

FAQ

Carroll Gardens Fire Alarm Installation FAQ

Is a wireless linked set a step down from hardwired alarms in a finished brownstone?
+

Not when the job does not call for hardwiring. New York's 2019 rule lets alarms sold in the state be either hardwired or sealed with a battery lasting at least ten years, and sealed wireless sets link every floor without fishing cable through finished plaster. We test the radio link through the brick and floors on site. Where a renovation is filed and the drawings show hardwired alarms, those drawings decide, and we follow them.

How many smoke and CO devices does a typical three-story Carroll Gardens brownstone end up with?
+

Usually between ten and fifteen for a house with a cellar, garden floor, parlor floor and two bedroom floors. Each bedroom gets one inside, each sleeping area gets one outside, each level including the cellar gets coverage, and CO goes near the bedrooms and by the boiler. The survey gives the exact number.

We are adding a roof deck reached through the bulkhead. Does the top of the stair need anything new?
+

Check that the top-floor stair landing has a smoke alarm linked to the rest of the house, since that landing becomes the route to and from the deck. Alarms do not belong outdoors on the deck itself. If the deck has a gas grill or heater fed by the building's gas line, tell us, because a rented building's gas detection follows its gas appliances. Deck construction itself is a question for your architect and the Department of Buildings.

Will drilling during the installation set off the alarms we already have?
+

Plaster and brick dust can trigger a nearby smoke alarm, so we plan for it. The technician uses dust control at each hole, works one area at a time, and briefly covers or removes only the alarm nearest the work, putting it back before leaving that floor. If the house is monitored, we put the account on test with the monitoring center first, so dust does not send the fire department.

Can our linked alarms turn on the stair lights when they go off?
+

Often, yes. Several hardwired alarm lines sell relay modules that switch a light or another device when the linked alarms sound, and lighting the stair at night helps everyone find the way down a narrow brownstone stair. The relay has to come from the same manufacturer as the alarms, and the lighting connection is electrical work that your electrician completes. We tell you whether your chosen alarms support it before you buy anything.

Every smoke alarm sold in New York now meets the 2019 battery law. Does that make any alarm we buy compliant once it is up?
+

No. The Department of State said the 2019 rule covers what stores may sell, sealed ten-year battery or hardwired, and that installation is still governed by the fire and building codes. An alarm that meets the sales rule can still be mounted in the wrong spot, miss a bedroom, sit too close to a stove or fail to link with the rest of the house. Placement, linking and records are what make it compliant in your house.

Does the 2027 gas detector rule cover a propane grill out on our back deck?
+

Not as HPD describes the rule. HPD ties natural gas detectors to buildings with gas piping and places them where a gas-burning device is located inside the home. A portable propane grill on a deck is not fed by the building's gas piping. A grill or heater plumbed into the house's gas line is a different case, and we would ask how that line runs indoors. Keep propane tanks outside, away from doors and windows.

Does each gas appliance get its own natural gas detector, or is it one per room?
+

HPD's wording places detectors where the gas-burning device is located, so in practice it is one per room or area with gas appliances. A cellar with the boiler, water heater and dryer side by side is usually covered by one well-placed detector, while a kitchen range on the garden floor and a gas insert on the parlor floor are in different rooms and need their own. We map each appliance on the survey before counting detectors.

Our renovation moves the kitchen from the garden floor up to the parlor floor. What happens to the alarms?
+

They move with the rooms. The smoke alarm for the new kitchen floor goes away from the range, the old kitchen space is planned for its new use, and a heat alarm can cover the new kitchen if a smoke alarm there would sound with every meal. In a rented apartment the gas detector moves to wherever the range ends up. CO stays tied to the bedrooms and the boiler, which usually do not move.

Nobody sleeps in our cellar. Does it still need a carbon monoxide alarm next to the boiler?
+

We recommend one. The FDNY's brochure suggests CO alarms on each level of a home, especially levels with fuel-burning appliances, and the cellar is where the boiler and water heater burn gas all winter. HPD's rules focus CO alarms near sleeping rooms, which you also need. A cellar alarm linked to the floors above warns of a problem at its source, often before it reaches the bedrooms.

Can you give our architect a device layout to put on the renovation drawings?
+

Yes. After a survey, or from the architect's floor plans, we mark each smoke, CO, heat and gas device by floor and room, show which alarms are linked in which groups, and note box locations so the electrician can rough them in before the walls close. The architect remains responsible for the drawings filed with the Department of Buildings. Our layout is a working document they can adopt or adjust.
