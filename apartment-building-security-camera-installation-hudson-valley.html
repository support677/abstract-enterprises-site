The Neighborhood

Why Hudson Valley Apartment Properties Need Security Cameras

North of the city line, an apartment building is usually not one building. It is a group of them. The suburban garden apartment — low-rise buildings set in green space, generally no more than three storeys, without elevators or standpipes, with parking close by, as a Fire Engineering guide to the building type describes it — is the backbone of rental housing from Yonkers and Port Chester up through Spring Valley, Middletown, Poughkeepsie and Kingston. When Hamilton Garden Apartments on South Hamilton Street in Poughkeepsie changed hands in January 2026, the sale listing described it the way thousands of valley rentals could be described: 58 units in seven two-storey brick buildings put up between 1925 and 1965, on 2.55 acres, with four laundry rooms, 70 parking spaces and a resident superintendent. Cameras for a property like that are a different job from cameras for a Bronx walk-up. The cable does not go up a riser; it goes across a parking lot, under a lawn and into the next building.

The problems that make owners call are the ones the local news keeps reporting. In December 2024, News 12 reported three people arraigned for stealing packages from a building lobby on Bronx River Road in Yonkers — and the case moved because officials had footage appearing to show them walking in and carrying the packages out. In April 2026 police in Haverstraw recovered multiple packages stolen inside a housing complex. In February 2025 a man in New Rochelle came out to a lot on John Street to find his car window smashed and the airbag gone; neighbours said cars there had been hit three times that week, and what he asked ABC7 for was simple: "I would like some cameras in the nighttime." Car prowling is a lot problem, and lots are where valley tenants keep their cars. Scarsdale's Detective Lieutenant Brendan Kellaher told News 12 in 2024 that nearly every break-in he could recall in four years involved an unlocked car — which is exactly why the footage, not the lock, is what identifies the person trying door handles at 3 a.m.

The rules are different up here too, and that changes what a camera system is for. New York City buildings are governed by the Multiple Dwelling Law. Everywhere else in this region — every city under 325,000 people and every town and village — falls under the state's Multiple Residence Law. Twelve Hudson Valley municipalities, from White Plains, Croton-on-Hudson and Nyack to Beacon, Newburgh, Middletown, both Poughkeepsies, New Paltz, Kingston and Hudson, have adopted their own versions of Good Cause Eviction, according to the State Senate's 2025 count. In June 2025 the Court of Appeals upheld Kingston's rent-stabilization emergency and a 15 percent rent cut. In buildings like these, a landlord's word against a tenant's is not enough. A dated, unedited clip of who propped the back door, who dumped the mattress in the lot or who damaged the laundry machine is the record that settles it fairly — for both sides.

Then there is the weather and the distance. In June 2024, thunderstorms with winds around 60 mph left a combined 60,000 Orange & Rockland and Central Hudson customers without power, the Times Union reported. A camera system that goes dark with the power is useless on exactly the night something happens. Many valley properties also sit at the far end of a long drive for any installer, so they need equipment that comes back by itself after an outage, recorders that can be checked remotely and a contractor who will actually make the trip to Saugerties or Pine Bush. That is the work this page covers. We are a licensed low-voltage contractor; we plan, cable, mount, configure and support camera systems for apartment properties, and we tell you plainly when something — a new circuit on a light pole, a new outlet in a closet — is an electrician's job instead.

Building Specialists

Security Cameras for Hudson Valley Apartment Architecture

The valley's rental stock spans a century, and each era hides cable differently. These are the building types we see most often between the Bronx line and the Catskill foothills.

Garden-Apartment Complexes

Two- and three-storey buildings around a lot, often in matched pairs or rows. Mid-century ones commonly have concrete-block walls and precast concrete floor planks; newer ones are wood frame. The camera plan is a property plan: entrances, stairs, laundry, lot, trash and the road.

River-Town Walk-Ups and Apartments Over Stores

Brick buildings on the main streets of Ossining, Peekskill, Nyack, Beacon, Newburgh and Kingston, with a storefront below and two or three floors of apartments. Westchester alone has more than 109,000 homes built in 1939 or earlier, per Census data reported by Stacker. Stair halls are narrow and the cellar is the only practical home for the recorder.

Mid-Century Elevator Mid-Rises

Six- to twelve-storey brick buildings in Yonkers, Mount Vernon, New Rochelle and White Plains, closer to city buildings in layout: a lobby, an elevator, a compactor room, a garage ramp. These get a riser plan like a city job, plus the outside lot.

New Transit-Oriented Buildings

Mid-size buildings going up next to Metro-North stations — including the 265-home mixed-income project announced in July 2025 for the state lot beside Beacon station. Cameras are best planned before the walls close, with pathways and a camera closet on the drawings.

Converted Mills and Factory Lofts

Masonry industrial buildings turned into apartments along the valley's rivers and creeks. Thick brick and timber, exposed ceilings and long corridors; surface-mounted conduit is often the only honest route, so we make it neat.

Two- to Four-Family Houses

Small rentals in Newburgh, Poughkeepsie, Middletown and Spring Valley where the owner may live on the first floor. A handful of cameras on the entrances, the driveway and the yard, recorded indoors, with tenants' doors left out of view.

Senior and Affordable Housing

Buildings with community rooms, long corridors and residents who may not open the door to strangers. Coverage leans toward entrances, the community room and the lot, with clear signage and a written policy on who views footage.

Condo, Co-op and HOA Communities

Townhouse-style associations where the common elements are roads, mail kiosks, pools and clubhouses rather than hallways. The board owns the system; the plan follows the declaration and what the association actually controls.

Commercial Security Cameras

Business Security Cameras in Hudson Valley Mixed-Use Buildings

A lot of valley apartments sit above a business, and the camera system has to serve both without either seeing too much of the other. The storefront owner wants the register and the sidewalk. The building owner wants the residential entrance, the stairs and the trash area. Tenants upstairs want their door left alone. We usually build one recorder with separate user groups — the shop sees its cameras, the landlord sees the common areas — or two systems on one shared network where the leases make that cleaner. Main Street and Mamaroneck Avenue, White Plains

Older low-rise buildings with shops below and apartments above, alongside newer residential towers downtown. Sidewalk cameras here must avoid neighbours' windows and focus on the building's own doors.

Route 59, Nanuet to Spring Valley

Strip plazas, garden complexes set back from the road and mixed-use buildings in Spring Valley's village centre. Lots are big and lighting is uneven, so plate-reading entrances matter more than wide shots.

Compliance callouts for Hudson Valley apartment cameras

Never in private spaces. New York's unlawful surveillance statute, Penal Law §250.45, makes it a class E felony to record people without consent in a bedroom, changing room, restroom, toilet, bathroom, washroom or shower for no legitimate purpose, and installing a device in those places creates a presumption that there was none. We do not put cameras in shared restrooms, in laundry rooms with changing areas, or anywhere a camera could see into a unit.
Video yes, overheard audio no. New York is a one-party-consent state: recording a conversation you are not part of, without any participant's consent, is a felony under Penal Law §§250.00 and 250.05, according to the Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press. Building cameras ship with microphones turned off and stay that way in common areas. Intercom audio, where a resident is a party to the call, is a different matter.
Multiple Residence Law, not the Multiple Dwelling Law. Outside New York City, apartment buildings fall under the state Multiple Residence Law, which covers every city under 325,000 people and every town and village. City-specific rules you may read about online — including New York City's lobby-door intercom requirement for larger buildings — don't automatically apply here; your local building department and code officer do.
Licensing. AESS holds New York State licence #12000287431. Westchester County requires home-improvement contractors to be licensed by its Consumer Protection Department, and Putnam and Rockland run their own home-improvement licensing; ask any installer for the card for your county before work starts.
Dig safely. Trenching across a lot or lawn requires a call to 811 so that gas, electric and water lines are marked first. We place the request and wait for the markings; nobody digs on a guess.
Footage is evidence, not a substitute for repairs. In Good Cause and rent-stabilized municipalities, cameras document what happens in common areas. They don't replace the owner's obligation to fix a broken door lock or light — and footage of a broken door that stays broken can cut both ways.

Six Counties, Six Different Apartment Markets

"The Hudson Valley" covers a wealthy inner suburb, a growing exurb and a rural county with a small city in the middle. The camera plans we draw look different in each, and so does the pricing. Three counties have their own dedicated page; the others are served from this hub.

Westchester

The most urban county in the region and the one with the most renters: 38.2 percent of occupied homes are rented, per Census QuickFacts. Yonkers, Mount Vernon and New Rochelle have true elevator buildings and dense blocks of walk-ups; White Plains and Croton-on-Hudson have adopted Good Cause Eviction; the river villages have old brick buildings over shops. Work here looks most like city work — risers, compactor rooms, garage ramps — with a lot of package theft and car prowling in the brief. See the dedicated Westchester County apartment building security camera installation page.

Rockland

Dense for a suburb, with high rents — a $2,752 two-bedroom fair market rent in 2024 — and a mix of garden complexes along Route 59, two-family houses and newer mid-size buildings. Spring Valley's buildings are now inspected by the county's Office of Buildings and Codes, and Nyack has its own Good Cause law. Haverstraw's housing-complex package arrest in 2026 is the kind of case we design mailrooms around. More on the Rockland County apartment camera page.

Orange

Newburgh and Middletown carry most of the county's multi-family housing, both with Good Cause laws on the books, and the I-84 and Route 17 corridors are filling with newer complexes built around big surface lots. Distances are longer and lighting is often weaker than in Westchester, so night-capable lot cameras and plate-reading entrances do much of the work. Visit the Orange County apartment building camera page.

Putnam

The most owner-occupied county in the valley, at 83.8 percent. Rentals here are mostly small complexes and apartments in the hamlets of Brewster, Carmel and Mahopac, plus condo and townhouse associations whose boards manage roads, mail kiosks and clubhouses. A Putnam job is often a dozen cameras over a wide property rather than fifty cameras in one building. Putnam requests are quoted and scheduled from this page.

Dutchess

Poughkeepsie city and town, Beacon and the Town of Fishkill have all adopted Good Cause. Downtown Poughkeepsie mixes walk-ups and garden complexes like the 58-unit property on South Hamilton Street; Beacon is adding transit-oriented apartments by the train; the Route 9 corridor has the larger complexes. Dutchess requests are quoted and scheduled from this page.

Ulster

Kingston is the centre of gravity: a small city with rent stabilization upheld by the state's highest court, Good Cause on the books, and a historic Uptown full of older brick buildings. New Paltz adds student-heavy rentals. The rural towns beyond have slower internet, which shapes how remote viewing is set up. Ulster requests are quoted and scheduled from this page.

Your Questions Answered

Apartment Building Security Cameras in the Hudson Valley: Questions Answered

These are the questions owners, boards and managers in the valley ask us on the phone and on walkthroughs, grouped the way the decisions actually get made.

Budget and Planning

What does a garden-apartment complex in the Hudson Valley budget for cameras, county by county?

Our installed, all-in range per camera runs from $625–$1,875 in Westchester to $765–$2,295 in Dutchess and Ulster, with Rockland, Orange and Putnam in between (full table in the pricing section below). A small complex covering entrances, the mailroom, laundry and part of the lot — four to eight cameras — lands between $6,250–$15,000 in Westchester and $7,650–$18,360 in Dutchess or Ulster. Those ranges include cameras, recorder, cable, trenching or poles where needed, backup power, configuration and app setup. The fixed number comes from the walkthrough, because the distance between buildings moves the price more than the camera count does.

Why does the same camera count cost more in Dutchess or Ulster than in Westchester?

Our published county multipliers reflect travel time, crew days and the kind of property we typically find. Westchester jobs are close to our crews and buildings tend to be compact. Upstate jobs mean longer drives, more single-visit scheduling, wider properties with longer cable runs, and more lots without existing poles or conduit. The equipment costs the same everywhere; the labour and logistics don't. We publish the multipliers so a manager with properties in two counties can see why the quotes differ before asking.

Our complex has eight buildings spread over several acres — do we need a recorder in every building?

Usually not. One recorder in the most central, heated and dry room can see the whole property if each building is linked to it — by fiber or outdoor-rated cable in a trench, by an existing conduit if there is one, or by a point-to-point wireless bridge where trenching across a lot or road isn't practical. Each outlying building gets a small weatherproof or indoor network cabinet with a switch and its own battery backup. One recorder means one app, one retention setting and one place to pull footage. Very large properties sometimes get two recorders for redundancy, still viewed in a single app.

Which spots on a garden complex get covered first when the budget is tight?

In order: the property entrance from the road (every car and most people pass it), the mailroom or mailbox kiosk, each building's main entrance, the laundry room door, and the dumpster enclosure. The lot comes next, starting with the rows farthest from occupied windows. Breezeways, rear doors and storage come last. A phased proposal lets a board approve phase one now and the rest next year, with the recorder and cabling sized for the full plan on day one so phase two isn't a rebuild.

Coverage on Garden Complexes

Can cameras cover the half-below-grade garden-level entrances and the breezeways between buildings?

Yes, and they are some of the most important views on the property because they are hidden from the street. Garden-level doors sit below the sightline of anyone passing, so a camera mounted under the eave or on the stair landing above looks down the steps toward the door. Open breezeways and stair towers get a camera at each end aimed along the walk, never into unit doors. Where a breezeway is the only route between the lot and the units, it is often the single most useful camera on the building.

The construction matters for the mounting. Mid-century garden buildings with block walls and precast concrete floor planks take masonry anchors and surface-mounted cable; drilling through a plank for a hidden run is rarely worth the risk. Newer wood-frame buildings allow concealed runs through attics and soffits. We look at both before quoting.

Parking Lots, Poles and Trenching

Our surface lot has no conduit — do you trench, go wireless, or mount on the light poles?

All three are on the table, and the walkthrough decides. Building-mounted cameras with good lenses cover a lot of most lots without any trenching. Existing light poles can carry cameras if there is power at the pole and the owner controls it; adding a camera circuit or a new pole base is an electrician's job we coordinate. Trenching gives the most reliable result for distant rows and the entrance, and we run conduit large enough for future cameras. Wireless bridges suit a single far pole or a detached garage row where trenching would mean cutting a paved drive.

Will cameras actually read plates in an unlit lot off Route 59 or Route 9 at 2 a.m.?

Not from a wide overview camera, and that is the most common disappointment we're called to fix. Plate reading needs a dedicated camera aimed at a choke point — the entrance lane or a speed bump — with a narrow field of view, a fast shutter and its own infrared illumination, set up at night with a test car. Overview cameras in the lot then show what a car did once it was inside. Colour night imaging helps identify vehicles and clothing; plates still need the dedicated camera. We check the entrance at night before signing off.

Do you have to call 811 before trenching across our property?

Yes. Any excavation means calling 811 first so the utilities mark their lines, and we don't dig until the markings are down and the waiting period has passed. Private lines the utilities don't mark — site lighting, irrigation, a feed to a detached garage — are a separate question; we ask the owner or super for any drawings and, where the risk justifies it, hand-dig near the marks. The trench schedule is built around this, which is one reason lot jobs are booked a little further out.

Power, Weather and Internet

What happens to the cameras when Central Hudson or O&R loses power for a day?

With no backup, everything stops the moment the power does. Every system we design puts a battery backup on the recorder and on each network switch that powers cameras, sized to keep the system recording through a typical outage and to shut the recorder down cleanly if the outage outlasts the battery. Cameras restart on their own when power returns, and the recorder resumes without anyone touching it. For buildings with a standby generator, we put the camera equipment on the generator-backed circuit where the electrician can arrange it. After the June 2024 storms that left 60,000 customers in the dark, that question gets asked at almost every walkthrough.

Our building is on a slow rural line — can owners still watch cameras remotely?

Yes, with the right settings. Recording happens locally on the recorder, so a slow internet line never affects what is captured. Remote viewing uses a lower-resolution sub-stream that works on modest upload speeds, and full-resolution clips can be exported on site or downloaded overnight. In parts of rural Ulster still waiting on the county's ConnectALL fiber build, a cellular router can serve as the remote-access path. We test remote viewing from a phone off the property's network before we leave.

How do cameras handle ice storms and drifting snow on the north side of the buildings?

Outdoor cameras rated for low temperatures and sealed housings ride out valley winters well. Problems come from placement: a camera under a roof edge where ice builds and drips, or one aimed where plowed snow piles up against a pole. We mount under eaves with a drip edge, keep lenses out of the plow's spray path and avoid aiming directly into low winter sun on a south-facing lot. Cable entries are sealed and connections are kept inside junction boxes so meltwater can't wick into them.

Tenants, Footage and New York Law

We're in a Good Cause Eviction city — does adding cameras change anything with our tenants?

Cameras in common areas are a normal building improvement, and nothing about Good Cause stops an owner from installing them. What changes is how the footage tends to be used. In a Good Cause municipality, disputes about lease violations, damage or nuisance are more likely to need evidence, and clear, time-stamped footage of common areas is exactly that. Treat the system as a neutral record: point it at shared spaces, keep a written policy on who reviews footage and why, and don't aim cameras at a particular tenant's door. This is general information, not legal advice; your attorney should review any eviction strategy.

Our Kingston building is rent-stabilized under ETPA — is a camera system a service change for tenants?

Adding cameras to common areas generally adds to the building rather than taking a service away, but in a rent-stabilized building anything that touches services, equipment or rent increases should be run past your attorney or the state housing agency before you plan on recovering the cost through rents. What we can do is document the installation clearly — what was installed, where, and when — so your records are complete. Kingston's emergency declaration and the 15 percent rent reduction were upheld by the Court of Appeals in 2025, so owners there are rightly careful.

Outside New York City, which state law covers our apartment building — the Multiple Dwelling Law or something else?

Something else. The Multiple Dwelling Law applies to cities of 325,000 or more, which in practice means New York City. Every Hudson Valley city, town and village falls under the Multiple Residence Law instead, along with the state building and fire codes enforced by your local building department. That's why advice written for city buildings — about lobby intercoms, for example — can be misleading up here. For cameras, the practical rules are the privacy statutes, your local code office's view on penetrations and fire-stopping, and the lease.

Can a camera go in a laundry room or a shared restroom at a Hudson Valley complex?

A shared restroom, never: Penal Law §250.45 treats a recording device there as presumptively unlawful, and it is a felony. A laundry room is usually fine when it is purely a laundry room — machines, folding table, no changing area — with the camera covering the door and machines and a sign posted. If the room has a restroom off it or people use it to change, we move the camera outside to cover the entrance instead. We'll walk the room with you and decide together.

A tenant asked for footage of a car break-in — do we have to hand it over?

There's no blanket rule in New York requiring an owner to give footage to a tenant, and most owners route requests through the police: the tenant files a report, the officer asks for the clip, and the manager exports it. Tenants on city forums often complain that a building's cameras were "down" or that management won't look without a police report — so the fix is a short written policy that says who can request footage, how fast it will be reviewed and how long recordings are kept. We set up export in a format police can play, and we train the super to pull a clip in a couple of minutes. Get legal advice for your own policy.

Do cameras in a Hudson Valley lobby or mailroom need audio turned off?

Yes, in common areas. Because New York requires at least one party's consent to record a conversation, a camera microphone that picks up residents chatting at the mailboxes records conversations nobody consented to. We disable audio on every common-area camera and confirm it in the recorder settings at handover. Video intercoms are different: the resident answering the call is a party to it. If an owner wants audio somewhere specific, such as a leasing office counter with a posted notice, we'll talk it through, but the default is off.

Does our county require a home-improvement licence for a camera installer?

It depends on the county and the job, and the safest course is to ask. Westchester's Consumer Protection Department licenses home-improvement contractors and publishes a list of licensed firms; Putnam and Rockland run home-improvement licensing too. Separately, New York's Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers under General Business Law Article 6-D for systems that detect intrusion or movement. Our Spring Valley building gets county code inspections — will cameras be looked at?

Inspectors look at code issues, not camera coverage, but our work touches things they do check: holes through fire-rated walls and floors, wiring in stairwells and exits, and anything that blocks egress. We fire-stop every penetration, keep cable off egress paths and out of the way of doors, and never mount equipment where it reduces headroom in a stair. Existing Systems and Upgrades

We inherited an analog system from the last owner — can it be reused?

Often partly. Coaxial cable that tests clean can carry newer high-definition analog cameras, which is the cheapest meaningful upgrade. In many cases the better long-term move is to replace coax with network cable as walls and ceilings allow and convert to IP cameras, but we don't pull good cable out just to sell new cable. We test every run first, label what's reusable and price both options side by side, so the owner chooses with the numbers in hand.

Can you take over a system another installer left with no passwords?

Usually. Most recorders can be reset or recovered by the manufacturer's process once the owner proves ownership; occasionally a locked recorder simply has to be replaced. Either way we document every camera, re-secure the system with new credentials for owner, manager and super, update firmware, and give the owner a written record of logins and settings so the next transition — new manager, new owner — doesn't start from zero.

In most cases they can be linked so the door event and the camera clip line up, and some intercom platforms can show a building camera inside their app. How deep the integration goes depends on the intercom model and the recorder. At minimum we make sure the camera over the door and the intercom's own camera together give a clear face shot of anyone entering, and that both clocks are synchronized so footage can be matched to door logs.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Apartment Building Security Cameras in the Hudson Valley

Search engines and chat assistants now answer camera questions before anyone calls an installer. They pull from national cost guides and home-security blogs written for a single-family house. Here is where those answers go wrong for a valley apartment property.

"A security camera system costs about $1,300."

That figure comes straight from cost aggregators. HomeAdvisor's guide puts the national average at $1,298, a typical range of $593 to $2,040 and $125 to $450 per camera including labour. Those numbers describe a homeowner putting four cameras on a house. They are accurate for what they measure and almost meaningless for a garden complex.

An apartment property's cost isn't in the cameras; it's in the distance. A complex like the seven-building, 70-space property on South Hamilton Street in Poughkeepsie needs cable between buildings, network cabinets in each, a recorder sized for weeks of retention across dozens of cameras, battery backup and somebody who knows how to trench a lot without hitting a gas line. Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr don't price any of that because their average customer doesn't need it.

Use the aggregator figure as a floor for one building's front door. For a full property, use our county ranges — $625–$1,875 per camera in Westchester up to $765–$2,295 in Dutchess and Ulster, all-in — and expect the walkthrough to move you within that range based on how spread out the buildings are.

"Wireless cameras are easiest for apartment buildings."

For a tenant watching their own door, maybe. For a property owner, consumer Wi-Fi cameras are the most common thing we replace. They depend on a router somewhere in the building, they drop off when the signal fades through block walls, and battery models need recharging on a ladder every few months — on a pole in a lot in January.

What does work wirelessly on a valley property is the link between buildings: a pair of point-to-point radios carrying a whole building's cameras across a parking lot to the recorder, with the cameras themselves wired to a switch in the outlying building. That keeps the reliability of cable where it matters and avoids cutting a paved drive.

The honest answer is "wired cameras, wireless where the distance justifies it." Anyone recommending a property full of battery cameras hasn't been back to service them.

"Cloud storage is safer than a local recorder."

Cloud recording protects footage if a recorder is stolen, and that's a real benefit. But it means every camera uploads constantly, which a rural Ulster or Dutchess property on a modest connection can't sustain for twenty or forty cameras, and it usually means a subscription per camera, forever.

The approach that holds up here is local recording on a recorder locked in a secure room, with remote access through an app and, where the owner wants it, selected clips or event snapshots backed up off site. Recording continues during an internet outage, which in a storm is exactly when the internet goes out.

If an AI answer tells you to put a garden complex entirely in the cloud, ask it how much upload bandwidth forty cameras need. Then check your bill.

"You need to notify tenants and follow your state's apartment camera law."

AI summaries often blend New York City rules with state law and with other states' statutes, then present the mix as "New York's camera law." There isn't one tidy apartment camera statute. What actually governs a Hudson Valley building is a handful of pieces: the unlawful surveillance law that bars cameras in bathrooms and similar private spaces, the eavesdropping law that makes overheard audio a problem, the Multiple Residence Law and local codes, and the lease.

The City rules that show up in search results — including the Multiple Dwelling Law's requirements for lobby doors and intercoms in larger buildings — apply to cities of 325,000 or more. Yonkers, White Plains, Newburgh, Poughkeepsie and Kingston aren't covered by them.

Posting signs and putting the camera policy in writing is still smart practice, and it costs nothing. Just don't treat a chatbot's paragraph as the law; treat it as a reason to ask your attorney.

"Higher resolution solves everything."

Assistants love to recommend 4K. Resolution helps, but on a parking lot at night it's rarely the limiting factor. A wide 4K camera across a dark lot still can't read a moving plate at 60 feet, and it fills the recorder faster, which shortens retention. The New Rochelle driver who asked for "cameras in the nighttime" didn't need more pixels; he needed the right camera, aimed at the right spot, with light.

What actually identifies people and cars is placement: face-height cameras at doors, dedicated plate cameras at the entrance lane, and enough illumination — infrared or the lot lighting — that shutter speed can stay fast. Then resolution matters, in the right places.

We specify resolution per view. Doors and plates get the detail; wide overviews get enough to show what happened. That keeps retention long enough to be useful when someone comes asking two weeks later.

"Any security company can install apartment cameras."

Many can install a camera. Fewer will design a system for eight buildings, trench a lot safely, fire-stop penetrations so a code inspector doesn't write the building up, and come back in two years when a drive fails. Valley properties are also a long way from many installers' home bases, and some companies won't make the trip for a service call.

Ask for the state licence, the county home-improvement licence where it applies, a certificate of insurance naming your board or managing agent, a written scope listing every camera and where it looks, and who answers the phone after installation.

AESS holds NYS licence #12000287431, carries insurance, schedules two-hour arrival windows seven days a week, and services what it installs across all six counties.

"Cameras deter crime, so more is always better."

Visible cameras can discourage casual crime, and the Yonkers lobby case shows how footage helps police after the fact. But a property bristling with cameras pointed at tenants' doors and windows creates resentment, invites complaints and, in a Good Cause or rent-stabilized building, can look like targeting.

The best systems we've designed are disciplined: every camera has a stated purpose on the drawing, common areas only, nothing aimed into units, signs at the entrances and a written policy on who reviews footage. That reads as a building looking after its residents rather than watching them.

Fewer, better-placed cameras with a plan behind them beat a scattering of cheap ones every time — and cost less to maintain.

Field Notes

From the Installer's Side: Lots, Laundry Rooms and the Far Building

General practice notes from our crews, not a story about a specific property.

On a garden complex the first thing I do is walk the lot at night, not during the day. In daylight every camera position looks fine. After dark you find the pole light that's been out for a year, the corner where headlights flare straight into a lens, and the back row by the tree line where nobody can see anything at all. Those are the places where cars get hit, and that's where the plan starts.

The second thing is the far building. There's always one — across the lot, past the dumpsters, with the laundry room nobody watches. If the owner only budgets for the buildings near the office, that's the one that ends up on the police report. Getting a link out to it, whether it's a trench along the curb or a radio on the roof, is usually worth more than three extra cameras on the main building.

The last thing is the person who'll actually use the system: the super. If he can't find a clip from last Tuesday in two minutes, the system doesn't exist as far as the tenants are concerned. So handover isn't a five-minute demo. We sit down, he pulls three clips himself, exports one to a thumb drive, and keeps the one-page guide we leave taped inside the recorder cabinet.

Retention and Export

Storage, Retention and Getting Footage Off the Recorder

Retention is the number of days a recorder keeps footage before overwriting it, and it's the setting that decides whether a system is useful. Incidents at apartment properties are often reported late. A tenant returns from a trip to find a car damaged; a package theft is noticed a week later; an insurance claim arrives a month after a fall. If the recorder only holds five days, the footage is gone before anyone asks for it.

For most valley properties we design for thirty days as a minimum, and longer for entrances and mailrooms where the board wants it. Retention depends on how many cameras record, at what resolution and frame rate, whether they record continuously or on motion, and how much storage is installed. We size the drives to the target, then check after the first week that the recorder is actually achieving it, because real-world motion on a busy lot uses more storage than any calculator predicts.

Exporting is the other half. When police ask for a clip, they need a file they can play, with the date and time visible and the original preserved. We set up export to USB and to a shared link, show the super how to export the right camera and time range, and recommend keeping a simple log of what was exported, when and to whom. That log protects the owner if the footage later becomes evidence in a dispute or a court case.

Footage Policy

Who Sees the Footage: Setting Up Owners, Boards, Managers and Supers

A camera system on a rental property has more stakeholders than one on a house, and most of the friction we see after installation comes from nobody deciding who gets access. We settle it before handover.

  • Owner or board: full access, including settings and user management. For HOAs and co-ops, typically one or two board officers rather than the whole board.
  • Managing agent: live view and playback for every property in their portfolio, in one app, with export rights.
  • Superintendent: live view and playback for their own property, with export rights so they can hand a clip to police without waiting for the office.
  • Storefront tenant in a mixed-use building: their own cameras only; nothing over the residential entrance or stairs.
  • Residents: generally no direct access. Some boards share a live view of the package room or the entrance; we can set up a limited view if the board wants it, and we recommend a written policy first.

We leave a one-page summary listing every camera, what it looks at, the retention setting and the logins created, plus a short template policy the owner can adapt: who may request footage, how requests are logged, how long recordings are kept and who decides on release. Your attorney should finalise the policy; our part is making the system able to follow it.

Hudson Valley Apartment Building Security Camera FAQ

Shorter answers to the practical questions that come up once an owner or board has decided to go ahead.

Do you install in all six Hudson Valley counties, including the rural parts of Ulster and Dutchess?
+

Yes. Our crews work in Westchester, Rockland, Orange, Putnam, Dutchess and Ulster, including properties well off the main highways. Far-valley walkthroughs and installs are grouped by area so we can give you a two-hour arrival window, and the county pricing multiplier already accounts for the travel.

How quickly can you walk a complex in Westchester or Rockland compared with upstate?
+

Westchester and Rockland walkthroughs are usually the quickest to schedule because our crews are nearby most days. In Orange, Putnam, Dutchess and Ulster we group walkthroughs with other work in the same area, so the date depends on when a crew is next up there; we give you the first open two-hour window when you call. Systems that are down take priority over new walkthroughs.

Which Hudson Valley municipalities have adopted Good Cause Eviction?
+

According to the State Senate in 2025, the Hudson Valley adopters include the cities of Kingston, Hudson, Poughkeepsie, Beacon, Newburgh, Middletown and White Plains, the towns of Poughkeepsie and Fishkill, and the villages of New Paltz, Croton-on-Hudson and Nyack. Check with your municipality for the current status, since more may opt in.

Do you work with HOA, condo and co-op boards in the valley, or only landlords?
+

Both. Boards are a large share of our Hudson Valley camera work, especially townhouse associations where the common areas are roads, mail kiosks, pools and clubhouses. We present to the board, provide a phased proposal if the budget needs two years, and issue a certificate of insurance naming the association and its managing agent.

Can one app show several complexes in different counties?
+

Yes. Each property keeps its own recorder, and a managing agent or owner sees all of them in one app with separate permissions per site. A super at one complex sees only that complex, while the office sees the whole portfolio.

Do you install cameras on new transit-oriented buildings while they are under construction?
+

Yes, and it is the cheapest time to do it. We work from the drawings with the general contractor, install pathways and back-boxes at rough-in, test before the walls close, and mount cameras at the finish stage, so the camera closet and cable routes are built in rather than added later.

Can the super or a management company get alerts after hours?
+

Yes. We set up phone alerts for events that matter, such as a person in the laundry room after it closes or a vehicle entering the lot overnight, and tune them on site so they are useful rather than constant. Alerts go to whoever the owner designates, and each person can mute their own.

What warranty comes with a Hudson Valley apartment camera system?
+

Equipment we supply carries a one-year parts-only warranty from AESS, on top of the manufacturer's own warranty, and the full terms are in the service agreement. The warranty covers parts, not labour; labour for warranty swaps is billed at the service rate unless the owner has bought an extended service plan.

Can you add cameras to a detached garage, carport row or storage building?
+

Yes. Detached structures are linked back to the main recorder by trenched cable or fiber, or by a point-to-point wireless link where trenching would cut through a paved drive. Each structure gets its own small network cabinet with battery backup.

Do you remove the old cameras and cable when you replace a system?
+

Yes. Abandoned cameras and brackets come down, holes are sealed, and dead cable is removed where it is reachable without opening finished walls. Cable that tests clean may be kept for reuse, and we note on the handover sheet what was removed and what was left.
