The Neighborhood

Why Hudson Valley Buyers Need Different Security Cameras Than City Buyers

Most security camera advice is written for a townhouse with the router in the hall closet and the farthest camera thirty feet away. The six counties north of the Bronx line are a different market. A house on two acres in North Salem has a driveway that bends out of sight of the porch. A horse farm outside Millbrook has a stable and a run-in shed a long walk from the kitchen. A weekend cabin above Phoenicia may sit empty for three weeks at a time on a cellular hotspot. A distribution building off the Thruway in Newburgh has a truck court the size of a city block. The cameras on the shelf at a big-box store were not chosen with any of those properties in mind.

That is why our Hudson Valley camera store starts with three questions before it starts with a product. How far is the farthest spot you want to see from the place the recorder will live? What happens to the system when the internet goes down, which on many Hudson Valley roads is not a rare event? And what will the camera face in January, when the coldest nights in the mid-valley fall well below zero? Answer those three, and the right camera, recorder, drive and cable for the property mostly choose themselves.

The numbers back up what we see on the road. Across the six counties, 54.8 percent of homes are single-family detached houses, according to the U.S. Census Bureau's 2023 American Community Survey, and in Putnam the share is 77.7 percent. Those are houses with yards, garages, outbuildings and driveways, which means more cameras per property and longer cable runs than an apartment or a row house. The same survey counts 18,380 homes in the six counties that sit vacant because they are kept for seasonal, recreational or occasional use, 6,545 of them in Ulster alone. Those second homes need cameras that record and alert with nobody there to reset a router.

A Hudson Valley owner is usually not buying cameras because break-ins happen on the block every week. They are buying because the property is large, the nearest neighbor cannot see the barn, the house is empty for part of the year, or the business has inventory, vehicles or equipment parked outside overnight. Cameras on those properties are about knowing what happened and when, at a place nobody else is watching, and the system has to keep recording for weeks without anyone touching it.

Internet that comes and goes

Plenty of Hudson Valley addresses still do not have a wired connection worth the name. When Ulster County announced a state ConnectALL broadband award in September 2025, it described the money as reaching nearly 1,300 unserved locations in the county. County Executive Jen Metzger put it plainly: “Thanks to the NYS ConnectALL award and Verizon's substantial match, nearly 1,300 homes, businesses, and community anchor institutions in rural Ulster County will finally be connected with reliable fiber optic service.” Until that fiber reaches every road, many properties run on DSL, a fixed-wireless antenna, satellite or a cellular hotspot.

That shapes the store more than any other single fact. A camera that records only to the cloud is useless on a farm where the upload speed drops to nothing every evening. A wired PoE camera on a recorder in the house records every frame to a drive on the property whether the internet is up or down, and phone viewing simply resumes when the connection comes back. That is the system we recommend first for most country properties, and it is why the store is built around NVRs, drives and PoE switches rather than around battery cameras with subscription plans.

Package theft is still the most common reason people call

The front porch matters here too. Police departments across the region warn residents every holiday season: in October 2025, News 12 Hudson Valley reported that Carmel police were warning Putnam residents about porch pirates ahead of the holiday shopping season, with advice to bring deliveries in quickly, track them and report suspicious activity. In June 2026, News 12 reported that package thefts were down in parts of Westchester County, with Yonkers seeing a 50 percent drop at its First Precinct and White Plains recording about 13 reports so far that year against 18 in the same stretch of 2025. White Plains Deputy Public Safety Commissioner Joseph Castelli told the station: “Cameras are still good. I think that in general, schedule your deliveries so you're home.”

A porch camera is not complicated, but on a Hudson Valley house the porch is often set well back from the road, the delivery van stops at the end of a driveway, and the package ends up on a side step nobody can see from the street. The camera that actually helps is the one aimed where the drivers really leave things, with enough resolution to show a face and a vehicle, recording locally so a weak signal does not drop the clip.

Cold, snow and a long winter

The National Weather Service office in Albany recorded a low of minus 9°F at Poughkeepsie on January 29, 2026, and five nights that month at or below zero. That matters because every outdoor camera has an operating temperature range on its spec sheet, and some popular models stop at 14°F. We do not guess about cold weather: when you buy an outdoor camera from us, we read you the operating range from the maker's spec sheet, and for exposed spots we pick models rated for the cold and mount them where the eave or a hood keeps snow off the lens.

Outdoor or indoor: what changes

Outdoor surveillance cameras have to survive rain, snow, ice, summer heat on a south wall, spiders building webs across the lens and the occasional woodpecker. On the spec sheet that shows up as an ingress protection rating (IP66 or IP67 on most of the outdoor cameras we stock, meaning dust-tight and protected against water jets or temporary immersion) and an operating temperature range. Some outdoor models add a vandal rating such as IK10 for spots within reach of the ground. We only quote an IP or IK rating for a product when its spec sheet says so.

Indoor surveillance cameras can be smaller and lighter, and inside a heated building the cold-weather spec matters less. What matters more indoors is the lens angle, because rooms are short and wide, and the mounting position, because a camera over a doorway that looks straight into a bright window will show silhouettes instead of faces. Many owners buy a mix: outdoor cameras for the driveway, the porches and the barn doors, indoor cameras for the front hall, the garage interior and the room where the safe or the inventory lives.

Resolution: 4K, 5MP, 4MP and 2MP side by side

Resolution is the first number people ask about, and it matters, but it matters most in proportion to distance. A 2MP camera five feet from a door shows a sharp face. The same camera covering a hundred feet of driveway shows a person-shaped blur. Here is how the common sizes compare.

First, higher resolution needs more light to look good at night, which is why many 4K cameras pair the sensor with full-color night vision, a starlight-class sensor or a built-in spotlight. Second, a 12MP or panoramic camera covers a very wide scene but spreads its pixels across it, so it rarely replaces a well-aimed 4K camera on a specific door or gate. PoE, HD-analog or Wi-Fi: how the camera connects

PoE security cameras (Power over Ethernet) run on one Cat6 cable each. The cable carries power from the recorder or a PoE switch and carries video back, up to about 100 meters, or 328 feet, per run under the Ethernet standard. Past that we add a PoE extender, a fiber link or a wireless bridge. PoE is what we recommend for most new systems because each camera is a network device that can be upgraded, configured and replaced independently, and the recorder records every camera locally.

HD-analog cameras run on coax to a DVR, with a separate power feed or a combined siamese cable. They are the right buy when a property already has good coax in the walls, such as an older system with cameras on the barn and the house. Modern HD-analog cameras deliver 5MP and 4K pictures over that same coax, and a video balun lets a camera use a Cat6 pair instead where that is easier. The trade-off is fewer smart features on some models and no upgrade path to IP without a new recorder.

Wi-Fi security cameras need power at the camera, from an outlet, a battery or a small solar panel, and a strong Wi-Fi signal at that spot. Inside a house or on a porch near the router, they are fine. On a barn 300 feet away, through trees, in an ice storm, they are the most common cause of the “my camera keeps going offline” calls we get. When a far spot truly cannot be cabled, we look at a point-to-point wireless bridge back to the wired system or, for a spot with no internet at all, a cellular camera.

Dome, bullet, turret, eyeball and PTZ

Bullet security cameras are the long cylinders on a bracket. They are easy to aim at a distant driveway or gate, visible enough to deter, and many carry longer lenses. Dome security cameras hide the lens direction behind a smoked or clear bubble and suit ceilings and lower walls where a vandal-resistant shape helps. Turret cameras, which many sellers also call eyeball cameras, sit in a ball-and-socket mount without a bubble, so there is no glare from built-in infrared at night; they are our usual pick under soffits and porch ceilings. PTZ security cameras pan, tilt and zoom under motor control, and on a large lot or a farm they can follow a vehicle across a field or zoom on a gate when an alarm fires. Varifocal and motorized zoom cameras let us set the exact field of view at installation instead of choosing a fixed lens in advance.

Specialty cameras have their place too. A fisheye or panoramic camera can cover an entire store floor or a barn aisle from one ceiling point. Dual-lens and multi-sensor cameras watch two directions, such as both ends of a long building, from one mount. Active deterrence cameras add a siren, strobe lights and sometimes two-way talk, which is useful on a gate, a loading dock or an equipment yard. Thermal cameras see heat instead of light and are a specialist tool for long perimeters; we quote them only where the job really calls for one.

NVR and DVR recorders: 4, 8, 16, 32 and 64 channels

The recorder is the heart of the system. An NVR (network video recorder) records IP cameras; a PoE NVR has camera ports on the back that power the cameras directly, which is the simplest setup for four to sixteen cameras. A DVR (digital video recorder) records HD-analog cameras over coax. Channel count is how many cameras the recorder can handle: a 4-channel NVR suits a small house, an 8-channel NVR covers most homes with a garage and a barn with room to grow, a 16-channel NVR fits larger homes and most small businesses, and 32-channel and 64-channel NVRs run warehouses, dealerships, campuses and multi-building farms.

When you buy an NVR from us, we ask how many cameras you have today and how many you might want in three years, because the cheapest recorder is often the one you have to replace when you add the fifth camera. We also check the recorder's total incoming bandwidth, how many drive bays it has, whether it decodes 4K on every channel, and whether it supports the smart detection of the cameras you are buying. A 4K NVR that can only play back four 4K streams at once is a frustrating box on a sixteen-camera property.

Surveillance hard drives and storage

CCTV hard drives are not the same as the drive in a laptop. Surveillance hard drives are built to write continuously, around the clock, from many cameras at once, and are rated for that workload. We sell them in common sizes such as a 4TB surveillance hard drive for a small system, an 8TB surveillance hard drive for a mid-size home or shop, and 16TB surveillance storage and up, including multiple drives, for 16 to 64 cameras or for longer retention. The right size comes from the math further down this page, not from a guess.

PoE switches, injectors and power

A PoE switch adds camera ports when the recorder runs out, or puts a cluster of ports in a barn or a warehouse corner so only one cable has to run back to the house or the office. A security camera PoE injector powers a single IP camera on a network that does not have PoE. For long runs we sell PoE extenders, which sit partway along the route in a weatherproof box, and fiber media converters for runs to outbuildings hundreds of feet away. For analog systems we stock CCTV power supplies and CCTV power adapters, including multi-output boxes that power several cameras from one outlet. We also recommend a battery backup (UPS) for the recorder and switch on any property where the lights flicker in a storm.

Mounts, brackets, junction boxes and cable

The small parts are where cheap kits fall down. A security camera mount has to hold a camera steady through wind on a pole or a barn gable; security camera brackets for corners, poles and parapets let a camera sit where it should instead of where it is easy. A camera junction box, also called a CCTV junction box, covers the cable connection behind the camera so water and wasps stay out. For cable we sell outdoor-rated Cat6 cable for security cameras and outdoor Ethernet cable rated for sun and direct burial where the job calls for it, security camera extension cable, CCTV connectors and BNC connectors for coax, and CCTV video baluns for running analog video over twisted pair. Our crew does the wiring on installed jobs; for equipment-only orders we sell the parts and leave the wiring to your electrician or installer.

Storage Math

Recording Days and Drive Size for Hudson Valley Systems

Security cameras with two weeks recording and security cameras with one month recording are two of the most common requests we get, especially from second-home owners who visit every few weeks. The drive size that gets you there is arithmetic. Here is the estimate method we use, with the inputs clearly marked as examples, so you can check any quote, ours included.

Estimate method (continuous recording):
Storage per camera per day = bitrate × 86,400 seconds ÷ 8 bits per byte
Total storage = storage per camera per day × number of cameras × days to keep

Worked example A, 4K option: 8 cameras, example bitrate 6 Mbps each.
6 Mbps ÷ 8 = 0.75 MB per second × 86,400 = 64.8 GB per camera per day.
× 8 cameras = 518.4 GB per day. 14 days ≈ 7.3 TB. 30 days ≈ 15.6 TB.

Worked example B, 2K option: the same 8 cameras at an example 3 Mbps each.
32.4 GB per camera per day × 8 = 259.2 GB per day. 14 days ≈ 3.6 TB. 30 days ≈ 7.8 TB.

The bitrates above are example inputs, not specs of any product. The real number depends on the resolution, the frame rate, the compression (H.265 or H.265+ files are smaller than H.264 for the same picture, on cameras whose spec sheet lists it), how much moves in the scene and how much noise the sensor makes at night. That last point matters here: a camera watching a tree line in the wind, or a snowy driveway under infrared, can send far more data than the same camera on a still hallway. Recording on motion or smart events only, instead of continuously, can stretch the same drive much further, at the cost of gaps between events.

So in practice we do three things. We set the bitrate and frame rate on the recorder to what each view needs. We size the drive from the worked method above with a margin. And after the first week, we read the recorder's real usage and confirm the number of days you are actually getting. To try different camera counts, resolutions and days yourself, use our security camera storage and system calculator, then call or text us the result for a quote.

Rules of thumb that hold up

  • Two weeks is a floor, not a goal. Many thefts at a second home or a farm are found days or weeks later. A month of recording is a sensible target for any property that sits empty.
  • Drive labels use decimal terabytes. The recorder will show slightly less usable space than the number on the label, and it needs some room for its own database.
  • Leave a spare bay if you can. A recorder with two drive bays lets you add storage later without replacing anything.
  • 4K doubles the question. Moving the same cameras from 2K to 4K roughly doubles the storage in the worked examples above, so a 4K quote usually comes with a bigger drive.
Your Questions Answered

Buying Security Cameras in the Hudson Valley: Questions Answered

These are the buying questions we hear most from Hudson Valley callers. Most of them come down to internet, distance, storage and cold rather than to brand names.

Do PoE security cameras need internet on a property where the connection drops out?

No. PoE security cameras need a network, not the internet, and those are different things. The network here is the cable from each camera to the recorder or the PoE switch, which lives on your property. The cameras send video down that cable, the recorder writes it to its hard drive, and that happens whether the internet connection at the house is fast, slow or completely down. You can watch live and recorded video on the monitor plugged into the recorder at any time.

The internet only matters for viewing from somewhere else: the app on your phone in the city, push alerts when someone walks up the drive, and firmware updates. When the connection drops, those pause, and when it comes back, you can scroll back through everything that was recorded in the meantime. That is the single biggest reason we recommend wired PoE systems on Hudson Valley properties with DSL, fixed wireless, satellite or a hotspot.

How much storage do security cameras need for a month away from a second home?

For a typical second home with six to eight cameras recording continuously, a month of 2K-class video usually lands in the high single-digit terabytes and a month of 4K in the mid-teens, using the worked examples in the storage section above. Recording on smart events only, which is common at an empty house, can cut that sharply. The honest answer for your house comes from three numbers: how many cameras, which resolution, and whether you want every minute or every event.

For a weekend house we usually recommend continuous recording on the driveway and the main entrance, and event recording on the rest, on a drive sized for at least 30 days. That way the approach to the house is always on record, the side yard does not fill the drive with branches moving in the wind, and the oldest footage is still there when you arrive three weeks later and notice the shed door is open.

How do you calculate CCTV storage for a system you are about to buy?

Multiply the bitrate of each camera by the seconds in a day, convert bits to bytes, then multiply by the number of cameras and the number of days you want to keep. Written out: megabits per second, divided by 8, times 86,400, gives megabytes per camera per day. The worked examples above walk through it for an 8-camera system at two resolutions. The part most calculators skip is where the bitrate comes from: it is a setting on the recorder, not a fixed property of the camera, and night scenes, snow and moving trees push it up.

If you already own a recorder, it will usually show the live bitrate of each channel in its settings or on screen, as in the recorder photo in our field notes below. Add those up and you have a real input instead of an estimate. If you are buying new, we calculate from the stream settings we plan to use and then check against the recorder's real usage after the first week.

Which outdoor cameras hold up to a Hudson Valley January?

Ones whose spec sheet says they will. Every outdoor camera lists an operating temperature range, and they are not all the same. As one example, Reolink's spec page for its RLC-810A 4K PoE camera lists an operating range of minus 10°C to plus 55°C, which is 14°F to 131°F, along with IP67 weatherproofing. Poughkeepsie hit minus 9°F on January 29, 2026, according to the National Weather Service. We read you the rated range of any outdoor camera before you buy it, and for exposed, north-facing or hilltop spots we pick models rated lower.

Mounting matters as much as the rating. A camera under an eave or a porch roof stays out of falling snow and freezing rain; a camera on an open pole needs a sun and snow hood. Wired PoE cameras have an advantage in deep cold, because they draw steady power from the recorder instead of a battery that loses capacity as the temperature falls.

How do I put a camera on a barn or outbuilding past the end of the cable?

There are four ways, and which one fits depends on distance, power and whether you can dig. Within about 328 feet, a single outdoor-rated Cat6 run from the recorder or a switch is enough. Past that, a PoE extender partway along the route gives another run. For hundreds of feet or more, a fiber link with a small media converter at each end carries the signal any practical distance and does not conduct lightning surges along the cable. Where trenching is impossible but there is clear line of sight, a point-to-point wireless bridge links the barn to the house network.

If the barn has power and several cameras, the cleanest design is usually a small PoE switch in the barn fed by one fiber or bridge link, with short Cat6 runs to each camera from there. We sell every part of that, and on equipment-only orders we will write out what goes where for your installer. On installed jobs, our crew does the run.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Buying Security Cameras in the Hudson Valley

AI answers about security cameras are mostly written from the point of view of a suburban house with fast internet and a camera ten feet from the router. Here is where that advice breaks on a Hudson Valley property, and what we tell buyers instead.

“A wireless kit is the easiest choice for most homes”

Easiest to unbox, yes. Easiest to live with on a property where the barn is 300 feet from the router and the trees are in leaf half the year, no. The most common call we get from owners who bought a Wi-Fi kit is that the far cameras drop offline, especially in wet weather, and that battery cameras stop recording on cold nights.

Wireless cameras do have a place: an inside room, a porch near the router, a spot no cable can reach. But for the perimeter of a country property, wired PoE cameras with a recorder on the property are the system that keeps recording through storms and outages.

If you already own a wireless kit, it is not wasted. Use those cameras indoors and close to the router, and put wired cameras on the long views.

“Cloud storage is safer than a recorder”

Cloud storage protects footage if a burglar takes the recorder, which is a real advantage. It also needs an upload connection that can carry every clip, a monthly plan, and a service that keeps running. On a farm with a thin DSL line or a cabin on a hotspot, uploads stall exactly when you need them.

The practical answer here is local recording with a well-hidden recorder: in a locked closet, a basement, or a utility room nobody would guess, with the cable routes concealed. Where an owner wants off-site copies too, we add them only on products that actually offer cloud backup.

Local first, cloud as an extra, is the right order when the internet is the weak link.

“More megapixels always means a better camera”

Resolution helps most at distance. On a porch, a 4MP camera shows a face just as usefully as an 8MP one and fills the drive half as fast. On a 200-foot driveway, the 8MP camera is the one that turns a blur into a vehicle you can describe.

Low-light performance, lens choice and mounting height often matter more than the megapixel count. A 4K camera aimed into a bright window or mounted too high to see under hat brims will lose to a well-placed 2K camera every time.

This is exactly why we quote every system as a 2K and a 4K option and talk through which views deserve which.

“Any outdoor camera works in any weather”

Outdoor ratings vary a lot. Ingress protection ratings tell you about water and dust, not cold. The operating temperature range is a separate line on the spec sheet, and some popular outdoor models stop at 14°F.

The mid-Hudson Valley saw minus 9°F in January 2026 at Poughkeepsie. A camera that is rated to 14°F may keep working below it, or may not, and that is not a gamble to take on the camera covering your gate.

Check the rated range, choose mounts that keep snow and ice off the lens, and prefer wired power over batteries for anything outside.

“Four cameras are enough for a house”

Four is a starting package, not a rule. A colonial on a small lot in Rockland may be well covered by four. A house in Dutchess with a detached garage, a barn and a long driveway usually needs six to ten, plus a recorder with spare channels.

The right count comes from listing what each camera must prove: who came up the driveway, who opened the barn, who took the package from the side step. Each of those is one view, and each view is one camera.

Buying an 8-channel recorder even when you start with four cameras is the cheapest insurance against replacing it later.

“A camera on the house covers the whole yard”

A camera sees a cone, and the far end of that cone is where detail runs out. On a big property, the event you need is often at the edge: the gate, the far side of the barn, the end of the drive.

Getting a camera out to those spots is a cabling problem, solved with extenders, fiber or a wireless bridge, and we sell all three. The camera on the house is still useful, but it is rarely enough by itself.

When you plan, walk the property and stand where you would want the camera to be, not where the outlet is.

“You have to pay a monthly fee for smart detection”

Some consumer brands put person and vehicle detection behind a subscription. Many professional cameras and recorders run that detection on the device itself, at no ongoing cost.

Every house-brand system we sell records locally with no monthly fee, and the AI detection on cameras that offer it, such as the human and vehicle detection on the D5 kit, runs without a plan.

Before you buy any camera, from us or anyone else, ask exactly which features need a subscription. We tell you up front.

Field Notes

Field Notes: What a 23-Camera Recorder Screen Teaches a Buyer

The most useful photo in our job library for anyone buying a recorder is not a camera at all. It is the screen of a commercial NVR, mounted below a ceiling full of blue and white network cable, showing a 32-tile grid. Twenty-three tiles are live, labeled D1 to D23, covering a showroom, a service area, offices, hallways and the outside. Nine tiles, D24 to D32, are black.

Under the grid, the recorder lists every channel with its current bitrate in kilobits per second. At the moment the photo was taken, the quietest channel read 86 Kb/s and the busiest 453 Kb/s, and the twenty-three live channels added up to roughly 5,268 Kb/s, a little over 5 megabits per second. The unused channels all read zero. Those figures are a single on-screen snapshot, not a spec, but they make three buying lessons concrete.

Buy channels you do not use yet. This recorder was bought with nine spare channels. When the owner wants a camera on a new door or a corner of the lot, it plugs in; nobody has to replace the recorder or migrate the recordings. On a Hudson Valley house, the same logic says buy an 8-channel recorder for four cameras if there is a barn, a pool house or a gate you might add.

Bitrate is uneven, so storage is too. The busiest channel on the screen was sending about five times the data of the quietest. Busy, detailed scenes cost more drive space than quiet hallways. When we size a drive, we size it for the busy cameras, not the average, and we read these numbers again after the system has been running for a week.

A zero tells you something. A channel reading 0 Kb/s is either unused or a camera that has stopped sending. Learning to glance at that column is the fastest health check an owner can do, and it is one of the things we show every buyer at handover.

The same job shows the value of simple placements. A bullet camera on a short ceiling mount at the end of a hallway sees everyone walking toward the service door, face first, with no glare and no wasted pixels. It is one of the least expensive cameras on the system and one of the most useful. On a farm the equivalent is the camera inside the barn aisle looking at the main door, and in a shop it is the camera behind the register looking at the front entrance.

The live tile of a storeroom camera from another job, shown earlier on this page, makes the night-vision point: in infrared mode the room renders in greys, every box edge is clear, and the open back doorway is in frame. That is what you are buying a camera for: a clear view of the way in, at the hours when nobody is there.

Laws and Privacy

Security Camera Laws and Privacy for Hudson Valley Owners

Security camera laws in New York are mostly about sound and about places where people expect privacy. The points below are general information, not legal advice; for a specific situation, ask a lawyer.

  • Audio is the sensitive part. New York Penal Law section 250.05 makes eavesdropping, which includes the mechanical overhearing of a conversation without the consent of at least one party, a class E felony. Audio recording security cameras and two way audio security cameras are useful at a gate or a door you answer yourself, but recording conversations between other people, such as customers at a farm stand or employees in a break room, is where the risk sits. We leave audio off by default at businesses.
  • No cameras where people undress or expect privacy. Bathrooms, changing areas and bedrooms of guests or tenants are off limits. We will not install or aim cameras into them.
  • Aim at your own property. On large lots this is usually easy, but a camera on a shared driveway or facing a neighbor's windows can cause disputes. We aim and mask cameras so they cover your property and your approach.
  • HOAs, condos and landlords. Associations often have rules about cameras on common areas and exterior walls. Get board approval in writing first. Landlords putting cameras on multifamily buildings should cover common areas and entrances, not inside apartments.
  • Employees. Businesses that record work areas should tell staff where the cameras are and post clear signs at camera locations.

CCTV regulations for businesses also include practical duties: keep the recorder secure, limit who can export footage, and keep recordings long enough to be useful to police or insurers. Most of our commercial buyers aim for 30 days.

What moves a security camera system cost up or down

  • Resolution. 4K cameras cost more than 2K-class cameras and need bigger drives for the same number of days.
  • Camera count. More cameras means more cameras, more channels and more storage; how much an eight camera security system costs depends mostly on resolution and cable distance.
  • Recorder channels. A 16-channel NVR costs more than an 8-channel, but less than replacing an 8-channel later.
  • Storage days. One month of recording needs roughly twice the drive of two weeks.
  • PoE vs Wi-Fi. Wired systems need cable and labor; Wi-Fi cameras save cable but often cost more per camera and need power at each spot.
  • Distance. Runs past the Ethernet limit add extenders, fiber or a bridge, and outbuildings add a switch.
  • Features. Active deterrence, full color at night, PTZ, LPR and AI detection add to the camera cost.

Cheap security cameras and budget security cameras exist at every big-box store; premium, professional grade and commercial grade security cameras cost more because of better sensors, lenses, housings and recorders. The best value security cameras for a country property are usually mid-range wired cameras on a recorder with room to grow. Is it legal to record audio on cameras at a farm stand or shop in New York?
+

Video recording in your own business or on your own property is generally allowed, but sound is different. Under New York Penal Law section 250.05, mechanically overhearing a conversation without the consent of at least one party is eavesdropping, a class E felony. Cameras at a farm stand or a shop counter can pick up customers talking to each other, so we leave audio off by default at businesses. This is general information, not legal advice.
