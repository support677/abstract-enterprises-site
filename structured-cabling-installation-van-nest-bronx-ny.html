The Neighborhood

Why Van Nest Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Van Nest is a small neighborhood of houses wedged between Bronx Park, Bronxdale Avenue and the Amtrak tracks, and almost every cabling problem here comes from the same place: buildings that were finished long before anyone planned a wire for data. Wikipedia puts the neighborhood at well under half a square mile, inside Bronx Community Board 11, with a population under 15,000. It says Van Nest is dominated by single-family homes of various types, with some tenements scattered across it, and that the majority of residents rent. The houses went up from the 1890s onward, after a land company cut old farmland into lots, in Italianate, Queen Anne, Art Deco and later contemporary brick styles. Each of those eras hid its walls, floors and stairs in a different way, and each one decides how a cable can climb from the cellar to a bedroom without anyone tearing open plaster.

The connection at the curb is rarely the weak point. BestNeighborhood's provider estimates list Verizon fiber, Optimum fiber and EarthLink fiber at 100 percent of Van Nest, Optimum cable and Starry at 100 percent as well, and Verizon's fixed wireless at about 96 percent, and they rate fiber as the fastest available service for every home in the neighborhood. Those numbers describe the street. Inside, the picture is a single coax outlet in the front room, a provider's gateway sitting wherever the installer found a hole, and a family of four, two home offices and a tenant downstairs all trying to share one wireless signal through brick and lath. Structured cabling is the part that turns a fast line at the front wall into a dependable connection on the top floor and at the back of the house.

Ownership makes the job more particular here than in a neighborhood of big rental buildings. A typical Van Nest property is a house with one owner and sometimes one tenant, or a house rented whole to a single household while the owner lives elsewhere. Someone has to decide where the shared equipment lives, who holds the key to it, and whose name is on each provider account. New York's Public Service Law section 228 adds one more layer: a landlord may not interfere with the installation of cable television facilities on the property, but may set reasonable conditions to protect the building's safety, function and appearance, and the provider or tenant pays for the work and any damage. A good drop plan answers all of that in writing before the first hole is drilled.

Then there is what is coming. In August 2024 the City Council approved the Bronx Metro-North Station Area Plan, which City Limits described as rezoning roughly 46 blocks around Morris Park and Parkchester/Van Nest to make room for about 7,000 new apartments, including letting housing go on parts of Parkchester/Van Nest that had been zoned only for commercial and manufacturing use. Marketproof reported a new building permit filed on February 25, 2026 for a four-story building at 542 Morris Park Avenue with six apartments and 5,556 square feet of commercial space, and New York YIMBY described plans for a three-story building at 841A Morris Park Avenue with eight apartments, ground-floor retail and a small doctor's office. Each new building is a chance to put cable in before the walls close, which costs far less effort than fishing it in later.

The last local fact is water. The city's Department of Environmental Protection lists Van Nest Avenue, along Radcliff, Colden and Lurting Avenues, among the flooding hotspots in its Parkchester/Morris Park cloudburst plan, with mapped areas of nuisance flooding and deep flooding of a foot or more. A cellar is where the provider's line usually enters a house, and it is where most people want to hide the router. On blocks like those, where the equipment goes matters as much as which cable we pull.

Van Nest facts that shape a cabling plan

  • Mostly houses, mostly renters: single-family homes of several kinds dominate, with scattered tenements, and most residents rent, so owner and tenant both have a stake in the wiring.
  • Old and varied construction: homes built from the 1890s on in Italianate, Queen Anne, Art Deco and contemporary brick styles, each with its own way into the walls.
  • Fiber everywhere at the street: three fiber providers each estimated at 100 percent coverage, with fiber the fastest option for every home.
  • New buildings on the avenue: mixed-use filings at 542 and 841A Morris Park Avenue, with stores, a doctor's office and apartments in the same structure.
  • A rezoning around the future station: about 46 blocks and some 7,000 planned apartments across Morris Park and Parkchester/Van Nest, approved in August 2024.
  • Known wet blocks: Van Nest Avenue near Radcliff, Colden and Lurting Avenues mapped by DEP as flooding hotspots.

What Van Nest owners tend to add next. Once a house has a cabinet and a labeled run to each floor, the next call is often for a wired video doorbell, a camera over the side alley or the garage, or a better entry panel at a two-family's shared door. All of those ride on the same cable, so on the first survey we mark where those runs would go and leave spare ports and power in the cabinet.

The Houses

From the Front Wall to the Back Bedroom: Cabling the Houses That Make Up Most of Van Nest

Most of our Van Nest work happens in houses, and a house is a harder place to run cable than an office. There is no drop ceiling to lift, no riser closet, and no plan on file showing where the joists run. What there is, almost always, is a cellar, a staircase, closets stacked one over another, and an attic or crawl space under the roof. Those four things are the map. A good route uses them and leaves the finished rooms alone.

Start where the service actually lands

Before anyone talks about rooms, we find the point where the provider's line meets the house today. On a Van Nest house that is usually a small box on the front or side wall, or a cable that drops from the pole and enters through the cellar wall, sometimes beside two or three dead entries from earlier companies. From there we follow the cable to the gateway and note every splitter, staple and splice along the way. That walk tells us whether the house already has one clean path into the cellar, which wall it uses, and whether the box sits low enough to get wet. It also tells us which entries can be sealed once the new system is in, so the front of the house stops collecting holes.

The cellar is usually the hub, but not always the right one

In most houses the best home for the cabinet is the cellar, because the line arrives there, it is out of the living space, and cable can fan out under the first floor to every wall. That changes when the cellar takes water, when it belongs to a tenant's lease, or when the house has only a crawl space. Then the cabinet moves to a first-floor closet or under the stair, still close to the service entry, and the runs to the cellar level are kept short and high. The drop plan names the spot and the reason, so nobody has to guess later.

Getting upstairs without opening a wall

The hardest part of any house job is the climb from the first floor to the second and on to the attic. In older frame houses the stud bays between floors are often open from cellar to attic, or blocked by a single fire block that can be drilled from below. In brick houses the interior partitions may be framed while the outside walls are solid masonry, so the route follows an inside wall, a closet stack or the back of the stair. Where no hidden path exists, a neat surface raceway inside a closet is better than a damaged ceiling. We show you each option, with photos, before any drilling.

Two households under one roof

Many Van Nest houses hold two households, whether the house was built as a two-family or later divided. The cleanest setup gives each apartment its own provider account and its own small hub, with a labeled run from the shared cellar or entry point to each one, so a reset upstairs never cuts off the tenant below. Where the owner prefers to supply internet as part of the rent, the cabinet stays locked and owner-controlled, and each apartment gets its own network segment off it. Either way the two households should be able to call their own provider without the other one noticing.

Old cable that came with the house

Almost every house we survey here carries cable from companies that are long gone: phone wire stapled along the cellar joists, coax splitters feeding rooms nobody uses, and the odd early network run that ends in a closet. Some of it can be identified, tested and reused; most is simply in the way. We trace what we can, mark what is live, and offer removal of the dead runs as its own line on the plan, so you decide whether it stays.

New Construction

New Mixed-Use Buildings on Morris Park Avenue: Cabling a Store, an Office and Apartments in One Small Structure

The new buildings going up in Van Nest are small by city standards, and that is exactly why their cabling goes wrong. A twelve-story tower gets a telecom consultant; a three- or four-story building on a 50-foot lot often gets whatever the electrician remembers to leave. Look at the two filings on Morris Park Avenue. At 542, Marketproof reported a four-story building with six apartments over 5,556 square feet of commercial space. At 841A, New York YIMBY described a three-story, eight-unit building of 10,130 square feet on a 51-foot-wide lot, with apartments on the second and third floors averaging 714 square feet, about 1,336 square feet of ground-floor retail and a 389-square-foot doctor's office. Other filings have surfaced on Muliner Avenue, Mead Street and Van Nest Avenue. None of them is our client. They are simply the kind of building a Van Nest owner, developer or first tenant is now dealing with.

One entry, three kinds of user

A store, a medical office and a stack of apartments want different things from the same building. The store needs counters, a card terminal, a back office and probably cameras. The doctor's office needs desks, printers, a vendor's equipment and a connection it can trust with patient records. Each apartment needs a provider of the tenant's choosing. The simplest building to live with has one telecom space where every provider terminates, a sleeve or conduit from it to each commercial unit, and a path up to a small panel in every apartment. Leave any of those out and the first tenant's installer will improvise one, usually on the outside of the building.

The window is short

The best time to cable a building like this is after framing and rough electrical, before insulation and drywall. Miss it and every run has to be fished through closed walls, often in finished apartments with tenants already in them. On a small project that window can be a matter of days, so we ask the builder for the schedule as soon as the drawings are set and agree the visit before the drywall delivery is booked. Cable gets pulled, left coiled and labeled at each panel location, and photographed in the open walls.

Telecom space in a building with no spare room

A building of eight small apartments rarely has room for a real telecom closet, and nobody wants to give up a rentable corner for one. A wall-mounted, locked enclosure in the cellar or a ground-floor utility room usually does the job, provided it has a dedicated outlet, some air, and enough wall for every provider's box. We mark that wall on the plans with the builder so it is not lost to a water heater or a meter bank.

What the owner should receive at the end

When the building is done, the owner should be holding a drawing or port map showing each conduit and cable from the telecom space to every unit, test results for each run, photos of the routes inside the walls, and labels that match the apartment and unit numbers on the leases. That file is what lets a provider's technician connect a new tenant in one visit, and it is what a future buyer will ask for.

The Station Area

The 2024 Rezoning and the Delayed Station: Wiring Buildings for Tenants Who Have Not Arrived Yet

The Bronx Metro-North Station Area Plan changed what can be built near the tracks. City Limits reported that the Council approved it in August 2024, rezoning roughly 46 blocks around Morris Park and Parkchester/Van Nest, aiming at about 7,000 new apartments and allowing housing on parts of Parkchester/Van Nest that had been zoned only for commercial and manufacturing use. Bronx Community Board 11 supported the plan while raising concerns about infrastructure, and Council members negotiated $500 million in local investments, drainage upgrades among them. For an owner of a one-story commercial building or an old industrial lot near the rail line, that means a building that may soon have apartments above it, and a service entrance that was sized for a single business.

The train itself keeps moving further out. Streetsblog NYC reported in October 2025 that the MTA now expects Penn Station Access to be finished in 2030 at the earliest, after earlier targets of 2027 and then 2028, while hoping to run some Metro-North service to temporary stations at Co-op City, Morris Park and Parkchester/Van Nest under a 2027 plan that depends on Amtrak. Wikipedia still lists the Parkchester/Van Nest station as intended to open at the earliest in 2027. Whatever the final date, the buildings being designed now will stand long after it.

Size the pathways for the building, not the first lease

Cable is cheap compared with the walls it hides in. The part that is expensive to change later is the pathway: the sleeve through the foundation, the conduit up the building, the space for providers' equipment. When a building may change from one commercial user to a store with apartments above, we plan empty conduit and pull strings for the units that do not exist yet, so each future tenant can be connected without a new hole in the street wall.

Converting an old commercial building

An older one-story building on a rezoned block often has a single service that comes in at the back, a tangle of business cable from tenants past, and no route to an upper floor because there has never been one. Adding apartments on top is the moment to set a proper telecom space and a riser, rather than extending the store's line upward. The old business runs can then be audited, reused where they test well, and removed where they do not.

Wet Cellars

Keeping the Network Dry on Van Nest Avenue's Flood-Prone Blocks

The DEP summary for the Parkchester/Morris Park cloudburst hub covers a 410-acre area and names Van Nest Avenue, along Radcliff, Colden and Lurting Avenues, as one of its flooding hotspots, with mapped areas of nuisance flooding of four inches to a foot and deep flooding of a foot or more. The city asks residents to report flooding to 311. A cloudburst hub is a set of street-level tools to absorb, store and move rainwater, and it will take time to build. In the meantime, the houses on those blocks still have their provider's line, their router and their network cabinet in the cellar.

What water actually damages

Cable that has sat in dirty water should be tested before it is trusted again, and is often replaced. What fails quickly is everything plugged in: the provider's box, the gateway, a switch, battery backup, and any power strip on the floor. A splash of water at an outlet can end the whole house's service until a technician comes. So the first rule on a wet block is simple: nothing powered goes on the floor or on a low shelf.

Raising the equipment, not just the cable

On a cellar that has taken water before, we mount the cabinet high on a dry wall, well clear of the floor, and route cable along the ceiling joists rather than down the walls to low outlets. Where the cellar floods more than once in a while, we move the cabinet up to the first floor and leave only the provider's entry below, with the shortest possible run up. The provider's own box belongs to the provider; if it sits low, we photograph it, note it on the plan, and tell you how to ask them to move it.

Keeping water out of the new holes

Every new entry through a foundation or a front wall is sealed on the outside and the inside, sloped so water runs away from it, and photographed. We prefer to reuse an existing entry that is already sealed rather than add another opening near grade, and we seal the dead ones left by earlier companies while we are there.

Providers

Three Fiber Providers at the Curb: What Still Stands Between a Van Nest Street and the Rooms Inside

On paper Van Nest is as well served as any neighborhood in the city. BestNeighborhood's estimates put Verizon, Optimum and EarthLink fiber each at 100 percent of the neighborhood, and fixed wireless from Starry and Verizon at nearly full coverage too. If you live here you can probably choose between several companies. What the maps cannot show is the path from the pole or the street box into a specific house and up to a specific room.

What "available" means at a single address

A thread on Verizon's own community forum lays out the gap well. A resident whose apartment had shown fiber as available for years asked whether Verizon could connect without the landlord. The replies explained that the technician usually needs access to a phone room or closet in the building, runs fiber from there to an ONT inside the unit, and then runs an Ethernet cable from the ONT to the router, and that Verizon cannot install fiber in a building without the landlord's knowledge or consent. In a Van Nest house the "phone room" is the cellar, and the landlord is often the owner upstairs.

What a landlord can and cannot decide

Public Service Law section 228 says a landlord may not interfere with the installation of cable television facilities on the property. It also lets the landlord require reasonable conditions that protect the safety, function and appearance of the building and the comfort of other tenants, and it puts the cost of installing, running and removing the equipment, plus any damage, on the provider, the tenant or both. In practice that means an owner can insist on where the line enters, how it is fastened, and that the hole is sealed, and a structured cabling plan is the easiest way to give every provider the same tidy answer.

Inside the house, the provider stops at one box

Whichever company you choose, its job usually ends at the ONT or modem and a gateway beside it. The provider's technician will not open your walls to reach a back bedroom or a third floor. That last part, from their box to every room and floor, is the structured cabling, and it works the same with any provider. Choose the company you like; the cable in your walls should never need to change because you switch.

Structured Cabling for Van Nest's Architecture

Van Nest's buildings cover more than 130 years, from the houses built when the streets were first graded in the 1890s to the mixed-use buildings filed in 2026. The cable is the same in all of them. What changes is the path, the wall material and who signs off.

1890s Frame Houses

Wood-framed houses from the first decade of building, with plaster and lath inside, often reworked by several owners since.

Queen Anne and Italianate Houses

Houses with bays, porches and the occasional rounded corner room, where the attic is often the key to the top floor.

Art Deco-Era Brick Houses

Solid masonry outer walls and framed interiors, built in the interwar years among the earlier frame houses.

Contemporary Brick Two-Families

Later brick houses, often with a garage or utility room at grade and two apartments stacked above.

Scattered Tenements

The small apartment buildings Wikipedia notes among the houses, with a stair hall, a cellar and several households.

New Mixed-Use Buildings

Three- and four-story buildings on Morris Park Avenue with commercial space at street level and apartments above.

Frame houses: use what the carpenters left open

A wood-framed house built in the 1890s was put together with long, continuous stud bays in many of its walls, and later owners have patched, blocked and rewired them in their own ways. The cable route usually starts at the cellar ceiling, climbs inside an interior wall or a closet stack, and finishes from the attic down. Plaster and lath crack if a tool is forced, so we drill small, check each bay with a scanner and a camera before we open anything, and patch any access hole we have to make. Old electrical wiring in these walls is left alone; data cable keeps its distance.

Bays, porches and rounded rooms

The decorative parts of a Queen Anne or Italianate house are the hardest to reach from below, because the framing under a bay or a corner tower rarely lines up with the walls of the floor beneath. We reach those rooms from the attic or the space above the ceiling, drop the cable down an inside corner or a flat section of wall, and set the jack low beside the baseboard where it will not fight the trim.

Brick houses: inside walls, never the outside wall

In a brick house, the outside walls are usually solid masonry with plaster directly on it, and there is nothing to fish through. The route moves to the framed partitions, the closets and the back of the stair. Where a cable must cross a masonry wall, we core a clean, small hole, sleeve it, and seal it. In attached houses the party wall belongs to two owners, so we never drill toward it.

Newer two-families: the garage and the fire separation

Where a brick two-family has a garage or utility room at grade, its ceiling is often the easiest way to get from the front of the house to the back. That ceiling and the door into the house usually separate the garage from the living space for fire safety, so every penetration through it is kept small and sealed with a rated firestop, and the cabinet goes on a wall of the utility room rather than over the car.

Small apartment buildings: the stair hall and the owner's rules

The few tenement-style buildings among the houses are wired like small apartment buildings anywhere: a locked cellar cabinet for the building, a riser up the stair hall or an old chase, and a small panel in each unit. The work is planned around tenants who are home during the day, and the owner approves the route before we start.

Shops on the avenue: wire what cannot drop

A neighborhood shop lives or dies at the counter. The register, the card terminal, the receipt printer and, at a bakery or deli, the label scale all need to keep working on a Saturday morning, so each gets its own run from a small locked cabinet in the back room rather than a long wireless hop from a gateway near the front window. We add a reserved run for a camera over the register and the rear door, and an access point for handheld devices if the shop uses them. Most of this can be done before opening or after closing so the store never shuts.

Heat, flour and grease in the back room

Bakeries, pizzerias and restaurants on Morris Park Avenue often keep the provider's line in the same back room as ovens, proofers or a dish area. Network equipment does not like heat, moisture or flour dust. We put the cabinet on the coolest, cleanest wall available, use an enclosure with a door and filtered air where needed, and keep it away from the oven wall and the sink.

Gyms, studios and the shop with a crowd

Field Notes: Why a Van Nest House Survey Starts Outside, Not in the Room You Called About

Houses and the people who live in them

Our Queen Anne-style house has a rounded corner room on the second floor that we use as an office. Can a wired connection reach a curved room?

Usually, yes, and the attic is the way in. The framing under a corner tower or bay rarely lines up with the walls below it, so climbing from the cellar is often a dead end. From the attic or the space above the room's ceiling, we drop the cable down a flat section of wall or an inside corner and set the jack near the floor, low enough to stay clear of the curved trim. If the room has a closet or a straight wall shared with the next room, that is often the cleanest spot. We show you the exact point on the wall before drilling, and if no hidden route exists, we will say so and offer a small, painted raceway instead of damaging the plaster.

We rent our whole single-family house to one household on a yearly lease. Should the cable and the cabinet stay ours, with the tenants bringing only their own router?

That is the arrangement most owners find easiest. The cable inside the walls, the jacks and a small locked cabinet near the service entry become part of the house, like the plumbing. The tenants open their own account with any provider they like, and the provider's technician connects to the cable you already own. When the lease ends, the next household plugs in without anyone drilling. Put a line in the lease about who may open the cabinet, keep the port map with your house papers, and leave the tenants a simple sheet showing which jack serves which room.

Our newer brick two-family has a garage under the front of the house. Can the garage ceiling carry cable from the street side to the back rooms?

Often it is the best route in the house, because the garage ceiling is open or easy to open and runs most of the depth of the building. The catch is that the ceiling and the door between the garage and the living space are usually there to separate them for fire safety. Every hole we make through that ceiling is kept small and sealed with a rated firestop, cable is supported rather than draped across, and the cabinet goes on a wall of the garage or utility room, not where a car door or a roof rack will hit it. We photograph each seal for your file.

Our cellar on Van Nest Avenue near Colden Avenue takes water in summer downpours. How high should the router, the switch and the cabinet sit?

High enough that the worst water you have seen cannot reach any outlet, and higher still if you can. DEP's cloudburst summary names Van Nest Avenue along Radcliff, Colden and Lurting Avenues as a flooding hotspot, with mapped deep flooding of a foot or more. On those blocks we mount the cabinet near the top of a dry wall, plug it into an outlet at the same height, run cable along the joists, and keep nothing powered on the floor or a low shelf. If the cellar floods often, we move the cabinet to the first floor and leave only the provider's entry below.

New buildings and the rezoned blocks

We have plans for a small building on Morris Park Avenue with a store, a doctor's office and apartments above. Does each use need its own path from where the providers come in?

Yes, and it costs very little if it is drawn in from the start. Put every provider's entry in one telecom space, usually a locked wall in the cellar or a ground-floor utility room, then run a separate sleeve or conduit from there to the store, to the office and up to a small panel in each apartment. The store and the office can each have their own provider and never share equipment with the tenants upstairs, and no future tenant needs a new hole in the front wall. Filings such as 841A Morris Park Avenue, with retail, a 389-square-foot doctor's office and eight apartments, show how many different users one small building can hold.

The apartments in our new building average about 700 square feet. Where should the panel inside each unit go when there is barely a spare closet?

A small recessed enclosure, roughly the size of a medicine cabinet, fits in an interior wall near the middle of the apartment, often in the hall beside a closet or in the closet itself. From there a short run reaches the living room and each bedroom, and the provider's equipment sits inside the enclosure instead of on the floor by the front door. We place it on a wall that is not shared with a bathroom or kitchen, give it a dedicated outlet, and size it so a provider's ONT and a small router will both fit.

The Parkchester/Van Nest station keeps being pushed back. Should our building's telecom space be sized for the tenants we have now or the ones we expect once trains stop here?

Size the pathways for the building you expect, and buy cable for the tenants you have. Streetsblog NYC reported in October 2025 that the MTA expects Penn Station Access to finish in 2030 at the earliest, with some service to temporary stations hoped for under a 2027 plan. Your building will outlast any of those dates. Empty conduit, pull strings and wall space for extra providers are cheap during construction and very hard to add later; the cable itself can be pulled when a unit is actually leased.

We own a one-story commercial building on a block the 2024 rezoning opened to housing. If we add apartments on top, can the store's existing internet line serve the whole building?

It can technically be shared, but it should not be the plan. A line sized and contracted for one business, coming in at the back of the store, leaves the apartments dependent on the store's account, its equipment and its hours. The better plan is a proper telecom space where every provider can terminate, a riser to the new floors, and a separate account for each apartment. The store's existing cable can be audited and reused inside the store if it tests well. We plan that layout with your architect while the addition is still on paper.

The avenue and its storefronts

The Morris Park Avenue revitalization plan proposed moving the avenue's overhead utility lines underground. If that happens, does the internet line into our store change?

The Greater Morris Park Downtown Revitalization Initiative application of October 2024 proposed undergrounding utility cables on the east side of Morris Park Avenue, at an estimated $20 million. It was a proposal in a funding application, not a scheduled project, so nothing changes until a utility and the city announce real work. If it ever does happen, the provider would move its own service drop into your building. What you control is the inside: a sealed entry point, a cabinet close to it, and labeled cable from there to every counter. Keep that tidy and a change of entry only means a short new run to your cabinet.

We are applying for a storefront façade grant on Morris Park Avenue. Should the cable work happen before or after the new storefront goes in?

Plan it before and finish it during. The revitalization application proposed ten façade grants for businesses on the avenue, and a new storefront is the one time the front wall, the sign band and the ceiling behind them are all open at once. Use that moment to set a sleeve for the provider's line, run cable for a camera over the door and any digital sign, and hide everything that is now stapled to the outside. If the grant program has its own rules for the façade, we follow the architect's drawings and keep anything we add out of sight.

Does New York law let my landlord refuse the cable company's installer at our two-family, or only set conditions on how the line goes in?

Public Service Law section 228 says a landlord may not interfere with the installation of cable television facilities on the property, but it allows reasonable conditions that protect the building's safety, function and appearance and the comfort of other tenants, and it puts the cost and any damage on the provider, the tenant or both. That law is written for cable television companies, and we are not your lawyer, so for a dispute speak to one. In practice most owners simply want the line to come in at one sealed point and not across the front of the house, and a cabling plan that gives every provider that point usually ends the argument.

FAQ

Van Nest Structured Cabling Installation FAQ

Starry and Verizon's fixed wireless are offered alongside fiber here. If we choose one of those, does the cabling inside the house change?
+

Very little. A fixed-wireless service puts a small receiver on a window or the outside of the house and runs one cable from it to a box or router indoors, instead of a line from the pole or the street. Inside, the structured cabling is the same: a cabinet, a run to each floor and each room, and access points. The one thing to plan is the receiver's location, which is usually high and facing the provider's antenna, so we leave a sealed path from that wall to the cabinet. If you later switch to fiber, the cable in the walls stays exactly as it is.

Our attached brick house has only a low crawl space under the back rooms instead of a full cellar. Where should the network hub go?
+

Usually in a closet or under the stair on the first floor, as close as possible to where the provider's line enters. A crawl space is fine as a path for cable, routed along the joists and supported, but it is damp, hard to reach and a poor home for anything powered. From a first-floor closet, runs go down into the crawl space to reach the back rooms and up through the closet stack to the floors above. We check the crawl space with a light and a camera before deciding, and if it is wet we keep cable high and in a protective sleeve.

A house has no suite numbers. How do you label the jacks so we can tell them apart years from now?
+

By floor and room, in words you already use. Each run gets a short printed label at both ends, such as second floor front bedroom, desk wall, plus a number that matches the patch panel. The port map taped inside the cabinet door lists every label in order with the room it serves, and a copy goes into your house papers. For a two-family we add the apartment, so upstairs and downstairs never get mixed up. When a jack stops working, you read the label on the faceplate and know exactly which port to check.

Our practice is taking a ground-floor office of under 400 square feet in a new Morris Park Avenue building. How many network outlets does a room that small need?
+

More than the size suggests, because the count follows the equipment, not the floor area. A small practice typically needs runs for the front desk computer and its card terminal or check-in tablet, a printer or fax, each exam or consult position, any vendor device such as an imaging unit or a medical equipment router, a ceiling access point, and a camera at the door. We build the list with you from the equipment you will actually use, add two or three spares at the cabinet, and keep the cabinet locked and out of the patients' area.

Our bakery's ovens and proofers share the back room where the internet line comes in. Where can the network cabinet safely live?
+

On the coolest, driest wall of that room, away from the ovens and the sink, or in a nearby office or storage area with a short run back to the provider's entry. Heat, steam and flour dust shorten the life of routers and switches, so we use a closed cabinet with filtered ventilation where the room is warm, mount it well off the floor, and keep the provider's box clear of the work area too. Counter equipment such as the register and the card terminal each gets its own run from that cabinet, so nothing at the counter depends on Wi-Fi.

Can the job leave pull strings or spare runs for cameras and a video doorbell we have not bought yet?
+

Yes, and it is one of the most useful things to do while the walls are already open or while a route is already being fished. We can run a cable now and leave it coiled and labeled behind a blank plate at the door, the side alley or the garage, or leave a pull string in a sleeve so the cable can be added later in minutes. Either way it goes on the port map as a reserved run. When you are ready for cameras or a doorbell, the installer connects to a tested cable instead of drilling again.

Should we choose between Verizon, Optimum and EarthLink fiber before the house is cabled, or does it not matter?
+

It matters very little for the cabling. Every provider ends at a box and a router, and our cable starts there, so the network inside works the same whichever company you pick. The only thing that helps to know is roughly where each provider prefers to enter the house, so we put the cabinet close to the likely entry and leave space on the wall for a second provider's box. If you switch providers in a few years, the new company connects at the same spot and every room keeps its connection.

The walls of our new building are concrete block. How do you get cable through block without cracking it?
+

With a core drill sized to the sleeve, set square to the wall and run slowly, after scanning for anything inside the block. A clean round hole does not stress the surrounding block the way a hammer drill and chisel do. A sleeve goes through the hole, the cable passes through the sleeve, and the opening is sealed, with a rated firestop if the wall is a fire separation. In new construction we prefer to mark the holes with the builder before the walls go up, so the sleeves can be set while the block is being laid.

I rent the first floor of a Van Nest house and want a wired desk. What should I get in writing from the owner before booking?
+

A short note or email from the owner that approves the route, the location of any equipment, and who will own the cable afterward. The route matters most if it passes through the cellar or the owner's part of the house. Agree whether the cable stays when you move out, which is usual, and whether the owner or you pays. We can send the owner the drop plan for that one run, with photos of where the hole and the jack would go, so the approval is quick. We will not drill in a rented house without the owner's yes.

Our house sits on one of the Van Nest Avenue blocks that floods in heavy rain. Can a flooded cellar take out the whole house's internet even if the rooms upstairs stay dry?
+

Yes, if the provider's box, the router or the power strip feeding them sits low in the cellar. The line from the street enters there, so whatever is powered at that point decides whether the house is online. That is why on flood-prone blocks we mount the cabinet high, keep its outlet at the same height, and keep the run from the provider's entry short and dry. The cable to the rooms can run along the joists, out of the water's reach. If the provider's own box is mounted low, ask the provider to raise it; we will photograph it for your request.

Who in our household should hold the cabinet key and the port map once the job is finished?
+

Whoever owns the house, with a copy of the port map wherever the household keeps important papers. The cabinet holds the router, the switch and the provider's connection, so it should be locked against curious children and visitors, but the key should never be with only one person who may be away when something fails. In an owner-and-tenant house, the owner holds the key and the tenant gets a simple sheet listing their jacks and what to check before calling. Keep a second copy of the map outside the cellar in case the cellar is wet when you need it.
