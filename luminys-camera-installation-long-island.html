The Neighborhood

Why Long Island Properties Need Luminys Camera Installation

Long Island is a place of detached houses with driveways, and that single fact shapes most of what a camera system here has to do. The Census Bureau's 2023 five-year American Community Survey counts 477,860 housing units in Nassau County, of which 364,529 are one-unit detached homes, and 579,790 units in Suffolk County, of which 463,095 are detached. The median Nassau home was built in 1955; the median Suffolk home in 1970. That is a fifteen-year gap, and you can see it from the street. Nassau is the Island of the postwar ranch and Cape Cod, the slab houses of Levittown and the block-foundation Capes of Uniondale and Hempstead. Suffolk, further out, is more often a colonial, a split-level or a hi-ranch from the 1960s through the 1980s, on a bigger lot with a longer driveway. Each kind of house hides cable differently, and the camera that watches the front door of one is fixed into a completely different wall from the camera on the other.

The Island's municipal map is just as layered. According to the state's county profiles, Nassau has two cities, three towns, 64 incorporated villages and more than 60 unincorporated hamlets in 453 square miles, while Suffolk is divided into 10 towns across 2,373 square miles. Uniondale, home to the Nassau Hub, is one of those hamlets: Wikipedia notes that residents tried to incorporate it as a village in the early 1970s, failed, and that it remains an unincorporated hamlet governed by the Town of Hempstead. Even the mailing address can mislead. A local forum post that compared official maps found the hamlet of Uniondale covers about 1,654 acres while the Uniondale 11553 ZIP code covers about 2,073. For a camera install, that patchwork matters because the rules on rentals, building permits and contractor licensing are set by different layers of government depending on exactly where your property sits, and we check the address before we promise anything about paperwork.

The reason people call is usually the driveway. In January 2024, ABC7 reported on a multi-jurisdictional task force of Nassau and Suffolk police, district attorneys, the NYPD, State Police and the FBI set up to fight a run of residential burglaries and stolen cars, and quoted police saying the burglars "are attracted by cars in people's driveways." The same report noted break-ins while residents were home had gone up. In November 2025, CBS New York reported that the Suffolk District Attorney charged 14 men in a package-theft ring that worked 31 communities, including Brentwood, Central Islip, Shirley and Deer Park, between October 2023 and February 2025, allegedly targeting deliveries of phones and tablets and taking more than 200 devices. On the Nassau side, Patch reported a car taken from Uniondale Avenue near Waverly Place after it was left running with the keys inside, and police released a screenshot of the suspects from video. The pattern in all of these is the same: something valuable sitting outside a detached house, and a camera that either saw it clearly or did not.

Businesses have the same story at a different scale. In September 2026, News12 Long Island reported that the owner of Morning Delights in Uniondale got a motion alert from his security camera, went to the restaurant and found a burglary suspect there. Daily Voice reported a robbery at the Pump & Stuff gas station on Jerusalem Avenue in Uniondale where police released the store's surveillance footage of the suspects. Those reports describe cameras doing their job: alerting an owner, and handing police a clear picture. They are also a reminder that a camera that alerts is worthless if the alert goes to an app nobody still has on their phone, or a recorder whose hard drive filled up last spring. Those are exactly the faults we find most often when we open an older system, and they are why the first part of any Luminys job is checking what is already there.

When the cameras can stay

The best case for an owner is the one where the cameras are fine and the recorder is the problem. Hard drives wear out, recorders lose their settings after a surge, and a recorder bought for four cameras cannot take the six you now want. If your Dahua cameras pass our tests on a Luminys recorder, you keep the cameras, the cables and the mounts, and only the box in the closet changes. That is usually the least disruptive upgrade we do in a Long Island house, because nothing in the attic or on the eaves has to be touched. We still check every camera individually rather than assuming. Some older models may lose features such as smart motion detection when they run on a different recorder, and we would rather tell you that before the old unit is gone than after.

When a camera has to be swapped

The opposite situation is harder. If your recorder works but one camera on the garage has died, a new Luminys camera may not run on that Dahua recorder, according to the forum reports above. Then the choice is between finding a replacement Dahua camera of the same generation, which gets harder every year, or changing the recorder now and carrying the remaining Dahua cameras over to it if they test well. We lay out both options with the price of each from the Long Island list, and we test before you decide. Many owners with a recorder that is already a few years old decide the recorder change makes more sense, because it buys them a current app and current security updates at the same time.

When the wire is the real problem

The Luminys wiki puts it bluntly: for many businesses the biggest obstacle to upgrading an old analog system is not the cameras but the wire. On Long Island that wire is often coax stapled along a basement ceiling in a 1950s house, run across a hot attic to an eave camera, or buried along a driveway to a detached garage. Some of it is still good; some has been chewed, crushed or soaked. We test each run before deciding. Where coax is sound, it may be reused with the right equipment; where it is not, we pull new network cable, which carries both power and video to a modern Luminys camera on a single line. Either way, the decision is made from a test result, not from the age of the cable.

Building Specialists

Luminys Camera Installation for Long Island's Architecture

Long Island's houses look similar from a passing car, but the way a camera cable gets from the eave to the recorder changes completely between a slab ranch, a Cape with a finished upper floor and a colonial with a full basement. These are the types the Census data, town histories and local listings describe here, and how we handle each.

Slab Ranches (Levittown and Its Kind)

One storey on a concrete slab, with no basement to run cable through and, in the original Levittown design, no garage. The attic is the only real pathway, and in an expanded attic it may be living space.

Cape Cods with Finished Upper Floors

The type NeighborhoodScout and Homes.com describe across Uniondale. Knee walls and sloped ceilings upstairs, block foundations below, and often a finished basement added later that hides the joists.

Split-Levels and Hi-Ranches

Common near Hofstra and the Coliseum, according to a local guide. Three or four short floors offset by half a storey, so a cable has to find a path between levels, not just up and down.

Suffolk Colonials and Newer Houses

Two full storeys with a basement and usually an attached garage, typical of the post-1970 houses that set Suffolk's median build year. More room for cable, but longer runs to the far corners.

Seasonal and East End Houses

Houses empty for weeks or months, where remote alerts matter most. The East Hampton Star printed a still from an indoor camera in one of two houses burglarized on Sammy's Beach Road.

Rentals and Multi-Family

Rented houses and apartments in East Garden City's condos and townhouses. Drilling needs the owner's agreement, and common areas belong to the association or landlord.

On slab ranches. The architect's history of Levittown describes the original houses as ranches built on concrete slabs, with an expandable attic, no garage and hot-water radiant heating pipes in the floor. That last detail is the one that matters most to us. We never drill down into a slab house's floor to run cable, because a heating pipe in the slab is expensive and disruptive to repair. Everything goes up instead: into the attic, across the joists, and down to the camera position through the soffit or the top plate of an exterior wall. Where the attic was expanded into a bedroom decades ago, as the design allowed, the remaining crawl spaces behind the knee walls are often the only route left, and we plan the camera positions around where those spaces reach.

On block foundations and finished basements. A Uniondale restoration contractor, which is a business selling its own services rather than an independent source, describes most local homes as Capes and ranches on concrete-block foundations with finished basements added in the 1960s and 1970s. The same contractor warns that postwar Capes and ranches there frequently contain asbestos materials. We take both points seriously. Block walls are drilled with a hammer drill and masonry bits, with anchors set in the block rather than the mortar joint, and any penetration to the outside is sealed. Where we see old materials that might contain asbestos, such as certain old basement floor tiles or pipe insulation, we do not cut or drill them; we route around them or stop and tell you, because disturbing them is a job for a licensed abatement contractor, not a camera installer.

On split-levels. The split-level is a Long Island classic and a puzzle for cable. A guide to the Hempstead area describes the homes near Hofstra and the Coliseum as mostly 1950s and 1960s split-levels and hi-ranches. Because each floor is offset by half a storey from the next, the wall cavities do not line up the way they do in a colonial, and fire blocking often sits where you would expect an open chase. We usually work from two ends: the attic over the bedroom level for the upper cameras, and the garage ceiling or lower-level utility room for the driveway and front door, meeting at the recorder in between.

What a Long Island business camera has to do

A house camera mostly watches a door and a driveway. A business camera on Long Island usually has to watch a parking lot, and that changes the hardware. Lots on Hempstead Turnpike, in the Route 110 office parks and around the Hauppauge flex buildings are wide, flat and lit unevenly at night, so a single wide-angle camera on the building corner will show that a car arrived but rarely who got out or what plate it carried. We plan commercial jobs camera by camera with the owner: a plate camera at the entrance lane, aimed within the 30-degree limit Luminys publishes for its plate readers; a wider camera for the whole lot; and tighter cameras on the doors where faces pass at a predictable distance. Inside, raised floors like the four-inch access floors RXR Plaza's listing describes make cable routing easier, while older flex buildings need J-hooks or conduit across a high open ceiling.

Storefronts, restaurants and late hours

The Morning Delights case in Uniondale is a good picture of what a small business needs: a motion alert that reaches the owner's phone after hours, and video clear enough to act on. For restaurants, delis and small stores in strip plazas, we set up motion zones that ignore the passing traffic on the Turnpike but trigger on the door and the register area, and we configure alerts on the owner's phone through the Luminys mobile app. We also make sure the recorder itself is somewhere a burglar will not simply carry it out, because the most common way to lose footage in a break-in is to lose the box it was stored on.

Rules and practical limits for business cameras

  • State license. New York General Business Law §69-m says no one may engage in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security systems without a license, and 19 NYCRR 195.2, as Kirschenbaum Esq. explains it, applies that to CCTV used to detect or monitor intrusion, theft or movement. Our work is done under NYS license #12000287431.
  • County contractor licensing. Nassau County's Consumer Affairs office says it is against the law to run a home improvement business in the county without a license, and its FAQ says work only on commercial buildings does not need that license. Suffolk County Code Chapter 563 has its own licensing for regulated trades, with exceptions for towns and villages that license separately.
  • Building permits. Town of Hempstead code requires a building permit for work that must conform to the Uniform Code or Energy Code. Low-voltage camera work usually falls outside that, but new electrical circuits for cameras or a rack do not, and those are an electrician's job.
  • Penetrations through rated walls. In office towers and multi-tenant buildings, any cable passing through a fire-rated wall or floor has to be fire-stopped. We check the building's requirements with the property manager before the visit.
  • Recording audio. Many Luminys cameras can record sound. Video of a parking lot is one thing; recording conversations is another, and we leave audio off unless the owner has a clear reason and has taken their own legal advice.
  • Insurance and access. Property managers at office buildings usually want a certificate of insurance and set working hours. Send us the requirements with the booking and we arrive with the paperwork.

Nassau or Suffolk: How the County Changes a Luminys Camera Job

This page covers all of Long Island, but in practice we quote and schedule by county, because the houses, the distances and the paperwork are different on each side of the county line. Nassau and Suffolk each have their own price page, and the rate depends on the property's address, not the mailing name of the post office.

Nassau: older, denser, closer together

Nassau's 1955 median build year means most of the houses we wire there are postwar ranches, Capes and split-levels on modest lots, close to their neighbors. Cameras on those houses often look straight across a narrow side yard at the next house, so we aim them to cover your property and keep a neighbor's windows out of the view. Cable runs tend to be short, but the pathways are tight: slab floors in Levittown-type ranches, finished basements in Capes, and small attics. Nassau's 64 villages each have their own building departments, and the county's home improvement license applies to residential work. For Nassau rates, see the Nassau County Luminys price page.

Suffolk: newer, bigger lots, longer runs

Suffolk's 1970 median build year and its far larger area, 2,373 square miles against Nassau's 453, mean bigger houses on bigger lots and longer driveways. A camera at the end of a Suffolk driveway may be well beyond what a single network cable can carry without help, and detached garages, sheds and pool houses are more common. Suffolk also has the East End, where seasonal houses sit empty for long periods, and it had the August 2024 flash flood, which the state Department of Financial Services described as the county's first-ever flash flood emergency, with the Stony Brook Mill Pond dam failing and more than 2,000 residents and business owners suffering flood damage. That is why we never put a Suffolk recorder on a basement floor without asking where the water went last time. For Suffolk rates, see the Suffolk County Luminys price page.

What stays the same across the Island

The method does not change at the county line. We still check any existing system first, still survey before we quote, still label every cable and still hand over a written record. The state license that covers the work is the same in Glen Cove and Montauk. And whichever county you are in, the price you see on the county page is the price we work from, with nothing added on the day unless you ask for it.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The First Half Hour at a Long Island House with an Old System

We do not publish stories about a particular customer's house unless we can document them, so this is not a tale about one address. It is the routine our technicians follow at the kind of property that is all over Nassau and Suffolk: a postwar house with a camera system someone installed years ago, and an owner who is not sure what still works.

We find the recorder first. Often it is in a basement corner, a hall closet or behind the TV, and often it is warm to the touch with its fan clogged. We photograph its label before touching anything and ask whether there is footage the owner needs. Then we walk outside and look at every camera: what model it is, which way it points, whether the lens is clean, and where its cable disappears into the house. We go into the attic and look at how the old cables were run, whether they are stapled across joists, lying on insulation or crushed under a board someone added for storage. Back inside, we check whether the recorder can still be logged into and whether its hard drive is healthy. Only after that half hour do we start talking about Luminys, because by then we know which cameras can stay, which cables are worth keeping, and where a new recorder can live without overheating.

Your Questions Answered

Luminys Camera Installation in Long Island: Questions Answered

Mixing old Dahua equipment with new Luminys equipment

The Dahua cameras on our Nassau ranch still look fine. Will they run on a new Luminys recorder?

Very often, yes, but we confirm it camera by camera. A St. Louis installer writes that most existing Dahua cameras are fully compatible with Luminys recorders, and an IPCamTalk user reports a Luminys recorder running Dahua cameras without trouble. What those reports cannot tell you is whether your exact models will keep every feature, such as smart motion detection or a particular night mode. So we connect each of your cameras to the Luminys recorder, check live view, recording and motion events, and only then retire the old recorder. If a camera fails, you learn it before you have paid for anything around it.

One camera died on our old Dahua system. Can a single new Luminys camera just be plugged into it?

Possibly not. Users on the IPCamTalk forum report that Luminys cameras do not work on a Dahua recorder, even though the opposite direction often works. We test before you buy, because the answer may depend on the recorder's age and firmware. If the test fails, the choice is between hunting down a matching older Dahua camera or replacing the recorder and carrying your remaining Dahua cameras over to it. We price both from the Long Island list so you can compare, and we will tell you honestly which one we would pick for a recorder of your system's age.

Is a Luminys camera really just a Dahua camera with a different name on it?

The installer community has been arguing about that since the sale. An IPCamTalk thread is literally titled with the question of whether Luminys is 100 percent rebranded or "more like 85," and one poster there says the quality is the same as Dahua's but the prices are higher, even at wholesale. What is clear from the record is that Luminys took over the former Dahua USA business, that its products carry their own firmware, and that ADI lists them as NDAA-compliant. For you as an owner, the important differences are practical: which app and software you use, and which older equipment works alongside it, and those we test on site.

Our old Dahua recorder keeps asking for an update. Can it be updated to Luminys firmware?

No. A forum poster on IPCamTalk notes that Dahua firmware cannot be loaded onto Luminys products, and cross-loading firmware between brands in either direction is a reliable way to turn a working recorder into a brick. Each device should only receive firmware published for its own brand and model. If your Dahua recorder still receives updates from its own source and works well, it can carry on; if it is past support or failing, replacing it with a Luminys recorder is the cleaner path. We check what your unit can safely be updated to before we touch it.

Software, apps and remote viewing

Our office manages cameras in DSS on a server. What changes if we move to LumiCenter?

The software on the server and on the desks changes; the cameras may not have to. Luminys publishes a FAQ for customers moving from DSS to LumiCenter, and its LumiCenter FAQ says the software supports native camera profiles, generic manufacturer profiles and ONVIF. That gives several ways to bring existing cameras across. Before the switch, we list every camera, user and permission in your DSS setup, check how each camera will connect to LumiCenter, and plan the change for a day when a short gap in live view will not matter. Recorded video on the old system is either exported or left accessible until you no longer need it.

Our family watches the cameras in DMSS on our phones. Does everyone have to switch apps?

If the recorder becomes a Luminys unit, yes, plan on it. Distributor listings for Luminys recorders describe remote viewing through LumiViewer Mobile, the Luminys app, and IPCamTalk users have been debating whether it is better than DMSS since it appeared. At handover we install the app on each family member's phone, log them in with their own user rather than one shared password, and show them live view, playback and how to save a clip. Older relatives who only want alerts can be set up that way, and anyone who never uses the app can be left off it entirely.

Storms, power cuts and the internet

When a storm knocks out our power and internet, do the cameras stop recording?

Without power, every camera system stops, which is why we connect the recorder and its cameras to a battery backup sized to carry them through short outages. Long ones, like Isaias in 2020, when ABC7 reported almost half of PSEG Long Island's customers in the dark at the peak, will outlast any reasonable battery, but they will not erase what was already recorded. Losing internet alone is a different matter: a Luminys recorder keeps recording to its own drive with no internet connection, and only remote viewing and phone alerts pause until the connection returns. Video recorded during the outage is waiting when you get home.

If we stop paying for a LumiCloud plan, do we lose access to our cameras?

No. Luminys's own LumiCloud page says that if a license expires, you keep full local access to your cameras and recorders. What you lose is the cloud feature you were paying for, such as off-site storage. LumiCloud plans are registered through the Luminys MVP program, which we handle as your installer. For Long Island owners who mostly want to check the driveway from their phone, the included tier usually covers it, and paid storage makes most sense for seasonal houses, small businesses that worry about a recorder being stolen, and anyone who wants a copy of the video somewhere other than the closet.

Compliance for businesses

We supply government agencies from a Hauppauge facility. Is a Luminys system NDAA-compliant?

Luminys markets its cameras, software and kits as NDAA-compliant, and ADI Global Distribution lists them that way. Distributor listings make the same claim for specific recorders such as the R42-16PA, and Luminys's LumiCenter FAQ addresses the software's NDAA status as well. If your contract carries specific equipment requirements, send us the clause and we will match each model we propose to the manufacturer's published compliance statement before you sign, so your compliance file has a document for every device rather than our word.

We read that the FCC went after Luminys. Is it still legal to install the equipment?

According to SecurityInfoWatch, the FCC set aside Luminys's equipment authorizations in 2025, then, in an order released January 20, withdrew that action and terminated the proceeding, clearing the equipment. The earlier order is published on the FCC's site as DA-25-488. We follow those rulings as they stand and check the current status of any model before we install it. If the status ever changes again, existing owners would hear about it from us rather than discovering it on their own.

Long driveways, parking lots and old wire

Our detached garage is at the end of a long driveway. How does a camera cable get out there?

There are three usual ways, and the site decides which. If the garage already has an underground power feed, there may be a conduit or trench path we can follow, though data cable should not share a conduit with power. If not, we bury a separate conduit along the edge of the driveway, or run cable overhead where a line already crosses. Very long runs may need a small PoE switch in the garage, fed by one cable from the house, which a Slickdeals poster suggested for a similar setup to keep drilling to a minimum. Wherever cable enters the house from outside, we protect it at the entry point against surges.

Can one camera read the plates on every lane coming into our office park lot?

Usually not, if the entrance is wide. Luminys's own plate-reader installation guide says the angle between the camera lens and the edge of the farthest lane must not exceed 30 degrees. On a two-lane entrance, a camera set back and placed in line with the lanes can often stay inside that limit; on a wide entrance or one with a curve, two cameras are more reliable. We measure the entrance, the height available on a pole or wall, and the distance to where cars actually slow down, then show you the planned view before we drill.

Our old analog cameras run on coax through the basement. Does all of it have to come out?

Not automatically. Luminys says that for many businesses the biggest obstacle to upgrading an old analog system is the wire, which is a fair description of a lot of Long Island basements. We test each coax run for damage and length. Sound runs may be reused where the equipment allows; damaged runs, such as coax crushed under a finished ceiling or soaked in a flood, are replaced with network cable. The result can be a mixed system for a while, and we label clearly which camera rides on which kind of cable.

Should we register our Luminys cameras with the police, and does that give them live access?

It depends on where you are, and no, registration does not hand over live access. The Suffolk County Police Camera Registry Program lets owners register the location of their systems, and the department may ask participants to check video for a date and time, with retrieval "at the convenience of the camera owner." In Nassau, the Garden City Police run a voluntary private camera registration and state plainly that it does not give them access to privately owned systems. We could not find a county-wide Nassau registry. If you want to register, we show you how to export a clip quickly so a request takes minutes.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Luminys Camera Installation in Long Island

We could not capture Google's AI Overview or People Also Ask boxes for Luminys installation on Long Island from our build environment, so we are not going to pretend to quote them. What follows are claims we see repeated in quick online answers about Luminys and about camera installs in this region, set against what the published record and our own field practice say. Marketplaces such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish cost guides that feed many of those answers; they are useful as a rough idea but they are not a quote for your driveway.

"Luminys is a banned Chinese brand."

This one comes from mixing up the parent and the former subsidiary. Dahua Technology, the Chinese manufacturer, is on the US entity list. Luminys is the former Dahua USA business, which SecurityInfoWatch reports was sold in early 2024, and which The Register reported went to a Taiwan-based buyer.

The FCC did act against Luminys's equipment authorizations in 2025. But in an order released January 20, it withdrew that action and terminated the proceeding, according to SecurityInfoWatch. The equipment was cleared, not banned.

For a Long Island business with government contracts, the more useful question is the NDAA status of each model, which Luminys and its distributors publish. We check that model by model rather than relying on a summary.

"Since Luminys came from Dahua, everything is interchangeable."

Quick answers often assume a Luminys camera drops straight into a Dahua system and the other way round. The installer reports do not support that. IPCamTalk users report Luminys cameras not working on Dahua recorders, even when Dahua cameras run on Luminys recorders.

Firmware is a separate wall. A forum poster notes that Dahua firmware cannot be loaded onto Luminys products, so you cannot "convert" one brand's device into the other with an update.

On Long Island, where many systems pre-date the sale, this matters more than any other Luminys question. We test your actual devices together before quoting, because a published claim about "most" cameras says nothing about yours.

"You'll pay a monthly fee just to see your cameras on your phone."

That is true of many consumer camera brands and repeated as if it applies to all of them. For Luminys recorders, a distributor listing for the R42-16PA says no subscription is required for basic remote access and that LumiCloud Lite is included at no extra charge for remote viewing in LumiViewer Mobile.

Paid LumiCloud plans exist for off-site storage and direct-to-cloud cameras. Luminys says that if a license expires, you keep full local access to your cameras and recorders.

For most Long Island houses, local recording plus the included remote viewing covers what owners actually want: checking the driveway from work and getting an alert when someone walks up the side yard.

"Wireless cameras are the easy answer for suburban houses."

Quick answers often treat wireless cameras as the obvious choice for a detached house. On Long Island lots, a camera at the end of a long driveway or on a detached garage is often far from the router, and a "wireless" camera still needs power at the camera.

A wired PoE camera gets power and video over one cable and records to a recorder inside the house, whether or not the Wi-Fi is having a good day. The trade-off is that the cable has to be run properly, which is the actual work on a slab ranch or a split-level.

Wireless and cloud cameras have their place, particularly for renters and for spots no cable can reach. We use them where they make sense and say so, but we do not default to them because the install looks quicker on paper.

"Anyone can install security cameras in New York; there's no license."

Some guides say no state license is needed, and for a homeowner putting up a camera on their own house, one permit guide does say exactly that. The rule changes for businesses. General Business Law §69-m says no person may engage in the business of installing security systems without a license.

The Department of State's guidance and the regulation, 19 NYCRR 195.2, as Kirschenbaum Esq. explains it, bring CCTV used to detect or monitor intrusion, theft or movement into that rule, while "passive" non-detecting devices do not require licensure. An industry association warns of substantial fines for unlicensed installers.

On top of the state license, Nassau's Consumer Affairs office requires a home improvement license for residential contracting, and Suffolk has its own licensing code. Ask anyone who quotes you for their license number. Ours is NYS license #12000287431.

"Camera installation costs about the same anywhere in New York."

Cost answers often quote a single New York figure, and the Angi figure most of them repeat is labelled for New York, NY, meaning the city. Long Island jobs are priced differently, and Nassau and Suffolk differ from each other.

Within a Long Island job, the house and the lot move the total far more than the camera does. A four-camera system on a Suffolk colonial with an open basement is a different job from four cameras on a Levittown slab ranch where every cable goes through the attic, or a plate camera at a Route 110 office entrance.

That is why our price pages list each item separately, so you can build your own estimate, and why we never quote a number that is not on that list. Start from the Long Island page and use the county page that matches your address.

"The old cable in the walls will handle new 4K cameras."

Quick answers sometimes suggest that whatever cable is already in the house can carry a new high-resolution camera. Old coax may or may not be usable, depending on its condition and the equipment, and Luminys itself calls the wire the biggest obstacle in many analog upgrades.

Network cable has limits too. A standard Ethernet link is designed for runs up to about 90 meters of permanent cable, and Fluke Networks notes that insertion loss rises with temperature, citing a standard that reduces the allowable length by 0.4 percent per degree Celsius between 20 and 40 degrees for unscreened cable. Long Island attics in July get hot.

So we test old runs before reusing them, keep new runs off the hottest part of the attic where we can, and certify long runs before a camera goes on the end. A cable that barely passes in October can fail in August.

Our Installation Process

Every Long Island Luminys job follows the same eight steps, whether it is two cameras on a Cape in Uniondale or a plate reader and twelve cameras at a Hauppauge warehouse. The order matters most when there is an old system involved, because pulling out a recorder before checking what is on it is how footage and settings get lost.

1. Send photos and what you have now

Photos of the house or building and the spots you want covered, plus the label on any existing recorder and the name of the app you use. That tells us whether this is a new install or a Dahua upgrade.

2. Walk the property

A technician walks the driveway, garage, eaves, attic, basement and any lot or entrance, and checks where the recorder, power, battery backup and internet can live.

3. Write the camera-by-camera plan

Every camera position, its view, its cable route and its recorder channel go into a written plan and quote from the Long Island price list before you book.

4. Prepare and update the recorder

The Luminys recorder is set up, updated with its own manufacturer firmware, given strong unique passwords and labelled before it is installed.

5. Run and protect the cable

PoE cable through attics, basements, ceilings or conduit, or old coax tested for reuse, with every run labelled at both ends and sealed where it goes outside.

6. Mount and aim the cameras

Cameras fixed into framing, siding mounting blocks, masonry or parapet brackets, sealed against weather and aimed at the agreed views.

7. Set recording and remote access

Schedules, motion and analytics zones, plate-reader lanes where used, and remote viewing on LumiViewer Mobile or LumiCenter for each user.

8. Test, label and hand over

Every camera checked by day and at night where possible, playback and clip export shown to you, and a written record left of channels, users and cable routes.

Why labor is a big part of a Long Island quote

People are sometimes surprised that the installation can cost more than the cameras. On Long Island, most of the work in a camera job is in the house, not the box: getting cable from a recorder in a basement or closet to an eave on the far side of a slab ranch, through a Cape's knee walls, across a split-level's offset floors, or out to a detached garage. Then each camera has to be sealed against weather, aimed, and set up with motion zones that ignore the street but catch the side door. That is skilled work under a state license, and it is the part that decides whether the system still works in five years.

A business that installs cameras used to detect or monitor intrusion, theft or movement needs a New York State security installer license under General Business Law section 69-m and the Department of State rules. Do I need a building permit for security cameras in Nassau County?
+

Usually not for the low-voltage camera work itself, but it depends on the town or village. Can summer attic heat affect the cables to my roof-edge cameras?
+

It can on long runs. Fluke Networks explains that cable insertion loss rises with temperature, and the standard it cites reduces allowable length as cable gets hotter. Long Island attics get very hot in July. We keep runs short where we can, route away from the hottest areas under the roof deck, and test long runs before mounting the camera so a marginal cable is caught early.

How do outdoor cameras hold up to hurricanes and salt air on the South Shore?
+

Outdoor-rated cameras are built for rain and wind, but the weak points are usually the cable entry and the mount, not the camera. We seal every penetration, use drip loops so water runs away from the connection, and fix mounts into framing or masonry rather than siding alone. Near the water we check mounts and connectors more closely at service visits, because salt air is hard on exposed metal over time.
