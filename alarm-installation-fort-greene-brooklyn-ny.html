The Neighborhood

Why Fort Greene Properties Need Alarm Installation

Fort Greene occupies a little over half a square mile of northwest Brooklyn. Wikipedia draws it between Flushing Avenue and the Brooklyn Navy Yard on the north, the Flatbush Avenue Extension and Downtown Brooklyn on the west, Atlantic Avenue and Prospect Heights on the south, and Vanderbilt Avenue and Clinton Hill on the east, with Fulton Street, Lafayette Avenue and DeKalb Avenue as its main arteries and the Brooklyn–Queens Expressway along its northern edge. Residents do not agree on those lines: a 2015 DNAinfo survey found newer arrivals pulling the northern border south toward Myrtle Avenue and the eastern one out toward Classon. Four ZIP codes, 11201, 11205, 11217 and 11238, divide it into quarters, and all of it sits in Brooklyn Community District 2.

The houses explain most of what an installer finds. In the 1850s and 1860s, builders filled the open land between the stagecoach lines on Myrtle Avenue and Fulton Street with blocks of Italianate brick and brownstone rowhouses, and named the best streets, Portland, Oxford, Cumberland, Carlton and Adelphi, after fashionable terraces in Westminster. By the 1870s building had largely stopped, and Wikipedia counts hundreds of Italianate, Second Empire, Greek Revival, Neo-Grec, Romanesque Revival and Renaissance Revival rowhouses still standing in close to original form. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Fort Greene Historic District and the neighboring BAM Historic District in 1978, and the area was listed on the National Register in 1983 and expanded the following year. Those houses are tall and narrow, with a stoop to a parlor floor, a garden level a few steps below the sidewalk, and windows at the back that look over a row of private yards.

What changed inside them matters more for an alarm than what stayed the same outside. A great many Fort Greene rowhouses are no longer single homes. They are an owner’s duplex over a garden rental, or three or four floor-through apartments sharing one street door, one vestibule and one stair. Real estate listings describe the arrangement constantly; one widely read 2023 Brownstoner roundup of a Fort Greene Place brownstone summed it up as a duplex above a garden rental. Every one of those apartments has keys, every tenant who has ever lived there has had keys, and the street door is opened dozens of times a day by people the owner has never met. That is why a striking number of the burglaries reported around the 88th Precinct involve doors that were simply opened, not forced.

The blotter bears that out. DNAinfo reported in September 2014 that burglaries in Fort Greene and Clinton Hill were up by roughly a quarter, from 90 to 114 for the year to date. Two of the incidents it described happened on the same night at about ten o’clock, at homes near Fort Greene Place and Hanson Place, and in both the burglars came in through an unlocked back window; a laptop, a desktop computer and an e-reader went from the first. A week earlier, an apartment near St. James Place and Lafayette Avenue was entered through a locked front door and emptied of a bicycle, a camera and two computers worth $3,300. The precinct’s commanding officer at the time, Capt. Peter Fiorillo, said home break-ins peaked between seven and nine in the morning and between three and five in the afternoon, the hours when people leave and before they return.

Brooklyn Paper’s police reports for the precinct add the detail that turns statistics into an alarm design. On one July day, in an apartment on Fulton Street between South Elliott Place and South Portland Avenue, a woman woke to find her wallet gone, along with the spare key she kept hidden in the hallway. The same week, a tenant on Clermont Avenue between Flushing and Park avenues came home to find the window air conditioner pushed into the room and a laptop, video games and cash missing. In another report, a bar on Adelphi Street lost its cash register at about 3:37 a.m. to a burglar who cut the wires of the outdoor security camera, broke the front window and was recorded by the cameras inside. A taqueria on Carlton Avenue had its front door broken at about six in the morning, and the owners found the entire break-in on their camera footage when they arrived. None of those Brooklyn Paper items carries a year on the page, and we say so in the sources below.

Read together, those reports describe three gaps an alarm is good at closing. Cameras in Fort Greene record, but recording is not responding: nobody is told while it happens. Keys circulate in divided buildings until nobody knows who can open the door. And the back of a rowhouse, which faces gardens rather than the street, is where unlocked windows and pushed-in air conditioners give way while people are home or asleep. The rest of this page takes each of those in turn, then the restaurant and bar corridors, where every reported break-in we found happened between half past three and six in the morning.

If you are adding or replacing cameras at the same time, tell us at the start. The two systems work best when the links between them are planned before anything is mounted, and new camera work is covered on our Fort Greene security camera installation page, and both can be installed on the same visit.

Repair calls from Fort Greene tend to follow a pattern. A restaurant’s alarm and its cameras were installed by two different companies and never talk to each other, so a camera that stopped recording three weeks ago went unnoticed until somebody needed the footage. A brownstone alarm from the 1990s still has its panel in the cellar and its communicator on a landline that was cancelled years ago, or on a 3G radio that went silent when the carriers switched those networks off. A garden-floor window contact stopped meeting its magnet after the sash was replaced, so the zone has been bypassed every night since. An existing recorder’s video-loss or tamper output wired to the alarm as its own zone, or the two joined in one app, so a camera that is cut, covered or unplugged after closing trips the alarm instead of quietly leaving a gap in the footage.

Front glass and door protection for restaurants

Glass-break detectors tuned for large storefront panes, contacts on the front door, the roll-down gate and the back door, and interior motion across the dining room and the bar, all reporting within seconds of the first blow.

Codes instead of keys

A contact on the shared street door and the vestibule, a keypad or entry reader that gives each resident a code that can be cancelled the day they move out, and a log that shows which code opened what and when.

Rear and garden-level coverage

Contacts on every back window a person could reach from a garden fence, an extension roof, a fire escape or an air-conditioner bracket, plus the garden door, with a night setting that keeps the back armed while the household is upstairs.

Separate areas for divided houses

One panel split into an area for each apartment and one for the shared doors, so the owner’s duplex and the garden rental each arm their own space with their own codes, and the street door protects everyone.

Closing and opening schedules

Automatic arming after the last motion of the night, reports when a place was not armed by a set time, and limited-hours codes for cleaners, bakers and delivery drivers who arrive while the owner is asleep.

Reviving 1980s and 1990s brownstone alarms

Many old systems still have sound contacts in the door frames and a dead brain in the cellar. We test the old cable run by run, keep what works, and put a modern panel and radio on it instead of chasing new wire through original plaster.

Alerts to the people who should get them

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Fort Greene’s Architecture

Fort Greene packs very different buildings into a few blocks: nineteenth-century rows, wartime public housing, a 1920s bank tower turned into condominiums and the glass towers that have gone up around BAM and Atlantic Terminal. These are the types we meet most and what each asks of an alarm.

Single-family brownstones and brick rows

Three or four stories over a garden level, a stoop to the parlor floor, a rear extension on some, and a roof hatch at the top. Front windows are visible from the street; the back windows, the garden door and the hatch are not, and that is where the sensor count goes.

Owner’s duplex over a garden rental

Two households under one roof, often with separate entrances: the owner at the top of the stoop, the tenant under it. Each needs its own area and codes, the shared back garden door needs a clear owner, and nobody should be able to disarm the other household’s floors.

Rowhouses divided into floor-throughs

Three or four apartments sharing a street door, a vestibule and a stair. Each apartment door is that household’s perimeter, the street door is everyone’s, and the keys to it have usually been copied more times than anyone can count.

Prewar walk-ups and apartment houses

Masonry buildings on the avenues and side streets, many with fire escapes at the rear or side and windows over light wells. Renters here want a wireless system inside the apartment that reports to their own phone and leaves no trace when they move.

Towers by BAM and Atlantic Terminal

From the Williamsburgh Savings Bank Tower, whose upper floors became condominiums between 2005 and 2007, to the newer rental and condo towers nearby. A doorman watches the lobby, not the apartment; owners want a quiet unit alarm with a door contact, a motion detector and alerts.

Public housing apartments

Apartments in the Whitman and Ingersoll Houses, where residents live under NYCHA’s house rules. Here we keep to self-contained, battery-powered devices that need no drilling, and we tell residents to confirm anything beyond that with their management office first.

Method notes for each type

  • Brownstones and brick rows. The panel lives inside the protected area on the garden or parlor floor, never in an unlocked cellar reached from the areaway. Cable runs from the cellar up through closets and old chases where it can, and wireless contacts go on upper sash and the roof hatch, so no original plaster or moulding has to be opened.
  • Duplex over a rental. A partitioned panel with an owner area and a tenant area; the garden door to the yard is assigned to whichever household uses it, and its alerts go to both if both use it. The street door, if shared, sits in a common area that either household can arm.
  • Floor-throughs. A contact on the street door and the vestibule door reporting to every apartment’s app, an entry reader or keypad in place of the old key where the owner agrees, and in each apartment a door contact, a motion detector and contacts on the windows that face the yard or a fire escape.
  • Walk-ups and apartment houses. Nothing screwed into the landlord’s walls unless the lease allows it; the hub talks over its own cellular radio, and a window onto a fire escape gets a contact plus a glass-break detector, never a lock or bar that could slow someone getting out in a fire.
  • Towers and condominiums. A small panel or hub inside the unit, a contact on the corridor door, motion in the main room, and a check that the cellular signal reaches the apartment before anything is mounted, since concrete and glass towers vary floor by floor.

Recorded, Not Stopped: What Fort Greene’s Break-Ins Show About Cameras Without Alarms

Three business break-ins in Brooklyn Paper’s 88th Precinct reports make the same point from different directions. At Dick and Jane’s on Adelphi Street, a burglar cut the wires to the outdoor security camera at about 3:37 in the morning, broke the front window, took the cash register and walked out of the front door, all of it recorded by the cameras inside. At Taqueria Fort Greene on Carlton Avenue, a man broke the front door at about six in the morning and left with more than $1,000 worth of equipment, down to the bathroom hand dryer; the owners found the entire break-in on their cameras when they came in later that morning. At the Brooklyn Hero Shop, a burglar banged on a broken window that had been propped shut with a stick, knocked the stick down at about four in the morning and climbed in; the footage showed the cleaning crew closing up properly before they left.

In each case the cameras worked. They produced evidence, and in the taqueria case an arrest followed. What they did not do was tell anybody, at the time, that something was wrong. A recording system’s job is to remember. Unless someone is watching a live screen, or the cameras are set to send alerts that someone reads at four in the morning, the first person to learn about the break-in is the owner opening up. An alarm’s job is the opposite: it does not remember much, but it reacts in seconds, sounds a siren that every neighbor above the restaurant hears, and calls the owner or a monitoring station before the burglar has reached the register.

The Adelphi Street case shows the most useful trick of all. The burglar’s first move was to deal with the outdoor camera, which means he expected it to matter. Most current recorders can report video loss when a camera’s signal disappears, and many can raise a tamper event when a lens is covered or knocked out of position. If that output is wired to an alarm panel as its own zone, or the recorder and the alarm share a platform that passes events between them, then cutting the outdoor camera at 3:37 is itself the alarm. The siren goes off before the window is broken, and the call goes out while the burglar is still standing on the sidewalk.

That is the core of how we design for Fort Greene businesses and the many brownstones that already have a doorbell camera and a camera over the garden. We do not tear out working cameras. We add what they lack: contacts on the doors and the gate, glass-break detectors on the front glass, motion detectors inside, and a link that turns a camera failure into an alarm event. Where the recorder sits on a shelf beside the register, we move it, or lock it into a cabinet inside the protected area, so that the footage does not leave with the cash drawer. And the alarm reports over a cellular communicator, so a cut cable outside silences neither it nor the call it makes.

An alarm will not stop a determined person from breaking glass; it shortens their stay and makes sure someone knows in seconds. And it only works if it is armed, which is why the restaurant section below deals with closing schedules.

Doors Nobody Forced: Codes Instead of Keys in Divided Brownstones

Read enough of the precinct’s apartment burglaries and a phrase keeps coming back: no sign of forced entry. One Brooklyn Paper roundup lists an apartment where the tenant returned to find a laptop, a watch and a bracelet gone with no forced entry, another where the door was simply found unlocked, and the Fulton Street case where a spare key hidden in the hallway disappeared along with a wallet. Another report on the precinct described a renter who suspected an ex-roommate who still had keys. DNAinfo’s 2014 story included an apartment entered through a locked front door. In a neighborhood of rowhouses divided into three or four apartments, a door that opens without force is the expected case, not the rare one.

The reason is arithmetic. A rowhouse with three apartments and a street door has, at minimum, four locks that matter, and every tenant who has ever lived in the building has carried at least one of those keys. Add contractors, dog walkers, cleaners, house sitters, a former partner, and the spare that somebody once tucked behind the radiator in the vestibule, and the owner of a Fort Greene brownstone often cannot say with any confidence who is able to open the street door tonight. Rekeying helps for a while. Then the keys start circulating again.

An alarm changes the question from “who has a key” to “who has a code.” Every person who needs to come and go gets their own code, or a fob or phone login tied to their name. The panel records which code disarmed which area and when, and a code can be deleted in seconds when a tenant moves out, a cleaner is let go or a relationship ends. A person who opens the door with an old key but has no code faces an entry delay and then a siren and a call. That does not stop the key from turning, but it makes the key on its own useless for a quiet burglary.

For the shared parts of a divided house, we usually suggest three things. A contact on the street door and one on the inner vestibule door, reporting to every apartment that wants the alert, so a door propped open or left ajar at night is noticed. Where the owner agrees, a keypad or an entry reader at the street door, so residents use codes or cards that can be cancelled instead of copies of a brass key. And no hidden spare, anywhere, ever: if someone needs to get in while you are away, give them a temporary code that works for the hours they need and expires on its own. Inside each apartment, the household’s own door contact and motion detector take it from there.

No household sees another’s codes or alerts: each gets its own area on a partitioned panel, and the shared doors sit in a common area anyone can arm.

The Back of the House at Night: Garden Floors, Rear Windows and Air Conditioners

The two homes DNAinfo described near Fort Greene Place and Hanson Place were entered at about ten at night, both through unlocked back windows, minutes apart. Brooklyn Paper’s Clermont Avenue report describes an air-conditioning unit pushed in through a window. BKReader reported in July 2014 that warm weather seemed to have set off a string of burglaries around the precinct, including a vacation burglary at an apartment in the Whitman Houses and a ransacked apartment in a building between Adelphi Street and Clermont Avenue whose tenant had left for work at 8:45 in the morning. Summer, open windows and the back of the building keep turning up together.

The back of a Fort Greene rowhouse is a private world. The yards behind a block of brownstones run together behind fences and walls, rear extensions put a flat roof at the height of a second-floor window, and fire escapes on apartment buildings put a landing outside a bedroom. Nobody on Cumberland or South Oxford Street sees any of it, which is exactly why back windows get left unlocked on warm evenings.

An alarm built for that problem needs a setting most people never use: the back of the house armed while the household is home. We program a night or stay setting that arms every garden-level door and window and every rear window within reach, while leaving the stair and the bedrooms free to move through. On a divided house, the garden tenant can arm their whole apartment at night while the owner’s duplex upstairs arms only its rear windows. A window opened from outside then sounds immediately, with no entry delay, because nobody legitimately comes in through a back window.

Air conditioners are the awkward part. A window that holds a unit all summer cannot close on a contact, and the unit itself is the weak point: it can be pushed in. Our approach is to put a second magnet on the sash at the height it sits with the unit installed, so the window is protected in its summer position and can still be armed; to add a glass-break detector in the room; and to make sure the room’s motion detector sees that window. Where a bracket or a fire escape makes the opening easy to reach, we also suggest a proper side panel and bracket kit, which is a carpenter’s job, not ours.

For renters on the garden floor, the conversation is often personal. One Fort Greene resident, answering a question on Reddit’s AskNYC about why people avoid first-floor apartments, said being on the first floor was the only downside of living in otherwise quiet Fort Greene, listing noise and burglary risk. A garden apartment with contacts on the door and every window, a motion detector in the main room, a night setting that keeps the windows armed while the tenant sleeps, and an alert that reaches the tenant’s phone directly, does a lot to close that gap with the floors above.

Rules and practical limits for Fort Greene businesses

  • Siren time. Under § 24-238 of the City’s Administrative Code, a building’s audible burglar alarm has to shut itself off within fifteen minutes. With families sleeping above most corridor restaurants, our sirens run for a few minutes, and the shut-off is proved on test day.
  • Who installs. New York State requires businesses that install security and fire alarm systems to hold a Department of State license under General Business Law Article 6-D. Ours is #12000287431; ask any installer for theirs.
  • Fire alarms are separate. A burglar alarm can report smoke and heat detectors, which helps a storage room or a closed kitchen at night, but it is not a code-required building fire alarm system. That work is covered on our Fort Greene fire alarm installation page.
  • Historic district buildings. Much of Fort Greene is in a designated historic district. An interior alarm normally touches nothing the Landmarks Preservation Commission regulates; anything on the facade, such as an exterior siren, a camera or a sign bracket, should be checked with the Commission before it goes up. We keep everything inside unless the owner has that answer.
  • Apartments above the shop. Many corridor buildings have residential tenants upstairs with a door beside the store entrance. Their door is not part of the store’s alarm, and the store’s siren should not run longer than it must.

When the cameras saw everything and nobody knew

Most current recorders can signal video loss when a camera’s feed disappears, and many report tampering when a lens is covered or moved. We wire that output to the alarm panel as its own zone, or connect recorder and alarm on a shared platform, and program it to sound and report while the place is armed. Cutting the camera then becomes the alarm, before the glass breaks.

The taqueria on Carlton Avenue found its six-in-the-morning break-in on video hours later. Which device would have turned that into a phone call at six?

A contact on the front door. The door was broken, and a door contact reports the instant it separates from its magnet; with the alarm armed, the panel sounds the siren and calls the owner or the monitoring station within seconds. A glass-break detector aimed at the door’s glass would have caught the first blow even earlier. The cameras would still have recorded everything, but someone would have known while it was happening.

Our recorder sits on a shelf under the counter. Where should it go so the evidence doesn’t leave with the register?

Somewhere a burglar does not pass on the way to the cash: a locked cabinet or closet in the back, inside an area covered by a motion detector, with its own small battery backup. Many recorders can also copy clips to the cloud, which keeps the important minutes even if the box is taken. We plan the recorder’s new spot during the survey so its cables can be rerouted in one visit.

Keys, shared doors and entries without force

Several Fort Greene apartment burglaries showed no forced entry at all. If somebody opens our door with a key, does the alarm even notice?

It notices the door opening, which is all it needs. The panel does not care whether a key turned or the lock was picked; it starts an entry countdown, and only a valid code, fob or app login stops it. Someone holding an old key but no code gets a siren and a call. The log also shows every disarm by name, so you learn whose code was used if anything goes missing.

Our brownstone’s street door and vestibule are shared by three apartments, and someone keeps leaving them open. Can the alarm help without anyone policing the others?

A contact on each of the two doors, set in a common area that reports to every apartment that wants the alert, does most of the work. The app can tell residents when the street door has been open for more than a few minutes, or when it opens late at night, without anybody having to stand watch. Nobody receives anyone else’s apartment alerts, and nobody needs anyone else’s code.

The owner’s duplex and the garden rental both use the door from the hallway to the back garden. Whose alarm should that door belong to?

Whoever needs to arm it when they are the last one out. If both households use it, we put it in the shared area so either can arm it and both are told when it opens while armed. If only the garden tenant uses it, it belongs to their area alone. The point is that the door has exactly one owner in the programming, so it is never left out of everyone’s system.

Back windows, parlor floors and garden apartments

The 2014 break-ins near Fort Greene Place and Hanson Place came through unlocked back windows at ten at night. Can the back of the house stay armed while we are upstairs?

Yes, with a night setting. It arms the garden-level doors and windows and every rear window within reach, with no entry delay, while the stair and bedrooms stay free. You set it from the keypad by the bedroom or from your phone at bedtime, and you can schedule it to come on by itself at a set hour. An unlocked back window then stops being an open door; opening it from outside sets off the siren.

Our parlor floor has tall front windows that open beside the stoop. Are those windows a real way in?

On many Fort Greene houses, yes. The stoop and its railing put a person within reach of the nearest parlor window, and those windows are tall enough to climb through once the lower sash is up. We put contacts on any parlor window that can be reached from the stoop or the areaway railing, and a motion detector in the front parlor, so an entry there is caught whether the sash is opened or broken.

People who rent in Fort Greene say the first floor is the one drawback of the neighborhood. What would make a garden apartment feel as safe as the floors above?

Coverage that matches how the garden floor is exposed: a contact on the door under the stoop, contacts on every front and back window, a glass-break detector for windows behind security bars, a motion detector in the main room, and a night setting that arms all of it while you sleep. Alerts go straight to your phone, not the landlord’s, and the whole system can be wireless so it leaves with you.

Restaurants, bars and the hours before dawn

We close the bar around two and the bread arrives before six. How do you schedule an alarm when the quiet hours are that short?

We set the panel to arm itself once the motion detectors have been quiet for a set number of minutes after closing, and to send you a message if it has not armed by a fixed hour. The delivery driver gets a code that works only during the delivery window and only disarms the back door area, so the bar stays armed. If that code is used outside its hours, you hear about it.

Our front window broke weeks ago and is held shut with a stick until the glazier comes. What protects an opening that doesn’t latch?

A glass-break detector in the room and a motion detector that sees the window, armed every night until the repair is done. If plywood or a temporary panel covers the opening, a contact or a shock sensor can be fixed to it. What should never happen is telling people, even your own crew, how the window is held closed; the Brooklyn Hero Shop break-in started with someone knocking exactly that stick away.

The kitchen exhaust and the walk-in compressor run all night. Will motion detectors in the back mistake them for a person?

Not if they are placed with that in mind. Infrared motion detectors react to moving warm objects, so we keep them out of the direct path of exhaust hoods, heater blasts and compressor vents, mount them on walls that face away from the line, and walk-test each one with the equipment running. Where a kitchen is too busy with heat sources, a door contact on the back door does the job instead.

A storage building on Carlton Avenue had a lithium-ion battery fire this August. Can our shop’s alarm warn us if a charging e-bike battery overheats in the back room at night?

Yes. Smoke and heat detectors can report through the same panel as the burglar sensors, around the clock, whether the alarm is armed or not, and send the alert to your phone or a monitoring station. We place a heat detector near the charging spot and a smoke detector in the room. That supplements, and never replaces, any fire alarm system the building is required to have.

Our café near BAM is packed before a performance and nearly empty once the curtain goes up, with one person on shift. Can part of the alarm be on while we are still open?

Yes, and for a lone closer it is worth doing. We put the back door, the storeroom and the office in their own area, which the person on shift arms from the counter keypad as soon as the pre-show rush is over. The front stays open for customers, but anyone who slips in through the back while the barista is busy at the machine sets off a chime at the counter, or the full alarm if you prefer.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Fort Greene

None of what follows is lifted from Google’s AI Overview, which we could not open while writing. Instead, we took seven assumptions that keep surfacing in chatbot replies and in alarm cost write-ups on Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, and checked each one against a bar on Adelphi Street, a taqueria on Carlton Avenue and the divided brownstones around the park.

“If you already have security cameras, you may not need an alarm.”

It sounds sensible, and Fort Greene has run the experiment. The bar on Adelphi Street had an outdoor camera and indoor cameras; the burglar cut the outdoor one and the indoor ones recorded him leaving with the register. The taqueria on Carlton Avenue had cameras that captured the entire break-in, which the owners watched when they arrived for the day.

Cameras are evidence. They tell you what happened after it happened, unless someone is watching live or the cameras send alerts that someone reads at four in the morning. An alarm is response: a siren while the intruder is still at the window and a call within seconds.

The right answer for most Fort Greene businesses and many brownstones is both, joined together, so that a camera being cut or covered is itself an alarm event. That costs far less than a new camera system, because the cameras are already there.

“Protect the front door first; that is where most burglars enter.”

The guides that say this are usually describing a detached suburban house. The Fort Greene cases we found point elsewhere: unlocked back windows at night near Fort Greene Place and Hanson Place, an air conditioner pushed in on Clermont Avenue, and apartment doors inside divided houses opened without force.

On a rowhouse, the front door at the top of the stoop faces the street and its neighbors. The garden door under the stoop, the back windows over the yard and the roof hatch face nobody. Those are where sensors do the most good.

The front door still gets a contact, of course. But a plan that starts at the front and runs out of budget before it reaches the back has protected the wrong end of the house.

“A professionally installed alarm costs $1,000 to $2,500 plus a monthly contract.”

National averages blend suburban houses, package deals and multi-year monitoring agreements. They say little about a garden apartment in a Fort Greene brownstone or a restaurant that already owns its cameras.

On our published Brooklyn list, a basic residential system starts at $600, a wireless apartment system typically runs $600 to $1,200, complete home protection starts at $2,500, and a standard small-business alarm is $1,500 to $3,000. Monitoring through our partners starts at $19.99 a month with no long-term contract, and self-monitoring has no monthly charge.

So treat any number given over the phone, ours included, as a range. The real price depends on how many households share the street door and how many back windows a fence or extension puts within reach, and those only show up on site.

“Wireless alarm sensors are easy to defeat.”

Older, cheap wireless sensors could be jammed or confused, and that reputation lingers. Current professional panels use encrypted, supervised wireless sensors that check in with the panel regularly and report jamming as a trouble or alarm condition.

In Fort Greene the bigger risk runs the other way: the wire people worry about is the one outside. A recorder cable on the facade or a phone line on the back wall can be cut. A cellular communicator and wireless sensors inside the walls leave nothing outside to cut.

For historic interiors, wireless is often the respectful choice as well, because it avoids opening original plaster and woodwork to run cable through a house that has stood since the 1860s.

“Renters can’t really have an alarm.”

A renter cannot rewire a building, but a renter can absolutely have an alarm. A wireless system with a door contact, window contacts, a motion detector and a cellular hub needs no drilling, no building wiring and no landlord’s internet.

In Fort Greene, where so many apartments are floor-throughs and garden units inside rowhouses, that kind of system is often the right one. It protects the tenant’s own space, reports to the tenant’s phone, and leaves with the tenant.

Check the lease first, and in public housing ask management; NYCHA’s house rules limit fixtures. Most of the time the answer is yes.

“Keep a spare key somewhere safe outside in case you get locked out.”

Home-improvement articles still suggest this. In Fort Greene, a spare key hidden in a hallway on Fulton Street disappeared along with a resident’s wallet, according to Brooklyn Paper’s report.

In a building where several households share a street door and a vestibule, a hidden key is a key for everyone who has ever seen where it lives. Once an alarm is installed, it is also unnecessary.

A temporary code for a guest or a contractor, valid only for the hours they need, does the same job and expires on its own. Combined with a keypad or a smart lock, nobody needs a spare key at all.

“Any handyman can install an alarm system.”

Putting a kit up in your own apartment is your business. Doing it for pay is another matter: the Department of State licenses companies that install alarms for others, and that license comes with required coursework, a test and fingerprint checks for the people sent into your home.

The license is not a formality in a building like a divided brownstone, where a careless installer can leave a shared door unprotected, give one household the ability to disarm another’s floors, or wire a siren that runs far past the City’s fifteen-minute limit.

Before anyone drills a hole, get two pieces of paper: the state license number and proof of insurance. Ours are license #12000287431 and a certificate we email to whoever manages the building.

Our Installation Process

Seven steps, the same for a tower condo and a four-apartment brownstone. The Fort Greene work happens in steps two and five: finding every entrance a key could open, and turning the cameras already on the walls into part of the alarm.

1. Photos of the front, the back and the cameras

You send a photo from the sidewalk, one of the back of the building from the garden or the yard, and, if there are cameras already, a picture of the recorder and its screen, and tell us who else holds keys.

2. Walk every entrance, including the ones nobody uses

A technician checks the street door, the vestibule, each apartment door, the garden door under the stoop, the back windows over the yard and any roof hatch, and notes which of them a key could open without force.

3. Map the households and price it in writing

We sketch who owns which door: one area per household, a common area for the street door and vestibule, a separate one for any business, each with its own list of codes and phones. The written price comes with that sketch, most often the same day.

4. Mount the core out of reach

Panel, backup battery and cellular communicator go inside the protected area, away from the street door and out of sight from windows; an existing camera recorder is moved or locked into the same space.

5. Sensors, then the camera link

Door contacts, window contacts on the garden and back elevations, glass-break and motion detectors go in, and where there is a recorder, its video-loss output or app integration is wired to the alarm as its own zone.

6. Program the hours and test everything

Bedtime settings, closing-time arming and the delivery driver’s limited code are entered, the siren timer is set well short of the City’s limit, and then we set off every device in turn, unplugging each linked camera too, until each one has reported.

7. Hand over, then check back

Each person who received a code gets a short lesson at the keypad and on the phone, we write down every brass key still out there so it can be collected, and a few weeks later we come back to tune delays, schedules and anything that false-alarmed.

Every season: a fifteen-minute key and code audit

Four times a year, open the app’s user list and delete every code that no longer needs to exist: a tenant who moved, a cleaner you stopped using, a contractor who finished. Then put the system in test mode, open the street door, the garden door and each back window, walk past each motion detector, unplug one camera for a moment to confirm the alarm sees it, and check the battery report. If anyone outside the household still has a brass key to your door, write their name down; that list is your reason for the next rekey.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Count the Keys Before You Count the Windows

This describes how we survey in Fort Greene; it is not the story of any particular building. Before a technician counts a single window in a divided brownstone, he asks for a list: everyone who has a key to the street door, the vestibule door and each apartment, as far as anyone knows. The owner usually starts confidently and slows down around the third name. Former tenants, a contractor from two renovations ago, the cleaner, a relative, the dog walker, the neighbor who waters the plants in August. Then he asks where the spare is, and there nearly always is one.

That list decides the design more than the floor plan does. A short, certain list means door contacts and codes are enough. A long or uncertain one means the alarm has to assume that any door can be opened quietly, so entry delays get shorter, interior motion matters more, every person gets their own code, and the street door is worth a keypad or an entry reader. Only then does he walk the back of the building from the yard, count the reachable windows and look up at the roof hatch. And if there is a recorder, he unplugs one camera for a moment to see whether anything at all happens. Usually nothing does, and that is the first thing we fix.

Where sources disagree, we say so. Wikipedia’s Fort Greene article dates the Olmsted and Vaux redesign of the park to 1864 and gives the monument as 143 feet; its Fort Greene Park article says 1867 and 149 feet, so this page gives neither the year of the redesign nor a height. Fort Greene’s population is given as 32,938 in the article’s summary box and 26,079 for 2010 in its text, measured over different areas, so we avoid a single figure. Several Brooklyn Paper police reports used here carry no year on the page, and two of the incidents in them took place on the Clinton Hill side of the same precinct; we cite those only for what they show about entry methods.

Gaps, stated plainly: the tools used to build this page could not load Google’s question boxes, related searches or AI Overview, nor Answer the Public, Bing or DuckDuckGo suggestions, nor Yelp.
