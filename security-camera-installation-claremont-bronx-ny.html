The Neighborhood

Why Claremont Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Claremont is easier to stand in than to find on paper. Type its name into Wikipedia and you land on the Morrisania article, which mentions Claremont inside its own pages. CityNeighborhoods.nyc draws Claremont from East 167th Street in the south to the Cross Bronx Expressway in the north, with the Grand Concourse on the west and a line down Fulton Avenue, along Claremont Parkway and over to Webster Avenue on the east. Rob Stephenson's neighborhood newsletter describes it simply as the area that grew up around the park at its center, wedged between Morrisania, Tremont, Crotona and the Concourse. For a camera installer the boundaries matter less than what stands inside them, and what stands inside them is mostly large, old, heavily used apartment buildings with a lot of doors and a lot of people passing through each one.

The land has a long history before any of those buildings. NYC Parks traces it to the Morris family estate, established in 1679. Gouverneur Morris II auctioned much of it off in 1848, as the railroad made suburban building possible. A mansion rose on the ridge in 1859; Parks credits the Zborowski family, while a neighborhood history gives a different builder and year, and we leave that dispute where it is. Parks says the mansion's lawns ran down to the Mill Brook, which is now Webster Avenue, so the slope from the Concourse ridge to the avenue is old and real. The city bought the land for a park in 1888 to 1890 under the New Parks Act. The mansion served as the Bronx Parks Department's headquarters until it was torn down in 1938, and the WPA rebuilt the park, which reopened on December 7, 1940.

The apartment buildings came with transit. Webster Avenue became a commercial strip with groceries, delis and movie theaters, streetcars ran up Third Avenue toward Fordham, and the Third Avenue El stopped at Claremont Parkway until the station was lost in 1973. NeighborhoodScout reports that many of Claremont's homes were built in 1939 or earlier. City lot data from the PLUTO file, for the census tracts our team uses to define Claremont, counts about 677 residential lots with roughly 14,750 homes, and about 64 percent of those homes, 9,492 of them, sit in 66 buildings of fifty or more apartments. Another 4,783 homes are in 299 walk-up buildings. Only around 524 homes are in one- and two-family houses. That is the shape of the camera work here: entrances, lobbies and stairs that dozens or hundreds of households share.

The largest buildings belong to NYCHA. Wikipedia describes four contiguous developments, Butler, Morris, Morrisania and Webster Houses, as collectively named Claremont Village, and cites a New York Times archive headline about the project housing 4,191 families. Butler Houses is six 21-story buildings between East 169th and East 171st Streets, from Park Avenue to Webster Avenue, completed in December 1964. Webster Houses is five 21-story buildings between Webster and Park Avenues and East 168th and 169th Streets, right beside the Metro-North tracks, completed in September 1965. Population figures vary from source to source, from the 4,191 families in that headline to more than ten thousand residents in other accounts, so we do not rely on a single number. NYCHA also manages walk-ups on Clay Avenue, from 1115 to 1195 Clay Avenue, as Claremont Rehab (Group 2), under its Claremont Consolidated management office.

Those developments are also the clearest public example of how cameras actually get added to a Claremont building. The Bronx Times reported in 2017 that Butler Houses was the first NYCHA property to receive the full set of security upgrades under the Mayor's Action Plan for Neighborhood Safety. The six buildings, 1,476 apartments in total, each got two new entrance doors and one new exit door. The development got 30 new cameras and upgrades to its 58 existing ones. And the grounds got 245 new exterior light fixtures. More recently, the NYCHA Journal reported that Bronx Borough President Vanessa Gibson's office presented a check for 294,000 dollars for security upgrades, including new cameras, at NYCHA's Teller Avenue-166th Street/Claremont Consolidated development. None of that work can be hired by a resident; it comes through NYCHA and the officials who fund it. What it shows is the pattern: when the city upgraded a Claremont campus, it treated doors, lights and cameras as one job.

The everyday pressures in the private buildings are the familiar ones of any large Bronx neighborhood, and some of them have been in the news. News 12 Bronx reported in June 2025 that a broken mailbox at Butler Houses had been causing tenants months of trouble, with management and the post office sending residents back and forth until NYCHA said it was hiring a vendor to repair it. Mail and packages are a constant theme in building camera calls everywhere in the borough. On the street, ABC7 reported that city officials put major crime in the Bronx down nearly 12 percent through September 30, 2026, compared with the year before. Days earlier, on October 29, 2026, a 15-year-old student, Javits Frimpong, was shot and killed near East 171st Street and Crotona Place; CBS News New York reported that police said the suspects were caught on surveillance video jumping a turnstile and taking the 5 train after running into Crotona Park. We mention it only because it is the most recent public example of how camera footage is used after a crime on these blocks, and our thoughts are with his family and school.

The buildings themselves set limits on the work. ABC7 reported that on February 2, 2026, a four-alarm fire in a five-story building off the Grand Concourse burned through the top floor and the cockloft and left at least 82 people without homes. In a walk-up of that type, the cockloft and the roof are tempting routes for camera cable, and how a contractor passes cable through floors, ceilings and that roof space matters. Our own Bronx pages note two other conditions we meet in prewar buildings across the borough: thick masonry walls that make Wi-Fi cameras unreliable from one floor to the next, and recorders tucked into a locked cellar room near the boiler or the meters. Each of those shapes a plan for a Claremont building, and we cover them below.

Claremont facts that shape a camera plan

  • A neighborhood of large buildings: about 64 percent of Claremont's homes are in buildings of fifty or more apartments, per city PLUTO data for the tracts we use, so most camera jobs involve shared entrances, mailrooms and stairs.
  • A public precedent for doors, lights and cameras together: Butler Houses' 2017 upgrade added 30 cameras, upgraded 58 more, replaced entrance and exit doors and installed 245 exterior lights, per the Bronx Times.
  • Fresh public funding next door: a 294,000-dollar Borough President allocation for security upgrades, including cameras, at Teller Avenue-166th Street/Claremont Consolidated, per the NYCHA Journal.
  • Mail as a sore point: a broken mailbox at Butler Houses that took months to fix, per News 12 Bronx in June 2025.
  • Old buildings with roof spaces: many homes built in 1939 or earlier, and a February 2026 fire that ran through a top floor and cockloft off the Concourse.
  • New buildings arriving: an eight-unit building with a roof terrace planned at 404 Claremont Parkway, the 66-home Claremont House, and supportive housing at 415 Claremont Parkway and 3500 Park Avenue, each needing camera and entry systems of its own.

What building owners often pair with cameras. A camera over a street door shows who came in; it does not stop the door from being left open. When a Claremont owner asks us to cover the entrance, the next conversation is usually about the door itself, the intercom and the lock, the same pairing Butler Houses' upgrade made. Those are separate systems, but they share the doorway and often the cable route back to the cellar, so tell us about the intercom and the lock when you book, and we will place the entrance camera so it works alongside them rather than competing for the same spot on the frame.

Door, Light, Lens

Door, Light, Lens: What the Butler Houses Upgrade Teaches a Claremont Building Owner

The most useful thing a private owner in Claremont can borrow from the city is not a camera model. It is the order of the work. When Butler Houses got its 2017 upgrade, the Bronx Times listed three things side by side: new entrance and exit doors, new and upgraded cameras, and 245 new exterior lights. A camera over a door that does not close records people walking in. A camera over a dark path records nothing worth keeping. A camera that is bright, sharp and well aimed, over a door that closes and locks, records the few people who should not be there. We plan every Claremont building the same way, even when the budget is small, and the table below is how we explain it on the walk-through.

The door first: a camera cannot close it

Butler Houses got new entrance and exit doors in the same upgrade as its cameras, and that is not an accident. In a walk-up on Clay Avenue or an elevator building near the Concourse, the street door is the single most important thing the camera watches, and its condition decides what the camera is worth. If the closer is weak, the latch misses, or tenants have learned to wedge it open, the entrance camera will mostly show a stream of people who did not need a key. We are a camera company on this job, and we do not pretend to be your locksmith, but we will tell you honestly if the door is the weak point, write it on the quote, and place the camera where it will be most useful once the door works.

The light second: 245 fixtures for a reason

The Butler upgrade put up 245 exterior light fixtures across its grounds. A private building does not need anything like that number, but it needs the same thing at every place a camera has to identify someone: steady light falling on the face, not behind it. The common failures in Claremont buildings are a vestibule lit from a single bulb behind the door, so everyone entering is a silhouette; a rear yard lit only by kitchen windows that are off at three in the morning; and a stair landing where the fixture is dead. We do our first survey in daylight and come back after dark, stand at each camera position with a phone, and write down where light is missing. Some cameras carry their own light, and we will use them where they fit, but the cheapest fix for a dark doorway is usually a working fixture.

The lens third: aimed at where people stop

Only after the door and the light do we pick cameras. The overview camera over the street door shows who came and went and when. The identifying cameras go where people stand still and face one way: at the mailboxes, at the intercom panel, at the elevator call button, at the inner vestibule door. A face that fills a good share of the picture at close range is worth more to a building owner, and to the police, than a wide view of the whole lobby with everyone the size of a thumbnail. In a fifty-apartment building that usually means fewer cameras than people expect, in better places, and that is what we put on the quote.

Claremont packs early twentieth-century walk-ups, elevator buildings of several eras, public housing towers, new affordable buildings and a small number of houses into a few dozen blocks. Each type changes three things about a camera job: what the camera mounts on, how the cable gets back to the recorder, and who has to agree before anything goes up. These are the building types the city's lot data and the neighborhood sources describe, and how we handle each one.

Prewar Walk-Ups

Five- and six-story apartment houses of the 1910s and 1920s on Clay, Fulton and Webster, the most common building in the neighborhood by count. One street door, a vestibule, a stair, a cellar and a roof bulkhead.

Elevator Buildings Near the Concourse

Larger buildings from the 1920s through the postwar decades, including a 17-story tower of the 1960s. Lobbies, mailrooms, elevator lobbies, side doors and long cable distances.

HDFC and Co-op Buildings

Around 56 lots with HDFC owners in our tract count, many in 1920s buildings. Boards decide, budgets are tight, and the work often comes in phases.

New Affordable and Supportive Housing

Buildings like 3500 Park Avenue, 415 Claremont Parkway and the planned 404 Claremont Parkway and Claremont House. Staffed desks, operator policies and new construction to work around.

NYCHA Towers and Rehab Walk-Ups

Claremont Village's 21-story towers and the Claremont Rehab walk-ups on Clay Avenue. Common-area cameras are NYCHA's; residents work within NYCHA's rules for their own door.

Frame Houses and 1990s Two-Families

Old houses on College, Teller, Clay and Findlay, and newer two-family homes on Claremont Parkway, Third Avenue, Crotona Place, Washington Avenue and East 171st Street.

Prewar walk-ups: brick at the door, plaster inside, a roof space above

The typical Claremont walk-up has a brick front, a vestibule between two doors, a plaster stair hall that climbs five or six floors, a cellar under it and a bulkhead at the top. The entrance camera goes into sound brick, not the mortar joint, with sleeve anchors sized for the bracket, and any hole through the facade is drilled once, sloped slightly toward the outside and sealed. Inside, cable can travel up the stair hall in a painted surface raceway that a super can follow later, or down into the cellar where it is out of reach. We avoid the cockloft and roof space as a cable route unless there is no better path; the February 2026 fire off the Concourse ran through exactly that space, and any cable that crosses a floor, ceiling or fire-rated wall in a building like this is sealed with a rated fire-stopping material where it passes through.

Elevator buildings: distance, mailrooms and the elevator lobby

The bigger buildings toward the Grand Concourse change the problem from height to distance. A lobby camera may be a long way from the recorder room, a rear service door further still, and the side entrance on a corner building may be around the block from the front. Over long runs we bring cable to a PoE switch in a locked cabinet, which powers the cameras and carries their picture on one cable each. The mailroom, the elevator lobby and the package area are where faces turn toward a wall, so they get the identifying cameras. Elevator cabs are a separate question with their own equipment and access rules, and we discuss them only with the owner and the elevator contractor together. In occupied buildings we work floor by floor, keep the lobby open, and save drilling for agreed hours.

HDFC and co-op buildings: the board decides, and the money comes in stages

In the HDFC and co-op buildings, which our tract count puts at about 56 lots and 2,257 homes, many of them in 1920s buildings, the people who live there also own and govern it. A camera plan has to pass the board, who sees what has to be agreed before any lens goes up in a hallway, and who holds the main login afterward. We write the plan in a form a board can vote on: each camera, its view, why it is there and what it costs, with a first phase that covers the doors and the mailboxes and later phases for the stair and the roof. Where a later phase is likely, we pull spare cable while the walls are open the first time, so the second phase does not repeat the disruption.

New and supportive buildings: someone else's policy comes first

The newer buildings on and near Claremont Parkway are run under operator rules. The NYHC describes 3500 Park Avenue as a 115-unit building with 69 apartments for formerly homeless residents, a live-in super and about 20 on-site workers. Affordable Housing Hub lists 415 Claremont Parkway with 18 low-income units serving homeless populations. In buildings like these, the operator sets who watches the cameras and when, and staff need access without tenants feeling watched in their own halls. We work to the operator's written policy, keep monitors out of visitors' sight, and set staff logins to view only. New construction, like the eight-unit building with a roof terrace that New York YIMBY reported for 404 Claremont Parkway, often arrives with conduit and a network room already built, which we use rather than adding new surface cable.

A shop under apartments is two jobs in one building

Most Claremont shops are tenants on the ground floor of an apartment building, and that splits the camera work. The shopkeeper decides about the inside of the store: the door, the counter, the register and the stockroom. The building owner decides about the facade, the shared entrance to the apartments upstairs, the cellar and any rear yard. If you lease the store, read the lease and ask the owner before we mount anything outside, and send us the answer in writing. Inside, we put the door camera where it sees faces as people walk in, not their backs as they leave; the counter camera above and slightly behind the staff side so it sees hands, cash and the customer's face; and the recorder in a locked back room rather than under the counter, where it would be the first thing grabbed.

Night on a busy avenue, morning after a quiet one

Webster and Third Avenues stay busy into the evening, and many shops close behind roll-down gates. A storefront camera behind a closed gate sees the inside of the gate; one mounted outside sees the sidewalk, the gate's lock and anyone working at it, but it needs the building owner's agreement and protection from being knocked out of aim. Headlights and shop signs across the street can wash out a camera aimed straight at the avenue, so we angle storefront cameras along the frontage rather than across it. Inside, a camera covering the back door and the stockroom matters more after closing than any view of the sales floor, because that is where an overnight entry usually happens.

Rules and practical limits for business cameras in Claremont

  • Licensed installer. NYC Business describes a New York State Department of State license as required to install or maintain security or fire alarm systems, and the Kirschenbaum law firm notes the license covers CCTV used to detect or monitor intrusion, theft or movement. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431.
  • Private places stay private. New York Penal Law §250.45 makes it a crime to put a camera where people have a reasonable expectation of privacy without their consent. No cameras in restrooms, changing areas or any room where people wash, and we will say so plainly if a requested view crosses that line.
  • The landlord controls shared space. As Impera Security's guidance puts it, tenants generally cannot install cameras in common areas without the landlord's permission, and that applies to a tenant business as much as to a tenant household.

Field Notes: Why We Walk a Claremont Building Twice

We do not publish stories about a particular customer's building unless we can document them, so this is not a tale about one address. It is the routine we follow at a prewar walk-up of the kind that lines Clay and Fulton Avenues, and the reason the first visit is really two.

The daytime walk starts in the cellar, not the lobby, because that is where the recorder will live and where every cable has to end. We look for a room that locks, stays dry when it rains hard and is not right against the boiler. Then we climb: street door, vestibule, mailboxes, every landing, the bulkhead and the roof door, noting what each wall and ceiling is made of. The night walk is shorter and tells us more. The vestibule that looked bright at noon is lit by one bulb behind the door, so every face comes out black. A landing fixture is dead. The rear yard is dark except for whatever kitchens are lit. We stand at each spot with a phone camera, look at what a lens would see, and write down the light that is missing. Those notes go on the written quote first, ahead of the cameras.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Claremont: Questions Answered

Large buildings and the Claremont Village edge

Butler Houses got 30 new cameras and 245 lights. Does any of that coverage reach the sidewalk in front of our private building across Webster Avenue?

Possibly, but you should not plan around it. Those cameras and lights belong to NYCHA, were placed for NYCHA's grounds and entrances, and their footage is not something a neighboring owner can log into or ask for directly. If something happens outside your building, the police can request footage from NYCHA and from anyone else nearby. Your own entrance needs its own camera, aimed at your door, your vestibule and your stretch of sidewalk, recorded on a box you control, so you are not depending on another owner's equipment, angle or retention time.

Our tenant association heard about the Borough President's camera money for Claremont Consolidated. How do residents find out where those cameras will go?

Ask the people running the project. The NYCHA Journal reported that Borough President Vanessa Gibson's office presented a 294,000-dollar check for security upgrades, including new cameras, at the Teller Avenue-166th Street/Claremont Consolidated development, but the report does not list camera locations or a schedule. Your tenant association, the development's management office and the Borough President's office are the right places to ask. AESS is not part of that work, and we would rather say so than imply otherwise. What a resident can control is their own apartment door, within NYCHA's rules.

If the budget covers only a few cameras in a fifty-apartment elevator building, which spots come first?

Every entrance first, then the place people stop. In practice that means the street door, any side or rear door, and the cellar door, each with a camera aimed along the approach, then one identifying camera at the mailboxes or the inner vestibule door where faces turn toward the lens. If there is budget left, the elevator lobby on the ground floor and the roof door come next, and stair landings last. A plan that covers every way in at a few good points beats one that films the lobby from four angles and leaves the back door unwatched. We write the priorities on the quote so the owner can approve them in order.

The mailboxes in our lobby keep getting pried open. Where does a mailroom camera go so it shows a face, not the back of a head?

Above or beside the mailbox bank, looking outward into the room, so anyone reaching into a box is facing it. A camera on the opposite wall, which is where many old systems put it, sees backs and elbows. We mount it high enough to be out of easy reach, with a vandal-resistant housing, and check that the light falls on the faces of people standing at the boxes rather than coming from behind them. Mail theft is often noticed days later, so we also set the recorder to keep that camera's footage long enough to go back and find it.

Our recorder sits in the cellar next to the boiler. Is that heat a problem for it?

It can be. A recorder is a small computer with a hard drive, and every model has an operating temperature range printed in its specifications. Right against a boiler, in a closed corner with no air moving, it can run hot all winter, and hard drives wear faster in heat. Cellars also flood. We look for a spot in the same locked area that is a few feet away from the boiler, has some air circulation and is raised off the floor, usually on a wall shelf or in a ventilated cabinet, with the battery backup beside it. Moving the recorder ten feet often costs far less than replacing a cooked drive.

People get onto our roof through the bulkhead door. Is a camera at the top of the stair worth it?

Usually, yes, because the roof door is one of the least watched ways into a walk-up, and people who come down from the roof are already past the street door. The camera goes inside the bulkhead or on the top landing, aimed at the door so it sees anyone coming in from the roof or going out to it. We do not touch the door's hardware; roof doors have their own rules about how they must open, and that is for the owner and their door contractor. A roof camera needs light at night too, and the top landing is often the darkest place in the building.

Walk-ups, light courts and houses

Our 1920s building wraps around a light court that tenants' kitchen windows face. Can a camera watch the court without looking into apartments?

Sometimes, with care, and often the better answer is to watch the way into the court rather than the court itself. A camera high on a court wall looking down sees the ground, but it also sees straight into the windows opposite, which is exactly what tenants should not have to live with. We prefer a camera on the court door or the gate where people actually enter, aimed along the ground. If the court must be covered, we mount the camera low, aim it at the floor and add privacy masks over every window in the frame, and then check the picture with the owner before we leave.

Our wood-frame house on Teller Avenue dates from the 1890s. Is it too old for wired cameras?

No. City lot data shows a handful of houses from the 1890s and 1900s on College, Teller, Clay and Findlay, and frame houses like these are often easier to wire than masonry ones. Cable can drop through a wood wall into the basement and run along the joists to the recorder, and cameras mount on trim boards or on a mounting block fixed to the framing. The care goes into the old materials: lath and plaster that cracks if you drill carelessly, old clapboard or siding that has to be sealed where it is opened, and knob-and-tube or other early wiring we keep well clear of. We look before we quote.

Our 1990s two-family off Crotona Place has a parking pad behind a front gate. Can one camera handle both the gate and the car?

One wide camera can see both, but it will see neither very well. A gate is where you want a face, close and lit; a car is where you want to see someone at the door or the window and which way they went. Two modest cameras usually do better than one expensive one: a camera near the front door aimed at the gate, set so a person coming through fills a good part of the picture, and a second camera on the corner of the house looking along the car. Many of the 1990s two-family houses on these blocks have a basement, which makes both runs short.

Can I mount a camera on the fire escape outside my window?

We will not. A fire escape is an escape route and part of the building's structure, and anything fixed to it, from a bracket to a cable tied along the rail, can snag someone climbing out in smoke. It also belongs to the owner, not the tenant. If you want to see the fire escape or the yard below, the options are a small camera inside your own window, looking out, or a camera the owner installs on the building wall with the cable out of the way. Either keeps the escape clear, which is the one thing that matters most on that ironwork.

Why does a Wi-Fi camera that works fine in the shop downstairs drop out on the third floor of the same building?

Because prewar Bronx walls and floors are thick masonry and plaster, and Wi-Fi signals weaken every time they pass through one. A camera near the router works; the same camera two floors up, behind a brick party wall and a tiled bathroom, keeps losing its connection, and a camera that is offline records nothing. For a building's common areas we run cable to each camera, which carries both power and picture and does not care how thick the walls are. Wi-Fi still has a place for a single device close to its router, but not as the backbone of a building system.

The park, the slope and the street

Our building faces Claremont Park and the edge of the park is dark at night. Will a camera see anything past the fence?

Not much, and it should not try. A camera's night vision reaches as far as its own infrared light or the light already on the scene, and a dark stretch of park beyond the street is out of range for most building cameras. Aim it at the park and you get a black frame and motion alerts from leaves. Aim it at your entrance, your stoop and your stretch of sidewalk, where your building's light falls, and you get clear pictures of everyone who comes to your door, including anyone who came from the park. If your front is dark, the fix is a light on your own facade, not a stronger camera.

Our block runs downhill from the Concourse toward Webster Avenue. Does the slope change how a door camera is aimed?

Yes. NYC Parks notes that the old mansion's lawns ran down to the Mill Brook, which is now Webster Avenue, and the land still falls that way. A camera mounted high over a door on the downhill side of a stoop looks down on the tops of heads as people climb toward it; on the uphill side, people walking down the sidewalk come toward it face first. We set the mounting height and tilt for the slope at each door, sometimes mounting lower than usual or moving to the side of the entrance, and check with a person walking the approach while we watch the picture.

Shops, worship and supportive housing

Our storefront mosque on Webster Avenue wants a camera at the door. What is different about cameras in a place of worship?

Mostly who decides and where cameras must never go. The congregation's leadership should agree the plan, including who can view footage and for how long it is kept. The entrance, the shoe area and the street door are sensible places for cameras; the prayer space is a decision for the congregation, and any area where people wash, including ablution facilities, is off limits under the same privacy law that covers restrooms. We mount discreet, visible cameras, keep the recorder in a locked office, and give view access only to the people the leadership names. The same approach applies to the storefront churches nearby.

A travel agency on Webster Avenue handles cash and passports at the counter. Where does the counter camera go?

Above and slightly behind the staff side of the counter, looking at the counter top and the customer, so it sees hands, cash and faces together. A second camera at the door catches each person arriving. One caution: a high-resolution camera close over the counter can record passport pages and other documents in readable detail, which means your recorder then holds sensitive customer information. We set the angle and zoom with you so the camera sees what you need and no more, and keep the recorder and its logins locked down accordingly.

Our building has a staffed front desk for formerly homeless tenants. How should the desk monitor be set up?

Facing the staff, out of sight of anyone in the lobby, and showing the doors rather than the residents' lives. In a supportive building like 3500 Park Avenue or 415 Claremont Parkway, the operator's policy decides which cameras the desk sees and who can play back footage. We usually give the desk a live view of the entrances, the package area and the elevator lobby, with view-only logins for staff and playback reserved for managers. Hallway cameras, if the operator wants them, are aimed along the corridors and masked where apartment doors open, so the desk is watching the building and not each tenant's comings and goings.

Our building is empty under a vacate order after a fire. Can cameras watch it until tenants return?

Sometimes, and it depends first on power and safe access. News 12 Bronx has reported on buildings in Claremont left under vacate orders after fires, and an empty building can attract people looking for copper or shelter. A wired system needs working power, and after a fire the building's power may be off until repairs are inspected. We talk to the owner and their contractor about what is live, whether a temporary arrangement is possible, and whether we can work safely inside. If the building cannot be powered or entered safely yet, we will tell you so rather than install something that cannot record.

Our HDFC co-op works from a small reserve. Can cameras be added in stages?

Yes, and it is often the sensible way. The first stage covers the street door, the vestibule and the mailboxes, with a recorder big enough for the whole future system and battery backup from day one. While the stair hall is open for that first run, we pull extra cable to the landings, the cellar door and the roof door and leave it labeled and capped. Later stages then become a camera and a connection at each point, without new drilling through the building. The board gets a written plan showing each stage separately, so it can approve one and budget for the next.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Claremont

We could not capture Google's AI Overview or People Also Ask results for Claremont from our build environment, so we are not going to pretend to quote them. Below are claims we see repeated in quick online answers about cameras in the Bronx, including one from our own website, set against the public record and what we find in the buildings. Marketplace cost guides from Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr feed many of these answers; they are useful as a rough starting point, but none of them has walked your cellar.

"For NYCHA tenants, wireless is the only legal option, full stop."

This line appears on one of our own Bronx pages, and we could not verify it as written, so we are correcting ourselves here. NYCHA has published guidance on what residents may attach to their own apartment doors, and it points toward wireless, no-hole devices, but a short slogan is not the rule.

The rule that matters is NYCHA's own, and its lease. AOL reported in 2012 that a resident at Parkside Houses, elsewhere in the Bronx, was told she had breached her lease by installing cameras. That is the risk of guessing.

If you live in Butler, Webster, Morris or Morrisania Houses or the Clay Avenue walk-ups, ask your management office what is allowed on your door before you buy anything. We will work only within what NYCHA permits, and we do not touch common areas.

"Anyone can put up security cameras in New York; no license is needed."

Not for a business doing it for you. NYC Business describes a New York State Department of State license as required to install, service or maintain security or fire alarm systems, and the Kirschenbaum law firm writes that the license covers CCTV used to detect or monitor intrusion, theft or movement.

A homeowner fixing a camera to their own wall is a different matter from a company or a handyman taking money to wire a building. Quick answers tend to blur that line.

If a board, an insurer or a managing agent asks who put up your building's cameras, the answer should be a licensed company. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431 and carries insurance.

"Your landlord has to keep the lobby cameras working."

Many tenants assume so, and many quick answers repeat it. Brick Underground has reported that no city law requires rental buildings to have working security cameras. A landlord who installed them is not obliged by that fact alone to repair them.

That does not leave tenants with nothing. Doors, locks and intercoms are covered by other rules, and a tenant association can press an owner to fix a dead system, especially when mail and packages are disappearing.

For owners, the practical point is the reverse: a camera that has been dead for months still tells everyone in the building it is there, and gives a false sense that something is being recorded. Fix it or take it down.

"Wi-Fi cameras are the easy answer for any apartment building."

They are easy to buy and easy to stick up. They are not easy to keep online in a prewar Claremont walk-up, where brick party walls and plaster floors weaken the signal at every level, as our own Bronx residential page notes.

A Wi-Fi camera also needs power, which usually means a battery to change or a plug and a cord running across a hallway. In a shared building, neither lasts long.

For common areas we run cable, which carries power and picture together and records whether or not the internet is up. Wi-Fi still makes sense for one device near its router, such as a single apartment door.

"Claremont is just another name for Morrisania, or for the park."

Wikipedia's "Claremont, Bronx" entry redirects to its Morrisania article, and quick answers sometimes follow that and treat the two as one place, or treat Claremont as nothing but Claremont Park.

The neighborhood sources disagree on details. CityNeighborhoods.nyc draws boundaries from East 167th Street to the Cross Bronx Expressway; it also says the old mansion still stands, which NYC Parks contradicts, recording that it was razed in 1938. We rely on Parks for park history.

"More cameras make a building safer."

More cameras make a building better recorded, which is not the same thing. A dozen cameras on a lobby with a door that does not latch will show a dozen angles of strangers walking in.

The public example in this neighborhood points the other way. When Butler Houses was upgraded, the Bronx Times reported new doors and 245 exterior lights alongside the 30 new cameras and 58 upgraded ones.

A private building gets more from four well-placed cameras over doors that close, in light that shows faces, than from twelve cameras placed to fill a quote. That is how we plan, and how we explain the quote.

"A statewide cost average tells you what your building will cost."

Marketplace guides publish New York averages, and AI answers repeat them as if every building were the same. A one-camera storefront door and a six-story walk-up with cameras on every landing are not the same job.

+

New York Penal Law section 250.45 makes it a crime to install a camera where people have a reasonable expectation of privacy without their consent, such as a bathroom, a changing area or inside someone's home. That is why we never put cameras in restrooms or washing areas, and why hallway and light court cameras are aimed and masked so they cannot see into apartments. Anything near that line, we refuse.
