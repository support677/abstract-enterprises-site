The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Navy Yard Properties Need Door Buzzer Repair

The Brooklyn Navy Yard has one of the most heavily guarded perimeters in Brooklyn, and almost none of that protection reaches the door of an individual tenant's space. BNYDC's access control system, a LenelS2 NetBox installation according to the manufacturer's case study, decides who passes the Yard's entrances. Its visitor system, which eWEEK described in 2020 as Proxyclick and which the 2026 Tenant Manual now calls Eptura Visitor, emails registered guests a QR code to scan at any gate and tells the host when they arrive. Employees carry BNYDC-issued ID cards, and Building 77's lobby takes a swipe card between 10 p.m. and 6 a.m. All of that is the landlord's. Past the lobby, the manual is just as clear about what is not: tenants install their own door locks when they take possession, BNYDC does not maintain or duplicate keys to tenant spaces, and tenants are responsible for maintaining all electric equipment within their leased space. A suite's reader, strike, magnetic lock, desk release and door station belong to that last category.

That split is why door buzzer repair in the Yard looks different from the same job in a Brooklyn walk-up. In a walk-up the owner's intercom serves every apartment, and HPD's rules decide how fast it has to be fixed. In the Yard, a company leasing a suite on the sixth floor of Building 77 or a production space in one of the older shop buildings usually inherited or installed its own door hardware, and nobody else is going to repair it. When the hardware fails, the people who notice are the receptionist who presses a button and hears nothing, the courier who has already been cleared at a guard booth and now stands outside a locked suite, and the employee whose badge turns the reader green while the door stays shut. HqO's 2024 profile counts more than 450 businesses and 11,000 people in the Yard.

The buildings themselves shape the work. BNYDC's 2023 leasing flyer describes Building 77, built in 1942 and reopened after a $185 million capital program completed in 2017, as sixteen floors of 60,000 to 67,500 square feet each, with eight passenger elevators, four freight elevators and a lobby desk with 24/7 security and keycard access. Dock 72, which The Bridge toured in 2019, is a sixteen-story, 675,000-square-foot office building with turnstiles at two elevator cores and a front desk in its lobby. Each of those suite doors is fed by cable that crosses a corridor ceiling, a column line or a riser that the tenant does not control, which is why a fault that looks like a dead reader is so often a cut cable, a tripped supply or a relay in a cabinet two rooms away.

Outside the wall the work changes again. The Yard is bounded by Navy Street, Flushing Avenue, Kent Avenue and the East River at Wallabout Bay, and across Flushing Avenue sit the wood-frame and brick houses of the Wallabout Historic District, the Federal and Greek Revival rows of Vinegar Hill, small walk-ups and co-ops in the 11205 and 11201 ZIP codes, and NYCHA's Farragut, Ingersoll and Whitman Houses. Those houses and private buildings have bells, buzzers and lobby intercoms of the ordinary Brooklyn kind, and we repair them for owners and boards. NYCHA residents' entry doors and intercoms are NYCHA's to repair, and we point residents to NYCHA rather than taking that work on. Where a Yard tenant also wants the corridor outside the suite recorded while the door system is open, we plan the two together and keep the camera on its own wiring. The Release Chain

Green Light, Locked Door: Following a Suite Release From the Badge to the Latch

The call we expect most from inside the Yard is the one that sounds like a reader problem and usually isn't: the badge is accepted, the reader flashes green or beeps, and the suite door stays locked. A reader's light only reports that the controller accepted the credential. Security Wholesalers' guide to magnetic locks puts it plainly: a reader showing green does not mean the lock released, because if the lock is still powered the door stays locked. Between that green light and an open door there are at least five more links.

The chain runs like this. The credential, a badge, a fob or a phone, is read by the reader, which passes its number to a controller, often in a small cabinet in a closet, above the ceiling or beside the network rack. The controller checks the number against its list and its schedule and, if the answer is yes, switches a relay. The relay either sends power to an electric strike, which then lets the latch go, or cuts power to a magnetic lock, which then lets the armature plate go. The lock is fed by a power supply, often with a battery, sized for that lock and anything else on it. And finally the door itself has to move: a latch pressed hard into a strike's keeper, or an armature plate twisted against its magnet, can hold a door shut even when the electrical side has done its job.

We work the chain in a fixed order, starting at the door because it is the cheapest link to rule out. With someone holding the door released, we push, pull and lift it to see whether the latch or the plate is binding. Then we try every way the door can be released, one at a time: a badge, a phone credential if the suite uses them, the desk button, the exit button and a call from the door station. If one method works and another does not, the fault is in the part they don't share. If none of them work, the shared parts, the relay, the supply, the lock and its wiring, are where we measure next, at rest and with the lock energized, at the supply and at the lock itself. Swiftlane's 2026 troubleshooting guide lists a failed door strike relay and a blown transformer fuse among the most common reasons an entry system stops opening doors, and those are the two readings that settle most visits.

Strikes and magnetic locks fail in opposite directions

Allegion's explanation of the two lock types is the one we give tenants. A fail-secure strike stays locked when it loses power, and a person inside can still leave by turning the lever. A fail-safe device unlocks when it loses power. A magnetic lock is always the second kind: it holds only while it is powered, which is why codes built on the International Building Code's sensor-release section, as I Dig Hardware's 2026 explainer describes the 2024 model text, expect a magnetically locked egress door to release on loss of power, from a manual release and when a fire alarm or sprinkler system activates. On a repair, that difference tells us where to look. A strike that will not open points to missing power or a binding latch. A magnetic lock that will not open points to power that is still present when it should have been cut, which is a relay, an exit device or a wiring fault, and very rarely the magnet itself.

Phones, badges and the network in between

Many Yard suites now open from a phone as well as a badge; LenelS2's case study notes that the HID iClass readers on the Yard's own shuttle buses read both cards and mobile credentials. Networked door stations add another layer: Swiftlane lists missing link lights, insufficient power over Ethernet and address conflicts among the causes of a video intercom that rings but shows or opens nothing. On those visits we bring a network tester alongside the meter, and we ask that someone who manages the company's network be reachable by phone.

Inherited Hardware

The Last Company's Door: Repairing Entry Hardware a Previous Tenant Left Behind

Because tenants in the Yard install their own locks, suites change hands with door hardware already on them. A company moving into a space on an office floor or into a subdivided workshop often finds a reader on the wall, a strike or magnet on the frame, a release button under the old reception desk and a controller somewhere nobody has looked. None of it came with a manual, the administrator login left with the previous tenant, and the installer's name may be on a sticker inside the cabinet or nowhere at all. When that system fails a year later, the repair starts with a question the new tenant usually can't answer: what is this, and who else can open the door?

We answer the first part on site. We open the controller cabinet, note the maker and model of each part, photograph the terminals, trace which relay feeds which door and which supply feeds which lock, and test the battery if there is one. Most commercial controllers can be brought back under the new tenant's control, either by recovering the administrator account through the maker's procedure or by resetting the controller and loading the new tenant's users, though a reset clears the old user list and has to be planned. Where the maker no longer supports the controller, we say so in writing and price the replacement of the controller alone, because the readers, the strike and the cable are often perfectly usable on a newer controller.

The second part, who else can get in, matters more than most tenants expect. An inherited controller may still hold the badges of the previous tenant's staff and contractors, and BNYDC's manual asks tenants to act immediately on the Yard ID of anyone who leaves, but a Yard ID and a suite badge are separate credentials. So we pull the user list, remove the credentials the tenant does not recognize, and hand over the administrator access without keeping it ourselves.

Wiring that crosses somebody else's ceiling

In a multi-tenant building the cable from a suite's reader to its controller, or from its door station to its switch, does not always stay inside the suite. It may run above a shared corridor, through another tenant's space or down a riser that belongs to the base building. When another company's fit-out crew opens that corridor ceiling, the cable can be cut, pinched by a new hanger or pulled loose at a splice. BNYDC's manual requires plans to be submitted and approved before alterations and electrical work, but a low-voltage cable that nobody marked on a drawing is easy to miss. We tone-trace the run from both ends before we cut anything, photograph and date what we find, and leave the tenant a record that shows where the cable was damaged, which is the document a property manager or the other tenant will ask for.

Outside the Wall

Wallabout, Vinegar Hill and the Co-ops on the Yard's Edge: Residential Buzzer Repair Across Flushing Avenue

Cross Flushing Avenue from the Yard and the doors change completely. The Wallabout Historic District, listed on the National Register in 2011 and designated by the Landmarks Preservation Commission on July 12 of that year, runs roughly between Myrtle and Park Avenues and holds 203 contributing buildings, many of them Greek Revival and Gothic Revival wood houses from before the Civil War with porches and cornices, along with Italianate and neo-Grec rows and tenements. Vinegar Hill, on the other side of the Yard, runs from the waterfront to Front Street and from the Yard to Bridge Street, about six blocks of Federal and Greek Revival houses on streets such as Hudson Avenue, Plymouth Street, Water Street and Front Street that still have their Belgian block paving. These are one-, two-, three- and four-family houses, small walk-ups and a few converted industrial buildings, and their bells and buzzers fail in the ordinary Brooklyn ways.

The city's numbers show where. HPD's housing maintenance code violations for ZIP code 11205 listed 210 intercom violations when queried in October 2026, 165 of them closed and 45 still open, all of them class A or B. That ZIP code takes in Wallabout but also much of Fort Greene and Clinton Hill, so most of those violations are not on the Yard's doorstep: Myrtle Avenue had 33, Lafayette Avenue 28 and Clinton Avenue 23, against 9 on Flushing Avenue and 8 on Park Avenue. Self-closing door violations in the same ZIP code came to 2,016, 1,360 of them class C, which HPD treats as immediately hazardous with 14 days to correct after notice. For an owner of a three-family on Clermont Avenue or a walk-up on Park Avenue, that is a reminder that the street door and the buzzer are judged together: a strike that holds the latch or a closer that stops short fails the door rule even if every apartment still rings.

The building classes in the same ZIP code describe the work. NYC's PLUTO records count 483 lots in class C0, the three-family walk-up class, holding 1,449 apartments, along with 249 lots in B3 and 128 in B1, both two-family classes, 217 four-family walk-ups in class C3 and 154 larger walk-ups in class C1. In a two-family the bell runs a few dozen feet from the street door to a transformer in the cellar and up the stairs. In a three- or four-family the house becomes a multiple dwelling and the buttons, the transformer and the release have to serve every apartment. PLUTO also records 37 North Elliott Place, a 1942 elevator co-op owned by the Navy Yard Housing Development Fund Company, as a reminder that some of the Yard's neighbors are lobby buildings with risers, not row houses.

Repairs on landmarked fronts

Many Wallabout and Vinegar Hill houses are inside historic districts, and owners often assume that any work at the front door needs a Landmarks permit. The Commission's permit guidebook says otherwise for ordinary maintenance and repair, which needs no permit, and it lets staff approve an intercom panel within or adjacent to a door opening, generally one panel per door opening, of a compatible design and not fixed through cast iron or decorative surfaces. A buzzer repair that replaces a button, a strike, a transformer in the cellar or a station inside the house is maintenance. A new or relocated panel on the facade is the part that may need staff approval, and we tell owners which side of that line their job falls on before we price it.

Public housing next to the Yard

Farragut Houses, completed by 1952 with ten buildings and 1,390 apartments, borders the Yard, and the Ingersoll Houses and the Walt Whitman Houses, both completed in 1944, sit to the south. Their doors have been in the news: Brownstoner, republishing Brooklyn Paper in January 2024, reported a lawsuit over a 2023 stabbing at Farragut that alleges entry through an "unlocked, unsecured, broken, and/or inoperable door," and cited city audits that found most of the development's doors unsecured. At a City Council hearing in March 2025, Council Member Gale Brewer summed up the cycle of NYCHA door repairs as "You break them, you fix them, you break them," and NYCHA's chief operating officer spoke of making sure property management is made aware as soon as a door is broken. The repair of a NYCHA building's entry door or intercom is NYCHA's responsibility, so we point residents to NYCHA's own repair channels and to 311 rather than taking that work on.

Before the Visit

Approvals, Insurance and a Way Through the Gate: Arranging a Door Repair Inside the Yard

A house repair in Wallabout starts when the owner opens the cellar. A suite repair inside the Yard starts with paperwork the tenant controls. We claim no special standing with BNYDC; this is how a repair visit fits the rules in its 2026 Tenant Manual.

First, approval. The manual requires plans to be submitted to and approved by BNYDC before alterations, says electrical work cannot be performed until BNYDC approves it, and states that "any and all electrical work must be performed by a licensed electrician." A like-for-like repair, a relay, a supply, a strike of the same size in the same pocket, is not an alteration in the ordinary sense, but the lease and the property manager decide that, not us. The Yard is divided into three zones, each with its own property manager, and the manual calls the property manager the tenant's primary point of contact. We suggest tenants send their property manager a one-line description of the repair before the visit and forward any conditions to us.

Second, the license question that the manual's electrician rule raises. The New York City Administrative Code defines low voltage electrical work as wiring designed to operate at less than fifty volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data transmission circuits, and it provides for that work to be done under a low voltage installer certification rather than a master electrician's license. The same definition excludes, whatever the voltage, wiring that forms part of a life safety system or connects to a control circuit that switches light, heat or power. Door releases, readers and door stations run on the low-voltage side. Anything on the line-voltage side, a new outlet or circuit for a power supply, or a connection into the building's fire alarm system, is work for a licensed electrician or the fire alarm contractor, and we quote it as theirs. How BNYDC applies its own rule to a given job is for the property manager to confirm, and we would rather the tenant ask before the visit than after.

Third, insurance and entry. BNYDC uses Jones, an insurance compliance company, to collect and track certificates of insurance, and tells tenants to expect correspondence from it. If the property manager asks for a certificate for our visit, tell us how and where it has to be sent. For entry, the manual describes two routes: visitors registered through Eptura Visitor receive an emailed QR code to scan at any gate, while recurring visitors and consultants on long-term projects should be given temporary ID cards by BNYDC Security instead. A single repair visit usually fits the first route. Tell us which gate you want us to use; the manual lists the Sands Street gate and the Building 77 and Building 3 entrances on Flushing Avenue as open 24/7, the Clymer Street gate on set hours and the Cumberland Street gate on varied hours, and notes that hours can change for construction and events.

How each building type changes the repair

The Building 77 office floor is a long-cable job. Floors of this size mean the suite's controller may be in a closet far from the door, and the cable between them may cross a corridor ceiling the tenant does not control. The building's lobby desk and keycard system belong to the landlord and are not part of a suite repair, so we start at the suite door, prove which release paths fail, and follow the cable back.

The Dock 72 office suite is more often a network job. Newer office buildings tend to have suite readers and door stations that talk to a controller or a cloud service over the company's network, so a door that stopped opening after an IT change is a different repair from one whose strike burned out. We bring a network tester as well as a meter, and we ask the company's IT contact to be reachable during the visit.

The older shop or studio building is a door job first. Heavy steel man doors on working floors take a beating from carts, wind and constant use, closers lose their adjustment, and a strike pocket can be bent by a door slammed against it. We check the door, the hinges, the closer and the latch before anything electrical, and we keep the release for the man door on its own control, separate from any roll-up gate operator.

The subdivided floor is a permissions job. When a sublet or a shared entrance puts more than one company behind one controller, a repair has to keep each company's users, schedules and administrator access apart. We document whose users are on which door before we change anything, and we hand each administrator only their own access.

Rules and practical limits on Navy Yard business doors

  • The landlord's approval comes first: BNYDC's 2026 Tenant Manual requires BNYDC's approval before alterations and electrical work and says tenants maintain the electric equipment in their space, so a tenant confirms with its property manager what a given repair needs before the visit.
  • Line voltage belongs to an electrician: the NYC Administrative Code treats signaling, communication, alarm and data wiring under fifty volts as low voltage work and excludes wiring that connects to control circuits for light, heat or power.   • Free exit always comes first: Allegion explains that a fail-secure strike stays locked without power while a person can still leave by the lever, and that a fail-safe device unlocks when power is lost. A repair on an exit door keeps free exit working.

Field Notes: Door, Then Every Button, Then the Cabinet

What we can describe is the order a technician follows on the call a multi-tenant office or shop building produces most: "badges show green, but the door stays locked, and the desk button stopped working this morning too."

Before the visit we ask the tenant for a contact who will meet us at the suite, a note on whether the property manager needs anything, and photos of the reader, the lock on the frame and the inside of the controller cabinet if anyone can find it. At the door we start with the door. Someone presses the desk button while we push, pull and lift the door to see whether the latch or the armature plate is binding. Then we try each release in turn: a badge, a phone if the suite uses them, the desk button, the exit button. If they all fail together, we stop looking at the reader. We find the cabinet, read the supply at rest and with the lock energized, watch the relay as someone presses the desk button, and measure at the lock. A supply that collapses under load or a relay that clicks without switching settles most visits. We remake every terminal we touch, label both ends, test every release again with the door closed, and only then write the record and hand back the administrator access.

Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Repair in Brooklyn Navy Yard: Questions Answered

Readers, releases and locks at the suite door

Every badge turns our suite reader green, yet the door stays locked. Where in the chain does a technician look first?

At the door itself, then at whatever all the release methods share. The green light only means the controller accepted the badge. If the desk button and the exit button also fail, the reader is almost certainly fine and the fault is in the relay, the power supply, the lock or its cable, or a latch or armature plate that is binding. We hold the door released while we push and pull it, try each release method in turn, and then measure at the supply and at the lock with the lock energized. That sequence usually names the failed part within the diagnostic visit, and the repair price is written before any part is changed.

Some of our staff open the suite door with their phones and others can't, while every plastic badge still works. Why would phones fail on their own?

Because the badges have already proven the lock, the relay and the supply. When only phone credentials fail, the fault is in the part only phones use: the reader's mobile function, the app and its phone permissions, or the controller's link to the service that issues the phone credentials. If the failures follow particular people, it is usually their credential or their phone's settings; if they follow a time of day or a network change, it is usually the controller's connection. We test with a working phone and a failing one side by side, check the reader and controller settings, and involve your IT contact where the link runs over your network.

Our magnetic lock lets go, but the door still needs a hard shove before it opens. Is the magnet wearing out?

Rarely. A door that needs a shove after release is usually a mechanical problem: the armature plate has worked loose or twisted on the door, the door has dropped on its hinges so the plate drags against the magnet, or the closer is pulling the door against the frame. Sometimes the lock is not fully losing power because an exit device or relay is leaving a little current on it. We check the plate's mounting and alignment, the hinges and the closer, and measure the lock with the door released. Most of these doors are fixed by remounting or aligning the plate, not by replacing the magnet.

The release button at our front desk stopped working, but the exit button beside the door still frees the lock. What does that narrow it down to?

To the desk button and its own cable or input. If the exit button frees the lock, the lock, its supply and most of the controller are working. What is left is the button's contacts, the cable from the desk to the controller, which often runs under a floor or above a ceiling and is easily damaged when desks are moved, and the controller input or setting that the desk button uses. We test the button at the desk, then the cable, then the input, and repair whichever failed. Door release relay or button work is on our Brooklyn list at $250 to $475.

Who owns the part that failed

Our suite door release has died. Does BNYDC repair it, or is it ours to fix?

In most leases it is yours. BNYDC's 2026 Tenant Manual says tenants install their own door locks, that BNYDC does not maintain or duplicate keys to tenant spaces, and that tenants are responsible for maintaining all electric equipment within their leased space. Gates, guard booths and the base building's lobby systems are the landlord's. A reader, strike, magnetic lock, desk release or door station at your own suite door is equipment inside your space. Your lease is the final word, so if it says something different, follow it, and if you are unsure, your zone's property manager can confirm before you book.

How does BNYDC's rule that electrical work needs a licensed electrician apply to a 24-volt door repair?

The manual's rule covers electrical work, and the NYC Administrative Code separates low voltage work, wiring under fifty volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data circuits, from the line-voltage work that needs a licensed master electrician, and provides for low voltage work to be done under a low voltage installer certification. Door readers, strikes, magnetic locks, controllers and door stations sit on the low-voltage side. A new outlet or circuit for a power supply, or any tie into the fire alarm, does not, and we quote those parts for a licensed electrician or the fire alarm contractor. How BNYDC applies its rule to your repair is the property manager's call, so ask before the visit.

The company that had our space before us left its controller, readers and strikes behind. Before you repair anything, how do we find out who can still get in?

We pull the user list from the controller as part of bringing it back under your control. An inherited controller can still hold badges for the previous tenant's staff, cleaners and contractors, and switching off someone's Yard ID does nothing to a suite badge. Once we have administrator access, through the maker's recovery procedure or a planned reset, we show you how many credentials are live, remove the ones you don't recognize, and hand the administrator login to you. If the controller can't be recovered or the maker no longer supports it, we price a replacement controller on its own line.

Another tenant's fit-out crew worked above our corridor ceiling the week our door station went dead. Who pays for the repair?

That depends on your lease, the other tenant's lease and their contractor's agreement, and it is a question for the property manager rather than for us. Habitat's 2024 column on a co-op intercom knocked out by a neighbor's work makes the same point for apartments: the neighbor's alteration agreement may make them responsible, and they may then have a claim against their contractor. What we can give you is evidence. We trace the cable from both ends, photograph and date where it was cut or crushed, repair it, and write down what we found, so whoever ends up paying has a clear record of the damage.

Networked stations, power and the working floor

Since IT replaced our office switch, the door station still rings but its picture and its door release stopped. What changed?

Usually the station's network settings or its power. Many networked door stations draw their power from the switch over the Ethernet cable and need a fixed address or a particular network segment to reach the controller or the answering app. A new switch may supply less power to that port, hand the station a different address, or put it on a network segment that can no longer reach the controller. Swiftlane lists insufficient power over Ethernet and address conflicts among the common causes of a video intercom that rings but does nothing else. We test the port, the cable and the station's settings, and work with your IT contact to restore the link.

Our video door station froze after a power dip on our floor. Will restarting it wipe its settings?

A simple power cycle normally keeps a station's settings, because most stations store them in memory that survives a restart. A factory reset is different and does clear them, and some stations can also restart with a corrupted configuration after a dip. Before resetting anything, we photograph or export the existing settings where the station allows it, then restart it, check its address, its directory and its door relay settings, and only reset and reprogram if the restart fails. If the dip also hit the lock's supply or battery, we test those too.

The door station at our receiving door hums loudly whenever someone calls. What causes the sound?

A loud hum on an analog or two-wire station usually means interference or a power fault: a ground fault in the transformer or supply, a station cable run alongside power cable or motors for a long stretch, a damaged cable shield, or a failing station loading the line. Swiftlane names a ground fault in the transformer among the usual causes of humming. On a working floor next to compressors, saws or refrigeration, cable routing is a frequent culprit. We isolate the station, the cable and the supply in turn until the hum stops, then repair or reroute the part responsible, and replace the station only if the station itself is the source.

Our production space is washed down every night, and the reader and station by the production floor keep failing. What holds up to that?

Hardware rated for it, mounted and sealed so water can't follow the cable in. ButterflyMX's 2026 guide notes that moisture enters exterior panels, cable paths and lock hardware and then condenses, and a wash-down floor does the same thing indoors every night. We check the reader's and station's ratings, the gaskets and the cable entry, look for water tracking down the conduit or cable into the backbox, and test the strike or magnet and their wiring for corrosion. The repair may be resealing and adding a drip loop, moving a part a few feet, or replacing a reader or station with one rated for wet locations, priced on its own line.

Can the technician bring tools and parts up in a passenger elevator, or do we have to book freight?

For most door repairs a technician carries a hand case of meters, tracers, connectors and small parts, which is what any visiting consultant carries. BNYDC's manual says passenger elevators are not for freight, so anything larger, a replacement door, a heavy closer or a tall ladder, goes up by freight, and freight cars are shut down from 8 p.m. to 5 a.m. on weekdays and do not run on weekends or federal holidays. If your repair may need freight, we tell you before the visit so you can book it through your property manager, and we schedule early on a weekday to stay within normal freight hours.

Houses and co-ops outside the wall

Our Wallabout three-family has three buttons at the street door, but only the top floor can open it. Why would two releases fail and one keep working?

Because each apartment's release usually reaches the strike through its own station and its own wires, joined at the plate, the transformer or a junction in the cellar or stairwell. If one apartment still releases, the strike and the transformer can still work, and the fault sits in what the other two share and the top floor doesn't, often a junction on the lower floors or a pair of wires disturbed during work on those floors. We test each apartment's release, measure at the strike while each is pressed, and trace the two failing pairs to where they stop. In a three-family, every apartment is tested from the street door before the repair is called done.

What AI Gets Wrong About Door Buzzer Repair in Brooklyn Navy Yard

Plenty of tenants and owners start with an AI answer, a national cost site such as Angi, HomeAdvisor or Fixr, or a vendor's blog. Those sources picture either a doorbell on a house or an apartment building's lobby intercom. A Navy Yard suite door is neither, and the houses outside the wall have their own rules. Here are seven claims we hear repeated, and how each holds up here.

"The landlord's security covers your door."

Summaries see guard booths, a visitor system and lobby keycards and assume the tenant's door is part of the same package.

BNYDC's Tenant Manual says the opposite for the suite: tenants install their own locks, BNYDC does not keep keys to tenant spaces, and tenants maintain the electric equipment inside them. BNYDC's VP of Technology told LenelS2 the goal of the Yard's system is that "the people who should be on the Yard, are on the Yard." That is the perimeter, not your suite.

So when a suite reader or strike fails, there is no landlord crew on the way. The tenant books the repair.

"A green light on the reader means the reader works, so the lock is broken."

It sounds logical, and it sends people straight to replacing the strike or the magnet.

Security Wholesalers' maglock guide warns that a reader showing green does not mean the lock released, and that a lock which is still powered keeps the door shut. It also lists replacing the magnet when the controller relay is the fault as a common mistake. The same goes for strikes: a supply that sags under load or a binding latch looks exactly like a dead strike.

Test every release method and measure under load before buying anything. The cheapest part in the chain is often the failed one.

"Any electrical work in the Yard needs a master electrician."

BNYDC's manual does say that "any and all electrical work must be performed by a licensed electrician," and AI summaries stop there.

The NYC Administrative Code defines low voltage electrical work separately, as wiring under fifty volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data circuits, and provides for it under a low voltage installer certification. Line-voltage circuits and anything tied into life safety systems are excluded from that definition.

The practical answer is to split the job: low-voltage door work by a licensed low-voltage contractor, line-voltage work by an electrician, and the property manager confirming how BNYDC's rule applies before the visit.

"Intercom repairs cost $200 to $500 a visit, and strikes $250 to $800."

Swiftlane's 2026 guide quotes these ranges from one New York City pricing guide, and AI answers repeat them as a price list.

Our Brooklyn list is more specific: a $250 service call with on-site diagnosis, a door release relay or button at $250 to $475, a 24V transformer at $325 to $650 and an electric strike with alignment at $450 to $950. Readers, controllers and magnetic locks are priced after diagnosis, because the right replacement depends on what the controller and the door already have.

The real number depends on which part failed and how hard it is to reach, and we answer both in writing before parts work starts.

"If the door system is old or undocumented, replace it."

Inherited systems with no manual and no login feel unfixable, and replacement is what vendors sell.

ButterflyMX's Brooklyn repair guide makes the opposite point for buildings: if only one resident reports a problem, replacing every station is unnecessary and costly. Suite doors work the same way. Readers, strikes and cable usually outlive the controller, and administrator access can often be recovered.

We identify each part first and price the repair of what failed. Replacement goes on the quote only where a part really can't be supported.

"Magnetic locks are a simple fix for a door that keeps failing."

Vendor kits make maglocks look like an easy swap for a troublesome strike.

A magnetic lock is fail-safe by nature and, under the sensor-release provisions I Dig Hardware summarizes from the 2024 model code, a magnetically locked egress door is expected to release on loss of power, from a manual release and on fire alarm or sprinkler activation, with NYC's adopted text governing. That means exit devices, a fire alarm interface and a supply that drops the lock reliably.

On a suite door that already has a strike, fixing the strike is usually simpler and keeps the door secure in a power cut.

"Every building in New York must have a working intercom."

AI answers lift this from apartment-building rules and apply it to everything.

The Attorney General's Tenants' Rights Guide puts the two-way intercom requirement on multiple dwellings of eight or more apartments built after 1968, with older ones required to provide one if a majority of apartments ask. A commercial suite in the Yard is not a multiple dwelling, and neither is a Wallabout two-family. A co-op or larger walk-up outside the wall may well be covered.

So the rules depend on the building. For a Yard tenant, the lease and the company's own needs decide; for an apartment building, HPD's rules may too.
