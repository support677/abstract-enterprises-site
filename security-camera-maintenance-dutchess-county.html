The Neighborhood

Why Dutchess County Properties Need Security Camera Maintenance

Dutchess is a long county, running from the Beacon waterfront up to Millerton and the Columbia County line. Between the river towns on Route 9 and the farms toward the Connecticut line sit horse barns, orchards, estates behind stone walls, and houses that are lived in only on weekends. A camera system there is rarely a cluster of domes in one building. It is a turret on the house, a bullet on the barn, a floodlight camera over the garage, maybe a camera at the gate a quarter-mile down the drive, all feeding one recorder in a basement closet. When one of those cameras fails, nobody walks past it. The owner finds out when they open the app — or when they need a clip and it is not there.

The weather is the second reason. On July 4, 2026 a severe thunderstorm cut power to about 50,000 Central Hudson customers at its height, more than 32,000 of them in central and southern Dutchess, and the county reported the worst damage in the City and Town of Poughkeepsie, East Fishkill, Hyde Park, LaGrange, Beekman, Union Vale and Pawling. Four months earlier, a St. Patrick's Day windstorm with gusts to 55 mph interrupted service to roughly 14,748 Central Hudson customers, with Dutchess and Ulster hit hardest. In the eastern towns served by NYSEG, Tropical Storm Isaias in 2020 left 2,317 of 2,321 NYSEG customers in Amenia without power two days after it passed. Every one of those events is a test of a camera system: does it ride through, does it come back by itself, and does anyone know if it did not?

The third reason is what has changed around private cameras. Dutchess County had built a network of license-plate cameras for its Analysis and Real-Time Crime Intelligence Center, but in September 2026, after a packed public forum at Pleasant Valley Town Hall, the District Attorney asked the vendor to shut down and remove all of its county-operated cameras. Whatever one thinks of that decision, it means the footage that answers a question about a break-in, a theft from a barn or a car in a lot is more likely than before to come from a homeowner's or a business's own recorder. A Poughkeepsie shop that shared its own video of a tip-jar theft in June 2026 is a small example of how often that happens. A recorder only helps if it was recording, clean and on the right clock the night it mattered.

So maintenance in Dutchess is built the other way round from a city plan. Instead of frequent visits to wipe grime off domes, we set the system up to watch itself — health alerts, offline alerts, a scheduled check of the oldest recording — and then time the hands-on visits to the seasons: a pre-winter visit before ice, snow and short days, and a check after any storm that took power or the internet down for long. Owners with a camera at the gate often pair the visit with a look at their gate and door access control, since the same technician is already at the end of the drive.

Dutchess facts that shape a maintenance schedule

  • Big storms reach most of the county. The July 4, 2026 thunderstorm interrupted service to more than 32,000 Central Hudson customers in central and southern Dutchess; two days later the county still counted more than 19,000 Central Hudson and over 3,400 NYSEG customers out.
  • Two utilities, two outage maps. Most of the county is Central Hudson territory, but NYSEG serves parts of the east — in August 2020 it had 15,860 Dutchess customers, including Amenia, Dover and Pawling, where most of them lost power after Isaias.
  • Winter is real. The county's Natural Resources Inventory puts average snowfall at 47.1 inches a year, more at higher elevations in the northeast, with temperatures falling below zero five to ten times a winter and three major ice storms since 2008.
  • Lightning is common. Earth Networks counted 166,641 lightning pulses in Dutchess in its 2020 New York report; the NRI also logs about one to two large-hail events a year since 1971.
  • Farms and open land. The USDA's 2022 Census of Agriculture counted 620 farms in Dutchess averaging 161 acres — the kind of property where cameras sit on several buildings with separate power.
  • Most homes are on broadband, not all. Census QuickFacts puts Dutchess households with a broadband subscription at 93.5%, which leaves a share of rural addresses relying on slower links for remote viewing and alerts.

Building Specialists

Camera Maintenance for Dutchess County's Architecture

The county's building stock is unusually mixed. Its median home was built around 1971, yet about 18% of homes date from 1939 or earlier; riverfront mills sit a few miles from 1960s corporate campuses and new warehouses. Each kind of building wears a camera system out differently.

Estates and Country Houses

Large houses along the river in Hyde Park, Staatsburg and Rhinebeck, and behind stone walls around Millbrook, often with a gatehouse, a long drive and a caretaker's cottage. Cameras end up far from the recorder, often on separate power, and the person who knows the system may be a property manager rather than the owner.

Working Farms and Horse Barns

Barns, run-in sheds and equipment buildings across the eastern towns. Dust, hay, birds and humidity reach every camera, cables cross open ground, and the barn's electric panel may be a long way from the house. Maintenance there is as much about the cable and power as the cameras.

Weekend and Seasonal Houses

Houses near the Taconic and in the northern towns that sit empty for days or months. The camera system is often the only thing watching, so remote alerts, recording schedules and backup power matter more than a weekly wipe of the lens.

Riverfront Mill Conversions

Brick mill buildings in Beacon along Fishkill Creek and similar conversions in Wappingers Falls, now lofts, studios and offices. Thick masonry, high ceilings, damp lower floors and many tenants sharing one system make access and ownership the hard part.

Former IBM-Era Office Parks

Large campuses built from the 1960s on, such as the former IBM East Fishkill site now known as iPark 84, with about 300 acres and more than two million square feet of buildings. Hundreds of cameras of different ages and brands, installed by different tenants over decades.

Village Main Streets

Two- and three-storey mixed-use buildings in Rhinebeck, Red Hook, Millbrook, Pawling and Beacon, with a shop downstairs and apartments above. One recorder often serves the store and the stairwell, and the camera over the sidewalk is the one police ask about.

How the work changes with the building

  • Stone and brick walls: exterior cameras on long steel arms are checked for creep at the anchor every visit; freeze-thaw cycles open mortar joints and loosen fixings more than wind does.
  • Outbuildings on their own service: each building with its own panel gets its surge protection and grounding looked at, because a cable between two buildings is a path for lightning energy.
  • Unheated spaces: recorders and switches in barns, garages or pool houses are moved into a conditioned space where possible; drives and batteries do not like a winter at single digits.
  • Office-park ceilings: cameras above drop ceilings in old corporate buildings are matched against a camera list every visit, because tenant fit-outs move tiles and cut cables without telling anyone.
  • Seasonal houses: heat may be set low while the house is closed, so the recorder closet is checked for cold and condensation when it is reopened.

What Dutchess businesses are held to

  • Cannabis licensees (9 NYCRR §125.3): the state rule asks for at least eight hours of operation through a power cut — the line that matters most in a county where July and March storms kept thousands dark for a day or more. The same section sets 60-day retention, continuous recording while open, a failure notice inside five minutes, and testing at least every 30 days with five years of test records.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Maintenance in Dutchess County: Questions Answered

These are the questions Dutchess owners, caretakers and facility managers ask about camera systems that are already on the wall — grouped by what tends to go wrong in the county. Prices are further down the page; short scheduling and access questions are in the FAQ near the end.

Distance, Alerts and Remote Checks

We live in Millbrook and your office is in the Bronx — how much of the upkeep can be handled without a truck in our driveway?

More than most people expect, and in Dutchess that is the point. Roughly half of what a maintenance visit checks lives inside the recorder, not on the ladder: whether every channel is recording, how far back the oldest footage goes, what the drive's health readings say, whether the firmware is current, whether the clock is right and whether alerts are reaching the right phone. With your permission and a secure remote session, those can be reviewed from a laptop, and the result tells us whether a trip is needed at all.

What cannot be done remotely is anything physical. A dome hazed with pollen, a sun shield packed with a wasp nest, a bracket loosened by a winter of frost, a cable a deer or a mower has pulled at, a surge protector that has quietly done its job and died — all of that needs eyes and hands. So a typical Dutchess plan mixes the two: remote reviews between visits, and one or two hands-on visits a year timed to the seasons that do the damage.

Remote access is set up so it is yours, not ours. We use the manufacturer's own secure connection or a temporary account you can switch off, we never leave a router port open to the internet for convenience, and every remote session is written into the visit log with the date and what was checked.

When the internet at our Red Hook farmhouse drops, the app just says "offline" — how can we tell an internet outage from a dead recorder?

From the app alone, you often cannot. "Offline" means the phone could not reach the recorder; it does not say whether the recorder is off, the modem is down, the router has hung or the provider has an outage on the road. That is why a remote property needs a second, independent signal. The simplest is to check whether anything else at the house is reachable — a thermostat, a smart plug, a Wi-Fi printer — because if they are all offline together, the problem is the connection, not the cameras.

A better answer is to make sure the recorder keeps recording when the internet is gone, which a wired system with local drives does by default, and to have something that can tell you power is still on. Many owners add a small cellular-connected power or temperature monitor, or ask a neighbour or caretaker to glance at the recorder screen on the next pass. Wide internet cuts do happen: in October 2019 a cut fiber line in the Poughkeepsie area took out Verizon internet and phone service for customers across parts of the county, including Hyde Park and Wappingers Falls.

On a maintenance visit we label the modem, router and recorder so a caretaker can restart them in the right order, plug the networking gear into battery backup alongside the recorder, and set the recorder to send an alert when it comes back online after an outage — a "back up" message is as useful as a "down" one when you are an hour away.

Our only internet past Amenia is a fixed-wireless link with a data cap — will remote viewing and health checks eat through it?

Live viewing can, health checks do not. A remote health check sends a few kilobytes — a status, an error count, a list of channels. Watching a 4K camera live, or letting an app pull high-resolution clips every time motion triggers, sends megabytes per minute, and a few cameras set to upload to a cloud service around the clock can use more data in a month than the rest of the household. With 93.5% of Dutchess households on a broadband subscription, the county is well connected overall, but the farms and back roads of the eastern towns are where the exceptions sit.

The fix is in the settings. Recorders and cameras usually have two video streams: a full-quality main stream that goes to the local drive and a smaller sub-stream meant for phones. We make sure the app uses the sub-stream by default, switch off continuous cloud upload you did not ask for, limit push notifications to the cameras and zones that matter, and record everything at full quality locally where it costs nothing to keep.

On a visit we also measure what the system actually sends in a normal day, so you can compare it with the plan's cap. If the numbers are tight, a smaller change — fewer snapshots attached to alerts, a lower sub-stream bitrate on one busy camera — is usually enough without giving up anything you would miss.

Storms, Ice and Lightning

The July storm cut power in Pawling and our generator started within a minute — so why is there a gap in the recording?

Because a minute is long enough for a recorder to shut off. A standby generator does not start the instant the grid fails: it senses the loss, starts the engine, lets it settle, and then the transfer switch moves the house over. Between those steps the recorder, the PoE switch that powers the cameras and the modem all lose power, and when it returns they boot up one by one. A recorder can take a couple of minutes to finish booting and start writing again, and cameras take their own time on top of that. The result is a gap of several minutes at exactly the moment something may have happened.

The cure is a small battery backup between the generator circuit and the camera equipment, sized to carry the recorder, switch and modem through the hand-off with room to spare. The battery covers the seconds or minutes until the generator is stable, and the cameras never notice the change. The July 4, 2026 storm, which the county said hit Pawling, Beekman, Union Vale and the Poughkeepsie area hardest, is a good reason to test this before the next one.

On a maintenance visit we test it for real: switch off the utility feed or simulate the outage, watch the generator take over, and check the timeline afterwards for any missing minutes. If the battery is too small, too old or not on the right circuit, the log says so and the fix is priced as a part.

After a lightning storm two barn cameras still work but the one at the end of the drive is dead — what usually fails, and what should be checked on the others?

The camera furthest away on the longest cable is often the one that takes the hit. Lightning does not need to strike a camera to damage it: a nearby strike induces a surge in any long conductor, and a cable running hundreds of feet across a field or down a drive is exactly that. Earth Networks counted 166,641 lightning pulses over Dutchess in its 2020 New York report, so on a farm or estate this is not a rare event. The usual casualties are the camera itself, the port on the PoE switch it plugs into, a surge protector that sacrificed itself, or the network port on a wireless bridge.

The cameras that still show a picture are not necessarily healthy. A surge can damage a switch port so it works intermittently, cut a camera's infrared so it goes black at night, or weaken a power supply so it fails in the next cold snap. After a strike we check every channel by day and by night, read the switch's port errors, test the surge protectors with a meter or replace those with a status light that has gone out, and look at the grounding where cables enter each building.

For the camera at the end of the drive, the lasting fix is protection at both ends of the long run and a solid ground at the building entry, or replacing that copper run with fiber or a wireless link that cannot carry a surge back to the house. We price the options after looking at the run; this is general practice rather than an engineering design for your site.

Ice built up on the dome over our driveway every February — does that hurt the camera, and what keeps the view clear?

Ice is rarely what kills a camera; it is what blinds it. Most outdoor cameras are rated to keep working well below freezing — datasheets we have checked list operating ranges down to −35 °C or −40 °C — but a dome coated in freezing rain shows a white blur, and an infrared camera at night bounces its own light off the ice. The county's Natural Resources Inventory counts three major ice storms in Dutchess since 2008, and nights below zero five to ten times a winter, so a camera that matters in February needs a plan for it.

The practical fixes are placement and shelter. A dome mounted under an eave or a small hood collects far less ice than one on an open wall. Cameras with a built-in heater or a housing heater clear faster, but only if the heater is working and the power supply can carry it — a PoE switch that powers a dozen cameras in summer may not have budget left for heaters in winter. Turret-style cameras, with no bubble over the lens, shed ice better than domes in exposed spots.

Our pre-winter visit checks heaters and power budget, cleans and treats the domes, re-aims cameras so a plow bank or roof slide will not fill the frame, and moves the worst offenders under cover where it can be done without losing the view you need. Scraping a frozen dome is not one of those fixes; it scratches the plastic for good.

The oaks along our drive in LaGrange have grown into the camera's view since it went up — is pruning part of maintenance?

Noticing it is; cutting it usually is not. Views in Dutchess change with the seasons more than in a city: a driveway camera that sees the road clearly in March can be looking at a wall of leaves by June, and branches that sway in the wind trigger motion alerts all night and fill the drive with useless clips. Because most cameras were installed and aimed in one season, they are often wrong for the other half of the year.

On each visit we compare every camera's view against its reference snapshot and note any growth, then give you the choice: re-aim or re-zoom the camera, move it a few feet, mask the swaying branches out of motion detection, or have the branches cut. For anything beyond a hand-pruner at the camera itself, we recommend your landscaper or an arborist — limbs near the overhead service line belong to the utility.

It is worth fixing before a storm rather than after. The same limbs that block a view are the ones that come down on a camera cable or the pole it is mounted to. The July 4, 2026 storm in Dutchess was described by the county as bringing down power lines and trees, and a camera aimed through a tree line is often the first casualty.

Spread-Out Properties and Outbuildings

The barn camera reaches the house over a wireless bridge — why does it drop out every spring, and how do you keep it aligned?

Point-to-point wireless bridges are a common way to reach a barn or gatehouse without trenching, and they work well when the two radios can see each other clearly. In spring that changes: leaves come out on the trees between them, and wet foliage absorbs the signal. A link that was strong across bare branches in winter can weaken or drop when the canopy fills in, which is why the complaint so often arrives in May.

Bridges also drift. They are usually mounted on poles, eaves or barn walls, and wind, frost heave and a winter of snow sliding off a roof can turn one a few degrees — enough to cut the signal on a long link. On a maintenance visit we read the signal strength and error rate from the bridge's own software, compare them with the last visit, re-aim the radios if they have moved and tighten the mounts.

If leaves are the problem, the cure is either a clearer path — higher mounts or a different route around the trees — or a different link. Where the run is short enough, buried conduit with fiber is the most reliable answer, and it also stops a lightning surge from travelling between buildings. We price those options separately; maintenance is where we spot that a link is failing slowly before it fails outright.

Our house, barn and pool house each have their own electric panel — what does maintenance check on a system like that that a one-building system doesn't need?

Mostly the connections between buildings. When cameras on one building are powered from another building's equipment, the copper network cable between them links two separate electrical systems. During a storm or a fault, those systems can sit at different voltages for a moment, and the camera cable becomes the path for the difference. That is how one storm can take out a camera, a switch port and the recorder's network card in a single event.

On these properties we check that each building where a cable enters or leaves has a surge protector on that line, that it is properly grounded to that building's ground, and that the protector still works — many have a status light, and once it goes out the device has done its job and is no longer protecting anything. We also confirm which panel and breaker feeds each camera power supply and label them, so a caretaker resetting a tripped breaker in the barn knows which one runs the cameras.

Finally, each outbuilding's equipment gets looked at for heat, cold and pests. A PoE switch on a barn shelf is exposed to dust, damp and winter cold in a way a switch in a house closet is not, and it often outlives its welcome long before anyone notices.

Mice have moved into the pump house where a camera switch lives — what does a visit look for in outbuildings like that?

Chewed cable is the obvious risk. Rodents gnaw on cable jackets, and an unheated outbuilding full of feed, hay or insulation is exactly where they settle in for the winter. A nicked jacket may still pass video for weeks before moisture gets in and the camera starts dropping in and out, especially in cold, damp weather. Nests in warm equipment are the other risk: a switch or power supply makes a little heat, and a nest packed around its vents makes it run hotter and fail sooner.

On a visit we open the enclosures, look along every visible run for chew marks, clean out nests and droppings from around the equipment with the power off, and check that the cable entries into the building are sealed. Where cable runs through open framing at mouse height, we recommend moving it into conduit or up out of reach.

If an outbuilding is a constant problem, the better long-term fix is moving the networking gear into a sealed enclosure or into the main house and running a single protected cable out to the cameras. Rodent control itself is a job for your pest service; our part is making sure the camera system is not the most inviting thing in the building.

Seasonal Houses, Office Parks and Local Rules

We close our Hyde Park house from November to April — what should be done to the camera system before we leave and when we open it again?

Before closing, the aim is that the system can run unattended for months and tell you if it stops. That means confirming every camera records to the local drive, checking that the oldest recording will cover the whole time you are away or that you are happy with what it overwrites, setting alerts for video loss, drive errors and recorder offline, putting the recorder, switch and modem on battery backup, and making sure the heat left on in the house keeps the recorder closet above freezing. If the internet bill is paused for the winter, the cameras still record — but nobody will see an alert, which is worth knowing before you go.

While you are away, a quick look at the app once a week costs nothing and catches most problems. Watch for a camera that has gone black at night, a drive warning, or the recorder clock being wrong after a storm. Owners with caretakers can ask them to check the recorder screen during their normal rounds.

When you reopen, check for what winter did: ice damage on domes, cameras knocked by snow sliding off the roof, a recorder that restarted after an outage with the wrong time, a drive that filled and started overwriting. Our opening visit runs through all of it in one trip and leaves a report of anything that happened while the house was closed.

Half the cameras in our Fishkill office-park buildings are past manufacturer support — how do you decide which to keep maintaining and which to budget to replace?

By sorting them on three questions rather than replacing everything at once. First, does the camera still do its job — is the picture usable by day and by night for what it is pointed at? Second, can it still be kept secure — is the firmware still updated, can default passwords be removed, and is it isolated from the rest of the network? Third, can it be repaired — are replacement parts or an identical model still available? A camera that fails on all three goes to the top of the replacement list; one that passes the first two can often run for years.

Parts are the wild card. Since July 16, 2026, an FCC order has stopped new imports and marketing of some covered camera equipment that was previously authorized; equipment already installed can stay in service. Hikvision's US business has warned that this could reach spare parts, warranty repairs and support. A campus with forty of one discontinued model should therefore hold a few identical spares now and schedule its swap-outs, rather than discover the shortage the week a lobby camera dies.

We build the list into the maintenance report: every camera, its model and firmware, its condition and a replace-by year. Facilities teams in office parks along Route 52 and I-84 usually use it to spread replacement over several budget years, starting with entrances, docks and server rooms, and leaving quiet interior corridors for last.

Since Dutchess pulled its county plate-reader cameras in September 2026, will police lean more on our business cameras — and does that change how we maintain them?

It may change how often you are asked. On September 25, 2026, the Times Union reported that the District Attorney had asked Flock to shut down and remove all of the county-operated cameras — 65 by that report; Mid-Hudson News had counted 64 deployed in July. Whatever replaces them, an investigator looking for a car or a person on a particular road will often start by asking the businesses and homes along it whether their cameras caught anything.

Being useful when asked comes down to three things maintenance controls: the camera covering your entrance, lot or roadside is clean and aimed where you think it is; the recorder actually holds as many days as you believe; and the time stamp is right, so a clip from 10:42 p.m. really is from 10:42 p.m. A recorder whose clock drifted by twenty minutes after the last outage makes every clip harder to use.

Whether and how you share footage is your decision and, for some businesses, a legal question; we do not hand footage to anyone without your instruction. What we can do is make sure that when you decide to share, the export works, the clip plays on someone else's computer, and the person on your staff who will do it has practised once.

For routine maintenance, usually not, but the town decides. AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Maintenance in Dutchess County

Our research environment could not capture Google's AI Overview for this search, so nothing below quotes it. These are seven claims that appear again and again in the general advice, product pages and cost guides that such summaries are built from — set against what Dutchess systems actually go through.

Claim 1: "Outdoor cameras are weatherproof, so winter is not a concern"

A weatherproof rating describes how well a housing keeps out dust and water. It says nothing about whether the picture is usable under a glaze of freezing rain, whether a heater can run on the power budget left on the switch, or whether a bracket survives a season of freeze and thaw in a stone wall.

Dutchess winters test all of those. The county's Natural Resources Inventory records three major ice storms since 2008, average snowfall of 47.1 inches, and nights below zero several times most winters. Cameras survive that; their views often do not.

That is why our plans put the hands-on visit in autumn, not spring. Heaters, seals, mounts and aim are checked while there is still time to fix them, and the worst-placed cameras are moved under cover before the first ice rather than after a month of white screens.

Claim 2: "With a standby generator, your cameras never go down"

Generators keep the house running; they do not make the switch-over seamless. Between the moment the grid drops and the moment the transfer switch moves the load, the recorder and cameras lose power, and then spend minutes rebooting.

In a county where one July storm took out power to more than 32,000 Central Hudson customers in central and southern Dutchess, those minutes come around often. They are also the minutes when a storm-damaged gate, a downed tree across the drive or an opportunist is most likely to appear on camera.

The answer is a battery backup that bridges the gap, tested with the generator rather than assumed. We run that test on visits and write down how long the ride-through lasted.

Claim 3: "Wireless cameras are the easy answer for barns and long driveways"

They are easy to put up. Keeping them working across a farm is another matter. Battery cameras lose charge quickly in cold weather, Wi-Fi coverage that reached the barn in March can fade when the trees between leaf out, and a camera that drops off the network stops recording unless it has its own storage.

On properties with several buildings, we see the same cycle: a wireless camera works for a season, then becomes the one the owner stops trusting. The fix is often a proper link to the outbuilding — a point-to-point bridge, or fiber in conduit — so the camera itself can be wired.

Wireless and battery cameras have their place, and we maintain them. The maintenance just looks different: charge levels, signal strength by season and the account they report to, rather than drives and cables.

Claim 4: "A surge protector at the wall outlet protects the whole system"

A plug-in surge strip protects against surges arriving through that outlet. It does nothing for a surge that arrives through a long camera cable from a barn, a gate post or a pole in a field — which is the path lightning usually takes into a spread-out camera system.

Earth Networks counted 166,641 lightning pulses over Dutchess in 2020. A cable that runs hundreds of feet across open ground is an antenna for nearby strikes, and the energy it picks up goes straight to the camera at one end and the switch and recorder at the other.

Protection for these systems sits on the data lines where they enter each building, bonded to that building's ground, and it wears out as it works. We test or replace it after storm season and after any strike that took out equipment nearby.

Claim 5: "If the app shows a live picture, the system is recording"

Live view and recording are separate jobs. A recorder can show you a perfect live picture on your phone while its drive has failed, filled up and stopped, or dropped one camera from its recording schedule after a firmware update or a power loss.

For owners who check in from the city or from another state, this is the most dangerous gap, because the app looks healthy. The first sign is often an empty timeline on the night something happened.

The check that matters is the playback timeline and the oldest recording on the drive, not the live grid. We read both on every visit and every remote review, and we set the recorder to send a drive-error alert so you hear about a failed disk the day it fails.

Claim 6: "Remote monitoring makes site visits unnecessary"

Remote checks have made upkeep in a county like Dutchess far cheaper and quicker — we rely on them. But they can only see what the recorder reports. They cannot see a dome scratched by a careless cleaning, a wasp nest behind a sun shield, a cable chewed in a pump house or a surge protector whose light has gone out.

They also cannot see slow changes in the world around the camera: the oak that has grown into the driveway view, the barn door that now blocks a corner, the plow bank that buries a low camera every February.

So our Dutchess plans combine both. Remote reviews catch most failures between visits and tell us whether a trip is needed; one or two physical visits a year, timed to winter and to storm season, catch everything the recorder cannot report.

Claim 7: "Camera maintenance costs the same wherever you live"

National cost guides, such as HomeAdvisor's guide to security system repair, are built around repair visits rather than ongoing upkeep, and they cannot account for distance. In Dutchess the drive to a property in Amenia or Pine Plains, the number of separate buildings and the need to time visits to the seasons all change what a plan costs.
