The Neighborhood

Why Staten Island Properties Need NVR Installation

A network video recorder — an NVR — is the box that takes in every camera's video, writes it to hard drives and lets you search it later. On Staten Island it is usually a homeowner's box. The Citizens Housing & Planning Council counts 83 percent of the island's homes in one- and two-family houses, so the typical recorder we install watches one family's driveway, front steps, side door and yard, and lives somewhere inside that family's house. Nobody else holds the keys to the room, and nobody else is going to notice if it stops recording.

That changes the job. In an apartment building the question is who controls the recorder room. On the island the questions are physical. Will the room it lives in flood? About 11,700 buildings stood in the area inundated by Hurricane Sandy, nine in ten of them one- and two-family homes, according to the city's recovery report, and the city's Build It Back program served 1,982 Staten Island homeowners afterwards — many of them in houses that were lifted or rebuilt with the living space a storey higher. Storms still find basements: on July 14, 2025 the Advance reported flooded basements on Poplar Avenue in Rossville while Hylan Boulevard ran like a river past New Dorp Lane. A recorder on a basement shelf is the first thing to go.

Will it keep its power? On July 25, 2025 a short, violent storm knocked out about 21,000 Staten Island Con Edison customers, most of them on the eastern shore, and NY1 reported that Con Edison cut voltage by 8 percent on the east side of the island while it made repairs, naming neighborhoods from St. George and Stapleton down through South Beach, Midland Beach and New Dorp. Recorders that lose power without a battery come back with the wrong time, a corrupted drive, or not at all. And will its video be any good at night? The island's camera footage that makes the news is almost always from a driveway or a curb in the small hours — someone trying car doors on Simonson Avenue in Mariners Harbor around 4:30 a.m. in 2022, a group checking cars in Annadale in 2017, and in February 2026 a crowbar burglary spree across New Dorp, Dongan Hills and Todt Hill that Hoodline reported in the early hours.

So the first half of every island recorder job is about the house: which floor stays dry, where the cables can run in a frame house with vinyl siding, where the internet comes in, how long the battery has to hold, and what the cameras must see after dark. The second half is the recorder itself — channels, drives, night settings, alerts, and the export routine you will use when the precinct calls. Many island owners pair the recorder with a floodlight camera on the driveway or a doorbell camera at the stoop, and plan both into the same visit so the cable runs are shared.

Staten Island facts that shape an NVR install

  • It is an island of houses. CHPC's 2024 housing-stock analysis puts 83 percent of Staten Island's units in one- or two-family homes — the highest share of any borough, and the reason most recorders here serve one or two households, not a building.
  • The shore was built low and rebuilt higher. The city's Sandy recovery report counted more than 11,700 buildings in the island's inundated area, about 90 percent of them one- and two-family homes; Build It Back served 1,982 Staten Island homeowners.
  • Flood rules decide where equipment goes. Appendix G of the NYC Building Code expects utilities and equipment to sit at or above the design flood elevation, or be designed to keep water out, and limits the space below a raised house in coastal high-hazard areas to parking, access, storage and crawl space.
  • Summer storms take out the East Shore. Con Edison said a brief, intense storm on July 25, 2025 cut power to about 21,000 Staten Island customers, mostly along the eastern shore, and NY1 reported an 8 percent voltage reduction on the island's east side.
  • Four precincts cover the island. The NYPD lists the 120th (North Shore), 121st (northwestern shore, on Richmond Avenue), 122nd (2320 Hylan Boulevard, New Dorp) and 123rd (far South Shore). The 121st opened in 2013 in territory previously shared by the 120th and 122nd, according to DNAinfo.
  • Plate readers are a live island debate. In September 2026 the Advance described privately owned plate-reader cameras at island businesses, including one on Forest Avenue in Mariners Harbor, and noted that New York has no statewide law in force that comprehensively governs police use of the technology.

Recorder Flooded, Dark or Stuck After a Staten Island Storm?

Storm water on the floor beside it, a monitor that went to a grid of black squares when the power returned, an error about the disk, whole days missing from playback — phone (347) 934-8335 and we look for a same-day slot. Once a technician is there, most faults are recording again within one to two hours of arrival. While you wait, run through the list below, and leave a wet recorder switched off.

Four checks before you call

  • Is the recorder or the floor around it wet? Unplug it at the wall once it is safe and leave it off. A wet drive powered up can destroy the footage on it.
  • Was the block without power, or on reduced voltage? Recorders with no battery behind them tend to reboot into a factory date, hang on the logo, or show live cameras while silently not recording.
  • Are all the dead cameras on one side of the house? That usually points to a switch, a cable cut by a contractor, or a junction box that took water — not the recorder.
  • Do you need a clip from the last few days? Say so first. We export what is on the drive before anyone changes settings or swaps parts.

Sometimes the recorder is fine and the trouble is outside: a camera full of water, or a line nicked by a landscaper. Those visits are handled under camera repair on Staten Island or island network cable repair, by the same technician, without a second trip.

Building Specialists

NVR Systems for Staten Island's Architecture

Island houses are not one type. A recorder that suits a detached colonial on a Mid-Island hill is in the wrong place in a raised bungalow at the shore, and a two-family with a tenant downstairs needs different accounts from a single-family. These are the six kinds of property we see most, and what changes for each.

Detached Mid-Island and Hillside Houses

Todt Hill, Emerson Hill, Grymes Hill and the streets around the Greenbelt: long driveways, garages under the house, wooded lots and steep yards. Recorder in a home office or upstairs closet; varifocal cameras for the end of the drive; extra attention to deer and branches tripping alerts.

Up-and-Down High-Ranch Two-Families

The common Great Kills pattern: two apartments stacked, a garage on the ground floor, a shared front stoop. One recorder in the owner's unit, cameras on the stoop, the driveway and the rear; a view-only login for the tenant's door camera if the owner wants it.

Semi-Attached Houses With Shared Drives

New Dorp and Dongan Hills side-by-sides, and the newer semi-attached rows in Charleston and Rossville. The camera covers your half of the drive with privacy masks over the neighbor's door and windows, and the recorder sits on the side of the house you own.

Raised and Rebuilt Shore Houses

Midland Beach, Ocean Breeze, New Dorp Beach and Oakwood after Sandy: the living floor a storey up, parking and storage underneath. Recorder, switch and battery on the living floor; cameras below with continuous cable runs and no electronics in the flood space.

North Shore Victorians and Walk-Ups

St. George, New Brighton, Stapleton and Tompkinsville: older wood and masonry houses, porches, some converted to two and three units, part of the St. George/New Brighton historic district designated in 1994. Recorder in a dry interior closet, cameras placed to avoid street-facing changes that need landmarks review.

Townhouse Condo Communities

Annadale, Huguenot and Eltingville townhouse communities with an association that owns the roads and grounds. Your recorder covers your unit; gate and common-area cameras belong to the association, so we confirm what the declaration allows before mounting on shared walls.

Hillside and Greenbelt lots bring one more island-specific problem: wildlife. NYC Parks estimated about 1,616 deer on Staten Island in January 2021, down from about 2,053 in 2017, and on wooded Mid-Island lots a deer crossing the yard at night is the most common false alert we see. Person detection on the recorder, detection boxes drawn away from the tree line, and cameras aimed at the drive rather than the woods cut most of them out.

How the install changes from house to house

  • Frame walls and siding. Most island houses are wood frame with vinyl or aluminum siding. Cameras go on siding blocks fixed to the framing; cables enter through a sealed sleeve; the run inside follows a joist bay or a closet corner rather than an exterior wall.
  • Attics and garage ceilings. On high-ranches and capes the attic or the garage ceiling is the cable highway. We check attic heat before routing a cable over insulation, and we never put the recorder or a switch in the attic.
  • Raised-house chases. On lifted houses one protected chase carries every camera cable from the flood space up to the recorder room, with the wall entry sealed against wind-driven rain.
  • Brick and stone on the North Shore. Older masonry houses take masonry anchors and careful core drilling; where the facade faces the street in a historic district we keep cameras small, under eaves and porch ceilings.
  • Two households, one recorder. The recorder lives on the owner's side; the tenant's entrance is covered from outside only; audio stays off near any door that is not the owner's.
  • Association walls. In townhouse communities we mount on the unit owner's own walls and eaves unless the board approves otherwise in writing.

Raised houses: recorder up, cameras down

When a house on the East or South Shore is lifted, the space underneath usually becomes parking, an entry and storage, and the city's flood-resistant construction rules expect equipment to sit at or above the design flood elevation or be built to keep water out. A recorder, its PoE switch, the modem and the battery backup are exactly the kind of equipment that should not stay in the old basement or move into the new crawl space. We put them in a closet, an office corner or a hall cabinet on the first living level, with the camera cables brought up through one protected chase.

The cameras themselves are outdoor-rated and can live below the flood line; the things that cannot are splices, switches and power adapters. Each camera's cable runs in one continuous length from the camera to the living floor, with no junction boxes or couplers in the parking space and a sealed wall entry so wind-driven rain does not follow the cable inside. If the space under the house floods, you might lose a camera mounted low on a pier; you do not lose the recorder or the rest of the system.

If the internet still arrives at the old ground-floor level, we ask the provider to move the modem up when they next visit, or run one network cable from the entry point to the recorder room so the modem can move later. Owners sometimes suggest the garage under the house because it has the most free wall. It is the room most likely to flood and the room a burglar reaches first, so a small recorder on an upstairs closet wall is the better trade.

Most owners of raised and two-family houses pair the recorder with keypad or app-controlled entry for the side door, because the tenant's door and the delivery door are usually the same door, and the same cable route and battery backup can serve both.

Commercial NVR Installation

Business NVR Installation on Staten Island

Island businesses mostly sit in strip centers and along a few long commercial roads, not on dense blocks. That means parking lots, rear delivery doors and landlords who own the lot cameras — and a recorder in a back room that has to hold more than a week of video, because a problem in the lot is often noticed days later. These are the corridors where we install most business recorders.

Hylan Boulevard

The longest street in New York City runs the length of the East Shore, lined with strip centers, medical offices, restaurants and car dealers. Typical job: storefront, register, rear door and a share of the lot, with the recorder in the stock room.

Richmond Avenue and the Mall

Around the Staten Island Mall, which opened in 1973 at Richmond Avenue and Richmond Hill Road, and down the avenue through the big-box centers. Large lots, deliveries at the back, and recorders that need plate-capture at the lot entrance.

Forest Avenue and Victory Boulevard

Older storefront strips on the North Shore and West Brighton: delis, pizzerias, auto shops, contractors' yards. Small units with rear and side doors, often a recorder under the counter that needs moving somewhere safer.

St. George and Bay Street

Beside the ferry: Empire Outlets' 350,000 square feet of retail, the Bay Street corridor rezoned by the Council in 2019, and new mixed-use buildings. Ground-floor tenants with building-owned cameras and their own recorders inside the unit.

The Teleport and the West Shore

The Port Authority describes the Teleport as a 100-acre business park in Bloomfield with Class A office and specialized buildings; across the West Shore, the 200-acre Matrix Global Logistics Park sits on a former oil-tank site. Corporate IT, server rooms and large yards.

Medical Offices Near the Hospitals

Practices around Staten Island University Hospital on Seaview Avenue and along Hylan: waiting rooms, rear doors and medication storage, with cameras kept out of exam rooms and recorders locked away from the front desk.

Offices and warehouses on the West Shore are a different kind of job again. The recorder usually joins a network someone else runs, so we agree the address range, the internet access and the update policy with the company's IT staff before install day, and we keep camera traffic on its own segment so a busy recorder does not slow the office. Yards and docks are wide, often lit unevenly, and watched by fixed cameras on the gates and fence lines with a pan-tilt-zoom camera for close looks. Rack recorders with mirrored drives are normal here, because losing a week of dock video to one failed disk is not acceptable to a logistics tenant.

In a strip center, the lot cameras usually belong to the landlord and the storefront and interior cameras to the tenant. We plan the tenant's recorder around what the tenant controls: its own fascia or the inside of its own windows, its own doors and register, and a recorder in its own stock room. If the landlord's network cameras can be added to the tenant's recorder, that needs the landlord's written agreement about who owns and maintains them; more often the tenant keeps its own system and the lease or a side letter says the landlord will export lot footage on request. Strip-center leases also tend to control what tenants may attach to the building, so we ask to see that clause before we drill. When the store changes hands or the lease ends, the recorder, its drives and its logins leave with the tenant, which is one more reason to keep the tenant's system separate from the landlord's from the start.

Rules and records that apply to island businesses

  • Ask for the license number. Video surveillance and CCTV work falls under the security installer licensing run by the New York Department of State. Ours is #12000287431, and any company putting a recorder in your store should be able to quote theirs.
  • Faces and biometrics. If a store turns on face recognition that collects customers' biometric identifiers, NYC Administrative Code §22-1202 requires a clear and conspicuous sign at every customer entrance and bars selling or profiting from the data. Most recorders ship with it off; we leave it off unless you decide otherwise.
  • Sound. Recording a conversation you are not part of, without the consent of at least one party, is eavesdropping under New York Penal Law, a felony. We leave camera microphones off by default.
  • Hidden cameras in private rooms. Penal Law §250.45 makes it a crime to secretly record people in places such as restrooms and changing rooms. No camera of ours goes in one.
  • Covered brands. The FCC's 2026 order (DA 26-635, effective July 16, 2026) stops continued importation and marketing of certain covered equipment, including Hikvision and Dahua video surveillance gear for covered uses, but does not affect the continued use of equipment already bought. A store's existing recorder can keep running; replacement parts may get harder to find.
  • Commercial Security Tax Credit. A Hylan or Forest Avenue shop with a small staff (the program's ceiling is 50 employees) may be able to recover up to $3,000 per retail location of what it spends on security equipment, cameras included. That is your accountant's call, so keep the invoice that lists each item.
  • Flood space. In a shore storefront at or below the design flood elevation, the recorder goes on a high shelf or upstairs office, not under the counter, in line with Appendix G's approach to equipment.

Most island business owners pair the recorder with a full commercial camera layout or lot cameras for the shared parking. We quote them together when the cable routes overlap.

Your Questions Answered

NVR Installation on Staten Island: Questions Answered

Island recorder questions fall into five groups, and each group below starts from something that actually happens here — a flooded basement, a car checked at dawn, a shared driveway, a call to the precinct, a busy strip-center lot. Price, channel counts and drive sizes have their own sections further down.

Water and Salt on the Shore

The recorder sat in water after the storm — is it safe to switch it back on to see whether the video survived?

No. Data-recovery firms give the same advice: a wet hard drive should not be powered up, because current on a damp circuit board can short it and a drive spinning with water inside can scratch the platters that hold the video. Unplug the recorder at the wall once it is safe to reach, leave it off, and call us before anyone tries to boot it to see if it still works.

We remove the drive, keep it in a sealed bag, and tell you honestly whether the footage is worth a professional recovery. If the flood brought something you need on video — a break-in during the storm, or a claim that depends on when the water arrived — a lab can often recover drives that were never powered on after getting wet. If it does not matter, we skip the lab and install a new drive in a new recorder on a higher floor.

The July 14, 2025 storm that turned Hylan Boulevard into a river also flooded several basements on Poplar Avenue in Rossville, according to the Advance. That is the kind of afternoon when a recorder on a basement shelf stops recording at exactly the moment you want it, and the kind of morning after when someone is tempted to plug it back in.

We live a few blocks from the beach in Midland Beach — does the salt air reach the recorder, or only the cameras?

Mostly the cameras and the connections outdoors. The recorder lives indoors, and in a normal heated room it does not see enough salt to matter. Outdoors, salt and damp attack bare copper and cheap steel first: the network plug at the back of a camera, an unsealed junction box, the screws in a bracket. Those are the parts we protect, with sealed connector boots, stainless fixings and drip loops, and by keeping every splice indoors.

On a pier, a marina building or a yard right on the water, we look at cameras built for corrosive air, with stainless-steel or treated housings. On a house a few blocks back, a good sealed camera is usually enough, as long as it is mounted under an eave rather than on a bare face that takes the spray.

The one indoor exception is a recorder in an unheated space that breathes outside air, like a porch closet or a shed. Damp, salty air in a cold room condenses on the electronics. That is another reason the recorder belongs inside the heated house.

Driveways, Cars and the Stoop

Someone tries car door handles on our block before dawn — why does the recorder's night video show a blur instead of a face?

Because most recorders and cameras leave the factory set for daytime pictures that look good on a phone, not for a person walking quickly through infrared light at 4 a.m. At night the camera slows its shutter to gather light, so anyone moving smears; the infrared bounces off a nearby wall or the hood of the car and washes out the face; and the recorder saves a compressed stream that throws away detail in the dark.

We fix this camera by camera: a faster shutter limit at night, infrared or white-light settings matched to the distance, the camera aimed so the driveway is in the middle of the frame and not the bright street light, and the recorder set to keep the full-quality main stream for the driveway and front-door cameras. We then walk the driveway after dark and look at the recording on the recorder itself, not just the live view.

The Advance has published owners' night footage from island streets more than once: in Annadale in 2017 a group was recorded trying car doors and taking from unlocked cars, and in Mariners Harbor in 2022 a camera on Simonson Avenue caught someone at parked cars around 4:30 a.m. Footage like that is only useful when a face or a gait can be made out.

A package went missing from the stoop — can the recorder show exactly when it arrived and who took it?

Usually, if the stoop camera records continuously or the recorder logs every person event. Start with the carrier's delivery time, then use the recorder's event search for people on the stoop camera in the hours after it. Most current recorders can filter to person events only, which turns a day of footage into a short list of clips: the driver putting the box down, and whoever picked it up.

For a stoop we usually suggest two views: a wide camera that shows the path and the street, so you can see which way someone came and went, and a closer camera or a doorbell camera that shows the face at the door. A dual-lens camera does both from one mount. A clip that shows the arrival, the pick-up and the direction of travel is far more use to the carrier, the seller and the precinct than a single blurry still.

Export both clips — the delivery and the theft — with the camera name and time, and keep them together. Sellers and carriers increasingly ask for the delivery itself before they refund or resend.

Two-Families and Neighbors

We rent the downstairs of our two-family — can the recorder cover the shared side door and the tenant's entrance?

Generally, a camera on the shared entrance, the driveway and the yard you control is ordinary home security; a camera that looks inside the tenant's apartment is not. New York's unlawful surveillance law is aimed at secretly recording people in places like bedrooms and bathrooms, and nothing we install points into anyone's rooms. We keep each camera's view to the outside of the doors, and we use privacy masks on the recorder if a window falls into the frame.

Tell the tenant in writing which cameras exist and what they cover, and put it in the lease at renewal. It costs nothing, it prevents arguments, and it makes the footage easier to use if you ever need it. Leave audio off on any camera near the tenant's door; recording conversations you are not part of raises a separate issue under New York's eavesdropping law.

If the tenant wants to see the camera at their own door, the recorder can give them a view-only login limited to that one camera. They cannot see the rest of the house, play back other cameras or change settings. This page is general information, not legal advice; for a lease question, ask a lawyer.

On our semi-attached block the driveway is shared — how do we keep the camera from recording the neighbors' comings and goings?

Aim the camera down the middle of the drive at your own car and your own door, and let the recorder black out the rest. Most current recorders have privacy masks: you draw a box over the neighbor's front door, side door, windows and steps, and that part of the picture is never recorded, live or in playback. On a shared drive the camera will still see the neighbor's car pass through; what it should not see is who walks in and out of their house.

Show the neighbor the masked picture on your phone before we finish. Most neighbors on semi-attached blocks are glad of a camera on the shared drive once they see their door is blacked out, and some ask for a view-only login to that one camera, which the recorder can give without exposing your other cameras or any playback controls you do not want them to have.

Aim the shared camera at the driveway itself and mask both front doors and any windows, so neither family is recording the other's comings and goings. New York's Civil Rights Law lets an owner or tenant sue someone who places a camera on adjoining property to record their backyard without written consent, with intent to harass, annoy, alarm or threaten — a shared, agreed driveway camera is the opposite of that, but keep it off both backyards anyway.

A neighbor's car was broken into and they want our footage — should we give it to them?

You can, and it is often the decent thing to do, but give a copy, not access. Export the minutes they need to a USB stick or send a clip from the app, and keep the original export yourself. Do not give out your recorder login or let anyone plug into the recorder, because a careless click can delete or overwrite days of footage.

If the neighbor has made a police report, it is better for the clip to go to the detective handling it, with the date, time and camera named, so the police have it from the source. Note whether the recorder's clock was right; a clip that is twenty minutes off is still useful if you say so.

Before you post a neighbor's incident on social media, think about who else is in the video. A clip of your own driveway is yours to share; a clip that shows other people's children walking to school or a neighbor's open front door is better kept to the police and the people involved.

Break-Ins, Precincts and Plate Readers

Our car was broken into overnight — what should we do on the recorder before we call the precinct?

Do not reboot the recorder, change its settings or try to fix the camera that missed the person. First, write down what the recorder's clock says compared with your phone. Second, play back from an hour before to an hour after on every camera that faces the street or the driveway, and lock or export those recordings. Third, call the precinct and tell them you have video and from which cameras.

Island precincts are large and each covers several neighborhoods. The 120th covers the North Shore, from St. George and Stapleton to West Brighton; the 121st covers the northwestern shore including Port Richmond, Mariners Harbor and Bloomfield; the 122nd, on Hylan Boulevard in New Dorp, covers New Dorp, Midland Beach, Great Kills and Todt Hill among others; the 123rd covers the far South Shore, including Tottenville, Huguenot and Annadale. Knowing which one answers for your block saves time on the phone.

After the report, keep the export somewhere other than the recorder, and check how many days the recorder actually holds. If it only keeps a week, the clip from last Saturday will be gone by next Monday unless it was saved.

Is a Staten Island business allowed to run its own plate-reader camera, and who owns what it records?

As of this writing, yes. The Advance reported in September 2026 that several island businesses run privately owned plate-reader cameras, including one outside a business on Forest Avenue in Mariners Harbor, and that data from privately owned cameras of that kind belongs to the owner, who can choose whether to share it with police. The same reporting noted that New York has no statewide law in force that comprehensively governs police use of plate readers, and that a 2026 state bill would add a new section to the General Business Law regulating them.

Ownership depends on how the camera is bought. A plate camera recording to your own NVR keeps the plates in your back office, and you decide who gets a copy. A subscription plate-reader service stores them on the provider's servers, and the provider's terms decide how long they are kept and who else can search them. Read those terms before you sign.

If what you need is to see which car pulled into your lot when the store was hit, a plate-capture camera on your own recorder, aimed at your lot entrance rather than the public road, usually does that job without joining any network. We can set one up and leave the data where it is.

Strip Centers, Offices and the West Shore

Our store closes at nine but the strip-center lot stays busy until midnight — how should the recorder treat the hours after we lock up?

Give the recorder a schedule that matches the store. While you are open, the inside cameras and the register record continuously and alerts stay off, because staff and customers would set them off all day. After closing, the recorder can switch the door and rear cameras to person detection with alerts to the owner's phone, keep the lot cameras recording continuously, and turn on a camera light or a recorded warning at the back door if someone lingers.

The busy lot is why we do not set lot cameras to motion-only after hours: cars and headlights would trigger them constantly, and the one event you need would be buried. Continuous recording on the lot, person alerts only at your own doors, is the combination that works on Hylan Boulevard and Richmond Avenue strips.

If the store has an alarm, its arming time can tell the recorder to change modes, so nobody has to remember. We test the switch-over on the install night, after closing.

Our West Shore warehouse yard is too big for fixed cameras — when does a pan-tilt-zoom camera make sense on the recorder?

When someone will actually drive it, or when it can follow a schedule of preset views. A PTZ camera records only where it is pointing, so on its own it misses most of what happens in a large yard. Paired with fixed cameras that always cover the gates, the dock doors and the fence line, it becomes the camera that zooms in on a plate, a trailer number or a face when a guard or manager sees something.

On the West Shore, around the Teleport and the logistics parks off Route 440, we set PTZ cameras on a patrol of preset positions overnight, return to the gate view after any manual use, and record their stream at full quality. The recorder needs the channels and the drive space to take them as well as the fixed cameras.

If nobody watches live, spend the money on more fixed cameras first. A yard covered by fixed views with no gaps beats one big camera looking the wrong way.

What an East Shore outage does to a recorder

A recorder that loses power mid-write can corrupt the file it was saving, and some models come back with the clock reset, the cameras waiting to reconnect, or a drive the recorder no longer recognises until someone intervenes. A voltage reduction like the 8 percent cut Con Edison made on the island's east side in July 2025 is a different stress: the lights stay on, but cheap power adapters and PoE switches can drop out while the recorder keeps running, so cameras go dark one by one.

The cure is the same for both. A battery backup sized to carry the recorder, the PoE switch and the modem through a short outage; the recorder set to take its time from the internet as soon as the modem is back; camera-offline alerts sent to the owner's phone; and a quick check of the playback calendar after any storm. On a house where outages run longer than a battery can bridge, we keep the battery on the recording equipment only, so its runtime is spent on the video rather than on the Wi-Fi in the living room.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About NVR Installation on Staten Island

We were not able to load Google's AI-generated summary for island recorder searches while preparing this page, so nothing here quotes it. What follows are seven statements that turn up again and again in cost articles, product blogs and online answers, each held up against published sources and what island houses actually do to a recorder.

"Put the NVR in the basement — it is out of the way and the cables are all down there."

This is the most common advice in DIY guides and forum answers, and on much of Staten Island it is wrong. The basement is where the cables are easiest to collect, which is why installers like it, and it is also where the water goes. On a raised house the old basement may now be a flood-vented crawl space that is designed to take water.

The city's flood-resistant construction rules point the same way: equipment at or above the design flood elevation, or built to keep water out. A recorder, a switch and a battery are not built to keep water out. The July 2025 storm that flooded basements in Rossville did not need a hurricane to do it.

Our rule on the island: the recorder goes on the first living floor or higher unless the basement is finished, dry, above the flood elevation and has stayed dry through the storms you remember. The extra cable to get there costs far less than a recorder and a drive full of lost video.

"Any 4K camera will catch a face or a license plate at night."

Resolution is not the problem at night. Most island footage that fails does so because the camera slowed its shutter in the dark and the person or car smeared, or because the infrared bounced off a car hood or a white wall and washed out the face. A 4K camera with factory settings does both of those things.

Plates are harder still. Reading one takes a narrow field of view, a fast shutter and infrared tuned to the reflective plate — a different camera from the wide one that covers your driveway. Nothing on the recorder can add detail the camera did not capture.

So we tune night settings camera by camera, and if plates matter we add one dedicated camera at the point where cars slow down. Then we check the recording after dark before we call the job finished.

"A cloud camera subscription is cheaper than buying an NVR."

For one or two cameras it can be, for a while. Cost guides give wide ranges for professional camera installation — Angi puts a typical professional security camera project at roughly $590 to $2,040, and HomeAdvisor's national average sits around $1,289 — and one 2026 guide lists $100 to $500 for the recorder itself and $3 to $30 a month per camera for cloud plans.

Do the arithmetic for a two-family with six cameras: per-camera monthly plans add up every year for as long as you own the house, and the video lives on someone else's servers under their retention rules. An NVR is paid for once, keeps the video in your house, and records even when the internet is down.

Neither number is a Staten Island price. Ours are in the pricing table below, with the island's area factor already applied and every line item listed.

"The recorder keeps a month of video automatically."

Retention depends on the drives, the number of cameras, the resolution, the frame rate and how much movement each camera sees. A recorder with eight 4K cameras on one modest drive may hold a week or less. A Bronx law firm's guide to preserving footage says store cameras “many overwrite in 7 to 10 days”, and that is a fair warning for houses too.

That matters on the island because problems are often discovered late: a car rummaged on Tuesday night is noticed on Thursday, a package dispute surfaces a week later. If the recorder only keeps six days, the evidence is already gone.

We set a retention target with you, size the drives to reach it, and then check the real number of days on the recorder a week after installation. If it falls short, we adjust the recording settings or add a drive.

"Private plate readers are illegal in New York."

They are not, as of this writing. The Advance's September 2026 reporting described privately owned plate-reader cameras at island businesses, with the data belonging to the camera's owner, and noted that New York has no statewide statute in force that comprehensively governs police use of plate readers. A 2026 bill would add a General Business Law section regulating them; it was a proposal, not law.

That does not mean anything goes. Aiming a plate camera up and down a public street and storing every passing car for months is a different thing from capturing the vehicles that enter your own driveway or lot. The second is ordinary security; the first invites the privacy questions the island is already arguing about.

We install plate-capture cameras for driveways, gates and lot entrances, recording to the owner's own recorder, and we do not sign customers up to plate-sharing networks. If the law changes, the setup can change with it.

"It is your property, so you can point cameras wherever you like."

Mostly, but not entirely. New York's Civil Rights Law gives an owner or tenant a right to sue anyone who puts a video device on adjoining property to record their backyard without written consent, with intent to harass, annoy, alarm or threaten. Penal Law §250.45 makes it a crime to secretly record people in places like bedrooms and bathrooms. Recording conversations without a party's consent is eavesdropping.

On a semi-attached block or a two-family, those rules are why we aim at your own driveway, doors and yard, use privacy masks over neighbors' windows and doors, keep every camera off the inside of a tenant's apartment, and leave audio off by default.

None of this stops a homeowner from protecting a house. It decides where the camera points and what the recorder keeps.

"NVR installation costs the same anywhere in the city."

National cost guides publish one range for the whole country, and some local installers publish one price for all five boroughs. Our published prices follow an area factor: Brooklyn and the Bronx are the base, and Staten Island carries a factor of 1.10 on the base rates, which reflects the bridge, the distances and the house-by-house work.

Field Notes: Find the Water Line First

On an island job my first question is not how many cameras. It is how high the water came. On the East and South Shore people know the answer to the inch, and it decides where the recorder goes before anything else. If the house was raised, I go up to the living floor and look for a closet with an inside wall, away from the bathroom plumbing and the boiler flue, close enough to the chase that the camera cables can come straight up.

Then I go outside after the owner tells me what has happened on the block. It is nearly always the driveway, the car or the stoop. I stand where the car is parked and look back at the house: where would a camera see a face, not a hood? Where does the street light flood the lens? Is the neighbor's bedroom window in the frame?

Only after that do we talk about channels and brands. A recorder in a dry room on a battery, with night settings checked on the recording and a clip exported once in front of the owner, will give you the video on the morning you need it. An expensive one on a basement shelf will not.
