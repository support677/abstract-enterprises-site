The Neighborhood

Why Cadman Plaza Properties Need TV Installation

Cadman Plaza is a park before it is a neighborhood. The Wikipedia entry and NYC Parks describe the long green between Cadman Plaza East and Cadman Plaza West, running from the Brooklyn Bridge approach down toward Borough Hall, on land the city condemned in 1935 and named in 1939 for the Reverend Samuel Parkes Cadman, the radio preacher who led Central Congregational Church for 36 years. The Cultural Landscape Foundation puts the park at about 10.4 acres. What gives the name to an address, though, is the ring of buildings around that green, and for anyone hanging a television those buildings fall into a few very different families. On the west side are the co-op towers and townhouses of the Cadman Plaza urban renewal project. On the east and south are federal and state courts, Borough Hall and a cluster of office towers. At the north end of Cadman Plaza West is One Clinton, the condo that rose over the rebuilt Brooklyn Heights library.

The residential side is where most of our calls come from, and its history explains its walls. The Center for Brooklyn History's finding aid for the Cadman Plaza complex lists the buildings between Cadman Plaza West and Henry Street: Cadman Plaza North at 140 Cadman Plaza West, Whitman Close at 75 Henry Street, and Cadman Towers at 101 Clark Street and 10 Clinton Street, all built in the late 1960s on the site of the old Brooklyn Bridge trolley terminal under the Mitchell-Lama middle-income program. CityRealty records 140 Cadman Plaza West as a 27-floor, 250-unit co-op completed in 1967. NYC Urbanism's history of Cadman Towers says it was begun in 1969 and finished in 1971 to designs by Glass & Glass with Whittlesey & Conklin, with a 32-story and a 13-story tower, about 420 co-op apartments and 50 duplex townhouses placed along the streets to keep a human scale, all of it in saw-toothed poured concrete. Those are not walls a stud finder understands.

That one fact changes most of the job. In a brownstone a few blocks south, a TV usually goes on plaster over lath or plaster over brick, and the cables can often be fished inside a framed partition. In a tower of cast concrete, the walls between rooms and between apartments may be solid concrete, concrete block or a later drywall partition, and the only way to know which is to look, knock and drill one small test hole where the bracket plate will cover it. Concrete holds a television superbly once the anchor is right, but SlashGear's guide to mounting on concrete puts the catch plainly: "you may run into rebar, a steel bar that strengthens concrete." When a bit meets steel in a tower wall, the right move is almost always to shift the bracket a little rather than cut through reinforcement in a building you do not own, and that is a decision the technician makes with you standing there.

The buildings are also changing hands. THE CITY reported in April 2024 that Cadman Towers, carrying about $62 million in debt, became the first Mitchell-Lama co-op approved by the City Council to convert to a Housing Development Fund Corporation, with more than three-quarters of shareholders in favor; Habitat magazine put the vote at 77 percent. Under the new rules, resales are capped at prices affordable to households at 80 percent of area median income, sellers give half of their profit to the building, the old waitlist was replaced by a Housing Connect lottery, and buyers must earn no more than 125 percent of area median income. For us that means new shareholders moving in, unpacking new televisions and deciding for the first time where a screen goes in an apartment whose walls they have never drilled. It also means co-op boards that pay close attention to what contractors do inside units, because a cracked wall or a mess in a hallway is the building's problem as much as the shareholder's.

Just north of Clark Street, the Whitman Close townhouses at 75 Henry Street add a different shape of home. Brownstoner's 2012 Building of the Day described about sixteen two-story units designed by William Conklin and built between 1967 and 1973, meant to echo the brownstones of the Heights beside a 33-story tower of about 370 apartments. CityRealty's later review of 75 Henry Street counts 32 stories and 352 apartments, lists a full-time doorman and a garage, and the sources disagree on the exact figures, so we quote both. A duplex townhouse means a private stair, a living room on one level and bedrooms on the other, and for a TV that means two floors of wiring questions inside one household rather than one.

Then there is the newest building in the ring. The Brooklyn Eagle reported in May 2022 that One Clinton at 280 Cadman Plaza West was nearing completion as a 36-story tower of 134 condos by the Hudson Companies and Marvel Architects, with a new library and a Department of Education STEM center at its base; CityRealty counts 133 apartments, describes "ceiling heights of at least 9 feet," white oak floors, a washer and dryer in every unit and a multi-zone heating and cooling system. CBS New York reported that the library below opened on June 8, 2022. A building of that generation is where we expect smooth drywall partitions, an outer wall of glass and masonry, heating and cooling equipment that has to stay clear, and a condo office that wants paperwork before a contractor walks in.

The east side of the park is a different city. The Federal Building and Post Office at 271–301 Cadman Plaza East, a Romanesque landmark from 1885–91 that now houses the bankruptcy court, faces the 15-story Theodore Roosevelt United States Courthouse at 225 Cadman Plaza East, finished in 2006 to a design by César Pelli. South of them stand Borough Hall, completed in 1848, the Supreme Court building at 360 Adams Street and the office towers of Court Street, including the 35-story Montague–Court Building at 16 Court Street from 1927. We do not work inside the courthouses, but the law offices, title companies, nonprofits and professional suites in the blocks around them buy conference screens and waiting-room displays, and those jobs follow office-building rules rather than co-op ones.

Six Cadman Plaza facts that shape a TV job before a bracket is chosen

  • The tallest residential walls are cast concrete. NYC Urbanism describes Cadman Towers as saw-toothed poured concrete with large windowless concrete walls. A hammer drill, concrete bits and anchors set to depth replace the screws and toggles of a drywall job, and nothing can be fished inside the wall.
  • The co-op towers are half a century old. 140 Cadman Plaza West dates from 1967 and Cadman Towers from 1971. Apartments that age have often had partitions moved, closets opened and drywall added, so one living room can hold two kinds of wall.
  • About 50 duplex townhouses sit at the tower bases. That is NYC Urbanism's count for Cadman Towers, plus roughly sixteen Whitman Close units on Henry Street by Brownstoner's. A duplex puts the cable entry, the router and the TV on different floors of the same home.
  • Apartments are being resold under new rules. The 2024 HDFC conversion replaced the waitlist with a Housing Connect lottery, according to THE CITY, so more first-time owners are setting up living rooms in units built in the early 1970s.
  • The newest tower sets ceilings at nine feet or more. CityRealty's description of One Clinton gives at least nine feet, which leaves room for a larger screen than most 1960s apartments can take without the viewers looking up.
  • Half the address is offices and courts. The Roosevelt courthouse, the Federal Building, the Supreme Court at 360 Adams Street and the Court Street towers make conference displays and waiting-room screens a real share of the work here, mostly scheduled outside business hours.

Same-Day Repair in Cadman Plaza

Need Repair in Cadman Plaza? A Screen Sagging Off a Concrete Wall, Anchors Spinning in an Old Partition, or a Bracket Left Behind by the Last Shareholder

The urgent calls we get from towers like the ones on Cadman Plaza West tend to come in three kinds. The first is a TV that has dropped at one corner or started to lean forward. On a concrete wall that usually means the anchors were too short or the holes were drilled oversize, so the fixing is gripping only the outer skin of the concrete, which is often harder and more brittle than the core. On a drywall partition added during a renovation, it usually means the bracket was screwed into drywall alone with plastic plugs, or into a thin metal stud with a wood screw that has slowly stripped. The second kind is the apartment someone has just bought: the previous owner's bracket is still on the wall, there are four or six old holes beside it, and there may be a cord pushed into a hole that should never have been there. The third is the office near the courts where a display was hung on a full-motion arm in a corridor or waiting room, and the arm is now pulling the wall plate away.

If a screen is tipping, do not try to push it back up. If two people can lift it safely, take the weight off the bracket, set the TV face-up on a blanket, unplug it and call (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list. When the same TV is going back on the wall properly, the visit is priced as a new mount on that wall, with the take-down added, and we tell you before starting whether the old holes can be reused, filled or avoided. If what we find is a problem with the wall itself, a crack running out from the old anchors, a spalled patch of concrete or a partition that was never fixed at the top, we say so plainly so you can bring in the super or the managing agent before anything heavy goes back up.

The cast-concrete co-op tower: a wall that holds anything, once you know where the steel is

The towers of Cadman Towers are the clearest case on this page. NYC Urbanism describes poured concrete inside and out, laid out in a saw-tooth so the apartments get angled windows and corners rather than one flat facade. Inside an apartment, that shows up as walls that ring dull and hard when you knock them, outlets set in boxes cast or cut into the concrete, and corners that are not always ninety degrees. A TV on a concrete wall is very secure if the anchor is right: we drill to the depth the anchor needs, clean the hole, set the anchor and torque it to the maker's figure, and we keep the holes clear of edges and of the corners where the wall meets the floor slab. What we will not do is fight the wall. If the test hole meets reinforcing steel, the bracket moves a little sideways or the anchor pattern changes; cutting rebar in a co-op tower is not a TV installer's call and almost never needs to happen.

Concrete also decides the cable plan. There is no hollow inside a cast wall for an in-wall kit, so the choice is between a paintable cord cover from the screen down to the outlet, a screen placed directly above a console so the cables drop straight behind it, or a different wall altogether. Many 1970s apartments have at least one drywall partition, often around a closet or a room that was divided later, and if that partition faces the sofa it may be the better TV wall because the cables can go inside it. We show you both options on the day, with the price of each, before the drill comes out. And because sound carries through a concrete frame, we keep the drilling short and in one burst; the neighbors two floors down may hear a hammer drill for a few minutes, not an afternoon.

The 1967 slab on Cadman Plaza West: older apartments, more renovations, more surprises

Cadman Plaza North at 140 Cadman Plaza West is the oldest tower in the complex, completed in 1967 with 27 floors and 250 apartments by CityRealty's count, and CityRealty lists no doorman. Half a century of shareholders means half a century of changes inside the units. In apartments of this age we routinely find a mix on one living-room wall line: the original masonry or concrete along the outside and around the shafts, an original partition between rooms, and a newer drywall wall where someone enlarged a kitchen or closed in a dining alcove. Each takes a different fixing, and the scanner is only the start. The test hole hidden behind the bracket plate is what tells us whether the wall in front of the sofa is solid masonry, a hollow partition or drywall fixed to something behind it.

The other thing about a building without a doorman is delivery. A 65-inch screen that sits in the lobby for an afternoon is a risk, so we suggest having the TV delivered to the apartment itself on a day someone is home, or booking the installation for the day after it arrives and keeping it in its carton until the technician comes. The carton is the safest way to carry a large screen through a lobby, an elevator and a corridor, and keeping it lets us check the screen for shipping damage before anything is fixed to the wall.

The duplex townhouse: two levels, one household and a cable that comes in at the wrong end

The duplexes at the base of Cadman Towers and in Whitman Close were placed along the streets so the complex would feel like part of the Heights; Brownstoner described the Whitman Close units as two-story homes meant to echo the brownstones around them. Inside, they live like small houses. The provider's line usually arrives at one point, often on the lower level, and the router sits wherever the installer left it. A TV on the upper level then depends on a wireless signal that has to climb through a concrete floor, and a TV on the lower level may sit on a party wall shared with the next duplex. We look at where the line comes in and where the router is before choosing a wall, because a screen in the perfect spot that buffers every evening is not a finished job.

The walls inside these townhouses are not brownstone walls, whatever they look like from the walkway. They belong to the same late-1960s and early-1970s construction as the towers beside them, so we expect concrete or block on the outer and party walls and framed partitions inside. A framed partition can take the $169 in-wall power and cable kit if the co-op allows a wall to be opened; a masonry wall cannot, and the cables either go behind a paintable cord cover or drop into the console. Stair walls are a special case. The wall beside a private stair may look like the obvious place for a bedroom-level TV, but it often carries the stair stringer or a handrail fixing, so we scan it carefully and keep the screen clear of anyone climbing past it.

The doorman tower on Henry Street: a desk that signs you in and a board that owns the shops

75 Henry Street is the tall tower of Whitman Close, completed in 1973 to designs by Glass & Glass with Whittlesey & Conklin, by CityRealty's description, with a full-time doorman and a garage. Brooklyn Paper reported in 2016 that the co-op owns the Pineapple Walk strip between Cadman Plaza West and Henry Street, with the Park Plaza Restaurant and other stores, and that its shareholders voted 191 to 112 against a proposed tower deal on that land. A building with a desk makes a TV visit easier in one way and stricter in another. The doorman can receive the carton and let the technician in, but the building will also expect the technician's name ahead of time, the certificate of insurance on file and the work done inside its contractor hours.

Inside the apartments, we expect the same family of walls as the other towers of the complex: concrete or masonry where the structure is, partitions between rooms, and renovated drywall where owners have opened or closed rooms. Larger apartments in a tower like this often have a long living-room wall on the window side and a shorter one opposite. The window-side wall puts the screen against the light, so in most layouts we suggest the wall facing the windows, with a tilting bracket if the screen has to sit a little high. We mark the outline with painter's tape and ask you to sit where you watch before anything is drilled.

The new condo over the library: smooth walls, a glass side and a condo office that reads the paperwork

One Clinton is a different generation. The Brooklyn Eagle described a 36-story tower of 134 condos by Marvel Architects, and CityRealty lists ceilings of at least nine feet, seven-inch-wide white oak floors and a multi-zone heating and cooling system in each apartment. Buildings of this age usually have drywall interior partitions, sometimes on light-gauge steel studs, and an outer wall that is largely glass. That combination leaves fewer places for a screen than the floor plan suggests, and the one solid wall facing the sofa may be a partition that hides ducts, a heating and cooling unit or a riser. We scan for all of it, and if the best-looking wall is hiding equipment, we show you why another position is safer.

Two details are specific to a new condo. First, the floors: wide white oak scratches easily, so drop cloths and blankets go down before a ladder or a carton touches the floor, and drill dust is caught at the hole rather than swept afterwards. Second, the condo office: most will want the certificate of insurance, the date and the name of the technician, and some will ask whether any wall is being opened. An in-wall power and cable kit at $169 puts a recessed outlet behind the screen and a power inlet near the floor, using the kit's own rated cable rather than the TV's cord, and it is usually accepted when installed that way. If the office says no to any opening, the paintable cord cover at $59 needs only a few small screws.

The office suite near the courts: the landlord's wall, the firm's screen and the hours in between

The office towers south of the park, from the Montague–Court Building at 16 Court Street, which Wikipedia lists as 35 stories and 462 feet, to the buildings along Adams and Joralemon Streets around the Supreme Court at 360 Adams Street, are leased space. A law firm, a title company or a nonprofit may want a conference-room screen for video calls and documents, a reception display or a training room TV, but the wall belongs to the landlord, the ceiling is usually a dropped grid owned by the building, and the network closet may be shared. Before an office job we confirm in writing who has approved the wall work, when the room can be used without clients in it, and who needs the certificate of insurance.

Office walls are usually drywall on metal studs with a dropped ceiling above, which makes cable routing easier than in the residential towers: cables can often travel above the ceiling tiles to a wall plate behind the screen, as long as they are rated for that space and the landlord allows it. The bracket still has to be fixed properly, because a thin steel stud will strip a wood screw, and in a corridor or a waiting room the screen has to stay out of the walking path. Our list prices an office TV the same way as a home one, by the wall and the extras: the $179.99 starting price is written for drywall on wood studs, a soundbar fixed under the screen is $129, and because steel studs are not a separate line on this list, we confirm the price for a steel-framed office wall from your photos before booking rather than adding a figure on the day.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The First Fifteen Minutes in a Cadman Plaza Tower Apartment

These notes describe how our technicians work in concrete towers like the ones on Cadman Plaza West. They are our standing practice, not a story about any one apartment or shareholder.

Sign in, then look. At a desk building the technician signs in, confirms the apartment and the elevator, and checks that the building has our certificate of insurance on file. Inside, before any tool comes out, he walks the room: which walls are on the window side, which wall is shared with the corridor, where the outlets and the cable or fiber jack are, and whether a heating and cooling unit, a thermostat or a riser cover sits near the wall you chose.

Knock, then scan. On a cast wall a knock sounds flat and dead; on a partition it sounds hollow or slightly drummy. A scanner on deep mode picks up framing, metal studs and sometimes the conduit that feeds an outlet, but on concrete it will not show rebar reliably, so the technician treats a clean scan on concrete as a hint, not a promise.

One test hole, where the bracket will hide it. A small bit goes in first, inside the outline of the bracket plate. Concrete gives fine grey powder and steady resistance; block gives grey grit and then a sudden give into a hollow core; drywall gives white dust and then air or a stud. If the bit stops dead on metal, the technician stops too and talks to you about moving the bracket an inch or two.

Tape, then sit. Painter's tape marks the outline of the screen at the height you want. You sit where you watch, and the technician checks the angle from the sofa and from the doorway, and looks at where the afternoon light from the windows lands. Moving tape costs nothing.

Drill once, catch the dust. The drilling for the bracket is done in one short burst, with a vacuum nozzle held at the bit so concrete dust does not settle on the floor or drift into the corridor. Then the noisy part of the visit is over, and the rest, hanging, leveling, cables and setup, is quiet.

Wall by Wall

Concrete, Block, Partition or Brick: What Holds a TV in a Cadman Plaza Wall and What Changes the Price

The starting price on our Brooklyn list, $179.99, covers a TV up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs, labor only, with the bracket sold separately. That is a common wall in a renovated room, but it is not the only wall here, and it is rarely the wall in an unrenovated tower apartment. Each line below exists because that material needs different tools, different fixings and more time.

Poured concrete (priced from your photos). The walls of the cast towers are dense, heavy and very strong in every direction, which is good news for a large screen. The work is in the drilling: a hammer drill, a bit matched to the anchor, a hole cleaned of dust before the anchor goes in, and enough distance from any edge that the concrete cannot break out under load. The risk is reinforcing steel. If the test hole meets it, the bracket or the anchor pattern shifts rather than the steel being cut. Our list has no separate line for poured concrete, so we confirm the price for a concrete wall from your photos before you book, and that price does not change on the day unless the wall turns out to be something else.

Concrete block (priced from your photos). Some walls in buildings of this period are block rather than cast concrete, especially around corridors and shafts. Block has thin solid faces with hollow cores behind them, and an anchor that lands in a core holds very little. We place fixings in the solid webs between the cores, or use anchors made for hollow block, and on a full-motion arm we use every fixing point the wall plate offers, because an arm pulled out from the wall levers its weight onto the top anchors.

Brick or stone (+$65). The older brick walk-ups and row buildings on Henry, Clark and Middagh Streets beside the complex have brick party and front walls under their plaster, and stone appears on fireplace surrounds. Brick is priced as a $65 add-on. The anchor goes into the brick itself, not the softer mortar joint, and we keep holes back from the edges of each brick.

Plaster (+$45). Plaster turns up in the older buildings around the complex and sometimes over masonry in the towers themselves. On our list it is a $45 add-on, because it needs slower drilling, different anchors and more care around cracks. When the test hole shows plaster sitting directly on brick, the wall is priced by what is behind the plaster, which we confirm with you before continuing.

Drywall on wood studs (the base price). Found in renovated rooms, closets that were moved and some newer partitions. The bracket goes into two studs where they fall in the right place, and the wall can take the in-wall power and cable kit. In buildings of this age the studs are not always on modern spacing, so we find them one at a time.

Drywall on steel studs (priced from your photos). Likely in a building of One Clinton's generation and in the office towers. A thin steel stud strips a wood screw, so the fixing is a toggle or anchor made for steel and rated for the load, spread across two studs. Steel studs are not a separate line on our Brooklyn list, so we price the wall from your photos.

Hiding the wires when the wall is solid

The request that follows "mount it" is nearly always "and make the wires disappear," and in a concrete tower the honest answer is that the wall will not hide them. The National Electrical Code does not allow flexible cords, including a TV's power cord, to be run concealed inside walls, floors or ceilings. On a framed wall the proper way to hide power is an in-wall power and cable kit: a recessed inlet low on the wall plugs into the existing outlet, the kit's own rated cable carries power inside the wall, and a recessed outlet sits behind the TV with a separate pass-through for HDMI. On our list that is $169. It needs a hollow framed cavity between the two openings, so it suits drywall and framed partitions and does not suit cast concrete, block or solid brick.

On a solid wall there are three good answers. The first is a paintable cord cover, $59 on our list, which runs from the screen down to the outlet on the surface and can be painted to match the wall. The second is placement: a TV fixed directly above a console, with the outlet behind the console, lets the cables drop straight down behind the furniture so the cover is short or not needed at all. The third is choosing a different wall, a framed partition across the room, where the in-wall kit is possible. We price each one for your room before the visit, so you can decide which matters more, the position or the clean wall.

Boards and Buildings

Co-op Boards, Condo Offices and Managing Agents: Who Has a Say Before a TV Goes on a Cadman Plaza Wall

Almost every residential building on this page is owned collectively. Cadman Towers and Cadman Plaza North are co-ops, Whitman Close is a co-op that also owns the Pineapple Walk shops, and One Clinton is a condominium. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice, but we have learned what these buildings tend to ask about a TV, and it is easier to get the answer before the visit than at the door.

Start with the alteration agreement or house rules. New York co-ops and condos use an alteration agreement to govern work inside apartments; Brick Underground, Habitat and Hauseit have all published guides to reading one. Most buildings treat hanging a picture or a TV bracket as minor work that needs no formal application, but many treat opening a wall, touching electrical wiring or working near a riser as an alteration that needs approval. An in-wall power and cable kit falls in between: it opens two small holes in a partition and uses its own cord rather than the building's wiring. Ask the managing agent which category it falls into in your building, and we will send whatever they need.

Expect contractor hours and a certificate of insurance. Buildings like these commonly allow contractor work only on weekday daytime hours and want a certificate of insurance naming the corporation and the managing agent before anyone starts. Tell us the exact names the office wants on the certificate, and we send it before the visit. If the building wants the elevator padded or reserved, we need to know a day ahead.

Think about the next owner. With Cadman Towers now reselling apartments through a lottery under its HDFC rules, and shareholders in the other towers selling on the open market, the TV you mount today may become someone else's wall. Four or six neat anchor holes in concrete, filled and painted, are easy for the next owner to live with. A cord pushed through a wall, a bracket held by plastic plugs or a cut into a riser cover are not. Subtenants and renters. Compliance notes for Cadman Plaza offices and shops

  • Keep screens out of the walking path. The 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design say wall-mounted objects with a leading edge between 27 and 80 inches above the floor may not protrude more than 4 inches into a circulation path. A full-motion arm in a corridor or waiting room can break that rule once it is pulled out, so in public areas we use low-profile brackets or mount above 80 inches.
  • No power cords inside walls or above ceilings. The National Electrical Code does not allow flexible cords to be concealed in walls, floors or ceilings, and that includes running a TV's cord above ceiling tiles. Hidden power behind a business screen goes through a rated in-wall kit or stays on the surface in a cover.
  • Leave life-safety devices alone. Screens are not mounted where they would block an exit sign, a sprinkler head, a fire alarm device or a door swing, and cables stay off the floor in any route people use to leave the space.
  • Paperwork before tools. Office landlords and co-op landlords usually require a certificate of insurance naming the owner and the managing agent before work starts, and many set contractor hours. We send the certificate ahead of the visit.

Walls shaped by the towers

Our living room in Cadman Towers has an outer wall that zigzags with the building's saw-toothed shape. Can a TV hang on one of the angled sections?

Often yes, if the angled section is wide enough and faces the seating. The saw-tooth that NYC Urbanism describes gives these apartments short wall faces set at an angle to each other, usually with a window in one face and solid concrete in the next. A solid face that is wider than the TV can carry a fixed or tilting bracket like any concrete wall. The question is the view: a screen on an angled face points diagonally across the room, which works well when the sofa sits in the corner opposite and badly when it sits square to the main wall. If the face is narrower than the screen, or the sofa does not line up, a full-motion arm on the solid face can turn the screen square to the room, but an arm levers more load onto its anchors, so we use every fixing point on its wall plate. We tape the screen's outline on each candidate face and you sit and look before we choose.

The long wall in our tower living room is covered floor to ceiling in old mirror tiles. Can a TV go on that wall?

It can, but not by drilling through mirror, and the first decision is whether the mirror stays. Mirror tiles from that era are usually glued straight onto the wall, sometimes with small clips, and drilling through glass risks cracking a tile and leaving a sharp edge behind the screen. If you are happy to lose the tiles where the TV will go, the safe route is to take down only the tiles behind the bracket area, fix the bracket into the concrete or partition underneath, and hang the TV over the gap so the remaining mirror frames it. If the whole wall is coming down later anyway, it is easier to remove it before the visit. If the mirror must stay intact, the TV belongs on a different wall or on its stand. Removing tiles is not a line on our list, so we confirm it from photos before booking, and a mirror on the wall opposite the windows can also throw glare onto the screen, which we check on the day.

A concrete beam drops below the ceiling right above the wall we want. Does it limit how big or how high the TV can be?

It limits the top of the screen, not the bracket. Cast-concrete towers often have a beam or a lowered strip of ceiling running along a wall, and the bottom of that beam becomes the real ceiling for anything on the wall below it. We measure from the underside of the beam, leave room for the TV to be lifted onto its bracket, which on most brackets means a few inches of clearance above the screen, and then check that the center of the screen still lands at a comfortable height from your sofa. For a large screen that sometimes means the TV sits lower than you pictured, which is usually better for viewing anyway. We never fix into the beam itself; it is part of the building's structure and belongs to the co-op, and the wall below it is almost always a better fixing.

The previous shareholder left a built-in wall unit where the old TV stood. Can the new TV be mounted onto the unit itself?

Usually not onto the unit, but often through it. A built-in wall unit is cabinetry, and its back panel is typically thin sheet material that cannot carry a modern TV on a bracket. If the unit has an open bay where the old set lived, there are two good answers. If the bay is deep enough and the new screen fits it, the TV can stand inside the bay on its own feet, with the cables tidied through the cabinet. If you want the TV on the wall at that spot, the bracket has to reach the wall behind the unit, which means opening or removing the unit's back panel at that point and fixing into the concrete or partition behind it, then hanging the TV in front. We look at how the unit is built from your photos and tell you which is possible. Removing or altering cabinetry is not a line on our list, so if it is needed we confirm that price before booking.

Signal, access and the people who hold the keys

Our apartment is high in one of the towers but faces away from Manhattan. Will an indoor antenna still pull in the broadcast channels?

Sometimes, and it is worth trying before paying for a service you may not need. Broadcast reception in New York depends on the direction and the path to the transmitting towers in Manhattan, and a concrete apartment facing the other way puts the whole building between the antenna and the signal. An antenna placed in a window on the side of the apartment closest to Manhattan, if you have one, does far better than one behind the TV on a back wall. During a setup visit we place the antenna, run the TV's channel scan in each position and show you what comes in. If very little does, a streaming service or the provider's box is the realistic answer, and we connect whichever you choose. An antenna outside the window, on the facade or on a shared terrace is a question for the board, not for us.

Our co-op asks every contractor to protect the elevator and the hallway. What does that look like for one large TV?

It is simpler than a renovation, but it is real. For a TV visit the technician brings everything up in as few trips as possible, carries the screen in its carton, keeps the cart and the carton off the corridor walls, and does not leave tools or packaging in the hallway at any point. If your building pads the elevator for contractors, we need to know a day ahead so the super or the desk can hang the pads, and if the building reserves the service elevator, we book the arrival window around that reservation. Inside the apartment, blankets and drop cloths go down from the door to the TV wall before anything is set on the floor. When we leave, the packaging goes with us or to wherever your building tells us to take it, never left by the chute.

Our firm sublets one room inside another firm's suite on Court Street. Who has to agree before a screen goes on our wall?

Normally two parties besides you: the firm that holds the lease on the suite, and through them the building's owner or managing agent. Your sublease will usually say whether you may fix anything to the walls, and the main tenant's lease will say what the landlord must approve. The easiest path is to ask the main tenant to forward the request to the building with a short description: which wall, the size of the screen, the type of bracket and whether any cable goes above the ceiling. We send the certificate of insurance to whoever the building names and work inside its contractor hours. If approval is slow, a screen on a stand or a mobile cart in your room needs no one's permission and can move with you if you change offices.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About TV Installation in Cadman Plaza

These are claims that turn up in generic online answers and in national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, paraphrased rather than quoted, each followed by what the buildings around Cadman Plaza actually show. We have not quoted any single AI Overview; the claims below are the common ones.

"Find two studs and screw the bracket into them"

The most common instruction online is to run a stud finder along the wall, mark two studs at the usual spacing and drive lag bolts into them. It assumes a framed wall, because most American houses have one.

The co-op towers west of the park were cast in concrete between 1967 and 1973. A concrete wall has no studs at all, and a stud finder on it either reads nothing or reads the steel inside. Even where a later partition was added, its framing may be steel rather than wood.

We confirm the wall with one test hole hidden behind the bracket plate and choose the anchor for what is actually there, whether that is concrete, block, a wood stud or a steel one.

"Concrete is the hardest wall to mount on"

Many answers warn people off concrete as if it were the worst case, difficult to drill and likely to fail.

Concrete is harder to drill, but it is one of the best walls to hang a heavy screen on once the anchor is set to the right depth. The real risk SlashGear names is meeting rebar, and in a co-op tower the answer to rebar is to move the bracket, not to cut the steel.

The difficulty in these apartments is rarely holding the TV. It is hiding the cables on a wall that has no hollow inside it, which is why the cable plan is settled before the bracket position.

"Hide the cords by running them through the wall"

A popular tip is to cut a hole behind the TV and another near the floor and push the TV's own power cord through the wall between them.

The National Electrical Code does not allow flexible cords to be concealed inside walls. On a framed partition the right method is an in-wall power and cable kit with its own rated cable and a recessed outlet behind the screen. On cast concrete nothing can be fished inside at all.

In a concrete tower apartment the honest options are a paintable cord cover, a screen placed directly above the console, or a framed wall elsewhere in the room.

"A TV bracket never needs anyone's permission"

Some answers treat a TV mount as too small for any building to care about, and others treat any drilling in an apartment as forbidden.

In the co-ops and the condo around Cadman Plaza, the house rules and the alteration agreement decide. Hanging a bracket is usually minor work, but opening a wall for an in-wall kit or working near a riser may need approval, and most buildings want a certificate of insurance and contractor hours respected.

Ask the managing agent which category your job falls into, send us their requirements, and we send the paperwork before the visit.

"Wall mounting costs about the national average"

Cost guides publish national averages drawn from many cities, nearly all of them for drywall in a house with a driveway.

An average cannot know that your wall is cast concrete, that the screen has to pass a doorman's desk and a reserved elevator, or that the cables need a surface cover because the wall cannot be opened. Those are the things that change a Cadman Plaza total.

Our Brooklyn list starts at $179.99 and shows each add-on, from $45 for plaster to $169 for an in-wall kit, and anything not on the list, such as a concrete wall, is priced from your photos before booking.

"Mount the screen at the same height in every room"

Generic advice gives one height for the center of a TV, as if every room were the same.

Rooms here range from 1960s living rooms with a beam running along the wall to One Clinton apartments with ceilings of nine feet or more, and from angled saw-tooth wall faces to duplex bedrooms where the viewer is lying down.

We mark the outline with painter's tape and check it from where you actually sit or lie, and the beam, the windows and the furniture decide the height.

"Wi-Fi reaches every room in an apartment"

Streaming advice tends to assume that if the router works in one room, a smart TV will stream anywhere in the home.

Cast-concrete walls and floors absorb wireless signal far more than drywall does, and in a duplex townhouse the router may be a full concrete floor away from the bedroom TV.

We check the signal at the TV's position during setup, test any wired jack before relying on it, and tell you plainly if a screen in that spot will struggle, before it goes on the wall.
