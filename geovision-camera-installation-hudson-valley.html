The Neighborhood

Why Hudson Valley Properties Need GeoVision Camera Installation

The Hudson Valley is not one place, and a GeoVision system that suits a Westchester office park would be the wrong design for a Dutchess horse barn or a pre-Revolutionary stone house in Kingston. The region stretches from the edge of New York City up the river toward Albany, and the six counties we cover, Westchester, Rockland, Orange, Putnam, Dutchess and Ulster, hold almost four centuries of building. The Hudson River Valley Heritage Area describes architecture that runs from the mansions of land-grant families and nineteenth-century industrialists to modest farmhouses and working-class homes. Dutch settlers followed Henry Hudson's 1609 voyage up the river, and one of New Netherland's three main outposts, Wiltwyck, is the city we now call Kingston. Later came the factory towns: Poughkeepsie, Newburgh, Kingston and Hudson all filled with mills and plants, then declined in the middle of the twentieth century. After that came the suburbs, the office parks along the interstates, the warehouses off Route 17K, and the weekend houses of people who live most of the week in the city. A camera installer here has to read all of those buildings correctly.

GeoVision sits in the middle of that history. GeoVision is a Taiwanese video and access control manufacturer, as IPVM describes it, and its PC-based recorders, GV-VMS software and network cameras were installed in a great many small businesses, schools and offices over the years. A recorder that went in fifteen years ago in a Main Street shop or a town office often keeps running quietly in a closet long after anyone remembers who installed it. That is exactly the problem that surfaced in late 2024. Researchers at the Shadowserver Foundation found a botnet exploiting a previously unknown flaw in end-of-life GeoVision devices, tracked as CVE-2024-11120 with a severity score of 9.8 out of 10, a command-injection flaw that needs no password. Security Affairs reported about 17,000 internet-facing GeoVision devices exposed to it, with 9,179 of them in the United States. In May 2025 CISA added that flaw and a second one, CVE-2024-6047, to its catalog of known exploited vulnerabilities. The affected models, GV-VS12, GV-VS11, GV-DSP LPR V3 and GV-LX4C V2 and V3, are past end of life, and Armis notes that GeoVision issued no patches and strongly recommended replacing them.

So in this region, the first question on a GeoVision call is often not "where do the new cameras go" but "what is the old box in the back room, and is it talking to the internet." We have not seen any public count of how many of those devices sit in the Hudson Valley, and our research found no local news story naming a hacked GeoVision system here, so we are not going to claim one. What we can say is that the risk is national, that the models are documented, and that checking for them is quick once someone reads the label. We would rather spend ten minutes confirming a recorder is a supported model than leave an unpatchable device open on a business network that also carries the point-of-sale terminal or a home network that carries the family's laptops.

The other reasons valley properties need careful camera installation have nothing to do with GeoVision itself and everything to do with the land. In June 2024 the Times Union reported thunderstorms that left a combined 60,000 Orange and Rockland and Central Hudson customers without power, and in July 2026 the Daily Freeman reported nearly 50,000 Central Hudson customers in the dark after another round of storms. Earth Networks' 2020 lightning report put Orange County first in New York for lightning pulses, with Ulster, Westchester and Dutchess also high on the list. Every camera cable that leaves a building, to a barn, a gate, a pole or a detached garage, is a path for a surge back to the recorder. Distance matters too. The USDA's 2022 Census of Agriculture counted 620 farms in Dutchess County averaging 161 acres, and 486 farms in Ulster averaging 150 acres. On properties that size, the camera at the far paddock can be well beyond the roughly 100-meter limit of a single copper Ethernet run, and the plan has to account for that before the trench is dug.

Then there is the question of how residents and towns feel about cameras right now, which has been loud in 2026. Hudson Valley One reported in September 2026 that Kingston police defended a city network that, beyond its license plate readers, includes 71 conventional surveillance cameras concentrated in Midtown and Uptown, while residents raised privacy concerns. The same report noted that the cameras belonging to private businesses are mostly bullet-style cylinders. In Newburgh, Mid Hudson News reported in June 2026 that 21 of the city's 129 street cameras, about 16 percent, were not working and 19 could not be repaired, on a system running on a mix of fiber, copper and wireless radio. In October 2026 the Newburgh City Council moved to drop its Flock plate-reader program, Poughkeepsie Common Council members called for shutting theirs down, Dutchess County began removing its Flock cameras after a packed meeting in Pleasant Valley, and Ulster County Executive Jen Metzger issued an executive order barring county departments from deploying Flock and similar AI-assisted surveillance tools. Whatever you think of public cameras, the message for a private owner is the same: a system on your own property should be recorded locally, under your control, with you deciding who sees the footage.

Private incidents keep the demand real. Patch reported State Police in Poughkeepsie asking residents of Hoofprint Road in Union Vale and Fountain Place and Weatherford Lane in Millbrook, among other streets, to check their home cameras after car break-ins. News 12 reported Port Chester neighbors sharing camera footage of car break-ins around 4 a.m. on and near Tower Hill Drive. Scarsdale homeowners found a hidden camera placed on their property, which police described as a tactic theft rings use to watch homes before a burglary. State Police have also investigated burglaries targeting Asian American residents in Westchester, Dutchess and Putnam counties. And on the business side, WRRV reported a tip-jar theft at Alimentari Rossi & Sons at Eastdale Village in Poughkeepsie that was caught on camera. None of those stories involves GeoVision equipment that we know of, and we do not claim any of those people or businesses as customers. They show what a camera in this region is actually asked to do: record clearly at night, at the driveway and the back of the property, and hand over usable footage when someone asks.

GeoVision Recorder Acting Strange, Cameras Offline After a Storm? Fast Help Across the Hudson Valley

Two kinds of GeoVision call get moved to the front of the line. The first is a recorder that has started behaving oddly: running hot, slowing down, freezing, or showing settings nobody changed. Researchers who studied the GeoVision botnet listed exactly those signs, excessive heat, slowness or unresponsiveness, and configuration changes, as indicators that a device may have been taken over. If you see them, unplug the recorder's network cable, leave the power on so recordings are not lost, and call (800) 486-0943. Do not factory-reset it yourself before someone has exported the footage you may need. The second urgent call is the system that went dark after a storm: cameras on the barn, the gate or the lot offline while the indoor ones still work. That pattern usually means a surge came in on an outdoor run. Tell us which cameras are down and where their cables go, and we will tell you honestly when we can get there and what to bring.

The End-of-Life GeoVision Problem: What We Check Before Installing Anything New

A lot of the GeoVision work in this region is not a blank-slate install. It is a building that already has GeoVision equipment, sometimes a lot of it, and an owner who wants better cameras without throwing away what still works. That is a reasonable goal. But it means the first part of every job is an inventory, and the inventory has to be honest about which devices are now a liability. How we find out what you have

Most owners do not know the model number of their recorder, and there is no reason they should. We read it from the label on the device, from the software's About screen if it is a PC running GeoVision software, or from the camera's own web page. For each device we record the model, the firmware or software version, its network address, and whether it can be reached from outside the building. That last check matters most. An end-of-life device that sits on an isolated camera network with no route to the internet is a much smaller risk than the same device with a port forwarded through the router so someone could watch from home. In older installs, the remote-viewing setup was often done exactly that way, by opening ports on the router, and the ports were never closed again. We look at the router as well as the recorder, because that is where the exposure lives.

What we do with an affected device

If a device is one of the end-of-life models, we follow the published advice in a careful order. First we take it off the internet, usually by removing the port forwarding and, where necessary, moving it to its own network segment. Second, before any reset, we export the footage you may want to keep, because a factory reset or a replacement wipes the history. Third, we plan the replacement. For a small business that might mean a new GeoVision NVR or GV-VMS server with supported cameras; for a site with good cameras on an old video server, it can mean keeping the cameras and replacing only the device that converts or records them, if the cameras themselves are supported. We do not tell anyone to rip out working, supported equipment to make a sale, and we put every keep-or-replace decision in writing so you can see the reasoning.

Signs a device may already have been compromised

The researchers who documented the botnet listed three warning signs: a device that runs much hotter than it used to, one that has become slow or unresponsive, and one whose configuration has changed without anyone touching it. On their own, none of those proves anything. Old recorders run hot when their fans clog with dust, and slow software is common on aging PCs. But if an end-of-life GeoVision unit shows any of them and has been reachable from the internet, we treat it as compromised: we isolate it, we do not trust its settings, and we do not move its configuration onto the new system. We rebuild the new system from scratch with fresh passwords.

Old PC-based GeoVision recorders

Many GeoVision systems in the valley are not stand-alone boxes but Windows PCs with GeoVision capture cards or GV-VMS software. Those bring their own issues. GeoVision's V20 manual notes that GV-VMS automatically disables Windows Memory Integrity on Windows 10 and 11 during installation, which is a security feature many IT people expect to be on. That is a trade-off worth knowing about, and on a business network it is one more reason to keep the recorder PC on its own segment, away from the office computers. Older PC recorders also tend to run on old versions of Windows. When we find one, we look at whether its cameras can move to a supported GV-VMS install on current hardware, or whether the whole thing is due for replacement.

Walk the property at night

The most useful thing you can do before a survey is walk your own property after dark. Notice where the light ends. In the Hewlett Harbor burglaries on Long Island, police believed the burglars came in from the rear of homes with minimal lighting; the pattern is not unique to Long Island. On a valley property the dark areas are usually the back of the house facing the woods, the side yard by the garage, the lane to the barn and the parking area behind a shop. Write down where you would want to see a face or a plate, not just movement. That list is the starting point for camera placement, and it tells us which cameras need stronger night performance or a light added near them.

Think about who should see the footage

Before the system is built, decide who gets access. A family might want two phones on the app. A business might want the owner and a manager, but not every employee. A board-run building might want footage reviewed only by the managing agent. GeoVision's software lets each user have their own login and their own permissions, and it is far easier to set that up on day one than to untangle a shared password later. Tell us who needs live view, who needs playback, and who should be able to export footage.

What a business GeoVision system has to do

A home system mostly needs to show you who came to the door. A business system has more jobs at once. It has to capture faces at the entry and plates in the lot clearly enough to be useful to police. It has to watch the register and the stock room without pointing anywhere the law forbids. It has to keep recording through a power cut long enough to cover the time the building is most vulnerable. It has to let the owner check in from a phone without turning the recorder into a door into the business network. And it has to be simple enough that a manager can export a clip the morning after an incident without calling anyone. We design commercial GeoVision systems around those jobs, camera by camera, with the owner, and we write the camera list and the purpose of each camera into the quote. GeoVision's GV-VMS platform records up to 64 channels of GeoVision or third-party IP cameras, according to 2N's integration documentation, which is ample for most single-building businesses in the valley; larger or multi-site operations can use GV-Control Center to bring several recorders into one view.

Rules and practical limits for business cameras

  • A state license for the installer. New York's General Business Law Article 6-D says no one may engage in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems without a license, and 19 NYCRR §195.2 extends that to closed-circuit television used to detect or monitor intrusion. Ours is NYS license #12000287431. An owner installing a system on their own property with their own employees is exempt.
  • Notice to employees. Under New York Civil Rights Law §52-c, employers who monitor electronically must give notice and post it in a conspicuous place visible to employees. We remind every business client of that before the system goes live.
  • No cameras in restrooms or locker rooms. Labor Law §203-c prohibits cameras in restrooms, locker rooms and changing areas, regardless of any posted policy. We do not install there, and we check that a hallway camera does not see into one through an open door.
  • Historic-district storefronts. In Kingston, Newburgh, Beacon and Hudson, street-visible exterior changes on designated buildings go through the local commission or board. We prepare the camera positions, conduit route and finish so you can bring a clear plan to the review.
  • Protection where cable leaves the building. The National Electrical Code's Article 800 deals with communications circuits that enter buildings, including protectors and grounding. Camera cable running to a pole, a separate building or a sign is an outside run, and we protect and bond it accordingly.
  • Digging. Before any trench for a lot camera or gate run, New York's 811 system must be notified so underground utilities can be marked; Central Hudson's Dig Safely guidance gives two to ten working days' notice. In the New York City watershed, DEP rules may apply to ground disturbance near water.
  • Fire-rated walls and ceilings. In commercial buildings, cable that passes through a rated wall or ceiling has to be sealed with an approved firestop so the rating is kept. We firestop our own penetrations.

Your Questions Answered

GeoVision Camera Installation in the Hudson Valley: Questions Answered

Old GeoVision equipment and the security advisories

Our shop has had a GeoVision recorder for years. How do we tell whether it is one of the models being attacked?

Look at the label on the device, or send us a photo of it. The models named in the CVE-2024-11120 and CVE-2024-6047 advisories are the GV-VS12 and GV-VS11 video servers, the GV-DSP LPR V3 plate-recognition unit, and the GV-LX4C V2 and V3. If your device is one of those, it is end-of-life and will not be patched. If it is a different model, that is good news, but it does not mean it is safe by default; we still check its firmware or software version and whether it is reachable from the internet through the router. The second question is often more important than the first. A supported recorder with an old firmware and an open port can still be a weak point, and an old one on an isolated network is far less exposed. We check both and tell you plainly what we found.

Can the old GeoVision video server just be updated instead of replaced?

Not if it is one of the affected end-of-life models. Armis reported that because those devices were already classified as end-of-life and unsupported, no official patches were issued, and GeoVision's own advisory recommended immediate replacement. The best you can do with one in the meantime is what the published guidance suggests: take it off the internet, factory-reset it, and plan to replace it with a supported model. For other GeoVision devices that are still supported, a firmware or software update may be exactly the right answer, and we would rather update a working device than replace it. The decision depends on the model, and we write it down for you device by device.

If our old recorder was hacked, can its settings be copied onto the new system to save time?

We do not do that. If an end-of-life device has been reachable from the internet, or shows the warning signs researchers listed, such as running hot, slowing down or having its configuration changed, its settings cannot be trusted. Copying them over could carry an attacker's changes, unknown user accounts or altered network settings onto the new equipment. We build the new recorder from scratch, with new administrator credentials and new user logins, and we re-enter camera names and schedules by hand. It takes a little longer, but it means the new system starts clean.

Mixing GeoVision with what you already own

We have a mix of cameras from different brands. Can a new GeoVision recorder use the ones that still work?

Often yes, with testing. GeoVision describes its GV-NVR as working with a wide range of third-party IP cameras through ONVIF, the industry standard that lets recorders and cameras from different makers talk to each other. GV-VMS also records third-party IP devices. In practice, ONVIF support varies from camera to camera; basic live video and recording usually work, while motion events, two-way audio or advanced analytics may not. Older forum posts also describe GeoVision charging for a software dongle to run third-party cameras on its software, so licensing is something we confirm for your exact version before quoting. We test each existing camera on the new recorder before we promise it can stay.

Our GeoVision system runs on an old office PC. Should we keep a PC recorder or move to a dedicated NVR?

It depends on how many cameras you have, who will maintain it, and what else is on your network. A PC running GV-VMS is flexible and can scale up to many channels, but it is a Windows computer that needs updates, a working hard drive and someone who keeps an eye on it. GeoVision's manual notes that GV-VMS turns off Windows Memory Integrity during installation, which matters if the same network carries your business computers. A dedicated NVR is simpler to live with for a small shop or a house. For a business with an IT person and a larger camera count, a properly built GV-VMS server on its own network segment can be the better choice. We lay out both options with the reasoning.

Nobody remembers the administrator password on our PC-based GeoVision recorder. Is the footage lost?

Not necessarily, but it can be hard. A forum post from a GeoVision dealer explains that on PC-based GeoVision systems the password database is encrypted under Windows, and that without enough Windows privileges to run GeoVision's password removal utility, the fix is to reformat the drive and reinstall Windows, which loses the software setup. Before anyone tries that, we look for whoever installed the system, check whether a Windows administrator account is still available, and copy off any recordings that can be reached at the file level. If the footage matters, tell us at the start, and we will not take any step that risks it without asking you.

Watching from away, and rural internet

We want to check the cameras from the city. How is that set up without opening ports on our router?

The old way was port forwarding: opening specific ports on the router so a phone could reach the recorder directly. Troubleshooting guides for GeoVision systems list ports such as 80, 4550 and 5550 for remote viewing, and GeoVision's Remote ViewLog guide uses port 5552 for remote playback. Every open port is a way in, which is how the end-of-life devices were found and attacked. GeoVision's current GV-Eye guide describes GV-Relay, which by default uses port 443 and, when that port is already taken, port 9443, to connect the app without exposing the recorder in the same way. Where your equipment supports a relay or a VPN approach, we use it; where it does not, we keep the exposure to the minimum and change every default password.

Our weekend house in Ulster loses internet in storms. Does that mean we lose the recordings?

No, if the system records locally. A GeoVision NVR or GV-VMS server records to its own drives on the property, so an internet outage stops you from watching remotely but does not stop recording. Power is the other half: a battery backup on the recorder, switch and modem keeps cameras recording through short outages. GeoVision's V20 manual also describes a feature that syncs recording from a camera's own SD card when the camera reconnects, which can fill gaps if the link between a camera and the recorder drops. For a remote property with unreliable service, local recording plus battery backup is the design we start from, and cloud-only cameras are the ones we steer away from.

Storms, distance and outbuildings

The barn is about 600 feet from the house. How do the barn cameras get back to the recorder?

Not on a single copper Ethernet cable; a standard Ethernet channel is limited to about 100 meters, roughly 328 feet. There are three common answers. If the barn has power, a small PoE switch in the barn can power its cameras, and a fiber link back to the house carries the video; fiber also does not conduct lightning between buildings. If the barn has no power, a new circuit by a licensed electrician may be needed first. Where trenching is impractical, a point-to-point wireless bridge with a clear line of sight can work, though we would rather hardwire where we can. We look at the ground, the line of sight and what already exists before recommending one.

How do we keep a thunderstorm from wiping out the recorder through the outdoor camera cables?

By treating every cable that leaves a building as an outside run. That means surge protection where the cable enters the building, bonded to the building's grounding, in line with the approach the National Electrical Code takes for communications circuits. It means keeping outdoor runs in conduit, using outdoor-rated cable, and not running a copper cable between two buildings with separate electrical services when fiber would isolate them. It also means a battery backup with surge protection on the recorder itself. In Orange, Ulster, Westchester and Dutchess, which all ranked high for lightning in Earth Networks' 2020 report, those steps are part of the design, not optional extras.

Historic districts and licensing

Our building is in the Kingston Stockade District. Can a camera go on the front of it?

Possibly, but not without review. Kingston's Resolution 156 of 2024 says no one may change exterior features visible from a public street or alley, including light fixtures and other exterior elements, without commission approval, and the city's process issues a certificate of appropriateness before building permits. We did not find a written rule that says specifically whether a single surface-mounted camera triggers review, so we treat a street-visible camera or conduit as something to raise with the HLPC. Often the better answer is a camera on a side or rear wall, one inside a window facing out, or one on an outbuilding. Where a front camera is needed, we prepare a small, painted, neatly routed proposal for the commission.

Does the person installing our GeoVision cameras actually need a state license?

If they are in the business of doing it, yes. Article 6-D of New York's General Business Law requires a Department of State license to install, service or maintain security systems, and 19 NYCRR §195.2 says that includes closed-circuit television used, full-time or part-time, to detect or monitor intrusion, theft or movement. An industry association warns that unlicensed installers can be fined. The exception is an owner who installs a system on their own property, with their own employees. Our license is NYS #12000287431, and you are welcome to check any installer's license with the Department of State before hiring them.

We are a town office that follows federal guidance. Is GeoVision equipment NDAA compliant?

It depends on the exact product. One reseller notes that GeoVision's NDAA compliance varies by specific model and model year, and an older IPVM teardown, from around 2020, found a HiSilicon chip in one GeoVision camera model and a different chipset in another. So there is no blanket answer for the brand. If your organization needs NDAA-compliant equipment, tell us at the start, and we will quote only models whose current documentation states compliance, and give you that documentation with the quote.

Why does running the cable cost more than the GeoVision cameras themselves on our property?

Because on most valley properties the cable is where the work is. A camera can be mounted in minutes. Getting its cable from a stone wall or a barn to a recorder can mean drilling masonry, fishing through finished walls, trenching after 811 marks the utilities, protecting outdoor runs from surges, firestopping commercial penetrations, and in historic districts running painted conduit along a route the commission accepts. The price pages for each county break the job into its parts, so you can see what the cameras cost and what the pathways cost, and the written quote names every run before you agree.

What AI Gets Wrong About GeoVision Camera Installation in the Hudson Valley

What follows are claims we see repeated in quick online answers, forum posts and national cost guides from sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, set against the published advisories, GeoVision's own documentation and what the buildings of this region actually demand.

"Just update the firmware on your old GeoVision box."

This is the most common shortcut answer, and for the most dangerous devices it is simply wrong. The GV-VS12, GV-VS11, GV-DSP LPR V3 and GV-LX4C V2 and V3 are end-of-life, and Armis reported that no official patches were issued for the command-injection flaws found in them.

GeoVision's own advisory recommended replacing those devices immediately, and CISA's listing gave federal agencies until May 28, 2025 to fix the problem or stop using the products. There is no update to download.

For supported GeoVision models, updating is still good practice, and we do it. The point is to know which category your device is in before deciding, which takes a look at the label, not a guess.

"Wireless cameras are the easy choice for a country property."

Wireless sounds easy where houses are far apart and cable runs are long. But Wi-Fi cameras on a rural property depend on a signal reaching across a yard or a field, and on a router and internet connection that, in parts of Ulster and the other counties, drop in storms.

Police on Long Island urged homeowners to hardwire their security systems after burglars used Wi-Fi jammers, and cloud-only cameras cannot store new footage at all without internet. Neither problem respects county lines.

The honest answer for a valley property is usually wired cameras recording to a local GeoVision recorder, with fiber or a dedicated point-to-point bridge to the far buildings. Wi-Fi can still be the right call for a single camera in a spot cable cannot reach.

"Open a few ports on your router to view your cameras remotely."

Many older guides, including GeoVision troubleshooting pages from installers, describe exactly this: forward ports such as 80, 4550 and 5550 to the recorder, and the phone app will connect. It works, which is why so many systems were left that way.

It is also how roughly 17,000 GeoVision devices came to be reachable from the internet when the botnet went looking for them, according to Security Affairs. An open port is an open door to whatever is behind it.

GeoVision's newer GV-Eye guide describes GV-Relay, which uses port 443 by default, or 9443 when 443 is taken, to connect the app without exposing the recorder in the old way. Where the equipment supports it, that, or a VPN, is how we set up remote viewing.

"Every camera sold in the US is NDAA compliant."

Quick answers sometimes treat NDAA compliance as automatic for products on a US store shelf. It is not, and it varies within a single brand.

A reseller of GeoVision equipment states that compliance varies by specific SKU and model year, and IPVM's older teardown found different chipsets in different GeoVision camera models. The answer has to be checked for the exact part number.

For homeowners this rarely matters. For schools, towns, fire districts and contractors on federally funded work across the six counties, it can decide whether a product may be bought at all, so we confirm it before quoting.

"GeoVision software will record any camera you plug into it, free."

GeoVision recorders and GV-VMS do support third-party IP cameras through ONVIF, and that flexibility is one of the brand's strengths. But "supports" and "free with every feature" are not the same thing.

Older forum threads describe GeoVision software being free with GeoVision cameras while third-party cameras needed a purchased software dongle, and ONVIF support itself varies, with basic video working more reliably than motion events or analytics.

So we check licensing for your software version and test each existing camera on the recorder before telling you it can stay. A camera that records but does not send motion events may be fine for a hallway and wrong for a gate.

"Historic districts don't allow exterior cameras at all." Or: "No approval is needed for a camera."

Both versions show up, and neither is accurate for the valley's historic cities. Newburgh's East End guidelines do not ban security equipment; they say it should not damage historic fabric and should be placed on side or rear facades, with a certificate of appropriateness from the Architectural Review Commission for exterior changes.

Kingston's Resolution 156 of 2024 requires commission approval for changes to exterior features visible from the street, and HLPC staff have recommended painting exterior lines to match the facade. Beacon's Planning Board reviews exterior alterations in its overlay zone, and Hudson's Historic Preservation Commission issues certificates before building permits.

We did not find a rule in any of those cities that names a single camera specifically, so we treat street-visible cameras and conduit as review items. Side and rear placements, interior cameras looking out, and painted conduit usually make the review simple.

"Camera installation costs about the same everywhere."

National cost guides tend to give one range for a whole state or city, and AI answers repeat it. Those figures do not know whether your camera is on a vinyl-sided ranch in Rockland or a stone house in Hurley, or whether the barn is 50 feet or 600 feet away.

3. Walk the property and plan positions

Each camera view marked with you, cable distances measured against the 100-meter channel, and the route chosen: copper, conduit, fiber or a wireless bridge.

4. Check approvals and access

Historic-district review where street-visible work is planned, landlord or board approval, employee notice for businesses, and 811 before any digging.

5. Run and protect the cable

Cable pulled, labeled at both ends, sleeved and sealed through masonry, firestopped through rated walls, and surge-protected where it leaves a building.

6. Mount cameras and build the recorder

Cameras fixed and aimed, the recorder or GV-VMS server built on supported equipment with battery backup, and old end-of-life devices disconnected.

7. Set up secure remote viewing

GV-Eye and playback connected without open recorder ports where the equipment allows, default passwords replaced, and a login for each user.

8. Test, train and hand over

Every cable tested, every camera checked by day and at night, export shown to you, and a port map, camera list and credentials record left with the owner.

Step 8 is where many old systems in the valley went wrong, years ago. Nobody wrote down which cable went where, nobody kept the administrator password, and nobody showed the owner how to export a clip. FAQ

Hudson Valley GeoVision Camera Installation FAQ

Does a GeoVision system need a monthly subscription to record or to view on a phone?
+

A GeoVision NVR or GV-VMS server records to its own drives at your property, so recording does not depend on a cloud plan. GeoVision's GV-Eye app for live view, playback and snapshots is listed as free on the App Store. Your internet service is the only ongoing connection remote viewing needs.

Can you install GeoVision cameras both inside and outside the same Hudson Valley property?
+

Yes. Indoor cameras usually cover entries, hallways, registers or stock rooms, and outdoor cameras cover driveways, lots, rear yards and outbuildings, all recording to the same recorder. Outdoor cameras get sealed mounts and protected cable; indoor ones are kept out of restrooms, locker rooms and other private spaces.

How quickly can you get to a property in Dutchess or Ulster?
+

Requests are booked within 2 hours of reaching us, and we give you a time when we book. The visit date depends on the job and the schedule across the six counties. We do not promise same-day installation, but sending photos and a site sketch in advance lets the first visit do as much as possible.

Do we need to put up signs about the cameras at our business?
+

For employees, New York Civil Rights Law section 52-c requires employers who monitor electronically to give notice and post it in a conspicuous place employees can see. Beyond that, rules depend on the setting, and we are not giving legal advice, so check with your attorney for anything specific to your business.

Is it legal to point a camera from our house toward the road and our neighbor's driveway?
+

Watching your own property and the public road is common, and footage of a street has helped police in cases across the region. What New York prohibits is secretly recording people in private places, which Penal Law 250.45 covers. We aim cameras at your own property and the approach to it, and mask views into a neighbor's windows or yard where needed.

Is GeoVision more expensive than Hikvision or Dahua cameras?
+

One reseller, IP Security Depot, says GeoVision hardware is typically 20 to 40 percent more than equivalent Hikvision or Dahua cameras. On an installed system, cable, labor and site work are often a large share of the total, so the brand difference on the finished job is usually smaller than the hardware difference.

What happens to our old GeoVision recorder and its footage after you replace it?
+

Before anything is reset, we export any footage you want to keep to a drive you hold. The old device is disconnected from the internet and the network. If you want to keep it for its archive, it stays offline; if not, it can be factory-reset before it leaves the building so its settings and recordings go with it.

Will you show us how to use the GV-Eye app before you leave?
+

Yes. We install the app on the phones of the people who should have access, sign each one in with their own login, and show live view, playback, snapshots and how to find a clip by time. We also show the person responsible how to export footage from the recorder itself, which is what police usually ask for.

Can a GeoVision system read license plates at our gate or lot entrance?
+

GeoVision has offered license plate recognition, and GV-Control Center is designed to bring plate recognition together with video and access control. One older unit, the GV-DSP LPR V3, is on the end-of-life advisory list and should not be used. Plate capture depends heavily on camera angle, distance and lighting, so we survey the entrance before recommending a setup.

If police ask for our footage after a break-in nearby, do we have to hand it over?
+

State Police in Poughkeepsie have asked residents in Union Vale and Millbrook to check their home cameras after car break-ins, and many owners choose to help. Whether to share is generally your decision unless there is a legal order. Consumer advocates note that once you share a clip, you lose control over how it is used, so share only the relevant time.

We live in Manhattan and only visit our Putnam lake house on weekends. Can the install happen while we are away?
+

Much of it can, once we have walked the property with you or with someone you trust and agreed camera positions. We need access arrangements, a contact on the day and the internet details. We finish by connecting GV-Eye on your phone remotely and confirming that each camera shows what you agreed.

Our camera cable has to go through a very thick fieldstone wall. Will drilling damage the house?
+

Drilling one small, well-placed hole through stone is routine when it is done slowly and in the right place. We test-drill first, go through stone rather than old mortar joints where possible, sleeve the hole and seal it against water. On historic houses we keep to a single entry point and run the rest in painted conduit.

Does our town require a permit for a new camera system?
+

Permit rules are set town by town, and we did not find a Hudson Valley town rule written specifically for low-voltage cameras. Historic districts in Kingston, Newburgh, Beacon and Hudson review street-visible exterior work, digging needs 811 notice, and new electrical circuits are an electrician's job. We tell you which of those your plan touches.
