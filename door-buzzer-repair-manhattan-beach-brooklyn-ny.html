The Neighborhood

Why Manhattan Beach Properties Need Door Buzzer Repair

In most of Brooklyn a broken buzzer is a building's problem. In Manhattan Beach it is almost always a homeowner's. The peninsula, bounded by Sheepshead Bay on the north, the Atlantic on the south and east, and Brighton Beach on the west, is a neighborhood of detached houses. CityNeighborhoods.nyc describes them as mostly brick and stucco, in Mediterranean or Colonial Revival styles, set back behind gardens, with some 1920s to 1940s rows and low-rise apartment buildings and a growing number of newer houses built where older cottages stood. A house like that has no lobby, no super and no managing agent. Its "door buzzer" is whatever the owners, or the owners before them, put in: a push button and a chime, a button on the gate post at the sidewalk, a master intercom station in the kitchen with speakers in the bedrooms, a video doorbell, or, in the largest rebuilt houses, a full intercom system wired to every floor and to motorized gates.

The city's own records show how private this kind of repair is. We queried NYC Open Data on November 11, 2026. HPD's Housing Maintenance Code violations dataset holds 145 bell, buzzer and intercom violations for ZIP 11235 since 1983, and 311 holds 233 "bell/buzzer/intercom" complaints for the same ZIP from January 2020 to late October 2026. Almost none of them are in Manhattan Beach. HPD shows no buzzer violations at all on West End Avenue, Oriental Boulevard, Shore Boulevard or any of the lettered streets; the nearest are three on Corbin Place, the street along the peninsula's western edge, the last of them written in 2019. 311 shows one buzzer complaint on Shore Boulevard, at number 50 in January 2023, and one on Corbin Place in March 2026. The ZIP's buzzer complaints sit instead on the apartment blocks of Ocean Avenue across the bay, Neptune Avenue, Ocean Parkway, Coney Island Avenue and the Brighton numbered streets.

That gap is not evidence that Manhattan Beach doorbells never fail. It is evidence that the people who own them have nobody to complain to. HPD's intercom rules and 311's buzzer category exist for tenants in multiple dwellings; an owner whose gate bell stops ringing simply lives with it, or calls someone. That changes how a repair here should work. Nobody is going to cite the house, so there is no deadline and no inspector, and no reason to sell an owner more than the fault needs. There is also nobody keeping records, so the first part of many visits is finding out what the system even is: where the transformer was mounted, which wire goes to the gate, whether the room stations are an original NuTone or M&S system or something newer, and what the previous owner changed.

The houses themselves add the rest. A garden in front means the first button a visitor touches can be a long way from the chime, with the wire buried under a path or a lawn, and a long low-voltage run loses voltage that a short one doesn't. Many houses here have been enlarged or rebuilt, and The Real Deal reported in 2006 that the city had halted work on nearly 40 oversized new houses in the neighborhood, so the wiring in one house may be three generations of work layered on top of each other. And the peninsula floods: after Hurricane Sandy, the president of the Manhattan Beach Community Group told City Limits that "Every house was flooded," and Brooklyn Paper reported the power out for more than a week. Basements are where bell transformers and intercom supplies usually hang.

So a Manhattan Beach buzzer call is rarely about one broken component in a riser. It is about a path: from the gate post across the garden, through the front door frame, down to the basement or up to a closet, and out to the rooms where somebody is supposed to hear the visitor. We follow that path, test each part of it, and fix the part that failed.

The Front Garden

From the Gate Post to the Chime: Long Bell Runs Across a Manhattan Beach Front Garden

The single most common shape of a Manhattan Beach doorbell is one nobody in a brownstone neighborhood would recognize. The visitor stops at a gate in a fence or a low wall at the sidewalk, presses a button on the post, and waits while the sound travels up a garden path to a house set well back from the street. Sometimes there is a second button at the front door itself. Sometimes the gate has its own speaker so the person inside can ask who is there before walking out. And sometimes the gate has an electric lock or a motorized operator that somebody inside is supposed to be able to release. Each of those pieces depends on a wire that crosses the garden.

What a long run does to a doorbell

Doorbells and simple intercoms run on low voltage. HomeTips puts the typical rated output of a doorbell transformer at 16 volts AC, and Electrical Contractor magazine, writing about a video doorbell in 2025, describes swapping a failed 16-volt transformer for a 24-volt one because the doorbell required it, and warns that wire size and distance can cause enough voltage drop that the chime and the camera stop working properly. On a short run inside a house, that loss hardly matters. On a run from a gate post fifty feet or more from the house, through thin bell wire that was buried decades ago, it matters a great deal. The button can be perfect and the transformer healthy, and the chime at the far end still receives too little to pull its plunger, or the gate strike receives too little to let the latch go. The symptom is a gate bell that works in dry weather and fades in wet weather, or a release that buzzes faintly at the gate without opening it.

What the ground does to the wire

Wire that runs under a garden lives a hard life. It may have been laid directly in the soil rather than in conduit. It crosses under paths that have been relaid, beds that have been dug, irrigation that has been added and fences that have been replaced. A splice made in a shallow box at the foot of the gate post fills with water every time it rains. Electrical Contractor magazine's story starts with a doorbell that simply had no output; in a garden, the equivalent is a run that was nicked by a spade years ago and has corroded slowly through. We test the run from both ends before deciding anything: at the transformer or intercom supply in the house, and at the gate post with the button pressed. If the run is open, a tone tracer shows roughly where, and the decision becomes whether to repair the break or run new cable along a better path, in conduit, at a depth where the next gardener won't find it.

What the gate adds

A gate is a door that lives outdoors. Its post moves with frost and with the weight of the gate, its latch drops out of line with its keeper, and a strike that worked when the gate was hung can bind a few winters later. When a Manhattan Beach owner tells us the release "buzzes but the gate won't open," the electrical side is only half the question; the other half is whether the gate is pressing on the latch so hard that no strike could release it. If the gate is motorized, the operator itself belongs to the gate company. Our part is the intercom's side of the handshake: the button, the speaker, the dry contact or relay that tells the operator to open, and the wire between them. We test that handshake directly so you know which trade to call.

The House Intercom

The Intercom in the Kitchen: Repairing Older Room-to-Room Systems in Manhattan Beach Houses

Many of the brick and stucco houses on the peninsula were built or modernized in the decades when a whole-house intercom was a selling point. The pattern is familiar: a master station in the kitchen or front hall, with a radio and a row of switches; room stations in the bedrooms and the basement; and a door speaker beside the front door, so whoever was in the kitchen could talk to the visitor without walking to the door. NuTone and M&S were the names on most of them. Decades later, some still work perfectly, some work in every room but one, and some have gone silent at the door while the rooms still chatter to each other.

Why the wire is usually worth keeping

These systems were wired once, while the walls were open, and the wire is rarely the part that fails. Home Controls, which sells replacement units, explains that the old NuTone and M&S systems come in four wiring configurations, three-wire, four-wire, six-wire and eight-wire, with three- and four-wire the most common, and that retrofit stations from NuTone, M&S and Intrasonic Technologies are made to reuse the existing wire. It tells owners to count the wires behind a room station, not behind the master. That matters in a finished house with plaster walls and good millwork: a repair that reuses the in-wall wire costs a fraction of one that opens walls.

Where these systems actually fail

In our experience the failures cluster in a few places. The door speaker, outdoors for decades, loses its cone or its connections to weather. The master station's power supply or amplifier ages. A room station's switch wears out from years of being pressed. And somewhere along the way a renovation cut or buried a wire: a kitchen redone without the master, a basement finished over a junction, a bedroom station painted over and forgotten. We test each station and each wire pair separately, so the answer to "can it be fixed?" is specific. Sometimes it is one door speaker. Sometimes it is a master station, which means choosing between a matching retrofit that keeps every room, or a simpler setup that keeps the front door and the rooms that matter and retires the rest neatly.

The door half and the house half

One distinction makes the conversation easier. The "door half" of an old house intercom is the speaker at the front door, the chime and any door release. The "house half" is the room-to-room talking, the music and the baby-monitor feature that most families stopped using long ago. Owners often care only about the door half. When that is the case, we say so in the quote and price the repair around it, rather than pricing a full replacement of a system whose house half nobody misses.

Video Doorbells

When a New Video Doorbell Meets an Old Manhattan Beach Doorbell Circuit

A great many of the "doorbell repair" calls we get from houses like these are really about what happened after a video doorbell went in. The owner, or a relative, or a handyman, takes off the old button, connects the two wires to a new camera doorbell, and for a while it works. Then the chime goes quiet, or starts humming, or the doorbell keeps dropping offline, or the gate bell that shared the circuit stops working altogether.

Electrical Contractor magazine's 2025 article on exactly this situation explains most of it. A video doorbell draws power all the time, not just when the button is pressed, so it needs a transformer with enough voltage and enough volt-ampere capacity; the author's original 16-volt transformer had failed and was replaced with a 24-volt unit. Some electronic chimes are incompatible with video doorbells, and the article says a mechanical chime "may be the only type compatible with your video-style system." Many installations need a small chime connect module at the chime so the camera can draw power without the chime buzzing, and the article notes that a humming chime can sometimes be cured with a resistor across two of the chime's terminals, as the maker allows. On a Manhattan Beach house, add two local complications: a gate button sharing the same transformer, and a long run from the basement that was sized for a brass button and a chime, not a camera.

Our repair follows the circuit, not the box. We find the transformer, read its rating, measure it at rest and with the doorbell, the chime and any gate button working, and check the chime for the module or setting the doorbell's maker calls for. If the transformer is too small or failing, it is replaced with one rated for everything on the circuit. If the gate needs its own supply so the doorbell and the gate stop competing, the quote says so. We repair and troubleshoot the doorbell brands people bring us, but we don't claim to be any maker's certified dealer; where a doorbell's own app or account is the problem, we tell you that and leave it to the maker's support.

The Basement

Bell Transformers and Intercom Supplies in Manhattan Beach Basements

Where is the doorbell transformer? In most Manhattan Beach houses the honest answer is "somewhere in the basement," and finding it is often the first fifteen minutes of a visit. Doorbell transformers are commonly mounted on or beside the main electrical panel, on a junction box on a basement joist, or near the furnace. A 2007 thread on the Brownstoner forum about a doorbell transformer buzzing loudly from the basement got a blunt reply from a fire-alarm technician: the transformer is either "mounted poorly and is vibrating" or failing, and the owner should "make sure the power coming out matches the label." He added that the transformer is often mounted next to the breaker panel, where a careless hand can meet deadly voltage. That is still the right order of thought: check the mounting, measure the output against the label, and remember what it is bolted to.

The peninsula adds water to that picture. City Limits quoted the Manhattan Beach Community Group's president in December 2012 saying "Every house was flooded," with damage ranging from a foot of water to total destruction. In 2017, the Department of City Planning's flood-resiliency presentation to the same group listed "Mechanical equipment below the DFE subject to flooding" as a risk, and noted that elevating critical systems "Reduces time it takes to get back into home after flood." A doorbell transformer is not a boiler, but it is part of the same basement. If water reached it once, its terminals, the splices beside it and the junction where the gate and door wires meet may have been corroding since. If the house's electrical work has since been raised, the bell circuit may have been left behind, or reconnected in a hurry.

When we repair a bell or intercom supply in a Manhattan Beach basement, we look at its height relative to any water line, at the terminals and splices for corrosion, and at what it is feeding. Where the circuit allows, we mount a replacement high and dry, with sealed connections below it. Anything on the line-voltage side, a new circuit, a new box, work inside the panel, is an electrician's job and is named as such in the quote; we handle the low-voltage side and coordinate the rest.

Who Owns the Repair

Houses, Rental Floors and the West End Avenue Walk-Ups: Who Is Responsible for a Manhattan Beach Buzzer

Because the peninsula mixes houses and small apartment buildings, it helps to know which rules apply to which door. The New York Attorney General's Tenants' Rights Guide says multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968 must have self-closing and self-locking doors at every entrance, and that those with eight or more apartments must also have a two-way voice intercom that can buzz the door open; older multiple dwellings must have self-locking doors and a two-way intercom if a majority of the apartments ask for one. HPD enforces those rules through the Housing Maintenance Code, which is why its violation records carry wording about repairing "the broken or defective bell buzzer intercom system."

A one-family house sits outside all of that. No city agency requires an owner-occupied detached house to have a doorbell, an intercom or a gate release, and no inspector will cite it if one fails. The repair is entirely the owner's choice, and the only test is whether it works the way the household needs. A house with a rental apartment is in between: the details depend on how many units the building legally has, and the owner and tenant should read their lease and, if needed, ask HPD or a lawyer, because we are not giving legal advice. What we can do is repair the bell and release so the tenant's door works as well as the owner's.

The low-rise apartment buildings on West End Avenue, Shore Boulevard and a few of the lettered streets are a different case. OpenIgloo lists about 50 rental buildings in Manhattan Beach, among them several on West End Avenue and Hastings Street and one on Shore Boulevard with 65 open violations of various kinds. These are multiple dwellings, the intercom rules can apply to them, and their tenants can and do call 311. For those buildings we work the way we do across Brooklyn: with the owner or managing agent, apartment by apartment, with a dated record.

Before the Visit

Reading the Symptom: What a Manhattan Beach Owner Can Note Before We Arrive

Brick Underground's intercom repair guide quotes a locksmith-intercom contractor saying "A lot of times it's user error," and another technician saying "It's all in the wire connections." Both are often true on the peninsula, and a few minutes of noticing before a visit can make the visit shorter. None of this involves opening anything; it is just watching and listening.

At the gate and the door

Press each button for a full second, once at a time. Does anything happen inside? A faint hum, a click, half a chime? Does the button feel the same as it used to, or mushy, or stuck in? HomeTips notes that a doorbell that buzzes continuously usually has a button stuck closed, and that left that way it can burn out the transformer, so a stuck button is worth reporting. If there is a gate release, try it from inside while someone watches the gate: does the latch move at all, or does nothing happen?

Inside the house

Note what the chime does. Rings normally, rings faintly, hums without ringing, or does nothing. HomeTips explains that a chime that hums but doesn't ring often has a stuck plunger. If the house has an intercom, try each room station to the door and to one another, and write down which work. If a video doorbell is involved, note whether it stays online, whether it restarts when pressed, and whether the chime still sounds.

What changed

Think back to anything that happened shortly before the trouble started: a new doorbell, a renovation, an electrician working on the panel, a new fence or gate, landscaping along the front path, a heavy storm or water in the basement. In a house with no records, that history is often the fastest clue to where the fault is. Then send it to us with the form, along with a photo of the button, the chime and, if you can find it, the transformer label.

Building Specialists

Door Buzzer Repair for Manhattan Beach's Architecture

Manhattan Beach began as a resort. Wikipedia records Austin Corbin's Manhattan Beach Hotel opening in 1877, with the Oriental Hotel beside it, and CityNeighborhoods.nyc notes that the hotels were gone by the end of World War I, after which the peninsula filled in with houses on its lettered streets. Those houses, and what has been done to them since, decide how a doorbell or intercom repair goes. These are the property types we meet here.

The Brick House Behind a Garden

The classic Manhattan Beach house: detached, brick, set back behind a front garden with a fence or wall at the sidewalk. A button at the gate, a button at the door, a chime inside and a transformer in the basement, joined by a run across the garden.

The Stucco Mediterranean House

Stucco walls, tile roofs and arched entries. Door buttons and speakers are often set into the stucco or into an arch, and a repair has to keep the opening sealed so water doesn't get behind the finish.

The Colonial Revival House

Center-hall houses with a formal front door, sidelights and a vestibule. Often the home of a mid-century room-to-room intercom with a master station in the kitchen and speakers in the bedrooms.

The Enlarged House

An older house that grew: a second story, a rear addition, a finished basement. The bell and intercom wiring is usually the original run plus whatever was spliced on during the work, sometimes behind new walls.

The Rebuilt House

A new house on the site of an older cottage, sometimes several times its size, with an equipment closet, networked gate and door stations, motorized gates, multiple floors and sometimes a private elevator.

The Low-Rise Apartment Building

The 1920s to 1940s rows and small apartment buildings, many along West End Avenue and Shore Boulevard: a street door, a lobby panel, apartment stations and the rules that come with being a multiple dwelling.

How the house type changes the repair

The brick house behind a garden is a distance problem first. The run from gate to house is the longest and most exposed part of the circuit, and it is where we start measuring. The transformer is usually old and sized for a chime, not for a gate strike, a gate speaker and a video doorbell together. Our usual repair is a correctly rated transformer, a sealed connection at the gate post, and, where the buried run has failed, new cable in conduit along the path rather than under a planted bed.

The stucco house is a sealing problem. Stucco is unforgiving of holes, and a door speaker or button set into it lets water in around its edges as its gasket ages. When we replace a button or speaker in stucco, we reuse the existing opening wherever we can, seal the box and the cable entry, and avoid enlarging the hole. If the replacement doesn't fit the old opening, we say so before ordering, so you can choose a part with the same footprint or plan for a stucco patch by a plasterer. We don't patch or paint walls ourselves.

The Colonial Revival house is often an intercom problem. The master station, the room stations and the door speaker were wired together when the house was built or modernized, and the wire is generally good. Rules and practical limits at institutional and apartment doors

  • Multiple dwellings: under the NY Attorney General's Tenants' Rights Guide, post-1968 multiple dwellings need self-closing, self-locking entrance doors, and those with eight or more apartments need a two-way intercom that can buzz the door open. HPD writes buzzer violations under the Housing Maintenance Code.

  • Historic listing: National Register listing is federal and, according to the state preservation office, doesn't by itself restrict a private owner's repairs; the peninsula is not a city landmark district in any source we found, so ordinary intercom repair needs no Landmarks permit here.

Field Notes: Starting a Manhattan Beach Doorbell Repair at the Sidewalk

We don't tell stories about particular Manhattan Beach addresses, because we only publish what we can document. What we can describe is the order a technician follows at a detached house on the peninsula, because it is the same every time and it is where these repairs succeed or fail.

The visit starts where a stranger would: on the sidewalk, at the gate. We press the gate button and listen, with someone inside telling us what they hear. We try the release from inside and watch the latch. Then we walk the path to the house, noting anything that could have disturbed a buried wire, a new path, a new bed, a sprinkler head, a replaced fence post. At the front door we press that button, check the chime, and if there is a video doorbell, watch whether it stays awake. Only then do we go down to the basement. There we find the transformer or supply, read its label, and measure its output at rest and again while someone presses the gate button and the doorbell is working, because a transformer that reads fine at rest can sag badly under load. If the reading is good at the basement and poor at the gate, the run across the garden is the problem. If it is poor at the basement, the transformer is. That sequence keeps an hour's job from becoming a day of digging.

Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Repair in Manhattan Beach: Questions Answered

Gates, gardens and the walk to the door

Our gate post is roughly sixty feet from the house. On rainy days the bell at the gate barely sounds, while the button at the front door rings normally. What does that pattern point to?

It points to the run across the garden, not the chime or the transformer. Both buttons share the same chime and usually the same transformer, so if the front door rings well, those two parts are healthy. What the gate button has that the door button doesn't is distance and dirt. Electrical Contractor magazine notes that wire size and distance cause voltage drop on doorbell circuits; on a long, thin run, a little extra resistance from a corroding splice or a nicked wire is enough to starve the chime. Rain makes it worse because water gets into the splice box at the foot of the post or into a damaged jacket underground. We measure at the transformer, then at the gate with the button pressed, and the difference tells us how much the run is losing. Often the fault is a single wet splice in the post, which is cheap to remake with a sealed connector. If the buried run itself has corroded, the quote will offer a new cable in conduit along the path.

Our iron gate hangs on a post that leans a little further every winter, and the electric strike on it now only lets go if someone gives the gate a shove. Is the strike wearing out, or is the gate the problem?

Most likely the gate. A strike releases by letting its keeper swing free, and it can only do that if the latch isn't pressing hard against it. When a post leans, the gate drops and twists, the latch rides against one side of the keeper, and the strike's small solenoid can't overcome the friction. The shove takes the pressure off for a moment, and then it opens. We check this by holding the gate in its proper position while someone presses release: if it opens cleanly, the strike is fine and the gate needs resetting. We can realign the strike and keeper to the gate as it hangs now, but a post that keeps moving needs a fence or masonry contractor to fix it properly, and we will tell you so rather than replacing a good strike.

Our gate has a speaker, but with traffic on Oriental Boulevard and wind off the water, we can barely make out who is talking. Is a louder station the answer?

Not always. Before replacing anything, we check whether the existing station is working as designed: its speaker and microphone, the volume settings at the inside station, and whether the wire between them is clean or adding noise. Old outdoor speakers lose sensitivity as their cones and gaskets age, and a station with a cracked or clogged grille will sound muffled no matter how high the volume goes. If the station is healthy and the setting is simply loud, a replacement with better noise handling may help, but sometimes the bigger improvement is moving the button and speaker a few feet into the shelter of the gate pier or a hood, out of the wind. We will tell you which applies before quoting.

Older intercoms in older houses

We bought a house the listing called "original," with intercom stations in several rooms, and nobody knows what brand it is. How do you identify an old room-to-room system and find out whether parts still exist?

By looking at the stations, not guessing from the house's age. Most older systems carry the maker's name and a model number on the master station, behind its cover or on its chassis, and the room stations usually match. NuTone and M&S were the most common names in houses of this era. Home Controls explains that these systems use three-, four-, six- or eight-wire configurations and that the wiring type is what decides which retrofit stations will work, so we also take one room station off the wall and count the wires behind it. With the maker, the model and the wire count, we can say whether original parts, compatible retrofit units, or a simpler replacement using the same wire is the realistic path, and price each one in writing.

Several bedroom stations in our old house intercom hiss all the time, even when nobody is talking. Does a constant hiss mean the whole system is about to fail?

No, but it does tell us where to look. A steady hiss or hum on every station usually starts at the master station's amplifier or power supply, which age with the electronics around them. A hiss on only some stations points to the wires or connections serving those rooms, or to a room station whose switch has worn and is leaving the line half-open. We listen at each station with the others switched off, then measure the supply. If the master's supply is the cause, it may be repairable or replaceable on its own. If one run is noisy, it can often be re-terminated. A whole-system replacement only makes sense if the master is beyond repair and the household actually uses the room-to-room features.

Rebuilt houses and newer systems

Our house was rebuilt by a contractor who has since closed. The intercom, the gate stations and the cameras were all his installer's work. Will another company take on a system it didn't install?

We will; most of the systems we repair were installed by someone else. Every intercom, gate station and door release can be identified from its hardware and traced from its wiring, whoever put it in. The first visit is partly a survey: we identify each station's maker and model, find the supply and the network equipment, trace which cable goes where, and write it down. Then we repair what failed. What we won't do is claim to be a maker's authorized dealer if we aren't; if a station's software or cloud account is locked to the original installer's dealer login, we tell you, and help you work out with the maker how to get control of it.

Our gate station opens one of our two motorized gates without trouble, but the other gate's operator ignores the same command. Which part is yours to repair and which is the gate company's?

The intercom tells a gate operator to open through a relay contact, a simple switch closure. Our side is that relay and the wire to the operator's input; the gate company's side is the operator, its control board and its safety devices. With one gate working, the station is clearly sending a command. We test the relay output for the second gate and the wire to the operator, and check that the operator's input actually sees the closure. If it does and the gate still doesn't move, the operator is the problem and we tell you plainly that it is a call for the gate company. If the relay or wire is at fault, we repair it.

Apartments, institutions and neighbors

We rent in one of the low-rise apartment buildings on West End Avenue, and our handset stopped ringing weeks ago. The landlord says nobody else has complained. What helps the owner get it fixed quickly?

Specific information. Tell the owner exactly what happens: whether your handset rings, whether you can talk to the lobby, and whether the door release works, along with the date it started. Ask a neighbor to try theirs, because "only us" and "the whole stack" point to different faults. The NY Attorney General's Tenants' Rights Guide sets out what entrance-door and intercom equipment different buildings must have, and tenants can contact 311 or HPD; that is your choice, and we are not giving legal advice. For the owner, the repair itself is usually a station, a pair in the riser or a connection at the panel, and we test apartment by apartment so the record shows which ones were fixed.

A house is going up next door, and since the excavator worked along our shared fence, our gate bell has been dead. How do we find out whether their work cut our wire, and what do we need to show them?

First we test, not assume. We measure the run from the house and from the gate and use a tone tracer to locate any break. If the break is along the fence line where the work happened, we photograph the location, the exposed cable if it can be seen without digging, and our readings, and we write a short dated report of what we found and what the repair involves. That is the kind of document a neighbor, their contractor or an insurer can act on. If the fault turns out to be somewhere else, such as a corroded splice in the gate post, the report says that too.

Our congregation's front door on West End Avenue is buzzed open from the office during weekday hours, but nobody is in the office in the evening. How should the door behave when the office is empty?

It should lock, and it should always let people out. Whatever the buzzer does, the door must close and latch behind visitors, and its release must never prevent anyone inside from leaving. For evening events, the usual answers are a person at the door or a schedule that keeps the door locked and relies on a second station somewhere staffed. We repair the existing release, closer and latch so they behave predictably; if the congregation wants new scheduling or keypad access, that becomes a separate written quote, and we will explain the fail-safe and fail-secure choices before anything changes.
