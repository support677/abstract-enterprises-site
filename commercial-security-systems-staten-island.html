TERMINOLOGY

Staten Island Commercial Security Terms, Translated

The vocabulary that decides what a Staten Island quote actually contains.

PoE / PoE+. Data and power on one network cable per camera, reader or intercom — one battery-backup point in the closet instead of a wall-wart behind every device.

NVR vs DVR vs VMS. The recorder generations: DVR for analog coax, NVR for IP, VMS as the software layer above recorders and sites. A 2026 Staten Island quote built around a DVR deserves a follow-up question.

ONVIF. The interoperability standard keeping cameras and recorders brand-independent — the difference between owning a system and renting a vendor.

OSDP. The encrypted reader-to-panel protocol replacing legacy Wiegand wiring, which can be skimmed at the door with a pocket device. Ask for it on every new opening.

Fail-safe vs fail-secure. What each locked door does when power drops — release or hold. Egress and fire code decide per opening; preference does not.

Electric strike vs maglock. Two ways to electrify a door: the strike cooperates with the latch; the maglock holds by force, suits storefront glass, and carries strict egress rules.

REX. Request-to-exit — the sensor or bar letting people leave freely while the street side stays locked. Skipped, the door either alarms all day or traps someone.

Door position switch. The contact reporting whether a door is actually shut — the cure for the rear door propped since the morning delivery.

Mobile credential. The phone as key: revoked in seconds, rarely lent out — which across seasonal and part-time staff is exactly the point.

Dual-path communicator. Alarm reporting over cellular and internet together, so one cut line or storm outage never silences monitoring.

Video verification. A clip rides with the alarm signal so the central station dispatches on evidence — the single best false-alarm reducer a business can buy.

Retention. Days of footage held before overwrite. Claims timelines and insurers set the number; the drives just have to obey it.

Edge analytics. Person, vehicle, line-cross and loiter detection on the camera itself, alerting live instead of after a scrub session.

Roof and skylight contacts. Alarm points on the openings burglars use when the front is too visible — the strip-center entry route nobody wires until after the first roof cut.

YOUR QUESTIONS ANSWERED

Real Questions from Staten Island Business Owners

What does a commercial security system cost on Staten Island?

Working borough numbers: a storefront package of cameras plus a monitored alarm lands in the low four figures; a small office or medical suite with cameras and a door or two on credentials runs mid four figures to low five; an integrated mid-size build — sixteen to twenty-four cameras, four to six controlled doors, monitored intrusion — commonly falls in the $15,000–$45,000 class; warehouse, yard and portfolio work starts around $50,000 and climbs with lot cabling and LPR. The spread between two quotes for the same address is rarely the cameras — it is doors, roofs, lots and what got quietly excluded. A fair quote itemizes all of it.

Are cloud subscription plans worth it for a Staten Island business?

Run the math over years, not months. Per-camera and per-door licensing compounds forever, and a twenty-four-camera cloud stack can quietly cost more than the cameras did. Cloud earns its fee for multi-location operators — a brand with units in three strip centers — and teams with no IT staff. A single-location business usually comes out ahead owning an on-premises recorder with off-site backup. We price both ways at a three-to-five-year horizon in every proposal; hybrid wins most often.

What should access control cost per door on Staten Island?

Most standard commercial doors land between roughly $1,500 and $3,000 installed — reader, electrified hardware, contact, request-to-exit and programming. Easy openings with existing pathway can come in near $500–$1,000; storefront glass leaves, fire-rated doors and enterprise openings reach $6,000–$8,000; new cable runs add $250–$600 per door, and gates and yard readers carry their own hardware lines. A per-door price quoted before anyone has seen your doors is a guess wearing a suit.

How do I verify a Staten Island security installer's license?

Two layers, both checkable in minutes. Statewide, security and fire alarm installation is licensed under General Business Law Article 6-D — verify any company with the Department of State; our number is 12000287431. Inside the city, electrical scope runs through DOB-licensed electricians and permits where the work requires them, and fire-alarm tie-ins bring FDNY requirements. Then ask the Staten Island question the databases cannot answer: who actually comes when it breaks. Get the service commitment in writing next to the license number.

Why do off-island security companies charge us more or never show?

Because every visit competes with the bridge. For a vendor based two boroughs away, your service call carries a toll, an hour of windshield time and a schedule slot that a closer job will always outbid — so quotes grow travel padding, warranty visits slip, and eventually the phone stops being answered. It is the single most common origin story behind the orphaned systems we take over here. The fix is structural, not personal: hire an installer whose pricing and dispatch already treat Staten Island as a service area, get the response commitment in writing, and keep the admin credentials in your hands so no vendor’s disappearing act can strand you twice.

Is paying in full before installation ever a good idea?

No. Deposit-and-balance is the professional structure — ours is fifty percent down, balance at completion — and full payment before installation removes every ounce of your leverage on the punch list. It is also the common thread in the installed-and-vanished stories behind half our takeover calls. A written scope, a deposit structure and a named warranty are the floor of a professional proposal.

Can I put up my own cameras at my Staten Island business?

On property you own, legally yes — Article 6-D licenses businesses installing for others, and owners on their own premises sit outside it. In practice the lane narrows fast: most strip-center units are leased, which puts the landlord’s alteration language in play; monitored certificates need a carrier-recognized installation; electrified hardware on egress and fire-rated doors is code territory; and anything near a building fire alarm is off the table. A camera over your own counter, in a building you own, is a fine Saturday project. A leased unit is where DIY gets expensive quietly.

Is the cabling from our old system worth keeping?

Often, and an hour on site answers it. Existing coax frequently carries IP video over converters, sound Cat5e regularly survives for cameras, and pathway — the expensive part across a lot or through finished ceilings — is reusable even when its cable is not. Access control is the usual exception: door hardware wants dedicated home runs, and legacy reader wiring is often the skimmable old protocol worth retiring. The proposal says keep, convert or replace, line by line, because billing you to rip out working cable is billing you twice.

Wired or wireless for Staten Island commercial buildings?

Wired PoE is the commercial default — one cable per device, centralized backup, no battery fleet, no gambling on radio across a lot or through block construction. Wireless earns specific Staten Island spots: a lot camera where trenching is absurd, a detached building or dock house, a short-term lease. The honest answer for almost every property is a wired backbone with wireless as a rare, documented exception, decided position by position at the survey.

How many days of footage should a Staten Island business keep?

Thirty days is the working floor. Slip-and-fall claims — a constant in lot-heavy retail — insurance investigations and police follow-ups reach back weeks, and discovering the recorder overwrote the incident is the most expensive storage lesson there is. Cash-heavy, regulated and higher-liability operations commonly hold sixty to ninety. Retention is drive math against resolution and camera count, so we spec the number in writing and size the recorder to deliver it.

What happens to our security when the power or internet drops?

Almost nothing, if the system was specified for it — and on this island, storm outages are a design input, not a surprise. Cameras keep recording to a battery-backed local recorder, door controllers keep enforcing cached credentials, and the alarm fails over to cellular so the central station never goes deaf. What pauses is remote viewing and cloud sync, which catch up on reconnect. Cloud-only cameras and internet-only alarm reporting are the two specs we talk Staten Island owners out of most often.

In a Staten Island strip mall, who handles security — landlord or tenant?

The lease decides, and the strip-center split usually runs: the landlord covers the shell, the lot, the lot lighting and any shared rear corridor; each tenant secures its own unit — alarm, interior cameras, its own door credentials. The contested zones are the shared rear doors, the roof and the lot cameras. Bring the lease to the survey; we design to your side of the line, issue the COI the landlord demands, and keep tenant systems separate so one unit’s footage and credentials never leak into another’s.

The building just sold — does our security system belong to us or the new landlord?

Depends on who bought it, and the paperwork decides. Equipment you purchased and installed as a tenant is your trade fixture in most commercial leases — yours to keep, usually yours to remove at term with restoration — but anything the old landlord installed, or anything wired into base-building systems, typically conveys with the sale. The trap is the undocumented middle: systems half-paid-through a vendor agreement, or credentials only the old owner held. When a sale is coming, get your ownership documented, your admin credentials in hand and your data exported before closing day — we handle exactly that transition, on either side of it.

Our installer is from off-island and stopped answering — can you take over?

Yes, and on Staten Island it is practically a product category. We audit what exists, recover or factory-rebuild locked recorders and panels, replace only what is actually dead, and put the system under our service and health monitoring — with response that does not evaporate at the toll plaza. Bring whatever paperwork survives; we have rebuilt from none.

How long does a commercial security install take on Staten Island?

On-site time: one to two days for a storefront package; two to four for a suite or shop with controlled doors; one to two weeks for an integrated mid-size build; two to five weeks, phased, for yards, industrial floors and multi-building work. Island jobs often move faster than the other boroughs — parking at the door, accessible ceilings, no freight-elevator lottery — while lot trenching and pole work add days when the scope crosses the parking field. The pre-crew calendar at managed centers (landlord consent, COIs) is where delay actually lives, so we open it at the survey.

In our strip-mall lease, is security the landlord's job or ours?

Split, and the lease draws the line: the landlord typically carries common areas — the lot, shared lighting, sometimes lot cameras — while everything inside your unit, front glass to rear door, is yours, installed under the alterations clause with a COI first. The friction points worth settling in writing now: whether you may mount cameras on the building’s exterior or lot poles, whose footage covers the sidewalk in front of your glass, and trade-fixture language so your system leaves with you at surrender instead of converting to landlord property. A center that recently changed hands makes the paperwork more important, not less.

An ex-employee still holds keys to our shop — what now?

Today: revoke every credential they had — on a managed access system fobs, mobile and PINs die in one click, logged; change the alarm code they knew, and retire shared codes entirely in favor of per-person codes so the next exit never forces a company-wide reset; pull their remote camera and monitoring logins; then read the recent audit trail and footage with fresh eyes. The physical keys are the expensive remainder: every lock they could open either gets rekeyed or stays a known hole — the recurring locksmith bill that is the whole argument for moving Island shops onto credentials.

Our cameras only save to the cloud — is that a problem here?

It is the single most common failure we diagnose: the internet drops — storm, ISP cut, a router someone rebooted — and a cloud-only system records nothing for the duration, discovered weeks later when footage from that window is needed. Add suburban-plan bandwidth caps throttling upload quality, and the crisp demo becomes mush at exactly the distances lot coverage requires. The fix is architecture, not brand: local recording — an NVR or on-camera storage — as the source of truth, cloud as the access layer on top, sized to bandwidth measured at the survey. One question exposes any proposal: where does video live when the internet is down?

How do we leave a monitoring contract without losing the equipment?

Start with the paperwork: the term and its auto-renewal window (many agreements renew for a full further term unless cancelled in a narrow slot — calendar it), then hardware ownership, because leased or firmware-locked panels are how exit fees become ransom. If the equipment is yours, a takeover is routine: reprogram the communicator to a new central station, test signals, monitoring never lapses. If the panel is theirs or locked, the sensors and wiring usually still survive into a new panel at a fraction of a full rebuild. We audit exactly this at takeover surveys — and structure our own monitoring month-to-month on equipment you own, so the question never arises twice.

Do I need a permit for commercial security cameras on Staten Island?

Inside your own unit, camera and low-voltage work frequently proceeds without a standalone permit; the triggers are electrified egress hardware, fire-system adjacency, structural or exterior mounting, and — at strip centers — the landlord’s alteration consent, which governs exterior walls and lot poles outright. Do security systems reduce business insurance premiums on Staten Island?

Sequence it broker-first: crediting requirements in writing, then an installation built to them, so certificate and policy describe the same system. Are security cameras tax deductible for a Staten Island business?

Generally yes as business equipment, and installed systems commonly qualify for accelerated treatment such as Section 179 in the year of purchase. Route the specifics through your accountant, then put after-tax cost into the bid comparison — on typical Island project sizes it meaningfully narrows the spread between quotes.

Who covers the parking lot at a strip mall — my cameras or the landlord's?

The lease and the site plan decide, and the honest answer at most centers is: partially, ambiguously, or nobody — which is how lot incidents end up on no one’s footage. Sort it in writing: what the landlord’s common-area cameras actually cover, whether you may mount coverage of your own frontage and approach, and whose recording an insurer will be asking for after a claim.
