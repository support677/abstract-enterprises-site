The Neighborhood

Why New Lots Properties Need Door Buzzer Installation

New Lots is the oldest name in this corner of Brooklyn. It was the Town of New Lots from 1852 until Brooklyn annexed it in 1886, and today it is usually treated as the southern part of East New York, centered on New Lots Avenue and running from the blocks along Livonia Avenue south across Hegeman Avenue to Linden Boulevard, with Brownsville to the west and Canarsie to the southwest. The 3 train ends here, at the New Lots Avenue terminal on Livonia Avenue, and the elevated tracks run the length of Livonia over a row of shops. Away from the avenues, the blocks are lined with brick two- and three-family houses, many of them attached to their neighbors, with a stoop or a few steps at the front, a cellar or basement below and, often, a door at the side.

That housing stock decides what a door buzzer installation means here. The Citizens Housing & Planning Council, studying the city's basement conversion pilot, counted about 16,000 one- to three-family properties in Community District 5, the district that takes in New Lots, and nearly 9,000 two-family homes with space below grade. In a house like that, the "building" is one family's home with one or two rental apartments in it. The owner is the landlord, the super and often the person who answers the door for everyone. A buzzer installation is the moment that house stops working like a one-family with extra tenants and starts working like a small building, where each household can see or hear its own visitor and let that person in without anyone else going downstairs.

The walk-ups are a different story with the same ending. On New Lots Avenue and the cross streets near it, three- and four-story apartment buildings carry the old pattern of a panel by the street door and a station in each apartment's foyer. The public record for one of them, 316 New Lots Avenue, shows what happens when that equipment ages out. In May 2024 an HPD inspector wrote up "the broken or defective intercom system in the foyer" of one apartment. In July 2024 the same apartment's complaint produced a class B violation for a "broken or defective bell buzzer intercom system from building entrance door," followed in August by two more complaints filed under "bell/buzzer/intercom." Between those dates and August 2026, inspectors also cited the building's self-closing doors, from apartment entrances to the roof bulkhead, more than once. Each of those entries is a separate job on paper. At the door they are one problem: a building whose entrance does not reliably call, release and relock.

Then there are the avenues themselves. The city's Department of Small Business Services counted 367 storefronts along New Lots, Livonia and Sutter Avenues and Van Sinderen Avenue in its 2025 study of the district, among them 48 salons and barbershops, 41 delis and bodegas, 33 houses of worship and nonprofits and 22 medical offices. Many of those storefronts have apartments above them, reached through a narrow street door beside the shop window, and many of the businesses themselves keep a door that should not be open to everyone: a salon's back room, a clinic's staff entrance, a church's office or the door to its basement hall.

Six facts that shape a New Lots buzzer installation

  • Houses outnumber buildings. About 16,000 one- to three-family properties stand in Community District 5, and nearly 9,000 of them are two-family homes with a basement or cellar, according to the Citizens Housing & Planning Council's 2023 report.
  • Many cellars have their own door. Of 102 homes the pilot assessed in detail, 69 percent had cellars and 31 percent had at least one door leading straight out of the basement or cellar, which is a door a buzzer plan has to account for.
  • Inspectors write up buzzers here. HPD's record for 316 New Lots Avenue includes a class B violation for a defective bell, buzzer and intercom at the building entrance in July 2024, and class B violations carry a 30-day correction period.
  • Doors get written up faster. The same building collected class C self-closing door violations in 2024, 2025 and 2026; HPD gives owners 14 days to correct those.
  • The ZIP code is busy on paper. LucidRents' summary of HPD data for East New York's 11207 ZIP lists 12,263 buildings, 107,658 violations and 164,750 complaints.
  • The avenues mix homes and storefronts. The 2025 district study counted 367 storefronts on New Lots, Livonia, Sutter and Van Sinderen Avenues, 21 percent of them vacant, with apartments above many of the occupied ones.

Repair or Replace in New Lots

Need Repair in New Lots? Fix the Part, Finish What Was Never Installed, or Start Over

When a New Lots owner calls about "a new buzzer," the house is usually in one of three states. Sometimes there is a working system with one dead piece: a release that hums but will not let the door go, a button on the plate that stopped ringing upstairs, a transformer in the cellar that has given out. Sometimes there is half a system: a bell that rings on every floor at once, so whoever is nearest goes down to open the door, and no release at all. And sometimes there is nothing left worth saving: buttons painted over, wire cut during a renovation, a panel from a manufacturer nobody stocks anymore.

We sort out which one before anyone prices equipment. A photo of the front door, the buttons and, if you can reach it, the box in the cellar where the wires meet, sent to (347) 934-8335 or on WhatsApp, often tells us enough. When the system has to be tested on site, that is the $250 service call booked online, and the $250 is credited toward whatever comes next, a repair or a new installation. Where both are realistic, you get both prices and a straight answer about which we would choose. Repairs have their own page on our site; this page is about installation: a first buzzer for a house that never had a release, a full replacement where the old system is finished, and adding apartments or doors to one that still works.

The reason for testing first is that appearances mislead in both directions. ButterflyMX, writing about small buildings, puts it plainly: whether old wiring can be reused "cannot be confirmed from a photo, the age of the building, or the look of a wall station," and owners should "not replace a system based on age alone."

Six kinds of property make up nearly every buzzer job we plan in New Lots. They differ in who lives behind the door, how many doors people actually use, how the cable can climb from the stoop to each floor and who has to agree before work starts, and each has one mistake that keeps showing up when the job is done in a hurry.

Two apartments stacked over a cellar, one street door to a shared hall, the owner usually on the ground floor, and a bell or buzzer that may never have had a release.

A third household on the top floor or below grade, often added years after the house was built, with its own door at the side or under the stoop.

Avenue walk-ups

Storefront buildings

Shops, salons, delis and storefront churches on New Lots, Livonia and Sutter with apartments above, reached by a separate narrow door beside the shop window.

Larger buildings put up along Livonia Avenue and the side streets in the last decade, with developer-installed entry systems and ground-floor community and retail spaces.

Attached brick two-families: one hall, two households, one person who usually answers

This is the house that defines New Lots. A brokerage listing for 690 to 702 New Lots Avenue describes a run of six two-family buildings two blocks from the subway, and a 2020 listing in the local paper offered a brick two-family on Berriman Street between New Lots and Hegeman Avenues. In the typical layout, the street door opens to a small vestibule or a hall with the stair up to the second floor, the owner's apartment door is on one side of that hall, and the cellar door is under the stair or at the back. The bell, where there is one, is a pair of buttons by the door wired to chimes inside, sometimes with a release button in the owner's apartment and none upstairs.

The installation that works here gives each household its own button outside, its own station or phone answering inside, and a release that opens only the street door. The upstairs tenant's station goes on the second-floor landing or inside the apartment door, depending on how the owner and tenant prefer it. The owner's apartment door and the cellar door are not part of the system at all; they keep their own keys.

Three-families and houses with a lower unit: the third household and its own door

Many New Lots houses have a third household that the original builder never planned for, on the top floor or below grade. The Citizens Housing & Planning Council's study of the district's basement pilot found that two-thirds of the below-grade spaces it assessed already had a bedroom and about three-quarters had a bathroom, and that 31 percent had at least one door leading straight outside.

If the lower household uses its own door at the side or under the stoop, it gets its own single-button station at that door, wired to its own answering unit and its own release, and it does not appear on the front plate at all. If everyone uses the street door, the plate gets a third button and the lower household gets a station like the others. What we will not do is put a release on a below-grade door that could stop someone inside from getting out. Every door on the system opens from inside with the lever or knob, no key or code, and that matters most on the door that may be the only way out of a basement.

One-family row houses: a doorbell that works, a camera and sometimes a lock

East New York also has long runs of narrow attached one-family houses, among them the owner-occupied Nehemiah homes that the East Brooklyn Congregations began building in East New York and Brownsville in 1982. What their owners usually ask for is a wired video doorbell that rings inside and on their phones, sometimes a release or a smart lock on the front door so a relative can be let in remotely, and a chime that can be heard at the back of the house and on the top floor.

Room is the constraint on these fronts: the wall between the door frame and the front window can be a few inches of brick or siding, already holding a mailbox, a house number and a light. We choose a station slim enough for the strip that is there, use the existing doorbell wire where it tests well, and run a new cable through the door frame or the wall behind the station when it does not.

Avenue walk-ups: a panel, a foyer station in every apartment and a public record

The walk-ups on New Lots Avenue and the side streets near it are the buildings that HPD inspects most often for door and buzzer problems, because they are multiple dwellings with tenants who can file complaints and inspectors who come out. Their systems usually follow the classic pattern: a panel of buttons by the street door, a pair of wires to each apartment, an audio station in each foyer with a talk button and a door button, a strike in the street door and a transformer in the cellar.

Replacing a system like this starts with the cable, not the panel. The panel is sized for the number of apartments plus any the owner plans to add, the strike and the closer are fixed so the door latches every time, and the owner gets a written test sheet for every apartment, which matters when an inspector comes back.

Storefront buildings: the apartments' door and the business's door never share a system

Along the commercial avenues, a typical building has a shop at street level and one to four apartments above, reached by a narrow door to one side of the storefront.

We keep two systems that never touch. The apartments get a panel at their own door that calls only the apartments and releases only that door, powered from the building owner's supply. The business, if it wants one, gets its own buzzer at its own door, answered at a counter, a chair or a desk. Nothing a customer or an employee presses downstairs can open the residential door, and no tenant's button can let anyone into the shop after hours.

Newer subsidized buildings: the developer's panel, the community room and the door nobody planned

Livonia Commons, built by Dunn Development, L+M Development Partners and the Housing Partnership along the Livonia Avenue corridor, is the largest recent example: four buildings with 278 affordable apartments and 51 supportive units, and about 28,000 square feet of shops and community space at street level, according to the New York Housing Conference.

Where we come in is usually the edge of the building: a nonprofit or arts group that leases a ground-floor space and needs its own door buzzer, a management office that wants a service or community-room door added to an existing panel, or a small owner nearby who has seen the new buildings' video panels and wants the same for a walk-up.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Close the Door, Count the Households, Then Go Down to the Cellar

This is the order our technician works in at a New Lots house or walk-up, before any price is written. It is our standard practice on these blocks, not an account of one particular job.

First, the door. The technician opens the street door, lets it go and watches it. Does it close by itself, and does the latch catch? Is there an iron gate or a storm door in front of it, a police lock or a chain behind it, a strike someone installed and later disconnected?

Second, the households. The technician asks who lives on each floor, who owns the house, whether anyone uses a side or cellar door, and who should be able to let visitors in. An answer such as "my mother on the first floor, a tenant upstairs and my son in the basement" tells us at once how many buttons, stations and releases the house needs.

Third, the cellar. Downstairs, the technician finds where the old bell wire comes in, where the transformer hangs and which ceiling bays run toward the stair. The power supply's new home is chosen there too, somewhere dry and reachable, away from the boiler and above the floor, before the cable route is drawn.

When the Landlord Lives Downstairs

The Owner Answers for Everyone: Planning a Buzzer for a New Lots House Where the Landlord Lives on the Ground Floor

In most of Brooklyn's apartment buildings, the person who owns the entry system never hears it ring. In New Lots, the owner of a two- or three-family usually lives in the house and has been the building's buzzer for years: the bell rings, the owner walks to the door, looks out and opens it, whether the visitor is a relative, a tenant's guest or a delivery driver. A new installation changes that arrangement, and the decisions behind it are not technical ones. They are about who controls the front door of a family home that also has tenants.

The first decision is who can release the street door. The usual answer, and the one we recommend, is every household: each one has its own button outside, its own station inside and its own release, so tenants let in their own visitors and the owner stops being the doorman. Owners who want more oversight than that get it through video and release records, never through a lock only the owner can open, because tenants have to be able to let people into their own homes.

The second decision is which doors stay out of the system. The owner's apartment door, the cellar door, the door from the hall to the backyard and any door to a storage room the owner uses should open with keys only. Only the street door, and a lower household's own entrance if it has one, gets an electric release.

The third decision is how the owner answers when not at home. Owners work night shifts, travel for weeks at a time, spend winters away with family or care for parents who live on another floor. Phone answering lets the owner's call ring a phone anywhere, see the visitor if the station has a camera and release the door remotely, while the wall station inside the apartment still rings for whoever is home.

The last decision is what happens when a tenant leaves. On a wired system, the button and the station stay; the owner changes the name or apartment label on the plate. On an app-based system, the departing tenant's account has to be removed and the new tenant added, and the owner has to hold the administrator login to do it.

The Walk-Up's File

Foyer Stations, the Entrance Call and the Door Hardware: Reading a New Lots Walk-Up's HPD Record Before Pricing a New System

For a walk-up owner, the public record is often where a buzzer project starts, whether or not the owner has looked at it. The HPD entries for 316 New Lots Avenue, republished by LucidRents, are a useful example because they show three different kinds of entry that all trace back to the same entrance. In May 2024 an inspector issued a class A violation to "properly repair or replace the broken or defective intercom system in the foyer" of apartment 2A. In July 2024 the same apartment's entry became a class B violation for a "broken or defective bell buzzer intercom system from building entrance door." In August 2024 two more complaints were logged under "bell/buzzer/intercom." Over the same two years, and again in January 2025 and August 2026, the building was cited for self-closing doors with defective hinges, a missing or defective latch and a defective door and frame, including the door to the roof bulkhead.

Read together, those entries describe a building whose entry chain is failing at several points at once: the station in the apartment, the call from the street, and the doors that are supposed to close and latch behind people. When we price a new system for a walk-up, we start from the building's own record, list every violation or complaint that touches the entrance, the intercom or the doors, and make sure the quote addresses each of them or says plainly that it is outside our work.

The timing matters as much as the scope. HPD's Clear Violations page gives owners 90 days to correct a class A violation, 30 days for class B and 14 days for a class C self-closing door violation, and the certification of correction has to be filed by the date on the notice, either through HPD's free eCertification system or on a notarized paper form. HPD's guide adds that eCertification is open only to owners, officers and managing agents on a current registration, so the installer cannot certify for the owner. It is also blunt about what a certification is worth if the work is not real: "A certification will be invalidated if, upon inspection, the condition still exists," and HPD "may audit any certification of correction submitted to the agency."

So the practical order is this. The door hardware, the closer, hinges and latch, comes first, because its correction period is the shortest and because a buzzer cannot pass a test on a door that does not close. The intercom work follows on a schedule that fits inside the class B period where possible, and the owner gets, at handover, a test sheet showing every apartment called, answered and released from the street door, with the date.

Tenants see the same problem from the other side. Brick Underground's guide to broken buzzers describes one renter who waited three months for an inspector after a 311 complaint about a malfunctioning intercom and a propped-open door. And a broken entrance does not stay a private inconvenience for long. In January 2025, BK Reader reported a resident of fifteen years at Boulevard Houses, the NYCHA development in East New York, describing what a broken entrance lock had done to her lobby: "A lot of the kids come in, and the door is not fixed." Doors in public housing are NYCHA's responsibility, and residents there go to NYCHA for repairs, but privately owned walk-ups a few blocks away face the same dynamic on a smaller scale.

Below the Stoop

Cellars, Side Doors and the Room Under the Stoop: Running Buzzer Cable and Power Through a New Lots Basement

In almost every New Lots house we work on, the cellar is the route. The cleanest way to get cable from one to the other is down through the floor near the door, along the cellar ceiling and up beside the stair or through a closet stacked above it.

The cellar is also where most of these houses keep their electric service, their boiler and their water heater, which is why the old bell transformer usually hangs beside one of them. A new buzzer's power supply is small and needs only an outlet, but where it goes matters. Close to a boiler it sits in heat and dust; low on a wall it is in the path of water. The U.S. Geological Survey documented decades ago that the water table in this part of Brooklyn rose sharply after groundwater pumping stopped, and reported basement flooding in the area from 1975 on. We mount the power supply and its battery high on a dry wall, away from the boiler and the floor, on a circuit the owner controls, and label it.

The basement study gives a sense of how often a cellar here is more than a cellar. Of 102 homes assessed in the pilot, 69 percent had cellars rather than basements, and two-thirds of the below-grade spaces already had a bedroom. Only 2 percent of the properties had active fire suppression. Nothing we install restricts anyone's way out of a below-grade room: every door with a release opens freely from inside, and we will not put a magnetic lock or a keyed-both-sides arrangement on a door that may be someone's exit. And where a lower household has its own door, its station and release are on the same battery-backed supply as the rest of the house, so that door still answers and still locks during a short outage.

The side door gets its own decision. Many New Lots houses have a door at the side, down a narrow passage or a driveway, that leads to the cellar stair or the yard. If a household uses it as its entrance, it can go on the system with its own station. If nobody should be coming in that way, it stays locked and off the system, and we say so on the quote, because a door that everyone assumes someone else locked is the one left open.

The Rules

What New York Law, HPD and the Building Code Expect at a New Lots Front Door

  • Which buildings must have an intercom. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires every class A multiple dwelling built or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments to have a self-locking main door and an intercommunication system that lets residents talk to a visitor and release the door. Older buildings must install one if tenants of a majority of apartments ask in writing. Most New Lots two- and three-family houses fall outside that rule; walk-ups that went up before 1968 are covered only if tenants ask.
  • Once it is there, it is inspected. HPD's record for 316 New Lots Avenue shows inspectors writing up a defective bell, buzzer and intercom at a building entrance as a class B condition, with a 30-day correction period, and self-closing door defects as class C, with 14 days.
  • Free exit, always. Every door with a buzzer still has to open from inside without a key, a code or a button. An electric strike leaves the inside lever or knob working, which is why it is our default; Allegion's guide notes that "you can exit by turning the lever" whether the strike is fail-safe or fail-secure.
  • Magnetic locks come with conditions. The building code's sensor-release provisions call for a sensor on the egress side, a push-to-exit device near the door, release on loss of power and on fire alarm activation, and listed hardware. On a house or walk-up door, a strike is almost always simpler and safer.
  • Fire-rated doors keep their rating. Allegion notes that fire doors need fail-secure strikes, which stay latched in a fire. Stair and hall doors in walk-ups and the doors between a storefront and the residential hall are often rated, and we read the label before choosing hardware.
  • Reach. On the doors of businesses and other public spaces, buttons and keypads go between 15 and 48 inches above the ground, the reach range in the 2010 ADA Standards, and we use the same range on residential doors wherever the stoop allows.

None of this is legal advice about a particular building; an owner with a specific question about obligations, a violation or a tenant's rights should ask an attorney or HPD directly. What we can do is install a system that meets the technical side of each rule and give the owner the paperwork that shows what was installed and tested.

Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Installation in New Lots: Questions Answered

Houses where the owner lives too

Our front door has a police lock, the steel bar that braces it from inside. Will an electric release still let a visitor in when the bar is set?

No. A brace lock holds the door shut no matter what the latch does, so while the bar is in place the electric strike can release the latch and the door still will not move. That is not a fault in the buzzer; it is what the bar is for. Most owners keep the bar for night-time, when everyone is home, and leave it off during the day so the buzzer works. If a household upstairs needs to let people in at night, the bar has to stay off or the house needs a stronger strike and frame instead, which we can price.

Our stoop is closed in by an iron enclosure with its own locked gate. Should the door station go on that gate or at the house door behind it?

Wherever the visitor stops. If the enclosure gate is kept locked, the visitor cannot reach the house door, so the station belongs at the gate and the release has to work the gate's lock, which means bringing power to a gate that swings, usually through a flexible loop at the hinge side. If the gate stands open during the day and only the house door locks, the station goes at the house door as usual.

Our tenant upstairs lets in her own visitors, which is fine, but we would like to know when the street door is released. Can the system tell us?

Some systems can. App-based and networked stations can keep a record of calls and releases, and some can send the owner a notification when the door opens; a door contact on the street door can do the same on almost any system. A basic wired audio system cannot. If you want this, tell us at the walkthrough so we choose equipment that supports it, and tell your tenant too: a household should know if the system records when its visitors are let in.

The old bell transformer is screwed to a joist right next to our boiler. Is that a bad place for the new power supply?

Usually, yes. A boiler throws off heat and dust, its area needs to stay clear for service, and the space around it is often where water collects when something leaks. We move the new power supply and battery to a dry wall a few feet away, high enough to stay clear of the floor, near the electric service and on a circuit the owner controls. If there is no suitable outlet there, a licensed electrician adds one before we install, and that cost is on the quote. The old transformer is disconnected and left safe.

Walk-ups and buildings on the avenues

Kids press every button on our walk-up's panel on their way home from school. Is there an installation choice that cuts down on those calls without making it harder for real visitors?

Nothing stops a hand from pressing a button, but a few choices help. Video stations let residents see who is calling and ignore it, which takes the fun out of it quickly. A camera on the panel, and a panel placed where it can be seen from the street, discourage it. A code panel, where a visitor has to enter an apartment number, makes random calls slower than a column of buttons.

A fire upstairs damaged the hall and the old intercom, and our insurer is paying for the repairs. What does an adjuster usually want from the buzzer installer?

A clear, itemized written price for putting back what was there, and a separate line for anything that would be an upgrade. We photograph the damaged panel, stations, wiring and power supply before anything is removed, note the make and model where we can read them, and list door hardware, cable, stations and labor separately, so the adjuster can match each line to the loss. Whether a particular item is covered is between the owner and the insurer; we provide the facts and the price, and do not order equipment until the owner approves the written quote.

The door at the back of our walk-up's hall opens to the yard where tenants keep the bins. Does that door need anything from a buzzer job?

It needs attention, not a station. Visitors should never be let in through the yard, so that door does not go on the system. What it needs is to close and latch on its own, open freely from inside, and lock from outside so that anyone in the yard who does not live there cannot walk into the hall. We check its closer and latch during the walkthrough, because a street door that locks perfectly does little good if the yard door behind it is held open with a brick.

I own a walk-up on New Lots Avenue and a two-family a few blocks away. Can both be managed from one account?

If both get app-based or networked systems from the same maker, often yes: one administrator account can hold more than one site, and you add or remove residents at either from your phone. If one building gets a wired audio system with no network connection, there is nothing to manage remotely, and the account question only applies to the other.

Getting the work done in a lived-in house

Our old bell wire still runs up inside the walls from the cellar. Can the new cable be pulled through on it, so nothing has to be opened?

Sometimes. If the old wire runs loose in a clear channel, we can tie the new cable to it in the cellar and pull from above, and it comes out where the old wire did. If the old wire is stapled to the studs or plastered into the wall, which is common in older houses, it will not move, and we fish the new cable separately with rods and tape through the same bays. Where a small opening is unavoidable we tell you first, and the patching and painting are left to your own painter or contractor.

How much dust and noise should we expect inside the house on installation day?

Less than most people expect, with a few loud minutes. Drilling through brick for the door station and through the floor near the door is the noisy, dusty part, and it is short; we lay drop cloths, hold a vacuum at the bit and clean up as we go. Mounting stations upstairs means a few small holes in each apartment. We arrive within a two-hour window, tell each household when we will need to be in their apartment, and the street door is locking again before we leave each day.

Old cable from three past doorbells and a cable company is stapled across our brick front. Can that come down as part of the job?

The doorbell and buzzer cable that is ours to remove, yes. Once the new system works, we take down the dead bell and intercom wire we can trace and confirm is out of service, and pull its staples. Cable belonging to a phone, internet or television provider is not ours to touch; if some of it is dead, the provider can remove it.

Choosing what goes in

Money is tight this year. Which parts of a buzzer installation are worth paying for now, and which can wait without being wasted?

Pay now for the door and the wiring. A closer and latch that work, a strike that fits the frame, a power supply with a battery, and cable to every household that tests clean are the parts that make any system reliable, and redoing them later costs twice. The stations themselves are where you can economize: audio now, with cable chosen so video stations can replace them later, or phone answering for one household now and the others when they want it.

For a stoop that faces the street all day, which holds up better: a metal push-button station or a touchscreen panel?

For a two- or three-family on a New Lots stoop, we generally prefer a metal station with physical buttons. It takes rain, cold and hard use well, it can be read and pressed by anyone, and a damaged button is a small repair. Touchscreen panels suit a larger walk-up or a building with a directory too long for buttons, but they need shade from direct sun, a sheltered spot and more careful mounting.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Door Buzzer Installation in New Lots

Search summaries and AI assistants answer buzzer questions from national cost guides and vendor blogs. Here are seven claims we hear repeated, each checked against what we find at New Lots doors.

"Intercom installation costs between $1,500 and $15,000"

That is HomeAdvisor's national range, with an average of $2,500. For a New Lots two-family it starts too high, and for a walk-up it says nothing useful about where in that span the job will land.

On our Brooklyn list, an audio buzzer with a release at one door is $400 to $900, a video station with answering for each household in a small house or building is $700 to $1,800, and a full panel for a larger walk-up starts around $2,500 and can pass $10,000. The difference comes from the number of households, the condition of the old wire and how many doors need work.

What a national range cannot show is the local cost driver that matters most here: the door. A closer that has to be replaced, a frame that has been forced and repaired, an iron gate in front of the house door. We price those as their own lines so an owner can see them.

"Wireless systems are the best option if you want to DIY"

HomeAdvisor says exactly that, and for a doorbell it can be true. A Brooklyn owner on a Brownstoner thread about a multi-family house described the common version: "The doorbell wiring was too old and we opted to get the plug in bells that sync with the doorbell cam."

That solves the ringing, not the door. A plug-in chime tells a household someone is there; it does not let that household open the street door, so someone still walks down. And radio signals have to get through floors and brick walls between the stoop and the top apartment, which in a solid New Lots house is not something to assume.

The release is the part that has to be wired: a strike in the frame, a power supply and a cable from each household's station. Once that cable is in, the stations on it can be anything from audio to video.

"App subscriptions only add a few dollars per apartment"

Swiftlane's cost guide puts software subscriptions at "$5 to $15 per unit per month, or $30 to $100 per door per month."

Multiplied out, it is not. A three-family at $5 to $15 per unit pays $15 to $45 a month, or $180 to $540 a year, for as long as the system runs; an eight-apartment walk-up pays $40 to $120 a month. For an owner-occupied house on a family budget, that can exceed the cost of the stations within a few years.

App answering is worth it for some households, and a wired system can include phone answering for one floor while the others use wall stations. We show the monthly cost, if any, next to the installation price, and a wired system can run with no fee at all.

"Only buildings with eight or more apartments need to worry about their intercom"

That is a simplified reading of Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a, which requires an intercom in newer class A buildings of eight or more apartments and in older ones when a majority of tenants ask in writing.

It leaves out what happens once an intercom exists. HPD's record for 316 New Lots Avenue shows a tenant complaint turning into a class B violation for a defective bell, buzzer and intercom at the building entrance, with a 30-day correction period. Inspectors write up what is installed and broken, not only what the law requires a building to have.

For a two- or three-family owner, the practical point is simpler: tenants expect the buttons by the door to work. A system that is installed properly and tested on handover keeps that expectation met.

"A magnetic lock is the simplest way to add a release"

Allegion's guide is clear, though, that "electromagnetic locks are only available fail safe," meaning the door unlocks when power is lost.

On a street door, that turns every outage into an open house. To be installed properly, a magnetic lock also needs the building code's sensor-release arrangement: an egress-side sensor, a push-to-exit device, release on loss of power and fire alarm, listed hardware. None of that is simple on a two-family's front door.

For nearly every New Lots door, a fail-secure electric strike, with a closer that shuts the door firmly, is the better answer. It stays locked during an outage, leaves the inside knob or lever free and adds nothing to the outside of the house.

"Old buzzer wiring can be reused"

Sometimes. Aiphone's upgrade guidance says "if the existing wires are in good condition, they can be reused," and one installer on Reddit's access-control forum described a typical plan: "keep the existing GT system, repair the damaged cable, and only upgrade those tenants that desire app connectivity."

The condition is the whole point. ButterflyMX warns that whether wire can be reused "cannot be confirmed from a photo, the age of the building, or the look of a wall station." In New Lots houses, old bell wire may have been cut and spliced during a renovation, run through a damp cellar or stapled to a beam that later got wet.

So we test each pair before quoting, mark which pass, and price new cable only where a pair fails.

"Installation takes one to two hours per station"

Brick Underground's intercom guide gives a building-level view: about a day for a five-apartment building and about two days for a hundred apartments.

In a New Lots house, the time goes into the door and the route more than the stations. Adjusting or replacing a closer, cutting a strike into an old frame, drilling through brick at the stoop and finding a path from the cellar to the top floor are what fill the day.

For most two- and three-family houses, that is one working day with the street door locking again before we leave. A walk-up with many apartments, or a house where the cable route is hard, can take longer, and the quote says how long.

Do you keep door stations and strikes on the truck, or is equipment ordered for each job?
+

If nobody is home, can the system keep a picture of who rang or let the visitor leave a message?
+

Can the indoor station sit on a table instead of being fixed to the wall?
+

Our front door has a knob that locks by itself every time the door closes. Does that work with a buzzer release?
+

Usually, yes. A self-locking knob works well with an electric strike, because the strike releases the latch from the frame side while the knob stays locked, and the door relocks the moment it shuts. We check that the latch lines up with the strike and that the door closes firmly enough to catch every time, and adjust or replace the closer if it does not.

After the warranty runs out, will you still come back to service a system you installed?
+

Yes. We keep the wiring record and test sheet from your installation, so a later visit starts from what we know about your house. Service after the warranty is booked like any service call and priced on our Brooklyn list.

Can the station inside our apartment be mounted low enough for a family member who uses a wheelchair?
+

Yes. Can one household turn off its ring at night without affecting the rest of the house?

Does cutting the frame for an electric strike make the street door easier to force?
+

Not when it is done properly. A strike replaces the existing strike plate and sits in a pocket cut to fit it, and a fail-secure strike holds the latch as firmly as the plate it replaces. Where an old wood frame is soft or split, we tell you before installing and quote a reinforcement or a carpenter's repair, because a weak frame is weak with or without a buzzer.

Is the old bell wire inside our walls a safety hazard once it is out of service?
+

Bell and buzzer wire is low voltage, and once its transformer is disconnected it carries nothing. The part of an old system that matters for safety is the transformer, which is wired to the house's 120-volt supply, so we disconnect it and leave it safe or have it removed by the electrician.
