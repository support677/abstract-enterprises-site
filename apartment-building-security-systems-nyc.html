The Neighborhood

Why NYC Apartment Buildings Need a Whole-Building Security System

An apartment building in New York City has more ways in than most owners count. There is the front door and its vestibule, of course, but also the service door by the trash, the cellar hatch or bulkhead, the roof door at the top of the stair, the gate to the rear yard, the laundry room that opens onto a side passage, and in many mixed-use buildings a second entrance shared with a store. Each one of those openings is somebody's shortcut. When an owner buys security one piece at a time, a camera after a theft, a new buzzer after a complaint, a padlock after a break-in, the building ends up with a strong front door and a soft back. A whole-building system starts from the list of openings and decides, for each one, how it is locked, who can open it, how the building knows it was opened and what records it.

The owner of an apartment building carries that responsibility personally in a way a tenant never does. HPD's owner guide, The ABCs of Housing, says owners must “provide and maintain security measures, heat, hot and cold water, and good lighting.” The state Multiple Dwelling Law spells out the front door and intercom duty in §50-a, the city's Housing Maintenance Code sets out locks for every apartment and doors that close on their own, and the city's Tenant Data Privacy Act governs any fob, app or biometric reader that collects tenant information. None of that applies to a house. All of it applies the day a building has three or more apartments and a tenant behind each door. We are not lawyers and this is not legal advice, but every system we design is built to those rules, and we name the source so your attorney can check it.

Money matters too, and it cuts both ways. Most apartment buildings in the city are old, many are rent-regulated, and the margin on a small walk-up does not leave room to replace the same intercom twice. On the other side, the most expensive security problems we see are the slow ones: a front door that has not latched properly in a year, a recorder that stopped saving months before the night it was needed, a stack of fobs still opening the laundry room for tenants who moved out in 2019. Those cost the owner in tenant turnover, HPD violations, insurance claims and lost rent long before anyone calls them a security problem. A planned system, maintained on a schedule, is how an owner stops paying for those failures.

This page is written for the person who owns the building, or is about to buy one: the family that has held a six-unit walk-up in Bushwick since the 1970s, the investor closing on a forty-unit prewar elevator building in Washington Heights, the developer whose new mixed-use rental just got its certificate of occupancy. Managing agents running whole portfolios will find their own version on our property management security page for NYC, and owners who only want cameras should go straight to apartment building security camera installation in NYC. Here we cover the whole building, door by door.

New York City facts an apartment building owner should know

  • The intercom duty is the owner's. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires self-closing, self-locking entrance doors and a two-way voice intercom from each apartment to the entrance, with a door release in the apartment, in class A buildings of eight or more apartments built or converted after January 1, 1968. Older buildings must install one when the tenants of a majority of apartments ask in writing.
  • Every apartment needs its own lock. Housing Maintenance Code §27-2043 requires a key lock and key for each apartment's entrance door, and in class A buildings a heavy-duty latch set, a deadbolt and a chain guard.
  • Doors must close themselves. HPD states that apartment and hallway doors are required by law to swing closed and latch by themselves (§27-2041.1). A self-closing door violation is class C, immediately hazardous, with 14 days to correct, and HPD's penalties page lists $250–$500 plus $250 a day.
  • Intercom violations are routine. HPD issued Housing Maintenance Code violations mentioning an intercom in 2025 at these counts: Bronx 1,637, Brooklyn 1,157, Manhattan 1,020, Queens 342 and Staten Island 24 (NYC Open Data).
  • Much of the stock is regulated. The city's Independent Budget Office counted roughly one million rent-stabilized apartments in May 2026, about 41 percent of the city's rentals, with Brooklyn (279,000) and the Bronx (233,000) holding the most.
  • Most buildings are old. About 54 percent of New York City's housing was built before 1947, according to the Citizens Housing and Planning Council's reading of the 2023 Housing and Vacancy Survey, which is why risers, cellar closets and worn door frames shape so many of our jobs.
  • Packages pile up in lobbies. The Manhattan Borough President's 2022 delivery report counted about 2.4 million packages delivered in the city each weekday, roughly 80 percent of them to homes. A June 2026 Patch report on an Ecigone analysis put New York City's estimated package-theft losses at $248.2 million.
  • Smart access is regulated. Local Law 63 of 2021 requires express consent before collecting tenants' credential data, a plain-language privacy policy, encryption and timely deletion, and it bars making a tenant use the smart system to enter their own apartment.

What goes wrong when each system is bought on its own

Owners who come to us after years of piecemeal purchases usually have the same picture. The intercom came from one company, the cameras from another, the fob system from a third and the alarm from a fourth, and each was installed with its own power supply, its own cable and its own login on somebody's personal phone. Nothing talks to anything else, so when a tenant reports a theft from the package room there is no way to match the fob that opened the door with the camera footage of the person who walked through it. When the internet goes down, three systems go dark and no one notices. When a tenant moves out, someone has to remember to remove them from the intercom directory, cancel their fob and delete their app account, in three different places. A whole-building plan does not mean buying everything at once. It means deciding the structure first, so each piece added later fits into it.

Who in the building depends on the security system

The owner needs to know what is installed, what it costs to run, who has the passwords, and what to fix first when the budget is limited. We give owners a written list of every device, every login and every recurring fee, and the logins sit in the owner's name.

The superintendent or handyman is the one who resets the door, swaps a fob and hears the complaints first. Supers need a labeled closet, a short written guide and someone to call. We walk the super through each system before we leave.

The tenants need a front door that opens for them and stays shut for everyone else, a way to let in a visitor or a delivery whether or not they own a smartphone, and a clear answer about what the building records. In a rent-stabilized building, they also need the service to continue without interruption, which shapes how a changeover is scheduled.

The lender, the insurer and the next buyer look at the same system from the outside. A documented, working system with recent service records reads very differently in a property condition report than a lobby full of dead equipment and nobody who knows the password.

What We Install

Apartment Building Security System Services in NYC, Opening by Opening

Each part below answers the question an owner asks about that system and then points to the NYC service page that carries the details and the published prices. Owners of co-op and condominium buildings will find the board-run side on our pages for co-op security systems in NYC and condo security systems in NYC; this page is for buildings with one owner and rent-paying tenants.

The entry intercom and video door station

The intercom is the system tenants use most and complain about most, and it is the one the law names. In an older building the typical setup is an audio panel at the vestibule, a power supply in the cellar, a riser running up through the walls and a handset in each apartment with a talk button and a door button. When it fails, the cause is usually one of five things: the panel, the power supply, a relay, the riser or the door hardware. Our repair visits start with the $250 service call, test from the panel to the apartment and back, and either fix the fault within that visit or quote the larger repair before doing it. The full repair breakdown is on our page for intercom repair in New York City.

Replacement is a different decision. An owner can keep the existing riser and put a new panel and stations on it, move to a video panel that calls tenants' phones so the apartment wiring no longer matters, or run new network cable for an IP system with video stations in the units. Which one is right depends on how the riser tests, how many tenants rely on an in-unit handset, whether the building has a dependable internet line, and whether the owner is willing to carry a monthly platform fee for years. We lay those options side by side in writing. The install side, including the panels we fit and what they cost, is on our intercom installation page for NYC, and owners comparing intercom types for a rental can read our explainer on the apartment intercom system in NYC.

Front door hardware: strikes, mag locks and closers

The best intercom in the city is useless on a door that does not close. Most “buzzer” complaints we answer in rental buildings are really door complaints: a closer that has lost its pull, a strike that has drifted out of line with the latch after years of slamming, hinges that have sagged so the door rubs the saddle, or a release button wired to a relay that sticks. We look at the closer, the hinges, the latch and the strike together on every entrance, because HPD treats a door that fails to close and latch on its own as a class C violation. Published prices on our door buzzer repair page for NYC include $450–$950 for an electric strike with the door aligned.

Magnetic locks suit some doors and not others. A mag lock holds a glass storefront-style door or a steel door with no room for a strike, and it releases cleanly from a reader or an intercom. It also has to release on loss of power and when the fire alarm activates, and the person leaving has to be able to get out without special knowledge. The New York City Building Code sets those conditions in §§1010.1.9.8 and 1010.1.9.9. On older wood doors in walk-ups, a properly set electric strike with a strong closer is usually the more reliable choice, and it keeps the door's mechanical lock working as a backup. We choose per door, and we tell the owner why.

Access control and key fobs for shared doors

Fobs change the economics of a rental building. When a tenant loses a key to the front door, the owner either re-keys the building or lives with an unknown copy in circulation. When a tenant loses a fob, the super cancels it in a minute. A fob or code system also lets the owner give the laundry room, the package room, the bike room and the roof different rules, and see which credential opened which door and when. We install readers, door controllers and the software the owner or super uses to add and cancel credentials, and we set the system up so routine changes never need a technician. Single-door access control starts from $1,500 per door on our access control installation page for NYC.

The Tenant Data Privacy Act sets the ground rules for any of this in a class A building. Before collecting a tenant's credential data, the owner needs express consent in writing or through the app; the owner must publish a plain-language policy; the system must be encrypted and kept up to date; authentication data has to be destroyed within 90 days unless it is anonymized; and a tenant can never be required to use the smart system to enter their own apartment. Biometric readers bring extra scrutiny: Legal Services NYC reported in 2019 that more than 130 tenants of a rent-stabilized complex in Brownsville filed objections with the state when their owner applied to install facial-recognition entry. We recommend fobs, codes and phone credentials for residential buildings and leave biometric entry to owners whose counsel has signed off.

Cameras on the entrances and shared rooms (the short version)

On a whole-building system, cameras have a narrow, practical job: show who came through each entrance, what happened at the mailboxes and in the package room, who was in the laundry and the cellar, and who used the rear door. Placement follows the list of openings above. City law does not require an apartment building to have working cameras at all, as Brick Underground pointed out in a December 2023 column on a Williamsburg tenant's package thefts, so owners decide coverage on its merits. We plan camera layouts as part of the building system, and the detailed camera work for rentals, including counts, retention and recorder choices, lives on our page for apartment building security camera installation in NYC.

Door alarms on the doors that should stay shut

Most buildings have doors that are supposed to stay closed and are not: the roof door propped with a brick for a smoke break, the cellar door left open by a contractor, the rear door held with a trash can so a tenant can bring in groceries. A door position switch wired to an alarm panel or to the access controller tells the building when a door has been held open too long, sounds a local warning and can send the super a notification. On a required exit the hardware has to let people out freely; the alarm only announces the opening. Door contacts, motion sensors and keypads for cellars, storage rooms and vacant units are part of our alarm installation work in NYC, where central-station monitoring is listed at $30 a month with a keypad or $45 a month with the phone app.

Package rooms and mail areas

Packages are the newest load on old buildings. Lobbies designed for a row of mailboxes now take dozens of boxes a day, and couriers need a way in that does not involve buzzing a random tenant. A workable setup gives couriers a code or credential that only opens the vestibule or the package room, expires when it should and can be cancelled without touching tenant fobs; gives the package room its own reader so only residents get in; puts a camera at the room's doorway at face height; and logs every opening. Shane Margereson of Ecigone, quoted by Patch in June 2026, put the goal plainly: “The safest parcel is usually the one that never waits in a lobby or on a doorstep.” We build around whatever shelves or lockers the building already has.

Laundry rooms, cellars, bike rooms and roofs

Shared rooms are where tenants meet strangers they did not let in. A laundry room with its own reader stops people from the street wandering in through a side door. A bike or storage room with a reader and a camera ends the arguments about who took what. Cellars hold the boiler, the meters and often copper pipe worth stealing, so they get door contacts and a camera on the approach rather than inside. Roof doors are the hardest, because they are usually a required exit and the fire code has a say in how they are secured; we use alarmed exit hardware where code allows, coordinate with the building's fire alarm contractor, and never padlock an exit.

Elevator access for amenity floors and commercial tenants

Elevator readers come up in newer rentals with a gym, a roof deck or storage floors, and in mixed-use buildings where an office tenant wants its floors locked after hours. The reader and the wiring to the car are ours; the licensed elevator agency handles the connection inside the controller, and we book that visit jointly. Published starting prices on our elevator access control installation page for NYC are $1,500 per car for a card, fob or code reader in Brooklyn and the Bronx and $1,875 in Manhattan.

The equipment room: recorder, network and backup power

Every modern system rides on cable and a closet. We build the equipment room deliberately: a locked space with ventilation, a small rack or shelf, the recorder, the access controller, the network switch that powers cameras and readers over the same cable, and a battery backup so a power blip does not drop the front door. Cable is pulled, terminated, tested and labeled, and the closet gets a written map. Low-voltage wiring under 50 volts generally needs no electrical permit in New York City under Admin. Code §28-105.4.7, with life-safety systems the exception. Per-drop prices of $150–$300 are on our structured cabling installation page for NYC. Any new 120-volt circuit for a power supply is run by the building's licensed electrician, and we connect from there.

Fire alarm coordination

Fire alarm work in New York City follows its own approval path and is not part of the low-voltage security system on this page. What we do in that field is described on our NYC fire alarm installation page. On security jobs, we coordinate with whoever maintains the building's fire alarm so that every mag lock and electrified lock on an egress path releases when that system activates, as the Building Code requires.

Internet service for the building's own systems

Remote access to cameras, fob changes from a phone and intercom calls to tenants' phones all depend on an internet connection in the building's name. In a lot of small rentals the only connection belongs to a tenant, the laundry vendor or the owner's relative on the top floor, and the security equipment has been borrowing it. We recommend a dedicated line for the building's systems, ending in the equipment closet, with the security devices kept on their own network segment rather than a shared tenant network. A building without one can still run a local system; the owner just will not be able to manage it from off site.

One plan, built in stages

Very few owners replace everything in one year, and they do not need to. What makes a staged approach work is a written plan at the start: which panel platform the building will use, where the controller and recorder will live, which doors get readers, which get alarms and which get cameras, and in what order. Each year's work then fits into that plan rather than becoming another island. When the plan is in writing, the owner can also hand it to the next super, the next contractor or the next buyer.

What we do not provide

We install and maintain equipment. We do not staff lobbies, watch cameras on the owner's behalf or sell security as a monthly subscription. If a building already pays a remote attendant or monitoring company, we can install the panel, cameras and door hardware that company's service runs on and connect to it as that provider documents. Central-station monitoring for intrusion alarms is available on the terms listed on our alarm page.

Building Specialists

Apartment Building Security Systems for New York City's Building Types

The same five systems look very different in a three-story walk-up and a twelve-story prewar elevator building. Before we price anything, we identify the building type, because it decides where the cable can go, where the equipment can live, how many tenants rely on in-unit handsets and which city rules weigh most.

Small walk-ups (3–8 units)

One entry, a narrow vestibule, an audio intercom and a cellar. Often below the eight-unit line in §50-a, but tenants still expect a working buzzer. A new panel on the existing wiring, a reliable strike and closer, and one camera at the mailboxes cover most needs.

Larger walk-ups (9–20 units)

Five or six stories, a rear yard and often a roof used by tenants. Door alarms on the roof and rear doors and a fob reader on the laundry room earn their keep here.

Prewar elevator buildings

Plaster walls, risers buried behind them and a cellar full of abandoned cable. We test the riser before quoting and decide between reusing it and moving to phone-based answering.

Rent-stabilized buildings

Long-term tenants, many without smartphones, and a state agency that treats a broken lock as a decrease in service. Any changeover keeps an in-unit or phone option and avoids gaps in entry.

Postwar elevator buildings

Larger lobbies, garages, several entrances and sometimes a desk. Access control and door alarms carry the load; the intercom may ring the desk first.

New mixed-use rentals

A residential entry beside stores, amenity floors and systems chosen by the developer. The first job is getting the admin logins and licenses into the owner's name.

Brownstone and rowhouse rentals

Two to four units behind a stoop, with a parlor-floor entry and a garden-level door. Doorbell-style video stations and good door hardware beat a commercial panel.

Landmarked buildings

Equipment on the facade follows the Landmarks Preservation Commission's rules on location and count. We plan panels within the door opening and keep cameras small and few.

How the method changes with the building

In a walk-up nearly everything happens at the front door and in the cellar, and a single day on site can change the whole building. In a prewar elevator building, the riser decides the budget: a riser that tests clean means a panel swap and new stations only where they fail; a riser that does not means either new cable through occupied walls, which takes apartment access, or a system that calls tenants' phones and no longer needs the apartment wiring at all. In a postwar building with several entrances, the work spreads across doors and the challenge is never taking two entrances down at once. In a new building, the work is administrative before it is physical: getting the developer's integrator to hand over the logins, confirming which software licenses the owner actually holds, and writing down what is installed.

Materials matter as well. Brick and stone take sleeve anchors and need a hood or recess for an outdoor panel; old plaster has to be opened carefully so a box does not take part of the wall with it; aluminum storefront frames need hardware rated for thin-wall tube. Cable follows existing chases and dumbwaiter shafts where they exist and surface raceway where they do not, and the owner is told before ordering where raceway will show. Nobody should learn on install day that a run had to be exposed across a lobby.

Rent-stabilized buildings: keeping service continuous

New York State Homes and Community Renewal tells owners of regulated apartments that they “are required to maintain all required services and equipment in good working order,” and it lists broken door locks among the examples of a decrease in services that tenants can complain about, apartment by apartment or building-wide. For an owner, that turns a security upgrade into a scheduling job as much as an equipment job. We build the new entry panel beside the old one and switch over in a short window, keep in-unit stations or phone answering for every tenant who needs them, and never leave a building without a working door release overnight. Whether an improvement qualifies for any rent adjustment is a question for HCR and your counsel, not for us; HCR's own pages on individual apartment and building-wide improvements are the place to start.

Landmarked buildings and historic districts

The Landmarks Preservation Commission protects more than 38,000 properties, most of them in its historic districts, and a lot of them are apartment buildings. Its Permit Guidebook chapter on health and safety work allows staff-level permits for intercom panels set within or next to the door opening, one per opening, and limits the number of cameras on a facade by its width. Ordinary repair and in-kind replacement is treated differently from new work. The owner files with LPC; we design to the locations and sizes that chapter describes, send the owner product cut sheets for the application, and install only after the permit is in hand.

Mixed-use buildings with stores on the ground floor

A residential entrance next to two or three storefronts is one of the most common building types in the city, and it needs clear boundaries. The stores keep their own alarms and cameras under their own accounts. The residential side keeps its own entry, fobs and recorder. Where the two share a cellar, a rear yard or a trash room, we put a reader or a contact on the shared door and agree in writing with the owner who can see which cameras. That keeps a store's employee from browsing the residential lobby footage, and keeps a tenant's fob from opening a shop's back door.

For Owners & Investors

Buying, Inheriting and Planning Security for a NYC Apartment Building

Security systems in due diligence before you close

Buyers spend weeks on the roof, the boiler and the rent roll and a few minutes on the security systems, then discover after closing that the intercom has three open HPD violations and the camera recorder belongs to the seller's nephew. A pre-closing look at the systems answers a short list of questions: what is installed at each opening, does it work, who holds the admin logins, which services carry a monthly contract and can that contract be assigned, and what open HPD violations mention the intercom, the doors or the locks. HPD's public violation records show the last of those for any address, and the seller's own service invoices usually answer the rest. A buyer who walks the building with those questions in hand knows, before closing, whether the front door is a line item or a project.

Getting control of the accounts after you take over

Hardware rarely goes missing when a building changes hands; access does. A cloud intercom may be registered to the old owner's email, a recorder may be tied to a former super's phone, and the fob software may live on a laptop that left with the last manager. Our first step on any takeover is to establish who owns each account and move administration into the new owner's name, with the super as a limited user. Where a manufacturer requires proof of ownership to reset an account, we tell the owner exactly what that maker asks for. Until that is done, we do not recommend new purchases, because a system the owner cannot log into is a system the owner does not control.

Capital planning over three to five years

After the survey, an owner has a list: each opening, what is there, what condition it is in and what we recommend, with the cost on each line. That list sorts naturally into years. Open violations and doors that do not latch go first. Equipment that can no longer get parts goes next. Systems that work but cost too much to run, such as a platform fee per unit for a panel the tenants barely use, come after that. Each year's budget then covers the next items plus repairs on everything else. Financing is available through Wisetack, subject to approval.

Renovations, gut jobs and the right moment to run cable

The cheapest time to fix security wiring is when the walls are already open. When an owner tells us early about a lobby renovation, a facade job or a run of apartment guts, we walk the space with the general contractor and mark where conduit, back boxes and cable should go for the entry panel, readers, door contacts, cameras and the network back to the closet. The finishes go up over cable that is already in place, and the devices go in at the end without cutting new plaster. Waiting until after the renovation usually means surface raceway across a new lobby or reopening finished walls.

Vendor paperwork, insurance and payment

Individual owners and owning LLCs both get the same paperwork on request: a W-9 for our contracting entity and our service agreement, which sets out the full terms of the job. When a lender, an insurer or a commercial tenant requires a certificate of insurance naming additional insureds, our insurer issues it to the exact wording once the job is awarded. Company owners pay by check or through the online payment link on each invoice.

Insurance, incidents and the records a system keeps

When a tenant reports a theft, a break-in or a fall, the owner's first questions are what happened and when. A camera system that keeps enough days of footage with its clock set correctly, and an access system that logs which credential opened which door, answer those questions in minutes, and insurers and attorneys often ask for exactly that record. We set retention and clocks at installation and show the owner and super how to export a clip and a door log. We do not promise insurance discounts; whether a carrier gives credit for a system is between the owner and the carrier.

Compliance callouts for owners

  • Entrance and intercom: Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a (self-closing, self-locking entrance door; two-way intercom with release in post-1968 class A buildings of 8+ units; older buildings on a written majority request).
  • Locks and doors: Housing Maintenance Code §27-2043 (apartment door locks, deadbolt, chain guard) and §27-2041.1 (self-closing doors), enforced by HPD.
  • Electrified locks on egress doors: NYC Building Code §§1010.1.9.8 and 1010.1.9.9 (release on power loss and on fire alarm activation; free egress).
  • Tenant data: Local Law 63 of 2021, Admin. Code §§26-3001–26-3006 (consent, policy, encryption, deletion, no required smart access to a tenant's own unit).
  • Facades of landmarks: LPC Permit Guidebook, chapter 11 (intercoms within or next to the door opening; limits on cameras).
  • Wiring permits: Admin. Code §28-105.4.7 (no electrical permit for low-voltage signaling and data wiring under 50 volts, life-safety systems excepted).

Repair or replace: how we advise an owner

Owners ask us every week whether to keep fixing an old system or replace it. We look at four things. Parts: can we still get stations, panels and boards for this model, or are we pulling them from other buildings? Frequency: how many service calls has the building logged in the past year, and are they the same fault coming back? Tenant impact: are complaints, HPD violations or HCR complaints building up? Capability: does the owner need something the old system cannot do, such as cancelling a fob from a phone or ringing a tenant's cell? If parts are available and calls are rare, we recommend repairing. If parts are gone or the same fault keeps returning, we put the replacement cost next to a year of repairs and let the owner decide.

What changes from 6 units to 200

At six units, the decisions are about one door: the panel, the strike, the closer and whether tenants get a handset or a phone call. At forty units, the directory, the fobs and the package area start to matter more than the panel itself, and the rear and roof doors deserve alarms. At a couple of hundred units with several entrances, the system becomes a small network: a controller for many doors, a recorder or server for many cameras, user permissions for staff, and a documented cable backbone. The cost per apartment usually falls as the building grows, while the share of the budget spent on cable, software and documentation rises.

Your Questions Answered

Apartment Building Security Systems in NYC: Questions Answered

Budget and priorities

How does the budget for a whole-building system differ between a 12-unit walk-up and a 90-unit elevator building?

The walk-up's budget is mostly one entrance: a panel, a strike and closer set together, stations or phone answering for twelve apartments, a door alarm or two and a few cameras. The elevator building adds several entrances, a longer riser that may or may not test clean, fobs across shared rooms, elevator readers if there are amenity floors, and a real equipment room. We do not quote either by the apartment before seeing it, because the riser test and the number of controlled doors move the number more than the unit count. Published component prices on our NYC service pages, listed in the pricing section below, show what each piece costs on its own.

If an owner can only afford one improvement this year, what should come first?

The front door. A door that closes and latches on its own every time, with a strike and closer that agree with each other, fixes more complaints and more violations per dollar than anything else in the building. After that, the order usually runs: a working intercom path to every apartment, then door alarms on the roof and rear doors, then fobs for the shared rooms, then cameras at the entrances and the package area. A building with open HPD violations should clear those first regardless of the list.

Is it cheaper to upgrade all the systems at once or spread them over several years?

Doing everything at once saves some labor, because cable for the panel, readers and cameras can be pulled on the same days and the closet is built once. Spreading the work saves cash flow and lets each year's experience guide the next. Most owners do a mix: one project that builds the closet, the backbone cable and the entry, then readers, alarms and cameras added in later years onto that backbone. What costs money is upgrading without a plan and having to redo cable later.

Doors, locks and the rules

What lock hardware does the Housing Maintenance Code require on apartment entrance doors?

Housing Maintenance Code §27-2043 requires a key lock and key for every apartment's entrance door and, in class A multiple dwellings, a heavy-duty latch set, a heavy-duty deadbolt and a chain guard. HPD separately requires apartment and hallway doors to close and latch by themselves. When we add a reader or an electrified lock to any door, the required mechanical hardware stays and keeps working.

Why do entrance doors in older rentals keep failing to latch, and what fixes it for good?

Usually three things wearing at once: a closer that has lost its pull, hinges that have sagged so the door drops against the frame, and a strike that has been adjusted so often its screw holes are stripped. Replacing only the strike, which is what most buzzer calls end with, puts a new part on a door that still does not reach it. We fix the hinge or frame first, set the closer's speed and latch zone, then set the strike to the latch, and test the door from a dozen slams before we leave.

Do rent-stabilized tenants have a say when the owner changes the entry system?

Tenants of regulated apartments can file a complaint with New York State Homes and Community Renewal if a required service is reduced, and HCR names broken door locks as one example. So the practical test for an owner is whether every tenant keeps a working way to get in and to let visitors in, before, during and after the change. Keeping in-unit stations or phone answering for tenants who need them, and switching over without a gap, is how we plan it. What the law requires in a specific case is a question for your attorney.

Shared rooms and back doors

How should a package room be secured in a building with no doorman?

Give it its own door and its own reader, separate from the front entrance. Couriers get a code that opens the vestibule or the package room only and that can be cancelled without touching tenant fobs. Residents use their own fob to get in. A camera at the doorway, mounted at face height rather than on the ceiling, records who entered, and the reader log records which credential opened the door at what time. Together they turn a complaint into a lookup.

Does the laundry room really need a reader of its own?

In most buildings, yes, because the laundry room is often the one shared room with a second door to a yard or a side passage. A reader limited to residents stops strangers using it as a way into the building, and a door position switch tells the super when the side door has been propped open. If the laundry vendor needs access to service machines, they get a credential of their own that can be switched off between visits.

How do you secure a roof door that is also a fire exit?

Carefully, and never with a padlock. A required exit must open from the inside without a key or special knowledge, so the security job is to make the opening known rather than to block it. We use exit hardware with a local alarm where code allows, a door contact back to the alarm panel or access controller so the super is notified, and a camera on the stair landing rather than on the roof. Fire code questions specific to a building go to the building's fire alarm contractor and the owner's architect, and we coordinate with them.

What is the best way to protect the cellar, boiler room and storage?

Treat them as a zone with an alarm rather than a room with a camera. Contacts on every door and hatch into the cellar, a motion sensor in the main corridor and a keypad the super uses on the way in and out will catch an intrusion as it starts. A camera on the cellar approach and the sidewalk hatch adds the picture. Copper theft and tool theft from cellars tend to happen when no one is around, which is exactly when an alarm is more useful than a recording.

Ownership, accounts and day-to-day running

Who should hold the admin logins once the system is installed?

The owner, in the owner's or the owning entity's name, with the super and any manager added as limited users. The installer should never be the only person who can get in. At handover we give the owner every admin login, a list of devices with serial numbers, and the renewal date and terms for any software or platform fee, so the system survives a change of super, contractor or owner.

What happens to a cloud intercom contract when an apartment building is sold?

It depends on the provider's terms, which is why a buyer should ask for the contract before closing. Some platforms transfer the account to the new owner on request; others require a new agreement and leave the remaining term with the seller. Either way, the panel and stations stay on the wall. We help the new owner move administration into their name and, if the platform does not make sense for the building, show what replacing it would cost next to keeping it.

Can a landlord without a super or a managing agent run the system from a phone?

Yes, if the system is chosen for it. A cloud-managed panel and access controller let the owner add and cancel fobs, change the directory, set a courier code and open the door for a plumber from an app. Door alarms can send a notification when the roof or rear door is held open. The building needs a reliable internet connection in its own name, and someone local still has to be able to reach the equipment closet when hardware needs attention.

What should a small owner send before the first visit?

The address and borough, the number of apartments and entrances, who will let us in, photos of the lobby panel, the front door from both sides and the cellar or closet where equipment lives, any logins you hold, and one sentence on what is wrong or what you want. If HPD has written violations at the building, send those too. With that list the first visit goes to fixing rather than finding.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Apartment Building Security Systems in NYC

Owners increasingly arrive with an answer from a search summary or a vendor's buying guide already in hand. Those answers are built from national averages and product marketing, and they tend to miss how New York City buildings, laws and tenants actually work. These are the claims we run into most, and what we find on site.

1. “An apartment intercom costs $1,000 to $7,000 per door”

That range appears in an intercom vendor's own cost guide, which also puts mid-size projects at $8,500 to $34,000 and adds software fees of $5 to $15 per unit per month. Those are real numbers for the vendor's product, and they are a reasonable picture of a cloud video panel on a new network.

They are a poor picture of most New York City rentals, where the panel is rarely the expensive part. In a prewar building, the riser, the apartment access needed to reach it, and the closer and strike on a worn door decide the cost. A building whose riser tests clean may need only a panel and a handful of stations; a building whose riser has shorted in three places is a different project at any per-door price.

That is why we publish prices for components, such as a station, a strike or a transformer, rather than per door, and price whole buildings only after we have tested the wiring and counted the doors and apartments.

2. “Access control runs $500 to $5,000 per door plus $20 to $50 per door per month”

Buying guides from access-control platforms quote ranges like these, and to their credit some say the figures come from vendors rather than an independent benchmark. One even states there is “no reliable universal access control cost per unit for apartments.”

What the ranges leave out for an owner is the monthly line. A fee per door or per apartment that looks small on a quote becomes a large number over ten years in a rent-regulated building where the owner cannot pass it on. Many small buildings are better served by a local controller with no subscription, managed on site, with cloud management added only where the owner is not.

We price both ways for the same building, so the owner compares the up-front cost and the ten-year cost before choosing.

3. “Landlords are required to have security cameras”

Summaries often blur what is required with what is common. New York City does not require apartment buildings to have working cameras, a point Brick Underground made in its December 2023 column on a Williamsburg renter whose packages kept disappearing.

What the law does require, in the buildings it covers, is a self-closing, self-locking entrance door, a working intercom with a release from each apartment, proper apartment locks and doors that close by themselves. Those are the items that generate HPD violations.

An owner with a limited budget should therefore spend first on the door and the intercom, then add cameras where the building's own history of incidents points.

4. “Smart locks and phone keys are the modern way into every apartment”

App and phone credentials are useful at the building entrance and on shared rooms, and we install them. But in New York City, Local Law 63 of 2021 bars an owner from requiring a tenant to use a smart access system to enter their own apartment.

The same law requires express consent before collecting credential data, a published privacy policy, encryption, firmware updates and deletion of authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized. Selling the data exposes the owner to statutory damages of $200 to $1,000 for each sale.

So the modern answer is smart credentials on the common doors, with mechanical keys kept at the apartment door, and the privacy policy written by the owner's counsel before the system goes live.

5. “Package theft is a porch problem”

National advice about package theft is written for houses with porches. In New York City the doorstep is a lobby, and the thief is often someone who followed a tenant in or was buzzed in by someone expecting a delivery.

The volume is real: about 2.4 million packages a weekday citywide in the Manhattan Borough President's 2022 count, and an estimated $248.2 million in package-theft losses for the city in an analysis Patch reported in June 2026.

The fix in an apartment building is access, not a doorbell camera: courier codes that open only the vestibule or package room, a resident-only reader on the package room, and a camera that sees faces at its door.

6. “Cloud cameras are always better than a recorder”

Cloud video suits a building with two or three cameras, a strong internet line and nobody to look after a box in a closet. It also adds a monthly fee per camera and stops recording when the internet goes down.

Many rentals have neither the line nor the appetite for the fee. A recorder in a locked, ventilated closet on battery backup keeps recording through an outage, stores weeks of footage for a one-time cost and can still be viewed from a phone when the connection is up.

We choose by camera count, the retention the owner wants and the building's connection, and we often use a recorder for storage with remote viewing on top.

7. “Any camera or intercom can go on the front of the building”

Generic installation guides skip the city's landmark rules. The Landmarks Preservation Commission protects more than 38,000 properties, and many of them are apartment buildings in historic districts.

LPC's Permit Guidebook allows staff permits for intercom panels placed within or beside the door opening, one per opening, and caps how many cameras a facade can carry. Work outside those limits needs a fuller review.

On a landmarked building we design to those limits from the start, give the owner cut sheets for the application and install after the permit is issued, so nothing has to come back off the wall.

Field notes: reading a walk-up in the first hour

Field notes: switching over a building's entry without locking anyone out

What drives the cost of an apartment building security system

Six things move the number. First, the condition of the existing cable: a riser that tests clean keeps the project small, and one that does not changes the plan. A useful quote for an apartment building puts equipment, labor and recurring fees on separate lines and says which existing parts are being reused. Yes. A door position switch tied to the access controller or an alarm panel sounds a local warning when the door is held open longer than it should be and can notify the super. On a required exit, the door still opens freely from inside; the alarm only reports it.

Does an elevator reader need the elevator company on site as well?
+

Yes. We install the reader in the car and the cable back to the equipment room, and the building's licensed elevator agency makes the connection inside the elevator controller. We schedule that visit together with them.

Do the stores in a mixed-use building need separate systems from the apartments?
+

Usually yes. Each store keeps its own alarm and cameras under its own account, the residential side keeps its own entry, fobs and recorder, and any shared door such as a cellar or trash room gets a reader or contact so each side's access stays separate.
