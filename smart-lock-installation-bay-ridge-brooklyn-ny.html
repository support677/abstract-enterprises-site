The Neighborhood

Why Bay Ridge Properties Need Smart Lock Installation Built Around a Timetable

Bay Ridge chose, decades ago, to stay the way it was built. In 1978, after neighbours fought the twin 30-storey Bay Ridge Towers and the 13-storey Shore Hill Apartments, the city created the Special Bay Ridge District, whose zoning text sets out to preserve the existing scale and character of the community. In March 2005 the City Council went further and rezoned 249 blocks inside the district, between 65th Street, Seventh Avenue and Shore Road, raising the number of blocks limited to one- and two-family homes from 33 to 101. The NYU Furman Center counts only 452 housing units added in Bay Ridge and Dyker Heights between 2010 and 2025. So the doors here are, overwhelmingly, the doors of old buildings: limestone and brick rows, tapestry-brick semi-detached houses, detached houses near the water, and the apartment blocks that line Shore Road, Ridge Boulevard and Fourth Avenue.

The people behind those doors stay too, and that is what makes a smart lock job here less about the hardware and more about the calendar. The Furman Center puts homeownership in the district at 41.0 percent in 2024 — well above the Brooklyn norm — and the share of residents aged 65 and over at 20.2 percent, up from 13.1 percent in 2010. A long-settled household is rarely one person with one key. It is a retired owner on the parlour floor, an adult child upstairs who commutes, an aide who arrives at eight, a grandchild who lets herself in after school, a cleaner every other week and a plumber once a year. Each of them needs the door at a different time, and a lock that only knows “open” and “closed” serves none of them as well as one that knows who and when.

The commute stretches that timetable at both ends. Bay Ridge–95th Street has been the end of the R line since 1925, and the Furman Center's mean travel time to work for the district is 46.4 minutes, with close to two-thirds of commuters getting there without a car. A household that leaves at 6:45 and walks back in at 7:30 at night has a house that sits empty, or nearly so, for twelve hours a day. Deliveries, dog walkers and service visits happen in that gap. A smart lock earns its place by letting the right person in during that gap, locking behind them on its own, and leaving a record that says so.

Then there are the houses that hold two households. Community Board 10 describes one- and two-family homes as the major part of its housing, and a two-family in Bay Ridge very often means an owner on one floor and a tenant on the other, sharing a street door and a hallway while keeping completely different hours. The tenant who works nights comes in at two in the morning; the owner wants the street door locked by eleven. Both are reasonable, and both can be built into the same lock if the codes, the schedules and the auto-relock are planned together. Where a house has crept past two households — and the council member for the area reported more than a thousand illegal-conversion complaints from Bay Ridge, Dyker Heights and Fort Hamilton in the four years after 2010 — the rules change, which we cover further down.

Finally, the avenues keep the latest hours of all. The State Liquor Authority lets Kings County bars and restaurants serve until 4 a.m., and Third Avenue has the restaurants and bars to use those hours. In the early morning of Friday, August 28, 2026, three Third Avenue restaurants between 87th and 94th streets were burglarised between 3 and 4:30 a.m., according to PIX11's report of the NYPD account. A Bay Ridge restaurant door therefore has a working day that ends at four, a dead zone until the prep cook arrives, and a strong reason for every staff code to stop working in between. Fifth Avenue and 86th Street, with hundreds of ground-floor businesses between their two business improvement districts, have their own opening and closing rhythms, and the medical offices on and around Bay Ridge Parkway have theirs.

Bay Ridge is not a high-crime neighbourhood — the Furman Center's serious-crime rate for the district was 5.2 per 1,000 residents in 2025, down from 11.4 in 2006. The lessons local news keeps repeating are about habits more than hardware. In August 2019 police connected two suspects to as many as 15 burglaries of Dyker Heights and Bay Ridge homes, which the Brooklyn Home Reporter said were committed “largely” through unlocked windows and doors. A door that locks itself, at a time the household chooses, closes that particular gap without asking anyone to remember anything.

Every job below starts with the same two questions: which doors, and who needs them when. These are the ones Bay Ridge's houses, co-ops and avenues ask for most.

Timed Codes for Every Person

A separate code for each family member, aide, cleaner and walker, each limited to the days and hours that person actually comes, and each named so the history reads “Aide, 8:02 a.m.” rather than “User 6”.

Two-Family Street Doors

One shared front door, two households: separate codes per floor, a nightly lock-up time both agree on, auto-relock so the door is never left on the latch, and admin rights that belong to the owner without exposing the tenant's own door.

Doors for a Parent Living Alone

A lock a parent can work without a phone, codes for the people who help, an alert to a son or daughter if the door hasn't opened by a set hour, and a plan for how emergency services get in without breaking the door.

Restaurant and Bar Doors

Practice and Office Entrances

Dental and medical suites in limestone rows and avenue buildings: a waiting-room door that opens when the first staff member arrives, inner doors that stay locked, and a record of who opened which door and when.

Shop Doors on Fifth Avenue and 86th Street

Opening and closing schedules with holiday exceptions, per-employee codes that come and go with staff, and a lock that suits the door behind the roll-down gate rather than fighting it.

Building Specialists

Smart Lock Installation for Bay Ridge's Architecture

Because the 1978 district and the 2005 rezoning kept so much of the older stock, a Bay Ridge job usually means fitting new electronics to a door that was hung long before anyone thought of them. These are the building families we meet most, and what each one means for a lock.

Limestone Row Houses

Two storeys over a basement, stone fronts, heavy wood doors with glass and sometimes ornamental iron over the glass. The 54 houses on Bay Ridge Parkway between Fourth and Fifth avenues, built between 1906 and 1913, became Bay Ridge's first historic district in 2019. Doors are thick and old; measure first, and keep anything mounted on the stone itself to a minimum.

Tapestry-Brick Semi-Detached and Two-Family Houses

Built in the 1910s and 1920s, many later split into an apartment per floor. The front door is often a shared street door into a small hall, with each unit's own door beyond it, and a side or rear door onto a driveway or yard. These houses are where shared codes, separate schedules and nightly lock-up times matter most.

Detached Houses Near Shore Road and the Narrows

Larger houses on the blocks toward the water, some of them a century old, with front, side, rear and garage-to-basement doors. More doors means more decisions about which ones go smart and which keep a good mechanical deadbolt, and a longer walk from the router to the farthest lock.

Shore Road Co-op Buildings

From pre-war buildings like the Art Moderne Normandy at 9437 Shore Road, which opened as a rental in 1940, to post-war towers like 9201 Shore Road, completed in 1960 with 254 apartments. Unit doors are usually steel or steel-clad in steel frames, and the board and managing agent have a say before anything is drilled.

Walk-Ups and Mid-Rise Rentals

Four- to six-storey buildings scattered through the side streets and lining Fourth Avenue and Ridge Boulevard. A tenant may add a lock of their own under state law; the owner is responsible for the street door. The two are different jobs with different rules, and we keep them apart.

Mixed-Use Avenue Buildings

Third-, Fourth- and Fifth-Avenue buildings with a business on the ground floor and flats above, reached by a separate street door beside the shopfront. The shop, the stair door and each apartment need three separate code plans that never overlap.

Materials and methods, door by door

  • Thick old wood doors. Many residential smart deadbolts are made for doors between 1⅜ and about 2 inches thick — Allegion lists 1⅜″ to 2″ for Schlage's Arrive deadbolt — so a heavy row-house door is measured before a lock is ordered, and a thickness kit or a different model is chosen where needed.
  • Backset and old bores. Standard smart deadbolts expect the bore centre 2⅓″ or 2¾″ from the edge. Older doors drilled for earlier hardware are plugged with glued hardwood and re-bored, rather than having a new lock perched over a hole that doesn't match.
  • Glass and ironwork. On doors with a glazed panel and decorative iron, the keypad has to clear the grille and the glass rail. Where glass sits within arm's reach of the inside thumb-turn, we say so plainly at the walkthrough.
  • Steel co-op doors. Steel-clad unit doors in steel frames call for the right drill bits, sleeves and fasteners, and for confirming with the managing agent whether the door is fire-rated before any change to it.
  • Strikes in old frames. A motorised bolt needs to throw cleanly every time or it drains batteries and reports faults. On frames that have shifted, the strike is moved or rebuilt so the bolt runs without rubbing — especially important when the lock relocks by itself on a timer.
  • Weather on the stoop. A keypad facing the street on an exposed stoop takes rain, sun and winter salt. A gasket, a model rated for outdoor exposure and sometimes a sheltered inner door are part of the choice.

Mid-rise rentals and co-ops with ground-floor offices and a few storefronts. The owner's street door and lobby fall under building rules; ground-floor tenants need their own entrance planned so it never opens the residential lobby.

Smaller shops, services and offices toward Fort Hamilton and Dyker Heights, many with flats above. Straightforward keypad deadbolts or reader-and-strike doors, planned so the shop's codes and the residents' codes never cross.

Compliance callouts for Bay Ridge business doors

  • Free way out. A door people leave through must open from the inside without a key, tool or special knowledge. A keypad on the outside is fine; a lock that traps staff or customers inside is not. Electrified locks are planned so they release on the egress side.
  • Roll-down gates. The city's long-standing requirement for more transparent gates on replacement was the subject of a 2026 Council vote to repeal its July 1, 2026 deadline (reported by NY1); DOB permit rules for gates did not change. We fit the door's lock to work with whatever gate is there and leave gate work to gate specialists.
  • Historic district. On Bay Ridge Parkway between Fourth and Fifth avenues, anything new on the facade may need the Landmarks Preservation Commission. The LPC's guidebook says replacing hardware such as locks and handles generally needs no permit, but readers, intercom panels and wiring on the facade are a different matter.
  • Staff data. A code log names employees and times. Keep it to the people who need it, and decide in advance how long you keep it.
Your Questions Answered

Smart Lock Installation in Bay Ridge: Questions Answered

Most of what Bay Ridge households ask us comes down to time: when a code works, when the door locks itself, what the lock should say when nothing happens, and how to read what it recorded. The questions are grouped that way.

Clocks, calendars and schedules

The clocks go back an hour in November — do the timed codes on our lock move with them, and what keeps the lock's own clock right?

On a lock that is connected to the internet, usually yes. The lock or its bridge takes the time from the manufacturer's servers, which know the local time zone and the daylight-saving dates, so a code set for 7:30 to 9:00 a.m. keeps meaning 7:30 to 9:00 local time on the Monday after the change. In the United States daylight saving time ends on the first Sunday in November and starts on the second Sunday in March, and a connected lock should cross both without anyone touching it.

The weak spot is a lock that has lost its connection, or one that never had it. A Bluetooth-only lock keeps time with its own internal clock and only resets it when a phone with the app comes close enough to sync. If it has been out of contact for weeks, or if its batteries ran flat and it restarted, its clock can be wrong, and every schedule moves with it. That is how an aide's code ends up working an hour late, or a curfew starting at midnight instead of eleven.

Our habit is simple. We set the lock's time zone explicitly during setup rather than trusting a default, we check the lock's reported time against a phone before we leave, and we show you where the app displays it. After every battery change, and on the Monday after each clock change, open the app near the door once and look at the time. If it's right, every schedule is right.

Our cleaner comes every other Thursday — can a lock schedule follow a fortnightly pattern?

It depends on the app, and many can't. Most residential lock apps build recurring codes from days of the week and a time window: every Thursday, 9 a.m. to 1 p.m. Far fewer offer “every second week” or a custom repeat. If yours doesn't, a weekly Thursday code gives the cleaner access on the off-weeks too, which is exactly the slack you were trying to remove.

There are three practical workarounds. The tidiest is a code with a start and end date for each visit, scheduled a few weeks ahead — tedious to set up, but exact. Some apps allow a code to be switched on and off with a tap, so you leave it disabled and enable it on the morning of a cleaning day; that only works if someone remembers. And some smart-home platforms that a lock can join through Matter, Z-Wave or a hub let you write a fortnightly rule that enables and disables the code automatically.

At the walkthrough, tell us the patterns you actually need — fortnightly cleaners, alternate-weekend custody, a relative who visits on the first Sunday of the month — and we'll choose a lock and platform that can express them, or set up the dated codes for you and show you how to extend them.

Can the street door of our two-family lock itself at 11 every night, whoever left it on the latch?

Yes, on many smart locks, and it's one of the most useful settings for a shared front door. There are two features to know apart. Auto-relock throws the bolt a set time after the door closes or is unlocked; it handles the everyday case of someone coming in and forgetting. A scheduled lock-up, where the lock or a smart-home rule locks the door at a fixed time each night, handles the cases auto-relock doesn't — a hold-open or “passage” mode someone switched on to bring in shopping, or a door unlocked from the app for a visitor who has since left.

For a two-family, we usually set both. Auto-relock with a delay long enough to get a stroller or a bag of groceries through, and a nightly lock-up time agreed by both households, so that whatever happened during the day the street door is bolted by eleven. Household codes still open it at any hour; the schedule only makes sure it doesn't stay open.

One condition: the bolt must line up cleanly with the strike every time, or a lock that relocks itself on a timer will jam, buzz and drain its batteries. That's why we adjust the strike and the door before we switch automatic locking on, and why we test it several times with the door closed gently, the way a tired person closes it at night.

Relatives fly in from abroad for six weeks every summer — can their code start the day they land and stop the day they fly home?

Yes. Most lock apps can create a code that is valid between two dates, sometimes called a temporary or date-range code. Set it to begin on the arrival day and expire the evening of the departure, and it switches itself on and off without anyone remembering. During those six weeks it works like a household code, at any hour; you can add a daily time window as well if you'd rather it didn't work in the small hours.

It is worth giving a long-stay visitor a code rather than a spare key, for the same reason as everyone else: a code can't be copied at a hardware counter, it ends on its own, and it leaves a record. If your visitors don't use smartphones, that's fine — a keypad code needs no app on their side at all.

Two practical points for visitors from overseas. Write the code and the lock's quirks (press the logo to wake the keypad, wait for the green light) on a card, because an unfamiliar keypad in the dark after a long flight is harder than it sounds. And make sure the code's dates are entered in New York time; an app opened on a phone set to another country's time zone can shift the window by hours.

Older parents, carers and the empty middle of the day

Can the lock tell us if Mom's front door hasn't opened at all by midday?

Some systems can, though few locks offer it as a single switch. Most lock apps notify you when something happens — a code used, the door unlocked, a wrong code entered. An alert when nothing happens by a certain time is a different kind of rule, and it usually lives in a smart-home platform that the lock reports to, or in a door sensor paired with the lock, where you can write “if the door has not opened by noon on a weekday, notify me”.

For an older parent living alone, especially one who goes out every morning — to church, to the shops on Third Avenue, to the Bay Ridge Center, which Healthbeat reported had tripled its membership to more than 2,000 — that silence is meaningful. It isn't a medical alarm and shouldn't be treated as one: a parent may simply be staying in with a cold. But it prompts a phone call hours earlier than anyone would otherwise make one.

We set it up only with the parent's agreement, explain exactly what the family can and can't see, and keep it to the information that matters — whether the door opened, not a minute-by-minute diary. Where a parent's health makes a fall a real worry, a proper personal emergency response device does that job far better than any lock, and we'll say so.

I'm deploying from Fort Hamilton for nine months — how should control of our lock pass to my spouse or a relative while I'm away?

Fort Hamilton, whose cornerstone was laid in 1825, is described by Military OneSource as the only active-duty military installation in the greater New York City area, so plenty of service households rent or own in Bay Ridge. If the lock's owner account is on your phone and your email, a deployment is the time to fix that, before you go rather than from overseas. Whoever stays behind should hold full administrator rights in their own name, on their own phone, with the ability to add and delete codes and remove users — not just a code of their own.

Most lock apps allow a second owner or administrator; we add it, confirm it works by having them change a test code while you watch, and record the account details somewhere safe. If nobody is staying in the home, the person who will look in on it needs admin rights too, and the lock's notifications should go to them, since you may not see an alert for days.

Two things beyond the lock. If you rent, tell the landlord who will be holding access while you're away, and keep the duplicate key or code arrangement the lease requires. And anything about legal authority to act for you — a power of attorney, a lease change — is a question for your installation's legal assistance office, not for us. We make sure the door does what the paperwork says.

Our ornate wood door has an iron grille bolted over its glass panel — does that change where the smart lock can go?

Often it does. Many older Bay Ridge doors, on limestone rows and on the 1920s semi-detached houses alike, have a glazed upper panel protected by decorative ironwork. The grille's frame and mounting bolts can come down close to the existing lock, leaving less clear wood than a smart deadbolt's outside keypad needs. A keypad squeezed against a grille is hard to press, catches rain running off the ironwork, and can't be removed for service without loosening the grille.

So we measure three things on these doors: the clear wood between the grille's lowest point and the bore, the width of the stile beside the glass, and the door's thickness, since these doors are often heavier than the 1⅜- to 2-inch range many smart deadbolts list. If the standard position doesn't clear, the choices are a compact keypad model, a retrofit lock that changes only the inside of an existing deadbolt and leaves the outside as it is, or moving the lock a short distance lower with the old hole properly plugged.

The grille does help in one way: it makes reaching through broken glass to the inside thumb-turn much harder than on an unprotected pane. We check that it is fixed from the inside or with security fasteners, because a grille held on by four ordinary screws on the outside protects less than it looks.

Reading the record

The app shows our front door unlocked at 3:12 a.m. and nobody remembers doing it — how do we work out what happened?

Start with how the lock says it was opened, because most apps record the method as well as the time. An entry tagged with a person's code or phone points to that person. An entry tagged “manual” or “thumb-turn” means someone turned it by hand — usually from inside, which is reassuring, though some locks record a key in the cylinder the same way. An entry tagged “auto-unlock” or “geofence” means a phone's location feature decided someone was arriving, which can misfire when a household member's phone briefly loses and regains its location nearby at night.

Then look at what came either side of it. A door that unlocked and relocked a few seconds later with no door-open event between suggests a glitch or a hand on the thumb-turn, not an entry. A door-position sensor, if the lock has one, tells you whether the door actually swung open. And a video doorbell or camera at the door settles the question in a way a log alone can't.

If you still can't explain it, change the codes that could have been used, switch off location-based auto-unlock until you've worked out whose phone is responsible, and tell us — we'll go through the history with you. Many locks don't record use of the mechanical key at all, which is one more reason to know exactly who still holds one.

Can each code carry a name, so the history reads “cleaner” rather than a slot number?

On nearly every current lock with an app, yes, and it's the single change that makes the history worth reading. Codes are stored in numbered slots inside the lock, but the app lets you give each one a label. A history that says “Upstairs tenant, 2:10 a.m.” and “Aide, 8:02 a.m.” can be scanned in seconds; one that says “User 4” and “User 7” needs a separate list nobody keeps up to date.

We name every code at setup and keep the names to roles rather than full names where privacy matters — “dog walker” rather than the walker's name, which also means you don't rename anything when the walking company sends someone new. We also give each person their own code instead of a shared one, because a shared code makes every name in the history meaningless.

Keypad-only locks without an app usually have no names at all, just slot numbers. If the history matters to you, that alone is a reason to choose a connected model.

Restaurants, practices and the avenues

Burglars hit Third Avenue restaurants between 3 and 4:30 a.m. in August 2026 — can our staff codes simply stop working between closing and opening?

Yes, and for a late-closing kitchen it's one of the best settings available. Give every employee their own code and attach a schedule to it that covers their shifts plus a margin. Outside that window the code is refused and the attempt is logged. A code that a former dishwasher shared with a friend, or one read over a shoulder at the back door, is then useless at 3 a.m., which is exactly when PIX11 reported three Third Avenue restaurants were burglarised on August 28, 2026.

The owner and one or two managers keep round-the-clock codes for emergencies. Every door — front, back, cellar hatch where there is one — should report any opening during the closed hours to the owner's phone. A smart lock can't stop a door being broken, and the 2026 reports don't say how the doors were opened; what it can do is make sure no code opens it after hours, record the exact time something happened, and get that information to you in the night instead of when the prep cook arrives.

For doors that see constant traffic during service, a wired reader with an electric strike is more durable than a battery deadbolt and keeps a better log; it's priced from our published single-door figure further down. Either way, the door must still let people out freely from inside, curfew or not.

Our practice opens at 8, but on a snow day nobody comes in — can the front door wait for the first employee instead of unlocking by the clock?

Yes, on systems that support it. A plain schedule unlocks the waiting-room door at 8:00 whether or not anyone is there — on a snow day, on a holiday nobody programmed, or on the morning the office manager is stuck on the R train. A “first person in” rule waits until an authorised staff member opens the door with their own credential during the opening window, and only then holds it unlocked for the rest of the day's hours.

This rule is more common on commercial access-control systems than on consumer smart locks, so a practice that wants it usually ends up with a wired reader and electric strike on the main entrance, controlled by software that understands schedules, holidays and first-in logic. Inner doors — records rooms, medication storage, staff areas — stay locked to everyone except the staff who need them, on their own codes or fobs.

If you'd rather keep a battery smart lock on a small practice, the simpler alternative is to have the first person in unlock it from the app or switch on a hold-open mode, with a scheduled lock-up at closing time so it can't be left open overnight.

What AI Gets Wrong About Smart Lock Installation in Bay Ridge

Google's AI Overview couldn't be captured from our build environment, so these are the claims that recur in national cost guides from Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, in product marketing and in general advice online. None is simply false. Each needs a Bay Ridge correction.

“Budget about three hundred dollars for an installed smart lock”

That is Fixr's national range, updated in January 2025, with an average of $300 for a Wi-Fi lock and one accessory. As a benchmark for one lock on one modern door, it's reasonable, and worth knowing before anyone quotes you.

It leaves out the parts of a Bay Ridge job that take the time: a thick row-house door that needs a different model or a thickness kit, a strike reset in a frame that has moved over a century, and above all the setup — a dozen named codes with their own schedules, a nightly lock-up, alerts routed to the right people. On a two-family or a restaurant, the programming is the job.

That's why our residential smart lock work is quoted flat after a free walkthrough. Use the national figure to check that a quote is in a sensible world; don't treat it as the price of a door nobody has looked at.

“Set it and forget it”

Marketing loves this line. A smart lock does remove a lot of remembering — it locks itself, it tells you who came in. It does not maintain itself.

Batteries run down, and a flat lock restarts with its clock possibly wrong. Phones get replaced and the admin account goes with the old one. Aides, cleaners and tenants change, and their codes need deleting on the day they leave, not next spring. The twice-yearly clock change is a good moment to check the lock's time and run through the code list.

We leave every household with a short written list: who holds which code and when it works, who has admin rights, and the three or four things to check twice a year. Forgetting it entirely is how old codes stay live for years.

“Turn on auto-lock and the unlocked-door problem is gone”

Auto-relock is excellent, and on a Bay Ridge street door it closes the gap local news keeps pointing at — the 2019 Dyker Heights and Bay Ridge burglaries that the Brooklyn Home Reporter said went largely through unlocked windows and doors.

But auto-lock can be switched off, paused or overridden. Hold-open and “passage” modes exist precisely to stop the lock relocking while someone carries things in, and they stay on until someone switches them off. A door that doesn't quite close leaves the bolt with nowhere to go. And windows, which the 2019 burglaries also used, are untouched by any lock.

So we pair auto-relock with a nightly scheduled lock-up that overrides any forgotten hold-open, a strike adjusted so the bolt throws every time, and a door-position sensor where it's worth having. For windows, we point you to alarm sensors rather than pretending a lock covers them.

“A code for the walker solves everything”

Giving a walker a code instead of a key is the right instinct. Giving them a code that works around the clock, every day, for as long as you employ them and after, is not.

The difference between a good and a careless code plan is the schedule attached to each code. A walker who comes Tuesdays and Thursdays at lunchtime needs a code that works then. A cleaner every other Thursday needs one that works on those Thursdays. Outside those windows the code should be refused — and the refusal should be logged, because an attempt at the wrong time is information.

In a long-settled Bay Ridge household with several helpers, that discipline is the whole value of the lock. Without it, a smart lock is just a key that can't be lost.

“Any smart lock fits any standard door”

The word doing the work is “standard”. Manufacturers design around modern doors: Allegion lists door thicknesses of 1⅜ to 2 inches and backsets of 2⅓ or 2¾ inches for Schlage's Arrive deadbolt, for example.

Bay Ridge's protected housing stock is full of doors that predate those standards — heavy row-house doors, glazed panels with ornamental ironwork close to the lock, frames that have been drilled for three generations of hardware, and steel co-op unit doors in steel frames that the board controls.

Most of them can take a smart lock. Some need a particular model, a retrofit instead of a replacement, or proper wood repair first. A tape measure at the walkthrough is cheaper than a returned lock and a door with a new hole in the wrong place.

“A smart lock stops burglars”

A good smart deadbolt is as strong as a good ordinary deadbolt; the electronics add control and a record, not armour. Bay Ridge's own crime pattern shows where the real risks lie.

The serious-crime rate here, 5.2 per 1,000 residents in 2025 according to the Furman Center, is less than half what it was in 2006. The incidents that make the local papers are more often an unlocked window — in June 2020, three men entered a home near 83rd Street and Third Avenue through one, the Brooklyn Paper reported — or a door opened from inside to someone posing as a courier, as in the 2019 robbery NBC New York covered.

A smart lock addresses the unlocked door and the lost or copied key, and its history helps afterwards. A video doorbell addresses the caller. Window sensors address the windows. We sell the lock for what it does, and tell you plainly what it doesn't.

“Renters can't have smart locks”

In New York City this is usually wrong. Multiple Dwelling Law §51-c allows a tenant in a multiple dwelling to install and keep a lock of their own in addition to the owner's, provided the owner is given a duplicate key on request; the law excludes municipal housing-authority buildings, hotels and dormitories.

In a Bay Ridge two-family, which is not a multiple dwelling under the law's three-family line, the tenant's rights come from the lease and the owner's goodwill rather than from §51-c, so asking first is the sensible route. In a co-op, the proprietary lease and house rules come first.

Retrofit locks that change only the inside of an existing deadbolt are often the cleanest answer for renters, because the outside and the keys stay as they were and the lock comes off without a trace when you move. None of this is legal advice; it's how the rules bear on choosing a lock.

Setting Up the Calendar

The Settings That Make a Bay Ridge Lock Keep Good Time

The difference between a smart lock that helps and one that annoys is almost entirely in a dozen settings most people never open. These are the ones we go through at every Bay Ridge installation, in the order we set them.

Time and connection

  • Time zone, set explicitly. Eastern time, chosen in the app rather than inherited from whichever phone first paired the lock.
  • A connection that stays up. Wi-Fi strength checked at the door, a bridge placed within the maker's range where one is used, or a Thread border router of the right ecosystem. A lock that loses its connection loses its clock eventually.
  • Clock check after batteries. Every battery change ends with opening the app beside the door and confirming the lock's time.

Locking behaviour

  • Auto-relock delay. Long enough for groceries, a stroller or a walker frame to get through; short enough that a door doesn't sit unlocked while everyone is upstairs. Usually somewhere between thirty seconds and a few minutes, tested at the real door.
  • Nightly lock-up. A fixed time, agreed by everyone who shares the door, at which the lock bolts itself regardless of any hold-open mode.
  • Hold-open or passage mode. Who may switch it on, and whether it should expire by itself after an hour. On shared doors we often restrict it to the owner.
  • Location-based auto-unlock. Off by default on street doors in dense blocks, where a phone wandering in and out of range can unlock a door nobody is standing at.

Codes and people

  • One code per person, named by role. Never a shared “helpers” code; the history is only as good as the names.
  • A window for every non-household code. Days of the week and hours, or start and end dates, matched to what that person actually does.
  • Two administrators. The owner and one trusted person, each on their own phone and account, so a lost phone or a deployment doesn't lock the household out of its own lock.
  • A leaving-day rule. When an aide, cleaner, tenant or employee stops coming, their code is deleted that day. We show you how, and we put it on the written list.

What the lock tells you

  • Quiet by default. Routine household comings and goings shouldn't buzz anyone's phone. Notifications are for helpers' arrivals, refused codes, after-hours openings and low batteries.
  • Silence as a signal. Where the household wants it and the platform supports it, an alert if a door hasn't opened by a chosen hour.
  • A history you keep. How long the log is stored, who can see it, and how to export a stretch of it if you ever need it for a landlord, an insurer or the police.

None of these settings is exotic. Together they turn a lock into the timetable the household was already keeping in its head.

Co-op Owners and Renters

For Shore Road Co-op Owners and Bay Ridge Renters

Not every Bay Ridge door belongs to a house. A large share of the neighbourhood lives in apartments — in the co-op and condo buildings along Shore Road, the pre-war and post-war blocks on Ridge Boulevard and Fourth Avenue, the walk-ups on the side streets and the flats over the avenue shops. The rules for the door depend on which of those you're in.

Co-op and condo owners

Shore Road has a high concentration of co-op buildings, from the 1940 Normandy at 9437 Shore Road to the 254-apartment building at 9201 Shore Road completed in 1960. In a co-op, the proprietary lease and the house rules decide what may be done to a unit door, and many leases require that the corporation hold a duplicate of any new key so staff can get in during an emergency such as a leak. Expect the managing agent to want a certificate of insurance before the visit and the work done within contractor hours; we provide the paperwork and work within the building's times.

Unit doors in these buildings are usually steel or steel-clad in steel frames, sometimes fire-rated. Before anything is drilled we confirm with the managing agent what the door is, and where a change to the door itself isn't allowed, a retrofit that works the existing deadbolt from the inside is often the answer. The board's duplicate key keeps working; you gain codes, schedules and a history on your own side.

Renters in walk-ups and larger buildings

In a building with three or more apartments, Multiple Dwelling Law §51-c lets a tenant add a lock of their own alongside the owner's, as long as it is no more than three inches in circumference and the owner gets a duplicate key on request; lease terms charging for this are void. The owner's own lock must stay, and city fire-safety guidance from HPD says apartment doors must open from inside without a key — so no double-cylinder deadbolt that needs a key on the inside.

If your building uses an app or fob system at its entrance, the city's Tenant Data Privacy Act, Local Law 63 of 2021, says owners can't make a smart access system the only way into your apartment and limits what entry data they may collect and keep. A lock you add to your own door is yours: its codes, schedules and history are on your account, not the landlord's.

Tenants in two-family houses

A two-family house is not a multiple dwelling under the state's definition, which starts at three families living independently. That means §51-c doesn't apply, and a tenant upstairs should agree any lock change with the owner first. In practice, most Bay Ridge owners are glad of a shared street-door plan that gives the tenant their own code, their own hours (all of them), and alerts about their own door that go to them, not to the owner. When a tenancy ends, the owner deletes the tenant's codes on the day the keys come back; while it lasts, the city's lockout law makes it unlawful to change or disable an occupant's entrance lock without giving them a key.

Field Notes

Field Notes on Doors That Keep Hours

Written from the way our crews work in general; it does not describe any one household, customer or job.

Before we take a lock out of its box, we ask for the week. Not the people, at first — the week. Who leaves first in the morning, who is last home, which days are different, who comes in while the house is empty. People are often surprised how much of it they've never said aloud, and how many keys turn out to be in circulation once they start counting.

Then we make the door behave before we make it clever. A bolt that has to be nudged, a strike that catches, a door that only latches if you pull it: all of that has to be fixed first, because a lock that relocks itself on a timer will find every one of those faults at two in the morning.

Last, we hand the phone back and ask each person to use their own code at their own hour while we watch the history fill in with their names. If the history reads like the week they described, the job is done. Through all of it, the inside of the door stays the same: one turn, and you're out.

Can the installer arrive at 7 a.m. so we can still make the R train or the express bus?
+

Ask for an early window when you book and we'll offer the first available one. A single smart deadbolt can often be fitted and programmed before a commuter has to leave; a larger job may need someone home for longer, or a second person to let us in. Co-op buildings set their own contractor hours, which we follow.

Can a shop or practice door schedule be told about public holidays?
+

Commercial access-control software usually has a holiday calendar, so the door stays locked on days the business is closed even though the weekly schedule says otherwise. Many consumer smart lock apps don't, in which case we set the schedule to lock by default and show you how to suspend it for a holiday, or move you to a reader-based system if holidays matter to your business.

Can we get one daily summary of the door instead of a notification every time it opens?
+

Some apps and smart-home platforms offer a digest or let you limit notifications to particular events, so you only hear about helpers' arrivals, refused codes and after-hours openings. Where a lock can't summarise, we turn off routine notifications and show you where to read the full history when you want it.

If a code only works on weekday mornings, does the physical key still open the door at any hour?
+

Yes. Schedules apply to codes, phones and fingerprints; a mechanical key in the cylinder turns the bolt whenever it is used, and many locks don't log it. That is why keys should stay with the household only, and why we recommend rekeying the cylinder if you can't account for every old key.

If our upstairs tenant uses the app for the shared street door, can she see when we come and go?
+

Not if the accounts are set up properly. Most lock apps separate administrators, who see the full history, from ordinary users, who can open the door but see little or nothing of other people's entries. We make the owner the administrator, give the tenant a user role on the shared door, and give the tenant full control of any lock on her own apartment door, so each household sees only what is theirs.
