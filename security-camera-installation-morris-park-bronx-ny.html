The Neighborhood

Why Morris Park Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Morris Park is a neighborhood of houses on a former racetrack, with a hospital campus on one edge and a shopping avenue through the middle. Wikipedia puts its borders at Neill Avenue and Pelham Parkway on the north, Eastchester Road on the east, the Amtrak Northeast Corridor tracks and Sackett Avenue along the southeast, and Bronxdale Avenue or White Plains Road on the west, with Williamsbridge Road and Morris Park Avenue as its main streets. It sits in Bronx Community Board 11, uses ZIP codes 10461 and 10462, and is patrolled by the 49th Precinct from 2121 Eastchester Road. The land was once the 360-acre Morris Park Racecourse, which ran from 1889 and hosted the Belmont Stakes from 1890 to 1904. After a fire in 1910 the grounds were cut into lots. Forgotten NY notes that streets were not cut through and houses built until the 1920s, and that the neighborhood was not really finished until the 1970s. Several of its avenues, Cruger, Holland, Radcliff, Colden, Paulding and Hone among them, carry the names of old New York mayors. That long, slow build explains the mix a camera installer finds here today: houses from almost every decade of the last century standing side by side on the same block.

The housing is overwhelmingly low-rise. Wikipedia describes mostly one- and two-family homes of various styles plus several apartment buildings; Reinvent NY lists semi-detached brick two-families, freestanding single-family houses on modest lots and pre-war multifamily buildings from the 1920s through the 1940s. A local broker quoted by Homes.com splits the neighborhood in two: the north part, called Indian Village, with classic Tudors on more land, and a south side that is more crowded, with more two- and three-family homes. City lot data for the ten census tracts HPD tags as Morris Park counts about 1,237 one-family houses, 2,089 two- and three-family houses, 138 walk-ups and 25 elevator buildings, most of the elevator buildings dating from 1947 to 1964 along Pelham Parkway South, the Esplanade and Williamsbridge Road. Wikipedia also records that 65.8 percent of occupied homes here are rented, which in a house district means a great many owners living on one floor with a tenant on the other. For a camera plan, that combination matters more than any crime figure. Two households share one front door, one stoop and one driveway. The owner decides where cameras go on the outside; the tenant has to live with them; and both may want to see the same front-door clip.

The local news over the last few years reads almost like a list of places on a Morris Park property where a camera does work. In May 2026, News 12 reported two burglaries inside 1600 Tomlinson Avenue on a Friday afternoon; a first-floor neighbor shared Ring footage of a suspect in a face mask and yellow gloves, carrying a toolkit, who is believed to have picked the locks of two apartments. A Morris Park mother told News 12 about two attempted break-ins at her home in a single week: a stranger caught on her door camera pressing on the handle, and three days later, in broad daylight, someone on camera trying to pick the lock. She said she was changing her locks and adding cameras and sensors. In March 2025, News 12 reported that two women entered a single-family house near Loreto Playground through the side door in the late afternoon, after knocking twice to check that nobody was home and pushing the house's security camera up, and left with a safe holding passports, documents and family heirlooms. Other News 12 reports describe a couple followed up their own front steps and into their building by a gunman who stepped out of a car that had just pulled up, all in under a minute by the video's timestamp; a family whose camera showed a man walking up to their home and peering in, where police said little could be done because no crime had been committed; a holiday package that sat outside for seconds before a thief took it; and a man who broke the same Morris Park restaurant's glass door twice. ABC7 reported a July 2024 robbery at a Morris Park Avenue deli in which two suspects attacked a worker and took cash, all on surveillance video. We are not ranking any block, and these stories do not describe every house. What they show is that the curb, the steps, the front and side doors, the hallway outside an apartment, the window line and the storefront glass are where cameras here have to earn their place.

Morris Park facts that shape a camera plan

  • Mostly renters in a house district: 65.8 percent of occupied homes are rented, so an owner and a tenant often share the doors a camera watches and need a written understanding about who sees what.
  • Two kinds of blocks: the Indian Village side to the north has Tudors on larger lots; the south side packs in more two- and three-family houses, which means narrower gaps between houses and more doors per lot.
  • Afternoon cases, not only night ones: the Tomlinson Avenue burglaries were on a Friday afternoon, the lock-picking attempt was in broad daylight and the Loreto Playground burglary happened in the late afternoon, so a camera must hold a clear daytime picture and record whether or not anyone is watching.
  • A commercial pattern in the precinct: an older Bronx Times report noted that while most burglaries across the borough were residential, in the 49th Precinct they were commercial, which is why the avenue's shops figure so heavily in this plan.
  • A managed shopping mile: the Morris Park BID covers Morris Park Avenue from Unionport Road and Amethyst Street to Williamsbridge Road, with a sanitation crew working 21 blocks of commercial corridor, and the district plan counts about 152 commercial and mixed-use properties.
  • Residents have asked for public cameras for years: Community Board 11's Public Safety Committee minutes from 2018 record residents requesting NYPD security cameras, and in 2024 the Borough President announced funding for a borough-wide mobile command unit and NYPD cameras. Public lenses still do not see behind your house.

One Camera, One Job

Caught on Camera, Then What? The Five Moments a Morris Park System Should Record

Read the Morris Park news closely and a pattern appears. The cameras were there. The doorbell saw the man at the handle. The neighbor's Ring saw the masked man in the hall. The family's camera saw the man at the window. But a single camera aimed at a single door can only show one moment of what was really a sequence: someone arrives, approaches, reaches the door, works at it with their hands, and leaves. A detective, an insurer or a neighbor asking "which way did he go?" needs the whole sequence, and the parts that matter most are often the ones the doorbell could not see. So instead of asking how many cameras a Morris Park property needs, we ask which moments it needs to record, and give each camera just one of them. Arrival: the curb, the driveway mouth and the parked cars

On most Morris Park side streets the house sits close to the sidewalk, with cars parked nose to tail along the curb and a driveway running down one side. The arrival camera is the one that looks at that curb. It sits high on the front of the house, usually under a soffit or at the corner of the porch roof, and looks along the street rather than straight across it, so that a vehicle stopping in front of the house or a person stepping out from between parked cars crosses the frame for several seconds instead of a fraction of one. It is not a face camera; at that distance faces are rarely clear. Its job is to show what happened first: a car slowing, a door opening, two people walking together, the color and shape of a vehicle. Community Board 11 minutes from 2016 recorded car break-ins with Morris Park as the most hit area in the district, and 2018 minutes describe more around Bronx Park East, so the same camera also watches the owner's car at the curb. We set it to record continuously and frame it to stop at the street, not to stare into the house across the road.

Approach: the few steps from sidewalk to door

The approach in a Morris Park house is short. On a semi-detached two-family, it can be a gate, a short walk and five or six steps up to a stoop; on a detached house, it is often a front path plus a long driveway to a side door set back halfway along the house. The approach camera covers that ground at an angle that shows the whole person: clothing, build, what they carry, whether they look up at the house or walk straight to the door. For the front, that may be a camera mounted at the corner of the house aimed back along the walk, so the person walks into the frame rather than across it. For the side, it is a camera at the back corner of the house looking down the driveway toward the street, so anyone walking in from the sidewalk is recorded the whole way. On tight south-side lots, where the driveway is narrow and the next house is a few feet away, we frame tightly to the driveway and mask the neighbor's windows in the recorder, so the camera shows only your ground.

The door: a face at every entrance, not only the front

Most Morris Park homes already have one door camera, and it is usually the doorbell. It does that job well at the front. The problem is that most houses here have two or three doors: the front door, a side door off the driveway, a rear door to the yard, and on many two-families a separate entrance to a basement or ground-floor unit. Each of those needs its own face camera, mounted at a height and angle where a person standing at that door has to look toward it. In a walk-up, the same thinking applies to the street door, the vestibule, and each apartment landing. In a store, it is the inside of the entrance, where every customer faces the camera as they come in. A face camera should be close: a few feet from where a person stands, slightly above eye level, never so high that the picture becomes the top of a cap. Where the side door sits under a small awning, the camera goes under the awning edge so rain does not run across the lens. Where a doorbell already covers the front door, we leave it in place and add the doors it cannot see.

Hands, and the way out

The fourth and fifth moments are the ones most systems miss. A camera that shows hands is set close to the lock, the window, the package spot or the register, with a narrow field of view, so that the clip shows what was being done rather than just that someone was there. In a hall, that is a camera on the landing looking along the doors; at a house, it can be the same face camera set slightly wider so the handle and lock sit inside the frame; in a store, it is the counter camera directly over the drawer. The exit camera is often the cheapest and most valuable of all. It looks at the path people take out: the rear yard toward the back fence, the end of the driveway, the far stair, the store door from inside. Because the people in these clips often leave by a different route from the one they arrived by, a camera that watches the way out is frequently the one that tells you which direction to point the police. On a Morris Park house, the arrival and exit cameras together usually cost less than a single extra door camera, and they turn a few seconds of doorbell footage into a story someone can act on.

Semi-detached two-families: the center wall, the stoop and two households

The pairs of brick houses on the south side of Morris Park share a center wall with the house next door, and that wall shapes the job. We do not mount on it or drill through it, because it belongs to both owners and carries the joint between the two roofs. Front cameras go on your half of the facade, under the porch roof or stoop canopy where one exists, or at the top corner of your side of the house. The driveway camera goes on the open side wall, at the rear corner, looking toward the street. Inside, the basement usually runs the full depth of the house, so a recorder in a dry corner of the basement can reach every outside camera with short runs that come up through the floor of the porch or the sill of a wall, one small hole per camera, sealed with exterior-grade sealant. Because one family lives on each floor, we ask before the job who should see which camera. The common answer is that both households can see the front door and stoop, while only the owner sees the driveway, yard and recorder settings. We set the accounts that way before we leave.

Detached houses and Indian Village Tudors: more doors, more yard, more walls

A detached house has four exposed sides, and usually more doors than a semi-detached one: a front door, a side door partway down the driveway, a back door, sometimes a basement door under a back porch and a detached garage at the end of the yard. That makes the exit and approach moments more important, since there are more ways in and out. Indian Village houses, built around 1940 on streets such as Pawnee Place, are brick with steep roofs and often arched front doorways. Their larger lots, which the broker Homes.com quotes points to, mean the rear camera has a longer yard to cover; their steep roofs mean there is often little or no eave to mount under. On brick, we anchor into the mortar joints rather than the brick faces where we can, because a joint can be repointed later and a cracked face brick cannot be put back. Where the doorway is recessed into an arch, the face camera goes just outside the arch or inside the vestibule, where the light is even, rather than deep in shadow inside the arch itself.

Walk-ups and three-family houses: shared halls with apartment doors on every landing

A three-family house and a pre-war walk-up have something a two-family does not: real common halls with several households' doors on them. That is where the Tomlinson Avenue burglaries happened, inside a building, at apartment doors. The camera plan has two parts. Downstairs, a face camera inside the street door or vestibule records everyone who enters. Upstairs, each landing gets a camera positioned at the head of the stair or the end of the hall, aimed along the corridor so it records who walks to which door, but placed and masked so an opening apartment door does not show the room behind it. In the walk-ups near Williamsbridge Road, cable usually comes up from the cellar through a pipe chase or a stacked closet; where none exists, a slim raceway painted to match the hall carries it up the stair. Every floor the cable crosses is sealed with rated firestop. The recorder goes in a locked cabinet in the cellar, away from any cellar door that opens to the yard, and the owner, not a tenant, holds the main account.

Post-war elevator buildings: lobbies, mailrooms, rear lots and long runs

The six- and seven-story buildings put up between the late 1940s and the mid-1960s along Pelham Parkway South, the Esplanade and Williamsbridge Road are a different job from anything else in the neighborhood. They have a lobby with mailboxes, an elevator, two stairs, a laundry room, a cellar, and often a second entrance from a rear parking lot. Cable runs are long and walls are often masonry, so we plan the route before quoting: one cored opening into a closet or riser rather than a row of small holes, and where a camera on an upper floor is too far from the recorder for one cable, a small switch in a closet on that floor. The camera list usually starts with the street door, the lobby, the mailroom, the elevator landing in the lobby, the rear-lot door, the laundry and the cellar door. Co-op buildings put the plan in front of the board, and rental buildings in front of the managing agent, before anything is ordered.

Morris Park Avenue storefronts: glass doors, family counters and back rooms

The stores on the BID stretch are mostly small, ground-floor shops in two- and three-story buildings with apartments above. News 12 reported a man breaking the same restaurant's glass door twice and taking cash, and ABC7 reported two suspects attacking a worker and taking cash at a deli on the avenue. A store plan here covers five things. The entrance camera, mounted inside just above and behind the door, records a clear face of each person as they come in, and its picture survives even if the glass is smashed. A floor camera from the rear corner looks back toward the door, so the back of a narrow shop is not a blind spot. A counter camera hangs directly over the register, showing hands, the drawer and the customer's side of the counter. A back-room camera watches the door to the stock or prep area and the rear exit. A frontage camera, mounted high on the facade with the landlord's written agreement, looks along the gate and the sidewalk. The recorder goes in a locked box above the ceiling or in the back room, away from the counter and from wherever cash is kept, on a battery backup, with key clips copied off the property so a theft that takes the recorder does not take the evidence too.

Practices and offices near the campus: entrance, reception, back door, nothing else

The offices along Williamsbridge Road and Eastchester Road, and the medical offices at Hutchinson Metro Center, need cameras that protect staff and property without recording patients in places they expect privacy. The entrance camera covers the street door and the first few feet inside. A reception camera covers the desk from behind and to one side, showing who comes to the window, while being framed so it does not read screens or paperwork. The back door, the supply room and any medication storage get their own door cameras. Exam rooms, treatment rooms, restrooms and changing areas are never recorded, and we check every view for mirrors or glass partitions that could reveal one. Waiting rooms are a judgment call each practice makes for itself; many choose a camera at the waiting room door rather than one inside it. Where an office sits in a converted house, as several on Eastchester Road do, the house's old doors and side entrances need to be covered as carefully as the office entrance.

Rules and limits for business cameras in Morris Park

  • Private spaces stay off camera. Restrooms, changing areas, exam and treatment rooms, and anywhere people expect privacy are never recorded.
  • The landlord decides outside the store. The facade, the sign band, the gate housing and any shared hall belong to the building owner, and we get written agreement before mounting anything there.
  • Apartments above stay private. Frontage cameras look along the sidewalk, never up at the windows of the apartments over the store.
  • City property is off limits. Nothing private goes on lampposts, street signs, trees or BID fixtures; sidewalk views come from your own wall.
  • Doors and exits stay free. Nothing is fixed to a door, its closer or its frame in a way that could stop it closing or opening for people leaving.
  • Openings are sealed. Every hole through a rated wall or floor gets rated firestop, and every exterior hole gets weather sealing.
  • Audio stays off. We set business cameras to record video only, which avoids the consent questions that come with recording conversations.
  • Integration can mean permits. If cameras connect to an alarm panel, door hardware or new electrical circuits, we check what that requires before work begins.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Morris Park: Questions Answered

Houses on the side streets

Police at a Community Board 11 meeting told residents to point cameras at "points of access." On a detached or semi-detached Morris Park house, which openings count?

More than most owners expect. The 2016 Community Board 11 Public Safety minutes record police recommending audible glass-break sensors and cameras aimed at the points where someone gets into a home. On a typical Morris Park house, that list runs: the front door, the side door off the driveway, the back door, any basement door under a rear porch or down a few steps, the gate to the yard, every window low enough to reach from the ground or from a porch roof, and the overhead door of a detached garage. On a two-family, add the separate entrance to the second unit. You do not need a camera for every window. A single camera at a back corner, looking along the rear wall, can cover the back door and every low window on that side. Walk around the house once and count; most houses end up with four to six openings that one or two well-placed cameras can watch together.

The burglars near Loreto Playground knocked twice to check that nobody was home. Can a door camera answer that kind of knock for us when we are out?

Yes, and it is one of the most useful things a door camera does. A video doorbell or a door camera with two-way talk sends a notification when someone rings or comes to the door, and you can answer through the phone as if you were inside: "Can I help you?" To the person on the step, that sounds like someone home. For it to work, the alert has to reach a phone that someone in the family actually carries, the camera has to be on a Wi-Fi signal strong enough for live talk, and the alert has to arrive quickly, which means checking the doorbell's settings rather than relying on defaults. For side doors, which a knocker often moves to next, the same camera system can send an alert when someone reaches that door, so the answer can come from there as well. A recorded voice message that plays automatically is a weaker substitute, since it repeats the same words every time.

In that same case the camera on the house was pushed up before the side door was pried. Should a second camera be watching the first?

Wherever one camera is the only witness to a door, yes. The fix costs little: place a second camera so that its view includes the first camera and the space in front of it. On a house, the exit camera at the rear corner, looking back along the side wall, can see both the side door and the camera above it, so anyone reaching up to it is recorded from a second angle. In a walk-up, the vestibule camera and the street-door camera can be set to see each other. The first camera should also be mounted where nobody can reach it without climbing, and in a housing that resists being turned by hand. Recorders can flag a view that suddenly goes dark or changes, but a second camera is what shows who did it. On a two-family, we usually find that one well-placed exit camera covers this job for two doors at once.

A stranger pressed on one Morris Park mother's door handle, and three days later someone worked the lock in daylight. What should change in a camera setup after a near miss like that?

Start by watching the clips you have, carefully, and ask what is missing. Usually it is the arrival and the exit: the doorbell shows a person at the door but not where they came from or went. Add a camera that covers the curb and driveway mouth, and one on the way out, usually the side of the house or the yard. Then check the doorbell itself: is it saving every visit, or only some? Is the clip long enough to show the person approaching? Make sure clips save to a recorder or a card you control, not only a cloud plan that may delete them. Change the alerts so a person at the door after dark or at an hour you are normally away sends a notification that cannot be confused with a delivery. The mother in News 12's report said she was also changing her locks and adding sensors; cameras record what happens, and locks and sensors are what slow someone down. They work best together.

A man was recorded peering into a Morris Park family's windows, and police said no crime had been committed. What is footage like that actually good for?

Three things. First, it documents a pattern. One clip of a person looking in may mean nothing; three clips over two weeks, with times and the same clothing, tell a precinct community affairs officer or a lawyer something real. Keep every clip, labeled by date. Second, it protects you if things change: if the same person later does commit a crime, earlier footage helps identify them. Third, it shows you which windows are exposed. If the clip shows someone standing on the front path looking straight into the living room, a camera on that path plus better curtains or a motion light may matter more than any further recording. Make sure the camera showing that window is not looking into anyone else's house, and that you keep clips rather than posting them, since a clip of someone who has not committed a crime is better shared with the precinct than with the internet.

Apartment buildings and two-families

In the Tomlinson Avenue burglaries, the useful clip came from a tenant's own doorbell camera. Can a building owner count on tenants' doorbells instead of installing a system?

Not safely. A tenant's doorbell belongs to the tenant: they choose where it points, how long it keeps clips, and whether they share them, and they take it with them when they move. It sees one door and a little of the hall, and the next tenant may not have one at all. Impera Security notes that tenants generally cannot install cameras in shared spaces without the landlord's permission, so many halls have none. In News 12's report, the building was fortunate that a first-floor neighbor's camera happened to catch the suspect. An owner's system covers the street door, the vestibule and each landing on one recorder the owner controls, keeps clips for a set number of days, and survives tenant turnover. Tenants' own cameras can then add to it. If you want them to, a written building policy can encourage tenants to save and share clips after an incident.

If we add a hallway camera to a walk-up after lock-picking break-ins, where does it go so it shows someone working on an apartment door?

At the end of the hall or the head of the stair, looking along the row of doors, not straight at any one of them. From that position, a person who walks to a door and stands at its lock is seen from the side and behind, with hands and tools visible, and a second camera at the other end of a long hall catches the face. The camera should sit high, near the ceiling, so people walking past do not block it, and its view should be checked with each apartment door open to make sure no room behind a door can be seen. Recorders can mask the area around each door opening if needed. In a building like the one in the Tomlinson Avenue report, where the suspect is believed to have picked two locks, a camera per landing covers every apartment door. Audio is left off, and the view is shared with residents only as the building's written policy allows.

A gunman followed a Morris Park couple up their front steps less than a minute after a car pulled up. What should the stoop camera on a house capture?

Two things at once: the street and the steps. In News 12's report, the video shows the car arriving and the man following the couple into the building, all in under a minute. A camera set just to the side of the front door, high enough to see past the stoop rail and down to the curb, can show the vehicle stopping, the person getting out and the path they take up the steps, while a separate face camera at the door, or the doorbell, records faces as people reach the top. For families who come home in the evening, a motion light over the steps helps both the cameras and the people using them. Set the curb-facing camera to record continuously, because a car pulling up does not always trigger motion detection. And give at least one adult in the house a quick way to see the front camera live before opening the door to come in or go out.

Stores and offices on the avenues

The same man broke a Morris Park restaurant's glass door twice. Should the camera be inside or outside a glass door when the door itself is what gets smashed?

Inside, with a second view from outside. An inside camera, mounted above and slightly behind the door, sees the person as the glass breaks and as they step through, and it is protected by the building rather than exposed on the facade. Its picture is not affected by reflections from outside lights because it is not looking out through the glass at night; it looks down at the threshold. An outside camera, high on the facade with the landlord's agreement and aimed along the sidewalk, records the approach and which way the person went afterward. A store that has been hit before should also check that the inside camera records continuously overnight, not only on motion, because breaking glass and the first second inside can be missed. Keep the recorder far from the front, and copy clips off the property, so the second visit cannot erase the first.

An older Bronx Times report said burglaries in the 49th Precinct were mostly commercial. Does that mean a shop needs more cameras than a house?

Not necessarily more, but different ones. The report contrasted the 49th Precinct with most of the borough, where burglaries were mostly residential. For a shop, the risk concentrates on the hours after closing, so the plan weights the back door, the back room, the roof hatch if there is one, the cellar door and the frontage with the gate down. A house's plan weights the approach and the side doors during the day and evening when families are out. A small Morris Park Avenue shop often ends up with four or five cameras, about the same as a house, but placed for an empty building at night: continuous recording on every door, a recorder hidden away from the cash, battery backup, and an alert to the owner's phone when a door camera sees motion after the store has closed for the night.

FAQ

Morris Park Security Camera Installation FAQ

Our store's glass door was just smashed and the glazier is coming in a few days. Should the new camera go in before or after the door is replaced?
+

Put a temporary camera up now and the permanent one after. In the days before the glazier comes, the store is most exposed, so a camera inside facing the boarded or temporary door, on the existing recorder or even battery-powered, is worth having immediately. Once the new door is in, the permanent entrance camera is mounted above and behind it, aimed down at the threshold, and checked against the new frame so nothing interferes with the door closing. If the glazier is changing the frame or adding a transom, mounting after avoids drilling into material that is about to be removed.

We are about to rent out the second floor of our two-family. What should the lease say about the cameras on the house?
+

Keep it short and specific. List where the outdoor cameras are, such as the front stoop, the driveway and the yard, and say that there are no cameras inside either apartment or aimed into its windows. State that audio is not recorded, how long footage is kept, who can view it and whether the tenant will have access to the front-door camera on their own phone. Add that the tenant should not move, cover or disconnect the cameras, and that any camera the tenant wants on shared areas needs the owner's written approval. When the tenant moves out, their access is removed and their password retired. Have a lawyer review the wording before you use it.

In Indian Village the lots run deeper than on the south side. Is one camera at the back of the house enough for a long yard?
+

Usually not, if you want to identify anyone at the back fence. One wide camera on the back of the house shows the whole yard but makes a person at the far end look tiny. The better plan is two cameras: one at the house watching the back door and the near part of the yard, and a second with a zoom lens aimed at the far fence or the gate to a rear garage, set so a person there fills a usable part of the picture. Where the far end is dark, a motion light there improves the night picture. Keep the rear view framed to your own yard so the neighbors behind you are not recorded.

Our house is near Jacobi and ambulances pass with flashing lights at all hours. Will the lights keep setting off our camera alerts?
+

They can, if the camera is set to react to any change in the picture. Jacobi is a Level I trauma center with an EMS station on its grounds, so emergency vehicles are part of life on nearby blocks. Flashing red and blue light sweeping across a wall can look like motion to a simple detector. The fix is to use person and vehicle detection rather than basic motion where the camera supports it, to draw the detection zone around your steps, door and driveway rather than the street, and to keep any curb camera on continuous recording with alerts switched off. That way the lights still appear in the footage but stop waking you up.

Our front door has a glass storm door in front of it. Where does the doorbell or door camera go?
+

Outside the storm door, not behind it. A camera behind the storm glass sees mostly its own reflection, especially at night when its infrared light bounces back off the glass. The usual spot is on the frame or wall beside the storm door, at the same height a doorbell button would sit, angled slightly toward where a visitor stands. If the frame is too narrow, a small wedge mount lets the camera face the step. Where there is no room at all, a camera mounted above the door under the porch roof, aimed down at the step, can do the face job while the bell stays where it is.

Our house still has its old doorbell wiring and transformer. Can that run a wired video doorbell?
+

Sometimes, but it has to be checked first. Many video doorbells need more power than an old bell transformer supplies, and a weak transformer can make the doorbell restart, lose its connection or stop charging. We measure the transformer's output, check the wiring from the button to the chime for damage, and look at what the chosen doorbell requires. If the transformer falls short, it can be replaced with one that matches; that part sits on the low-voltage side of the wiring. If the work would touch the household electrical side of the transformer, a licensed electrician does that part, and the quote says so before anything starts.
