The Neighborhood

Why South Williamsburg Properties Need TV Installation

An honest TV page for South Williamsburg has to start with something most installers' pages skip. This is the heart of one of the largest Hasidic communities in the country, centered on Lee Avenue and the blocks south of Broadway, and in many of its homes there is no television. Shoe Leather Magazine, in its 2015 report on the Satmar community, wrote that the first Satmar Grand Rebbe banned television ownership in 1955 and that a poster announcing the ban went up around Brooklyn, and it described the filtered internet that families are required to use if their children attend Satmar schools. We are not going to market TVs to households that have chosen not to have one. The same report noted that the Yiddish-speaking population of the area grew from about 4,500 in 1961 to 57,365 in 2011, which tells you how much of the neighborhood that choice covers.

The rest of South Williamsburg is just as real, and it is who this page is for. Puerto Rican and Dominican families have lived on these blocks for generations, many of them in buildings run by Southside United HDFC, better known as Los Sures, which residents and volunteers founded in 1972 and which became one of the first groups in New York to contract and operate city-owned housing. Thousands of people live in the public housing at Independence Towers, Taylor–Wythe Houses and Berry Street–South 9th Street. On the waterfront just south of the Williamsburg Bridge, the three rental towers at 420 Kent Avenue added 857 apartments when leasing opened in 2019. And around Flushing Avenue, the former Pfizer plant at 630 Flushing has been turned into space for chocolatiers, distillers, bakers and artists, while the 2017 rezoning of the old Pfizer sites nearby set the rules for new apartment buildings with a large share of studios and one-bedrooms.

Each of those buildings puts a different wall behind the TV. A brick walk-up from the early twentieth century usually has plaster laid directly on brick on its outside and party walls, and plaster on wood lath on its inside partitions. A tower from 2019 has smooth drywall that may hide steel studs, concrete or both. A public housing tower built in 1965 or 1974 can have masonry where a renter from elsewhere would expect hollow drywall. A converted factory has thick brick, columns and ceilings that were built for machines. None of these is hard for an installer who knows which one he is standing in front of. All of them go wrong for an installer who assumes every Brooklyn wall is drywall on wood studs, which is what a generic booking app tends to assume.

Then there are the households themselves. UJA-Federation's 2023 community study counted about 36,000 Jewish adults and 32,000 children in some 21,000 households across Williamsburg, more than three people per household on average and nearly half of them children. That matters for a TV in a way it does not matter in a one-bedroom in Manhattan. The Consumer Product Safety Commission reported in February 2024 that 217 people died in tip-overs from 2013 through July 2023, that 137 of them were children, and that 47 percent of those deaths involved a television. A heavy TV on a dresser in a room where four or five children play is the most common kind of risk we talk families out of, and a properly anchored wall bracket is usually the fix.

Age matters in a second way. Much of the housing south of Broadway was built long before 1960, and under New York City's Local Law 1, buildings from before 1960 where a child under six lives carry duties for anyone disturbing paint. Mounting a TV drills a few small holes and disturbs far less paint than the two square feet per room that HPD says triggers lead-safe work practices, but drilling still makes dust, and in an old apartment that dust can come from old paint. We ask, at the photo stage, whether a young child lives in the apartment, because the answer changes how the technician works at the wall.

  • The towers are big and recent. 420 Kent Avenue is three towers on a three-acre parcel between Broadway and South 9th Street, reported as 22 stories in 2016 and 24 in earlier renderings, with 857 rentals and leasing from August 2019. New towers mean drywall, often over steel, and a management office with rules about contractors.
  • The public housing has just been renovated. In a studio, the TV wall is also the bedroom wall and often the kitchen wall.

Same-Day Repair in South Williamsburg

Need Repair in South Williamsburg? A Screen Tilting Off Tenement Plaster, a Bracket in Loose Mortar, or a TV That Came Down in a Child's Room

The urgent TV calls from older buildings tend to follow one of three patterns, and the walls south of Broadway make all three likely. The first is a screen that has started to lean forward or drop on one side. In a walk-up, that usually means the anchors went into plaster alone, or into the mortar joints between bricks, and the plaster or the mortar is giving way. A Brownstoner forum regular described exactly this on a party wall: short anchors that held for "a few weeks," while long shields set through the plaster into the brick held for decades. Plaster in a building old enough to have cracked ceilings does not hold a screw by itself, and lime mortar a century old can turn to sand around a fastener.

The second is a bracket that was fine until someone pulled on it. Full-motion arms are the usual victim. In a home with several children, an arm that swings out is also an arm that gets hung on, and the lever that a child's weight puts on the top bolts is far more than the TV alone ever put there. The third pattern is a TV that has already come down, usually from a dresser or a low stand rather than from the wall, and a family that now wants it somewhere it cannot fall again.

If a screen is tipping, do not push it back against the wall. If two adults can lift it safely, take the weight off the bracket, unplug it and lay it face up on a bed or a soft surface, then keep children out of the room and call (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list. Remounting the same TV is priced like any other job, by the wall it goes on: the $179.99 base for a screen up to 55 inches, plus $45 for plaster or $65 for brick, and the bracket is either yours, if it is rated for the TV, or a new one at list price. We book within two hours of a request, give a two-hour arrival window, and tell you on the phone what we expect to find, so the visit is spent fixing the wall rather than discovering it.

One thing we will not do on a repair is put the screen back into the same holes. Holes that have already pulled out of plaster or mortar are wider than the anchor that came out of them. The new fixings go into fresh brick, a stud or framing, usually a short distance away, and the old holes end up hidden behind the bracket plate where possible. Because we do not patch or paint, any old holes that stay visible are yours or your landlord's to fill, and we tell you which ones before we start.

Building Specialists

TV Installation for South Williamsburg's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for nearly every South Williamsburg TV job. They differ in three ways that decide the plan long before anyone talks about TV brands: what the wall is made of, how the TV gets to the apartment, and who besides you has to say yes to a hole in the wall.

The brick walk-up

Three- to six-story buildings from the early twentieth century on the side streets, many run by Los Sures or small landlords. Stairs, plaster on brick and plaster on lath.

The family-size condo

Newer mid-rises with staggered balconies, big kitchens and a dining room as the main room. Drywall, steel framing and long walls taken up by a table.

The waterfront tower

The three 420 Kent Avenue towers between Broadway and South 9th Street: 857 rentals, elevators, amenity rooms and a management office with contractor rules.

The public housing tower

Independence Towers (six 21-story buildings, 1965), Taylor–Wythe Houses (five buildings, 1974) and Berry Street–South 9th Street. Renovated under PACT; NYCHA house rules apply.

The row house and brownstone

Older single-family and two-family houses, some with modern balconies added to the front. Plaster on brick party walls, chimney breasts and the occasional working fireplace.

The converted factory

630 Flushing Avenue, the former Pfizer plant, and smaller industrial buildings toward the Navy Yard. Thick brick, concrete columns and ceilings built for machinery.

The brick walk-up: plaster that may be resting on nothing but old brick

The classic South Williamsburg rental building is a brick walk-up, three to six stories, with a narrow stair and apartments running front to back. In these buildings the living room wall that everyone wants for the TV is often either an outside wall facing the street or the party wall shared with the building next door. Both are usually solid brick under a coat of plaster that may be an inch thick and may no longer be bonded to the brick everywhere. Tap along the wall at screen height. A dull, solid sound means the plaster is tight to the brick. A hollow, papery sound over a patch the size of a dinner plate means the plaster has let go of the wall there, and a bracket fixed through that patch can crack it further.

Interior partitions in the same building are different. They are typically plaster on wood lath over wood framing, and a scanner reads them badly because the lath and old nails confuse it. The technician finds framing by measuring from a corner or a door frame, by the pilot hole, or by the nails in the baseboard, then fixes the bracket into the framing. Plaster on any of these walls is the +$45 add-on on our list, and solid brick is +$65, which covers the masonry bits, the longer fixings and the extra time to drill cleanly without blowing out the plaster face.

Two more things come with the walk-up. The TV has to come up the stairs, so for a big screen we ask about the turns on the stair and whether the box will fit. And the landlord matters more here than anywhere: a small building with a resident owner, or a nonprofit landlord like Los Sures with a property manager, will often want to know which wall and how big the holes will be. A two-line message to the landlord before the visit saves arguments later, and Brick Underground's advice, in its 2022 guide for tenants, is the same: even where the lease does not ban it, ask before putting up a TV bracket.

The family-size condo: when the dining room is the main room

The newer mid-rise buildings that went up across South Williamsburg over the last two decades were designed for big families. Frieda Vizel's widely shared account of Hasidic building design, and Brooklyn Magazine's 2021 piece on the neighborhood's staggered balconies, both describe the same plan: large kitchens, many small bedrooms, a dining room rather than a living room as the main space, and balconies stepped so that a sukkah on each one is open to the sky. In a home that keeps a TV, that plan raises a question the generic guides never ask: where does the screen go when the longest wall belongs to a dining table long enough for the whole family?

The answer is usually one of three. A shorter wall at the end of the room, where the screen faces down the length of the table. A wall in a den or a bedroom rather than the main room. Or a TV on its stand that can be moved when the room is set for a meal. We talk this through on the photos, because the right wall in these apartments is rarely the obvious one. The walls themselves are almost always drywall, and in many of these buildings the framing is light-gauge steel rather than wood. Steel studs take a different fastener from wood, and a TV bracket screwed into a steel stud with a wood screw will hold for a while and then let go. The technician confirms what the framing is with the scanner and the pilot hole before choosing the fixing.

The balconies matter for one more reason. The wall with the balcony door is the outside wall, and the door itself takes up a large part of it. A TV next to that door gets the glare from the balcony glass all afternoon and is in the way every time someone carries a tray outside, which in a sukkah week is many times a day. We usually steer the TV away from that wall entirely.

The waterfront tower: elevators, rules and the window wall

The three towers at 420 Kent Avenue, designed by ODA for Spitzer Enterprises on the parcel between Broadway and South 9th Street, are the largest new rental buildings in the neighborhood. According to 6sqft they hold 857 apartments, 20 percent of them affordable, with more than 25,000 square feet of indoor amenities and a 400-foot waterfront esplanade. For a TV job, a tower like this means three things. The walls between and inside apartments are drywall over framing, with concrete above, below and sometimes behind. The elevator has to be booked or at least shared with other residents, so we bring the TV and tools up in one trip. And the management office usually has rules for contractors, often including a certificate of insurance and set working hours, which we settle before the visit rather than in the lobby.

The window wall in these towers is mostly glass, and the views across the river are the point of living there, which leaves the TV on one of the solid walls. The solid wall shared with the next apartment is a demising wall built for fire and sound separation, and we are careful with it: the bracket goes into the framing, the fixings are short enough not to break through the far side, and we do not cut into a demising wall for an in-wall kit. If the wall you want is the one between you and your neighbor, a paintable cord cover at $59 is the cleaner answer for the cables.

Inside the apartment, interior partitions between the living room and a bedroom are usually the better TV walls in a tower. They can often take a $169 in-wall power and cable kit, which puts a power connection and a cable pass-through behind the screen using a kit made for the purpose, so nothing but a short, rated cord runs on the surface. Whether the building allows it is a question for the management office, and we ask it before we quote.

The public housing tower: renovated walls and NYCHA's rules

Independence Towers, between Clymer and Wilson Streets, Wythe Place and Bedford Avenue, is six 21-story buildings that took their first tenants in January 1965. Taylor–Wythe Houses, finished in 1974, is five buildings of 8 to 13 stories with 525 apartments. Berry Street–South 9th Street sits near the waterfront. Independence Towers and Berry Street–South 9th Street were both part of the Brooklyn PACT bundle whose $434 million of repairs Brownstoner reported complete in May 2022, which covered roofs, elevators, pipes, new boilers and work inside apartments. For residents, PACT means a private manager now handles repairs and day-to-day operations while the apartments remain public housing.

NYCHA's House Rules are the starting point for any TV on a wall in these buildings. Rule 6 says residents "may not damage, deface or destroy the apartment or any NYCHA property." The rules do not mention TV brackets directly, but they do say, in Rule 34, that residents "may only install TV antennas inside their apartments" and "may not install antennas on the exterior of the building," and Rule 12 requires a signed agreement with the development office before a resident brings in a major appliance such as an air conditioner. Read together, they point the same way: ask the development or property management office before drilling, and get the answer in writing. We will not drill in a NYCHA or PACT apartment on a guess about what management allows.

The walls in towers of this age are often masonry or concrete rather than hollow drywall, and the drill tells the story at the pilot hole. Concrete and block take masonry anchors, not the toggles that a drywall bracket kit ships with. If the answer from management is no drilling, the alternative is a TV on its stand for $119.99, set back from the edge and away from where children climb.

The row house and brownstone: party walls, chimney breasts and fireplaces

South Williamsburg has fewer brownstones than neighborhoods to the south, but it has plenty of older row houses and two-family houses, and some of them have the modern front balconies that Brooklyn Magazine described being added to old facades. Inside, the walls behave like the walk-up's: plaster on brick on the party walls, plaster on lath on the partitions. The chimney breast is a common TV spot, because it is centered in the room and juts out to frame the screen. It is also usually the hardest wall in the house, brick or stone with flues running inside it, and every hole in it has to miss a flue.

Mounting above a fireplace is a listed add-on of $130 on our Brooklyn list, on top of the base and the brick or stone add-on. Before we quote it we ask whether the fireplace is ever lit. A working fireplace sends heat straight up the face of the wall into the back of the TV, which a Brownstoner forum thread on exactly this question flagged years ago, and the same thread made the other point that matters: a screen above a mantel can sit "awfully high" for comfortable viewing from a sofa. If the fireplace is decorative and the room is long enough that the couch sits well back, the chimney breast can work. If not, a side wall usually works better.

The converted factory: brick, columns and ceilings built for machines

Around Flushing Avenue, the old industrial South Williamsburg is still visible. The former Pfizer plant at 630 Flushing Avenue, an eight-story building that Pfizer closed in 2007, was bought by Acumen Capital and turned into space where, as Untapped Cities put it, "sculptors and kite-makers work alongside chocolatiers and whiskey distillers." Smaller industrial and commercial buildings line the blocks toward the Navy Yard. A screen in one of these spaces might be a production display, a sales screen in a showroom, or a TV in a break room.

Factory walls are thick brick or concrete, which is strong enough to carry any TV but needs a hammer drill, masonry anchors and dust control. Columns are often the most tempting place to hang a screen in an open floor, but a column may carry pipes or conduit on its far side, and a building owner may not allow fixings into structure at all. Ceilings in these spaces are often high and carry sprinkler pipes and ductwork. We look at all of it before quoting, and anything outside our standard list, such as a ceiling or column mount, is quoted in writing before you book.

How the method changes from one South Williamsburg building to the next

  • Plaster on brick. Hammer drill, masonry bit sized to the anchor, fixings set into the brick face at least a brick's depth, never in a mortar joint where the mortar is soft. Hollow patches of plaster are found by knocking first and avoided.
  • Plaster on lath. Framing found by measurement and pilot hole, lag bolts into the wood framing, plaster scored around each hole so it does not crack outward.
  • Drywall on steel. Fasteners made for steel studs, or a spreading anchor rated for the load where the bracket plate falls between studs, with the TV's weight checked against the anchor's rating.
  • Drywall on wood. The $179.99 base case: lag bolts into two studs, which in a tower usually means the interior partitions.
  • Concrete and block. Masonry anchors chosen for solid concrete or hollow block, which the pilot hole tells apart by how the drill feels and what the dust looks like.
  • Any wall in a pre-1960 apartment with a young child. The drilling dust is caught at the hole as it is made, the area below is covered, and it is wiped down afterward with damp cloths rather than swept.
Field Notes

Field Notes: Working at a TV Wall in a Home Full of Children

These notes describe how our technicians approach a TV job in a home with young children, which in South Williamsburg is a large share of homes. They are our practice, not a story about any one address.

Ask before arriving. The request form asks whether small children live in the apartment, and in an older building we also ask roughly when it was built. Those two answers tell the technician whether lead dust is a concern and how the room needs to be set up. If you do not know the year your building went up, the city's property records will usually tell you, and we can look it up from the address.

Clear the floor, then cover it. Before any drilling, toys and small objects come off the floor in a wide area under the wall, and a drop cloth goes down. A child who walks into the room barefoot after the visit should find nothing on the floor that was not there before.

Catch the dust at the hole. In an older apartment, a hole drilled through plaster brings up whatever paint layers sit on top of it. The dust is caught at the hole as it is drilled, and afterward the wall and floor are wiped with damp cloths. HPD's lead guidance sets the formal threshold for lead-safe work practices at disturbing two square feet or more of paint in a room, and a bracket's four to six holes disturb a tiny fraction of that, but we treat the dust as if it matters, because in a 1920s walk-up it might.

Think about who will pull on it. A full-motion arm is the right bracket in some rooms, and the wrong one in a playroom. When children are young, we usually suggest a fixed or tilting bracket that holds the TV close to the wall, mounted with its bottom edge above where a standing child can reach, and with the cables covered all the way to the console. If you want an arm anyway, we fix it into brick or framing rated well beyond the TV's weight, because a child hanging from an extended arm multiplies the load.

Look at the TV that is not being mounted. When a new TV goes on the wall, the old one often moves into a bedroom on a dresser. That old TV is frequently heavier and deeper than the new one, and the CPSC's figures make the point: nearly half of tip-over deaths in its 2013 to 2023 count involved a television. Walls and Wires

Plaster on Brick, Plaster on Lath, Drywall on Steel: Reading a South Williamsburg Wall

Most of what goes wrong with a mounted TV in an older Brooklyn neighborhood goes wrong at the wall, not at the TV. A TV bracket only holds as well as the thing its fixings are biting into, and in South Williamsburg that can be five different things inside the same building. Here is how each one shows itself and how we deal with it.

Plaster on brick is the outside and party wall of most walk-ups and row houses. It knocks dull and solid, the scanner shows nothing useful, and the pilot hole goes through white or grey plaster and then hits red or orange grit. The fixing goes into the brick itself. StreetEasy's guide to brick walls quotes a mason who says mortar is usually easier to drill but that in older buildings it "may be crumbling and [too] unstable" for something heavy like a TV, and that matches what we find in century-old buildings. When the mortar between the bricks rubs away under a fingernail, every fixing goes into a brick face, away from the brick's edges.

Plaster on wood lath is the inside partition of most older buildings. It knocks a little more hollow, the scanner beeps all over because of old nails, and the pilot hole goes through plaster, then a thin strip of wood, then a gap. The framing behind it is wood, and lag bolts into that framing carry a TV well. The plaster around each hole needs care, because old plaster cracks outward from a hole drilled too fast.

Drywall on steel studs is common in the newer family-size buildings and towers. It knocks hollow between studs, the scanner shows metal in narrow lines, and the pilot hole goes through a half-inch of gypsum and then a thin sheet of steel or nothing at all. Steel studs need fasteners made for them. A bracket kit's wood lag bolts are the wrong part.

Drywall on wood studs is the case every price list is built around, and the one our $179.99 base covers. In South Williamsburg you are most likely to find it on interior walls of houses that have been renovated, and on some newer interior partitions.

Concrete and block turn up in postwar public housing and in the converted factories. The drill slows right down, the dust is grey, and the anchors are masonry anchors chosen for solid or hollow units. Tell us if your wall feels like this when you knock on it, and we will bring the right tools on the first visit.

Hiding the wires without breaking the code or the wall

Everyone wants the wires gone, and there are three honest ways to do it. The cheapest is to keep the TV low enough that a short cable reaches a console or cabinet directly below, where the cable box, streaming device and soundbar live, and the cables drop straight down behind the furniture. The second is a paintable cord cover, $59 on our list, which runs from the TV down to the outlet on the surface of the wall and can be painted to match. On brick or old plaster walls that cannot be opened, and on demising walls in towers, the cord cover is the right answer.

The third is the $169 in-wall concealment kit. It is a code-compliant kit that puts a power connection and a cable pass-through behind the screen and another low on the wall, so the TV's power and signal cables run inside the wall properly instead of an ordinary power cord being stuffed into the cavity. An ordinary TV power cord inside a wall is the shortcut that generic "hide your cables" videos show, and it is not allowed. A StreetEasy discussion of TV mounting makes the same point more bluntly about one popular kit, with a commenter warning that its cable was "not NYC code compliant" for New York City walls. We use a kit that meets the code, and only where the wall is hollow, the building allows the cutout, and the outlet that feeds it already exists.

What we do not do is electrical work. If you want a new outlet behind the TV, or a new circuit, that is a job for a licensed electrician and needs to be booked separately, before or after our visit. FAQ

South Williamsburg TV Installation FAQ

Is the $179.99 starting price for the labor alone, or does it include a bracket?
+

It is the labor for a TV up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs. The bracket is sold separately at list price, or we hang your own if it is rated for the TV's weight and screen size. Plaster adds $45 and brick or stone adds $65.

How soon does someone get back to a South Williamsburg request?
+

We book within two hours of a request. A technician looks at your photos, tells you the likely wall type and the total, and offers a two-hour arrival window. The job goes on the schedule once it is paid.

How precise is the arrival time you give us?
+

Every visit is booked as a two-hour arrival window, never an exact time. The technician arrives inside that window and never before it, so you are not caught by a knock at the door an hour early.

How are screens larger than 55 inches priced?
+

Our listed base covers TVs up to 55 inches. For a larger screen, send the model number and the wall photos, and we quote the job in writing before you book, including whether it needs a second technician for the stairs in a walk-up.

What happens if the wall turns out to be different from what the photos showed?
+

The technician drills one small pilot hole where the bracket plate will hide it. If it shows plaster or brick where we expected drywall, you hear the new total before any more drilling, and you can go ahead, choose a different wall, or have the TV set on its stand instead.

Do you fill and paint the holes if we take the TV down later?
+

No. We do not patch or paint walls. When we take a TV down, we remove the bracket's fixings and point out the holes so you or your landlord can fill and touch them up. Keep photos of the wall before and after for your deposit.

Can I send a video of the wall instead of photos?
+

Yes. A short video on WhatsApp is often better than photos, especially if you knock along the wall while filming so we can hear whether it sounds solid or hollow. Include the outlet, the sticker on the back of the TV and the view from where you sit.

If the TV shifts on its bracket a few weeks after the visit, what do we do?
+

Stop using any swing arm, keep children away from it and call us. Our warranty terms are set out on the pricing section of this page and in the service agreement, and we will tell you what is covered once we see photos of how it has moved.

Our landlord wants to see the wall before and after. Will you photograph it?
+

Yes. Ask when you book and the technician will take photos of the wall before drilling and after the TV is up, which you can forward to the landlord or managing agent along with the description of the holes and any cover used.

We are repainting soon. Can the TV come down now and go back up later?
+

Yes. Taking down the TV is $85, and putting it back up later is priced like any mounting job by the wall it goes on, from the $179.99 base. If the bracket stays on the wall, tell your painter, and if it comes off, the holes are left for the painter to fill.

Is a wall-mounted soundbar a good idea in a tower apartment with neighbors on the other side of the wall?
+

Often the console is the better place. A soundbar fixed to a shared wall passes more bass into the next apartment than one sitting on furniture. A soundbar placed on the console is $79 and one mounted under the TV is $129, and we can set the bass and night modes so it plays well at low volume.
