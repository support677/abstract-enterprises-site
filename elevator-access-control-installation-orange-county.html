Orange County by the Numbers

Where Orange County's Elevators Are: Three Small Cities, Senior Buildings and a Few Large Campuses

Orange County is not a place most people picture when they hear the words elevator security. It is a county of villages, farms, commuter subdivisions and three small cities, and most homes here have no elevator at all. That is exactly why the elevator buildings that do exist matter so much. They are concentrated in a handful of places, they house people who depend on the elevator more than most, and many of them are managed by a small staff who are not on site every hour of the day.

Three practical lessons come out of those numbers. The first is that most Orange County elevator buildings are small: one car, sometimes two, serving three to seven floors. The right answer for a building like that is usually a reliable reader in the car and a well-run credential list, not an elaborate floor-by-floor system. The second is that a large share of the riders are older adults, people with disabilities, home health aides and visiting family, so a system that is confusing or that strands someone at the lobby is a real failure, not an inconvenience. The third is that the people who run these buildings are rarely on site around the clock. A senior building in Port Jervis may share a property manager with a complex in Walden, and a county office building in Goshen is run by a facilities department with a long list of other buildings. The system has to be manageable from a desk, with an audit trail that shows who rode where, without a technician in the lobby.

The Problems It Solves

What Orange County Owners Are Usually Trying to Stop When They Call About Elevator Security

Very few people call us because they want an elevator access system for its own sake. They call because something has happened, or keeps happening, and the elevator is how it gets upstairs. In Orange County the same handful of problems come up again and again.

  • Tailgating at the front door. A visitor slips in behind a resident, or a door is propped for a delivery, and then rides straight to the residential floors. A locked front door does nothing once someone is inside. A car-enable reader means the person who got through the door still cannot ride.
  • People sleeping in stairwells or on upper floors. Some managers find people sheltering on the top-floor landing or near the roof door. Stopping uncredentialed riding to the top floor and the roof is usually the first request.
  • Former tenants and staff who still have a way in. A shared elevator code or a key switch cannot be taken back from one person. Individual credentials can be cancelled in a minute, which is the main reason owners replace old systems.
  • Medical or office floors reached after hours. A practice or tenant finds that someone reached its floor in the evening when the suite was closed. A time schedule on that floor ends it.
  • Garage and basement stops used as back doors. An unattended garage or rear lot entrance lets anyone call the car. A reader at that landing closes the gap without affecting the lobby.
  • Insurance, board or funder requirements. Nonprofit sponsors of senior and supportive housing, condominium boards and government facility managers increasingly want an audit trail showing who entered which floor. An elevator access control system with audit trail provides it.

Knowing which of these is the real problem decides the design. If it is outsiders riding the elevator, a Level 1 reader in the car is usually enough. If it is insiders reaching floors that are not theirs, the answer is floor restriction for those floors only. We ask about the problem before we talk about equipment, because the cheapest system that actually stops it is almost always the right one.

How It Works

What Elevator Access Control Actually Changes Inside a Small Orange County Elevator

The simplest way to understand an elevator access control system is to think about the moment just after the doors close. Without access control, the car will go wherever anyone asks it to. With access control, the car first asks a question: has this rider shown a credential that is allowed to go there? Everything else about the elevator stays the same. It still levels at each floor, opens its doors, runs its own safety circuits and responds to firefighters. Our equipment only decides whether a floor button, or a call button at a landing, is allowed to register for the person standing in front of it.

In Orange County buildings the decision is usually made in one of three ways:

  • Car enable. A reader in the car wakes the floor buttons for a few seconds after a valid fob, card, code, phone or face. A rider with a valid credential can choose any floor the car serves; a visitor without one can press buttons all day and the car stays put. This is Level 1 or Level 2, and it covers most single-car senior buildings and small apartment buildings in the county.
  • Floor-by-floor restriction. Each floor button is tied to its own relay, and each credential unlocks only the floors it is assigned. A medical office building that rents the third floor to one practice and the fourth to another, or a county building with a records floor that only certain staff should reach, needs this. It is part of Level 3.
  • Landing control. A reader at the lobby, garage or basement call station stops the car from being called to that landing without a credential. In a building with an underground garage or a rear parking-lot entrance, this is often the stop that matters most, because it is the one nobody is watching.

The hardware on our side is similar in every design: a reader, an access control panel, an elevator interface (usually a set of relays or an input/output board with one point for each floor that is controlled in each car), a power supply with a backup battery, the cable that joins them, and software where your manager adds and cancels credentials. The elevator contractor connects our outputs to the elevator controller or car operating panel. In New York State that connection is elevator work, which the Department of Labor licenses; we plan it with the elevator company and never do it ourselves.

Building owners use a lot of different words for the same thing: elevator security system, elevator key fob system, elevator card reader, lift access control, elevator floor control, elevator authorization or elevator access management. Whatever the name, it is some mix of those three designs plus the software behind them. In Orange County the design that fits is decided by the building, by who rides it and by who will run it after we leave, far more than by the brand on the reader.

Planning for Distance

Designing Elevator Security for a Building Your Installer Cannot Reach in Ten Minutes

This is the part of the job that is most different in Orange County than in the city, and it shapes almost every recommendation we make here. The county stretches from the Hudson River at Newburgh and Cornwall to the Delaware River at Port Jervis, and from the Shawangunk Ridge down to the New Jersey line at Greenwood Lake and Tuxedo. A building in Warwick and a building in Walden are both in Orange County, but they are a long drive apart, and neither is around the corner from any installer.

We do not pretend otherwise. We schedule Orange County visits in advance, give a 2-hour arrival window for the scheduled day, and do not promise same-day service. What we do instead is design each system so it rarely needs a visit at all:

  • Remote administration from the start. Credentials are added, changed and cancelled from a browser or app, so a manager in Middletown can cut off a lost fob at a building in Port Jervis in a minute. Where the building has no reliable internet in the machine room, we plan a cellular or network link as its own line in the quote rather than leaving the system stranded.
  • A spare reader and spare fobs left on site. The most common service call on any elevator access system is a damaged reader or a resident who lost a fob. If a spare reader is in the office and blank fobs are in the drawer, the manager or super can solve most of those problems the same day without us, and we confirm the swap remotely.
  • Battery backup sized for real outages. Storms that knock out power for hours do reach Orange County; Hurricane Irene in 2011 flooded and closed the county's own government center in Goshen. The access panel's battery is sized so the system stays in its chosen state through a typical outage, and the building decides in writing whether the car should free up or stay controlled when that battery finally runs down.
  • Fault alerts by email or text. Panels that support it send a message when the reader goes offline, the battery is low or the cabinet door is opened. A manager who learns about a problem on Tuesday morning instead of from a resident complaint on Friday saves a lot of grief.
  • One clear diagram in the machine room. We leave a labeled drawing of every relay, floor and wire, so the elevator mechanic on a periodic test can see exactly what our equipment does and leave it alone.

The same planning helps when a board, a nonprofit sponsor or a county department changes managers. The next person inherits a system with documented credentials, a clear audit trail and an administrator login, not a box of mystery fobs and a phone number for someone who retired.

Building Types

Orange County Building Types and the Elevator Access Control Each One Usually Needs

Elevator access control for apartment buildings, elevator access control for commercial buildings and elevator access control for office buildings are three different conversations in Orange County, and the county adds a few building types that barely exist in the city. These are the ones we plan for most often, what each usually needs, and where the design tends to go wrong.

Senior and age-restricted apartment buildings

The county's own list of affordable housing for adults reads like a map of Orange County's elevator buildings. It names elevators at Middlecrest Crossing II (100 apartments) and Senior Horizons at Silver Lake (84) in Middletown, at Southeast Towers in Middletown (106 apartments for residents 62 and older or with disabilities, listed with keycard access), at Cerone Place (60), High Point (82) and Hudson Pointe (65) in Newburgh, at New Windsor Senior Housing (92), at Monroe Commons (35, listed with an intercom security system), at Park Plaza and the Waters Edge buildings in Port Jervis, at The Cedars in Walden, at Rocky Point in Washingtonville and at The Promenade at Tuxedo Place. Most are one or two cars serving three to six floors.

For a building like this the goal is almost always the same: keep people who do not live there out of the residential floors without making the elevator harder for a resident with a walker, poor eyesight or arthritic hands. That points to a Level 1 reader in the car that accepts the same fob or card residents already carry for the front door, with a large, well-lit reader face mounted at a height everyone can reach. Floor-by-floor restriction is rarely worth it here; residents visit each other, and a resident who can only reach their own floor cannot get to the community room or a friend's apartment. Where floors are restricted, it is usually the roof, a mechanical floor or a staff-only basement, not the residential floors.

Newburgh's new mixed-use and waterfront buildings

Newburgh is where most of the county's new elevator construction is happening. The City of Newburgh has announced state support for more than 200 mixed-income homes on Liberty Street and for a mixed-use building beside Washington's Headquarters with 145 homes and 10,000 square feet of commercial space on a parcel that has sat vacant for more than fifty years. Buildings like these usually have stores or community space at street level, apartments above, and at least one elevator that serves both. The design question is how to keep the retail or community floor open during business hours while the residential floors stay closed to anyone without a credential. That is a time-scheduled floor rule, which is Level 3, and it is easiest to price and install while the building is still under construction.

Large renovations: Bourne tower and buildings like it

The Bourne & Kenney redevelopment in Newburgh, led by Contour Housing Partners with the Newburgh Housing Authority, plans upgrades for 205 affordable homes, including new building systems and enhanced safety and security in the Bourne tower. A renovation of that kind is the cheapest moment to add elevator access control to an existing building, because the elevator contractor is usually already modernizing the controller and the ceilings and walls along the cable route are already open. When an Orange County owner tells us an elevator modernization is coming, our advice is to add the access control interface to the elevator contractor's scope at the same time, rather than paying for the elevator company to come back a year later.

Medical office buildings near the hospitals

Medical offices cluster around Garnet Health Medical Center on East Main Street in Middletown and around Montefiore St. Luke's Cornwall's campuses in Newburgh and Cornwall. We name those hospitals only as landmarks, not as clients. The medical buildings near them typically have two to five floors, several practices with different hours, a pharmacy or lab on the ground floor and patients who arrive in wheelchairs or with walkers. Elevator access control for a building like this usually means floors open to the public during each practice's posted hours and locked to staff-only credentials outside them, so a patient can always reach their appointment and nobody wanders into a closed suite at night. That is Level 3 floor scheduling, and it needs a careful list of which floor belongs to which tenant.

County, municipal and court buildings

Goshen is the county seat. The Orange County Government Center there, designed by Paul Rudolph and completed in the late 1960s, was closed after Hurricane Irene in 2011, partly demolished and reopened in 2017 after a long renovation. Buildings like it, along with courthouses, city halls in Newburgh, Middletown and Port Jervis, and town halls across the county, have the clearest need for floor restriction: records rooms, server rooms, finance offices and secure corridors that the public should never reach by elevator, alongside public counters that must stay open. Government buildings also usually need a full audit trail of who rode to which floor and when, and they often have their own card platform that the elevator has to join rather than replace. We integrate the elevator with the building's existing access control where the platform allows it.

Campus buildings and garages

SUNY Orange's Kaplan Hall in Newburgh is an 87,000-square-foot building with an underground parking garage on First Street. Any campus or office building with garage levels raises the same question: who should be able to call the elevator from the garage at 10 p.m.? A card reader at the garage landing, or a car-enable rule that only applies to the garage stop after hours, keeps the building open to students and staff without turning the garage into a back door to every floor.

Multifamily buildings in Monroe and Palm Tree

The southeastern corner of the county around Monroe and the Town of Palm Tree (formerly the Village of Kiryas Joel, which became its own town) has a large and growing stock of multifamily buildings. The village code's affordable housing chapter allows the planning board to require an elevator, or space reserved for one, depending on what the state building code requires. Buildings here are often family buildings with many children, strollers and grandparents, so the elevator is used heavily all day. Car-enable with fobs for each household, plus a way to give a visiting relative temporary access, is the usual fit.

Office, flex and light-industrial buildings

Office parks along the Route 17 and Interstate 84 corridors, and office and flex space in the Stewart Airport area near New Windsor, tend to have one or two elevators shared by several tenants. For these buildings, elevator access control for property management companies is mostly about tenant turnover: when a tenant leaves, their floor and their staff credentials must be removed the same day, and the new tenant's people must be added before move-in. A cloud-managed platform where the property manager controls that list is the right tool.

Licensing and Code

Who Is Allowed to Work on the Elevator Itself in Orange County, and What That Means for Your Project

Every elevator access control job has two halves, and in New York State the law draws a hard line between them. Our half is low-voltage security work: readers, panels, relays or interface boards, power supplies, cable, credentials and software. Abstract Enterprises Security Systems does that work under NYS low-voltage license #12000287431. The other half is anything inside the elevator itself: the controller, the car operating panel wiring, the traveling cable, fire-service behavior and any change to how the elevator responds to calls.

Outside New York City, that second half is governed by the state's elevator licensing law, Article 33 of the New York State Labor Law. The New York State Department of Labor explains that businesses and individuals who design, install, alter, service or repair elevators and other conveyances must be licensed by the Department, and that individuals who perform work on an elevator must hold an elevator mechanic's license (with narrow exceptions for accessibility lift technicians). An elevator contractor's license, in turn, requires the contractor to employ licensed elevator mechanics. In practice, that means the connection between our relays and your elevator controller is made by your licensed elevator contractor, or by a licensed elevator mechanic working for one, and never by a security installer.

That is not a technicality, and it shapes how we run every Orange County project:

  • Your elevator maintenance company is on the survey. We ask for the name of the company that holds your maintenance contract before we visit, and we invite their mechanic to look at the controller with us or send us their notes. Their answer about spare inputs, the condition of the traveling cable and the controller type decides what is possible.
  • The scope is written in two columns. Our proposal lists every reader, relay, cable run and credential we supply; the elevator contractor's proposal lists their controller work. Owners in Orange County sometimes discover that their elevator company is booked out for weeks, so we build their timing into the schedule from the beginning.
  • Local permits are the building's and the elevator contractor's call. Elevator alterations and building work outside the city are overseen locally under the New York State Uniform Fire Prevention and Building Code by the city, town or village code enforcement office. Whether a given elevator change needs a permit or inspection is a question your elevator contractor answers with that office; we provide the drawings and equipment data they need.
  • Nothing is energized until both sides sign off. We test our outputs, the elevator mechanic tests the controller response and fire service, and only then are floors locked for real.

One more note on law, because owners sometimes read about it online. New York City's Local Law 63 of 2021 sets rules for apartment buildings in the five boroughs that use electronic or biometric entry. It is a New York City law and does not apply to buildings in Orange County. We still recommend that any Orange County building using face or fingerprint readers adopt a written policy explaining what is collected, who can see it and when it is deleted, and offer residents a fob or card as an alternative. It is simply good practice, and it avoids arguments later.

Fire, Power and Storms

Fire Service, Power Outages and Storm Damage in Orange County Elevator Buildings

The questions owners ask us most nervously are about emergencies: what happens to a locked elevator when the fire alarm goes off, when the power fails, or when water gets into the building. The answers are straightforward, but they need to be decided on paper before installation, not discovered during an emergency.

Fire alarm: the elevator stops taking orders from our system

When the fire alarm recalls the elevator, the elevator follows its fire-service program, and our access control has no say in it. Firefighters' service, recall to the designated floor and in-car firefighter operation all run in the elevator controller. The interface the elevator contractor installs is arranged so that our floor-release relays cannot block or delay that behavior. This is one of the main reasons the elevator contractor makes the connection: they know exactly how their fire-service circuits are wired and they test them after the change.

Power failure: choose fail-safe or fail-secure ahead of time

Our panel has its own battery. During a short outage, if the elevator has emergency power, the access control keeps working as normal. If the outage outlasts the battery, the system ends up in one of two states, and the building chooses which in writing. Fail-safe means the car's floors free up when our system loses power, so nobody is stuck unable to ride. Fail-secure means the controlled floors stay locked. Most senior and residential buildings in Orange County choose fail-safe for the residential floors and fail-secure only for a mechanical floor or roof. A county or court building may choose differently for a records floor.

Water and storm damage: keep the brains high and dry

Hurricane Irene in 2011 did enough damage to the county's own government center in Goshen that it stayed closed for years. Flooding along the Wallkill and the Neversink, and in low streets near the Hudson and Delaware, is a real risk for some buildings. We do not mount access panels, power supplies or batteries on the floor of a basement that has ever taken water. Where the machine room is in the basement, we look for a mounting spot above the known flood line, or put the panel on a higher floor and run cable down. It costs a little more cable and saves the whole system the next time a storm comes through.

Accessibility and the override

Every controlled elevator needs a documented way to override access control for emergencies and for residents who cannot use the reader. That might be a keyed switch held by staff, a remote unlock from the management software, or a schedule that frees floors during staffed hours. In senior buildings we also test the reader with residents who use wheelchairs and walkers before we finish, because a reader that is technically accessible but mounted at the wrong angle is a daily problem for the people who need the elevator most.

The credential is the part of the system residents and staff touch every day, so it deserves more thought than it usually gets. In Orange County the right choice depends heavily on who rides the elevator.

Most buildings end up with two credentials: a fob or card for residents and regular staff, and individual PIN codes or phone passes for aides, contractors and visiting family. Elevator access control with key fob readers, with RFID card readers and with smartphone credentials can all run on the same reader in many platforms, so a building does not have to pick just one.

A practical tip from Orange County senior buildings: when you switch from an old shared code to individual fobs, plan a few days with staff in the lobby handing out fobs and showing residents how to tap them. The technical cutover takes an afternoon. The human cutover takes a week, and it goes much better when residents get their fob from someone they know.

Visitors and Aides

Home Health Aides, Visiting Nurses, Deliveries and Family in Orange County Elevator Buildings

In a lot of Orange County elevator buildings, especially senior buildings, the residents are not the only regular riders. Home health aides arrive every morning. Visiting nurses, physical therapists and meal deliveries come several times a week. Adult children drive up from the city on weekends. A system that only thinks about residents will be propped open, borrowed or bypassed within a month.

The tools for this are simple, and the trick is setting them up on day one:

  • Time-based permissions for aides. An aide's code or fob works only during their shift hours and only on the floor where their client lives, and expires automatically when the assignment ends. This is elevator access control with time-based floor permissions in its most useful form.
  • Intercom floor release. When a visitor buzzes an apartment from the entry intercom, the resident's answer opens the front door and releases the resident's floor in the elevator for one ride. The visitor never needs a credential and cannot reach any other floor. This is elevator access control integrated with building security, and it is the feature senior buildings appreciate most.
  • Temporary passes. Many platforms let staff send a visitor a phone pass or a one-time code that works for a day or a weekend. That is elevator access control system with visitor management, without a front desk.
  • Delivery handling. Most buildings simply keep couriers in the lobby or a package room, so they never need elevator access at all. Where a building delivers to apartment doors, a delivery code limited to set hours keeps that door from becoming permanent.
  • An audit trail. Every credential use is logged with a time and a floor. When a resident reports something missing, or a family asks whether an aide arrived, the manager can check the record instead of guessing. That is what an elevator access control system with audit trail is for.

All of this depends on someone keeping the lists current. We set up the first lists with your manager, train them on adding and cancelling people, and leave a one-page guide in the office. A building that reviews its aide and contractor list once a month rarely has problems.

Decisions to Make First

Decisions an Orange County Board, Sponsor or Facilities Office Should Make Before the Survey

The survey goes faster, and the proposal is more accurate, when the people who own the building have already talked through a few decisions. None of these need technical knowledge. They are policy questions, and only the building can answer them.

List everyone who rides

Write down every group of people who ride the elevator in a normal week: residents or tenants, building staff, the managing agent, aides and caregivers, cleaners, contractors, delivery drivers, visitors, and emergency services. For each group, decide whether they should ride freely, ride only to certain floors, ride only at certain hours, or not ride without someone letting them up. Name the people who will run it

Name at least two people who will add and cancel credentials: usually the property manager and the superintendent, or a facilities supervisor and an assistant in a county or municipal building. Agree what happens when something fails

Decide in principle whether residential floors should free up or stay locked if the system loses power for a long time, who holds the override key or login, and who the elevator company should call if they find our equipment during a test. These choices go into the written proposal and the labeled diagram we leave behind.

Plan how residents and staff are told

A change to how people ride the elevator affects everyone in the building, so plan a notice, a date, and in senior buildings a few hours of help in the lobby. Boards and sponsors that announce the change early, explain why, and make fobs easy to collect have far fewer complaints than buildings that switch overnight.

Common Misunderstandings

Five Things People Get Wrong About Elevator Access Control in Orange County

1. "It is a city thing; buildings up here do not need it."

Orange County has fewer elevator buildings than the city, but the ones it has are senior residences, medical buildings, government offices and new mixed-use buildings in downtowns. Those are exactly the buildings where an uninvited rider on an upper floor causes the most harm, and where staff are least likely to be standing in the lobby to notice.

2. "The security company rewires the elevator."

No. In New York State, work on the elevator itself is licensed by the Department of Labor and done by a licensed elevator contractor. A security installer that offers to make the controller connection itself is offering to do work it is not licensed for. Our job is the reader, the panel, the relays, the cable and the software; the elevator company connects them.

3. "Locking the elevator will trap people during a fire."

The elevator's fire-service program overrides access control. When the fire alarm recalls the car, our system has no say, and the elevator contractor tests that after the connection is made. Power-loss behavior is chosen in writing ahead of time, and every building gets a documented override.

4. "We need floor-by-floor control on every floor."

Most Orange County apartment and senior buildings do not. If the problem is outsiders riding the elevator, a car-enable reader solves it at a fraction of the cost and keeps residents free to visit neighbors and use the community room. Floor restriction earns its cost in medical, office, government and mixed-use buildings where floors genuinely belong to different people.

5. "Once it is installed, it runs itself."

The hardware does, mostly. The credential list does not. A system whose list still includes former tenants, old aides and lost fobs is not secure, no matter how good the reader is. That is why we train two people at every building and recommend a monthly list review.
