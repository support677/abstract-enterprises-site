The Neighborhood

Why Starrett City Properties Need Alarm Installation

Most of the alarm pages on this site are about houses: which back window a burglar can reach, which side door nobody can see. Starrett City needs a different conversation, because almost nobody who lives here owns the walls around them. Wikipedia describes the complex as 5,881 apartments in 46 buildings, rising from 11 to 20 stories, designed by Herman Jessor in a “towers in the park” layout and opened in 1974. It sits on a peninsula on the north shore of Jamaica Bay, with Fresh Creek on the west, Hendrix Creek on the east, Flatlands Avenue along the top and Seaview Avenue and the Belt Parkway along the bottom. Pennsylvania Avenue runs straight through the middle. The whole place has one ZIP code, 11239, and a single owner.

That owner, and the way the complex is run, is the first thing that shapes an alarm here. The residential part of the complex is divided into eight sections named after New York State towns: Ardsley, Bethel, Croton, Delmar, Elmira, Freeport, Geneva and Hornell. Each has its own field, its own play area and a five-story parking garage for the people who live in it. The apartments are rentals. According to Mann Publications’ 2018 report on the sale, about 60 percent were in HUD’s Section 8 program and the other 40 percent in HUD’s Section 236 program and New York State’s Mitchell-Lama program. When Brooksville Company and Rockpoint Group completed their purchase in May 2018, Wikipedia records a price of $905 million.

For a household that wants an alarm, those facts translate into something practical. The apartment door, the frame, the windows and the walls are the landlord’s. An alarm that is screwed into them, wired through them or left behind when you move is a conversation with management, and in a complex this size that conversation is more formal than it would be with the owner of a two-family house. So the systems we put into Starrett City apartments are designed from the start to be removable: wireless sensors, panels that sit on a shelf or a table, cellular communicators that need no phone jack, and mounting that comes off cleanly.

The second thing that shapes an alarm here is electricity. Starrett City does not buy its power from Con Edison the way the rest of the borough does. Wikipedia describes the Starrett City Cogeneration Facility at 165 Elmira Loop, opened in 1973, which supplies electricity, heating, cooling and hot water to the residents. In 1977, while the rest of the city went dark in the blackout, the complex kept its lights on. The other side of that independence is that when the plant fails, the complex fails together. CBS New York reported more than 1,000 apartments without power on the evening of December 10, 2014. ABC7 reported an outage across all eight sections and 46 buildings on the morning of April 20, 2015, with people stuck in elevators and an emergency light that did not work in one building; residents told CBS it was the third outage in five months. Patch reported thousands of residents without power before dawn on July 29, 2018, and firefighters freeing at least six people from elevators. An alarm that depends on a Wi-Fi router plugged into the wall goes quiet at exactly the moment the building is most confused.

The third thing is policing. Starrett City has its own private force, the Spring Creek Towers Department of Public Safety. Wikipedia says its officers are armed on duty and patrol around the clock on foot, on bicycles and in vehicles, and that the force was created because the surrounding 75th Precinct had one of the highest crime rates in the city when the complex opened. Mann Publications put it at 90 people in 2018. Edwin Donovan and William Walsh, quoted by Wikipedia, wrote that statistically the complex “must be considered one of the safest communities in the United States.” The NYPD still patrols here too, from the 75th Precinct station house at 1000 Sutter Avenue. A household that adds an alarm therefore has a real decision to make about who hears about it first, and a monitoring company that has never heard of the Department of Public Safety will not make that decision for you.

None of this means the complex is dangerous. Residents on Reddit ask what people ask anywhere; one AskNYC thread is simply titled “Is Starrett City Safe?”, from a parent with two young children. What an alarm adds here is personal: it tells you when your own door opens while you are at work, tells a son in Queens whether his mother’s door has opened today, records who came in with a key, and keeps working when the building does not. For the stores and offices on Pennsylvania Avenue, it watches the back room the patrol never sees.

Two problems come up again and again in tower apartments. The first is the system that never recovered from a power cut: a panel whose backup battery ran flat while the building was dark and now chirps every few minutes, or one that lost its connection and has been sending nothing for weeks without anyone noticing. Eight kinds of job cover nearly everything we are asked to do in the complex.

No-damage apartment alarms

A wireless panel or hub that stands on a shelf, contacts on the apartment door and any reachable windows, and a motion detector in the main room, mounted with removable fixings rather than screws wherever the landlord’s surfaces are involved.

Outage-proof communication

A cellular communicator and a backup battery sized for the outages the complex has actually had, so the alarm keeps reporting when the power plant trips and the Wi-Fi router dies with it.

Retail center store alarms

Office and clinic alarms

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Starrett City’s Architecture

Starrett City was built in one campaign, to one plan, by one architect. Wikipedia describes Herman Jessor’s design as “towers in the park” in a simple foursquare style, and the 2002 capital program brought new elevators, laundry rooms, windows and lighting. Most apartments therefore share a few layouts and a few window and door types. What changes from one household to the next is height, who lives there, and who should be told what.

Upper-floor tower apartments

Above the third floor or so, nobody reaches the windows from outside, so the alarm concentrates on the apartment door and on the inside: a door contact, a motion detector covering the hallway that every room opens onto, and nothing on windows unless there is a balcony or terrace door to think about. The aim is a small system that does its one job well and comes off the walls cleanly.

Low-floor apartments

Ground-floor and low-floor apartments are the exception. A window that faces a walkway, a play area or the low roof of something next to the building is a real way in, and those windows get contacts or a glass-break detector. We stand outside and look before we decide, because the grounds are open and some low windows are in plain view of the patrol while others are tucked into corners.

Apartments with older relatives

Many households in a complex this size include a parent or grandparent living alone. For those apartments the alarm is mostly about the door: when it opens, when it has not opened, and who is told. A keypad that is easy to read and a key fob for someone who does not want to remember a code matter more than extra sensors.

The retail center

The commercial listing for 1279 Pennsylvania Avenue describes a standalone retail center across the street with office space on its second floor. Stores on the ground floor need what any small store needs: the front, the back door, the stock room and the register. The offices upstairs share a stair, which changes where their alarm begins.

Community-facility and medical suites

The same listing describes 6,900 square feet at 1279 Pennsylvania Avenue restricted to community-facility users such as schools, nurseries, senior day programs, medical offices and nonprofits, built out as medical space; another listing advertises 4,000 square feet of medical space at 1305 Delmar Loop. Suites like these mix public hours with locked rooms that hold records or supplies.

Garages and shared spaces

The section garages, laundry rooms and grounds belong to the owner. A home alarm’s sensors cannot reach a car in a section garage, and we do not put sensors in shared spaces for a tenant; a car there relies on its own alarm.

Outages

When the Plant on Elmira Loop Trips: Designing an Alarm for a Complex That Makes Its Own Power

Every alarm salesman in New York says the same thing about power cuts: the panel has a battery, so you are covered. In most of Brooklyn that is close enough to true, because an outage on one block is usually short and the cellular network around it keeps working. In Starrett City the question deserves more care, because the record shows a pattern that is different from the rest of the borough. The complex generates its own electricity, so when the plant has a problem, the whole complex, or a large part of it, loses power together. And because the buildings are tall, a power failure is not only dark rooms. It is stalled elevators, dark stairwells and thousands of people trying to reach their apartments or leave them at the same time.

Look at what the news reports describe. On the evening of December 10, 2014, CBS New York reported that more than 1,000 apartments had lost power at about a quarter to nine, that the fire department carried out elevator rescues, and that firefighters checked on elderly residents and people with special needs; residents told the station it was the second outage that week. On April 20, 2015, ABC7 reported an outage across all eight sections and 46 buildings beginning around 9 a.m., traced to a failure at the complex’s power plant; stairwells went dark and emergency lights in one building did not work. CBS New York reported the same morning that power was back before 12:30 p.m. but that elevators were still out after that, and residents described it as the third outage in five months. On July 29, 2018, Patch reported thousands of residents without power from around 5 a.m., at least six people freed from elevators, and a fire department chief noting that the complex has more than 100 elevators.

We take three design rules from that record. The first is that the alarm’s path to the outside world must not depend on anything plugged into the apartment’s outlets except the panel itself. That means a cellular communicator, not a Wi-Fi connection through the household router, as the main way the panel reports. Routers, cable modems and the boxes that feed them all go dark when the apartment does. A system that only talks through Wi-Fi will simply stop reporting at the start of an outage, and if the door opens during the confusion nobody will hear about it.

The second rule is that the backup battery has to be sized for the outages that have actually happened here, not for the brochure. The reports above describe outages lasting from about an hour to most of a morning. A panel battery that is a few years old and was never tested can fail well short of that, and the first time most households discover it is when the keypad starts chirping after the lights come back. We fit a fresh battery, write its install date inside the panel cover, and set the panel to report a low battery to your phone rather than only beeping in an empty apartment. Wireless sensors run on their own small batteries and do not care what the building is doing.

The third rule is about what the alarm should say during an outage. When the plant trips, every panel in every apartment that has one loses mains power at the same moment. If each of those panels immediately tells its central station that power has failed, the station is flooded with messages that mean the same thing and require no action, and the households get calls they cannot do anything about. Most panels let the installer delay the power-failure report. We usually set that delay so that a short flicker produces nothing, a longer outage produces a single notice on your phone, and the alarm itself, the door contact and the motion detector, keeps working throughout on its battery. If you are away and want to know the moment the apartment loses power, for example because someone at home relies on equipment that needs it, we set it the other way. It is your choice, and it is written down.

There is one thing an alarm cannot do in an outage, and we would rather say it here than have anyone believe otherwise. It does not light the stairwell. Emergency lighting in halls and stairs is part of the building and is the owner’s responsibility; the 2015 report of emergency lights failing in one building is a building problem, not something a burglar alarm fixes. A keypad has a small backlight and a siren makes noise, and a monitored smoke detector on the alarm will still report a fire while the power is out, but none of that helps anyone find the way down twelve flights in the dark. A household that worries about outages should keep a working flashlight by the door, and anyone who depends on powered medical equipment should talk to their provider and to management about it. The ABC7 report quotes a resident on kidney dialysis; that is a conversation for a doctor, not an alarm company.

What an alarm can do during an outage is narrower: keep watching the apartment door on battery, record who disarmed it, tell your phone that power has gone and come back, and keep reporting monitored smoke and carbon monoxide detectors. For a household at work when the plant trips, that is the difference between wondering and knowing.

Two Patrols

Two Kinds of Officer and One Phone Call: Who Your Alarm Should Tell First

In most of Brooklyn the chain of events after an alarm trips is simple. The panel reports to a central monitoring station, the station tries to reach the household, and if nobody answers or the household asks for help, the station calls the police. Starrett City adds a second kind of officer to that picture. The Spring Creek Towers Department of Public Safety is a private force employed for the complex; Wikipedia describes its officers as armed on duty, enforcing state and city law, and patrolling around the clock on foot, on bicycles and in vehicles. Mann Publications described it in 2018 as a 90-person public safety force. The NYPD’s 75th Precinct, at 1000 Sutter Avenue, still patrols the complex and is the agency a central station contacts through 911.

That leaves households with a question nobody else in the borough has to think about: when the alarm trips, should the Department of Public Safety be told, and in what order? We do not answer that question for you, and we do not claim to know how the department handles calls from residents’ alarm companies, because we have not found anything published about it. What we do is make sure the question is asked and the answer is written into the system. Before installation, we suggest you ask the department directly whether it wants to be on residents’ alarm call lists, what number it wants used, and what information it needs to find an apartment quickly. If it says yes, we add that number to the list in the position you choose. If it says no, or you prefer not to, the list stays as it would be anywhere else.

Whatever the answer, the information on the monitoring account matters more here than in a neighborhood of private houses. An address on Pennsylvania Avenue or one of the loops, with 46 buildings and eight sections, is not enough for someone arriving in a hurry. We put the building, the section name, the floor and the apartment number on the account, along with which entrance to use and anything a responder needs to know about getting upstairs. A central station operator reading that to a 911 dispatcher saves time that a bare street address would cost.

The order of the call list is the other half of the design. Most households want their own phones first, then a family member, then the central station’s call to the police; for an older relative living alone, a relative who lives nearby often goes first. We set the order you choose, write it on the sheet we leave in the apartment, and change it whenever you ask.

What goes on the monitoring account for a Starrett City apartment

  • Building and section: the street address, the section name (for example Geneva or Hornell) and the building’s entrance, not only the street name.
  • Floor and apartment: written the way the building numbers them, plus which elevator bank or stair is nearest.
  • Call order: who is called first, second and third, with a note if anyone on the list should be called only during certain hours.
  • Public Safety: included or not, as you decide after asking the department; if included, the number it asked to be used.
  • Passcode: a word or number that anyone on the list can say clearly, used to cancel a false alarm.
  • Special notes: a resident who cannot use stairs, a pet that is loose in the apartment, or a medical device in the home, if you want responders to know.

One Landlord

One Landlord, 5,881 Front Doors: Fitting an Alarm Into an Apartment You Rent

The most common question we get from people in large rental complexes is not about sensors at all. It is whether they are allowed to have an alarm. The honest answer is that it depends on your lease and on the rules the management office applies, and we have not seen Spring Creek Towers’ lease or house rules. So we do not tell anyone that management will or will not object. What we can say is how the system can be designed so that the question is as easy as possible for management to answer: nothing wired into the building, nothing drilled into the door without written permission, and nothing left behind when you move.

In practice that means a wireless system. The panel, or a hub that does the same job, sits on a shelf or a table near an outlet. The door contact is a small two-part sensor fixed to the inside face of the door and the frame. Where a landlord prefers, it can be mounted with removable adhesive strips designed to come off without lifting paint, rather than screws, and we will say before we start which fixing we are using and why. The motion detector stands on a bracket that can be screwed into a wall or, if you want nothing in the walls at all, placed on a shelf or the top of a tall piece of furniture where it still sees the hallway. The siren is built into the panel or plugs into an outlet. None of it touches the building’s wiring, the intercom, the lobby or the hallway outside your door.

If you want to ask management first, we give you a one-page list of the equipment and how each piece is fixed. If management sets conditions, we design around them. If management says no, we do not install, and we do not suggest ways around a refusal.

Ownership is the other side of the same question. Everything we install and you pay for is yours, and it leaves with you. When you move, whether to another apartment in the complex or elsewhere, the system comes down, the sensors come off and the panel goes in a box. We can do that for you, or show you how to do it yourself. If you are moving within Starrett City, the same equipment usually goes back up in the new apartment in under an hour, because the layouts are similar. Nothing about the alarm should ever end up as a fixture the landlord has to remove.

The renovation program is a reason to think ahead. The 2002 capital program included new windows and elevators, according to Wikipedia, and the 2021 agreement commits the owners to about $140 million of additional improvements by 2030, according to the Brooklyn Eagle; the 2018 sale announcement in Mann Publications mentioned power plant upgrades including back-up power and an overhaul of the underground piping. We have not seen a schedule for which apartments get what work or when; the Questions section below explains what to do about sensors if your windows or door are replaced.

Height

Floor 2 or Floor 17: How Height Changes a Starrett City Alarm

With buildings from 11 to 20 stories tall, the single biggest difference between two apartments in Starrett City is not the number of rooms. It is the floor. A burglar alarm protects the ways into a home, and the ways into a seventeenth-floor apartment are very different from the ways into a second-floor one. A plan that treats them the same either wastes money upstairs or leaves the windows unprotected downstairs.

High up, there is one way in that matters: the apartment door. Nobody climbs to a fourteenth-floor window, and in apartments without a balcony or terrace the windows can be left off the alarm entirely. The design for an upper-floor apartment is therefore small and focused. A contact on the door tells you when it opens. A motion detector in the hallway inside the apartment, placed so it sees anyone who walks from the door toward the bedrooms, catches the person who comes in with a key or follows someone in, which a door contact alone would treat as a normal opening. Indoor cameras are optional and, if you want them, go in the living room, pointed away from the windows and never into a neighbor’s view.

Low down, the windows come back into the conversation. A ground-floor or second-floor apartment may have windows that face a walkway, a play area, a parking area or a low roof. Some of those windows are in full view of passers-by and the patrol; others are around a corner, behind plantings, or above something a person could stand on. Our technician walks outside before deciding which windows get sensors. Where a room has several windows, one glass-break detector can cover them more cheaply than a contact on each sash.

The middle floors, roughly the third to the fifth, are treated like upper floors unless something below, such as a canopy, ledge or low roof, brings a window within reach. We do not guess; we look.

Family

A Parent in Hornell, a Daughter in Delmar: Alarms That Keep Families in Touch Across the Complex

In a complex of 5,881 apartments, relatives sometimes live in different buildings or different sections, or a parent stays in the complex while grown children live across the borough. For households like that, the most useful thing an alarm does is often not sounding a siren. It is telling the right person, quietly, what the front door is doing.

The tools for that are ordinary features of a modern panel, set up with some thought. A door-activity notice tells a family member when the apartment door opens during hours when nobody is expected. An inactivity notice tells them when the door has not opened by a time they choose, which for an older person who goes out every morning is a gentle sign that something may be wrong. Each family member gets their own login in the app, with permission to see what is happening without being able to change codes or disarm the system unless you want them to.

An alarm is not a medical alert service, and a door that has not opened is not proof of a fall; a family worried about a parent’s health should ask the parent’s doctor about a medical alert device. What the alarm adds is a picture of comings and goings the family can check without phoning every hour.

One account can cover two apartments. If a son wants an alarm in his own apartment and another in his mother’s, both can appear in the same app, each with its own codes, its own call list and its own notices. Each apartment still needs its own panel, sensors and, if wanted, its own monitoring, and each is priced as its own system on the Brooklyn list.

Rules that apply to every Starrett City alarm

  • Siren limit. NYC Administrative Code § 24-238 requires an audible burglar alarm on a building to be able to shut itself off, and to do so automatically within fifteen minutes of activation. We set every siren to stop well inside that limit, which also matters in towers where a siren in one apartment is heard through the walls of several.
  • Who may install. New York State requires anyone who installs, services or maintains security or fire alarm systems to hold a license from the Department of State under Article 6-D of the General Business Law. Ours is #12000287431. Ask any company for its number before work starts.
  • Smoke and carbon monoxide. HPD rules put the duty to provide smoke and carbon monoxide detectors in apartments on the building owner. Monitored detectors on your alarm are an addition, not a replacement, and we never remove or disconnect the owner’s detectors.
  • Permits. We have found no New York City permit that a tenant or a small business must obtain to install a burglar alarm. Building alterations are different, which is one more reason we keep apartment systems wireless and removable.
  • Fire alarm systems. Building fire alarm systems in commercial spaces are a separate trade and a separate approval process. A burglar alarm with monitored smoke detection is not a building fire alarm system. If your space needs one, see our fire alarm installation page for Starrett City.

When the complex goes dark

Spring Creek Towers makes its own electricity, and when that plant has failed, whole sections have lost power together. Should my panel report a power failure the moment it happens, or wait?

Usually it should wait. When the plant trips, every panel in every apartment loses power at once, and an instant report from each of them tells the central station nothing it can act on. We normally set the power-failure report to wait long enough that a brief flicker sends nothing, while a longer outage sends one notice to your phone. The alarm itself keeps protecting the door on its backup battery the whole time. If someone at home depends on powered equipment, or you simply want to know at once, we set the report to go immediately. Whichever you choose is written on the sheet we leave in the apartment.

The 2015 report described dark stairwells and emergency lights that did not work in one building. Can my alarm do anything for us in a dark stairwell?

Not much, and we would rather be honest about it. Emergency lighting in halls and stairs is part of the building and the owner’s responsibility; an alarm panel inside your apartment cannot light a stair. During an outage your alarm keeps watching the apartment door on battery, keeps reporting monitored smoke and carbon monoxide detectors if you have them, and can tell you on your phone that the apartment has lost and then regained power. For the stairs, keep a working flashlight by the apartment door, and report any emergency light that does not work to management.

Two patrols, one alarm

Starrett City has its own armed Public Safety officers on patrol around the clock, plus the NYPD’s 75th Precinct. When my apartment alarm goes off, who should the monitoring station call first?

You decide, and we write it down. A central station normally tries the household, then anyone else on your list, and calls 911 when help is needed, which brings the 75th Precinct. Whether the Spring Creek Towers Department of Public Safety should also be called is a question for the department; we have not found anything published about how it handles calls from residents’ alarm companies. Ask it before installation. If it wants to be included, we add the number it gives you in the position you choose and put your building, section, floor and apartment on the account so anyone responding can find the right door.

The crime figures we see quoted are for the 75th Precinct, which covers most of East New York. Do they tell us anything about the risk inside the complex?

Not much on their own. Renters, renovations and the shopping strip

They will come off with the old windows or door unless someone removes them first. Our office is on the second floor of the retail center, reached by a stair the other tenants share. Which door does our alarm treat as the front door: the street door, the stair door or our own suite door?

Your own suite door. AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Starrett City

Search engines now answer alarm questions with a generated summary. We could not view the Google AI Overview for this search from our build environment, so we do not quote it. The claims below are the generic statements that appear again and again in cost guides and national advice, and each is followed by what changes when the address is an apartment in a self-powered, privately patrolled rental complex on Jamaica Bay.

“Every alarm needs Wi-Fi to work”

Many popular kits do lean on Wi-Fi, and summaries tend to describe a home alarm as one more smart device on the home network. That is the arrangement that fails first in Starrett City. When the complex’s power plant trips, the apartment’s router, modem and cable box all go dark at the same moment, and a system that only talks through Wi-Fi goes silent with them.

The fix is not exotic. A cellular communicator sends alarm signals over the mobile network, which is powered from outside the complex and kept running during local outages. The panel itself runs on its backup battery. Wi-Fi can still be used for cameras and for convenience, but not as the alarm’s only way out.

The practical test is simple: ask any installer what happens to your alarm if you unplug the router. If the answer is that it stops reporting, it is not the right design for a complex whose residents told CBS New York it had gone dark three times in five months.

“Your landlord can’t stop you installing a security system”

General advice often says renters are free to install wireless alarms because nothing is permanent. Whether that is true for you depends on your lease and the management office’s rules, which we have not seen and which no generic summary has seen either. In a complex of 5,881 apartments with one owner, rules about alterations tend to be written down and applied consistently.

What is true is that a wireless system can be designed so the question is easy to answer: nothing wired into the building, nothing drilled into the door without permission, everything removable. We give households a one-page equipment list they can show management.

If management says no, the right response is to accept it or ask what would be acceptable, not to install anyway because a website said you could.

“A gated or patrolled community doesn’t need alarms”

The logic sounds reasonable: Starrett City has an armed private force patrolling around the clock, so why add an alarm? A patrol and an alarm do different jobs. A patrol watches the grounds, the walkways and the outsides of buildings on a schedule. An alarm watches the inside of one apartment or one store all the time and knows the moment a particular door opens.

For a store in the retail center, that difference is the whole case for an alarm: a patrol car can pass the front at 3 a.m. without seeing the back door that has just been forced. For an apartment, the alarm is less about burglary statistics and more about knowing what your own door is doing while you are away.

The patrol is also a reason to design the call list carefully, as described above, so that a real alarm reaches someone who can act quickly.

“A home alarm costs a few hundred dollars plus a monthly fee”

Cost guides quote national averages, and DIY kits do sell for a few hundred dollars. What a summary rarely says is what the money covers. A professionally installed system for a Starrett City apartment on our Brooklyn list starts at $600 for a basic residential system with panel, door and window sensors, a motion detector, keypad and cellular communicator, and a wireless system typically runs $600 to $1,200.

Monitoring is not required. Many households here run their alarm from their own phones; others add central station monitoring from $19.99 a month through our partners, month to month, often for an older relative’s apartment.

For a store, a standard small-business alarm on our Brooklyn list is $1,500 to $3,000. Every price on this page is the Brooklyn base, which is what a Starrett City address pays.

“Put sensors on every window”

Generic checklists say to protect every door and every window. In a house that is sensible. In a tower apartment above the third or fourth floor, a contact on a window that nobody can reach from outside protects nothing and adds a battery to change every year or two.

The opposite mistake is just as common: treating a ground-floor apartment like a high one. A window that faces a walkway or sits above something a person can stand on is a real way in and should be on the alarm.

We decide window by window after looking from outside. That is why the price for two apartments with the same layout can differ: the floor changes the parts list.

“Battery backup means a power cut is no problem”

Every panel has a backup battery, and summaries often stop there. The questions that matter are how old the battery is, whether it has ever been tested, and whether the panel will tell you when it is low. A battery that has quietly lost its capacity over several years can die partway through a long outage, and the first sign is often a chirping keypad after power returns.

The Starrett City outages reported in 2014, 2015 and 2018 ran from about an hour to most of a morning. We fit a fresh battery, date it, and set the panel to send a low-battery notice to your phone.

There is also the question of what the panel says when power fails. In a complex where every panel loses power at once, an instant power-failure report from each one is noise. We set a delay unless you ask for an immediate notice.

“You need a city permit before installing an alarm in New York”

Some cost guides and summaries mention alarm permits and registration fees, which exist in many American cities. We have found no New York City rule requiring a tenant, homeowner or small business to obtain a permit before installing a burglar alarm.

The city rule we do apply is NYC Administrative Code § 24-238: an audible alarm on a building must shut itself off within fifteen minutes. The state rule is licensing; installers must hold a Department of State license, and ours is #12000287431.

In Starrett City the permission that matters is not a city permit. It is whatever your lease and management require, which is why apartment systems here are wireless and removable.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Start at the Door, Then Unplug the Router

This is how a Starrett City apartment visit goes, written as our method rather than as a story about any particular family. The first thing I do is stand in the apartment doorway with the door open and look down the hallway inside. Almost every apartment here has one path from the door to the rooms people sleep in, and the motion detector belongs where it sees that path without seeing the window. I hold the detector up where I think it should go and ask someone to walk from the door to the bedroom. If the test light catches them in the first two steps, that is the place.

The second thing I do surprises people. With the household’s permission, I unplug their Wi-Fi router. Then I watch the panel. If it keeps reporting over cellular, good. If it was set up to depend on Wi-Fi, this is where we find out, in daylight with everyone calm, rather than during the next outage. Then I plug the router back in and apologize to whoever was on a video call.

Last, before I write anything down, I ask two questions: what did management say, and did anyone ask Public Safety about the call list? If the answer to either is “not yet”, I leave the equipment list and come back. An hour of waiting costs less than a system that has to come off the door again.
