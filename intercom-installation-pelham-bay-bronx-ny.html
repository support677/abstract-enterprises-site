The Neighborhood

Why Pelham Bay Properties Need Intercom Installation

Pelham Bay is a neighborhood of houses with apartment buildings along its edges, and that layout is the reason intercom work here comes in two very different shapes. One neighborhood write-up notes that zoning has kept multi-story buildings mainly at the edges, and the City Planning study behind the 2006 rezoning described the same pattern in numbers: detached homes made up roughly 72 percent of the housing stock in the study area, semi-detached about 17 percent of residential structures, attached houses around 6.6 percent and multifamily buildings about 4.5 percent of lots, with the apartment buildings concentrated on the wider avenues. So on the inner blocks off Buhre Avenue, Crosby Avenue and Hobart Avenue, an intercom is mostly a door station for a family or two, and on Pelham Parkway South, Middletown Road and Westchester Avenue it is a lobby panel serving dozens of homes behind one door.

The apartment edge is where the legal questions sit. StreetEasy and Compass list a string of elevator co-ops on Pelham Parkway South: 1870, built in 1960 with 57 units; 1910, built in 1964 with 52 units and classed as an elevator cooperative; and 1874, known as The Pelham, built in 1970 with 95 units on six floors. Homes.com lists Pelham Bay Towers at 3121 Middletown Road, completed in 1963, twelve stories, 157 units and two elevators. Those dates matter because the Multiple Dwelling Law's intercom requirement, as Parker Custom Security summarizes it, applies to Class A multiple dwellings of eight or more apartments erected or converted after January 1, 1968. The Pelham falls after that line. The others fall before it, and ButterflyMX describes pre-1968 buildings as needing a more careful review rather than an automatic pass. Boards in these buildings often ask us first not what a panel costs but which rule they are under, and we answer that question in the rules section below before anything is designed.

The middle of the neighborhood is older than most visitors expect. NeighborhoodScout puts the share of residential real estate built in 1939 or earlier at 57.0 percent, and the residential boom that followed the arrival of the Pelham Line in 1920 lined Buhre Avenue, Crosby Avenue and Middletown Road with one- and multi-family houses, apartments and small businesses. One guide describes most apartment buildings here as brick walk-ups and notes Tudor and Colonial Revival details on Wilkinson, Hobart and Crosby Avenues. Listings show what that means at the door: a brick semi-detached duplex on Hutchinson River Parkway, a semi-detached two-family with three large units plus a cellar with its own rear entry, and a detached three-family on an oversized lot on Hobart Avenue. Houses like those often have an original bell, one or two added buttons, and a household on each floor that cannot tell whose visitor is ringing.

The newest buildings add a third kind of job. New York YIMBY and the Bronx Times have reported permits for an eight-story building with 36 homes at 1609 Crosby Avenue, a four-story building with 13 homes at 1536 Gillespie Avenue, a 17-story residential building at 3250 Westchester Avenue, an eight-story mixed-use building at 2135 Colonial Avenue and a five-story building at 3251 Westchester Avenue. In January 2026 the Bronx Times covered the housing lottery for 11 new units at 3217 Westchester Avenue, steps from the Pelham Bay Park station, listing intercommunication devices, security cameras, covered parking and an elevator among the amenities. Every one of those buildings with eight or more homes falls squarely under the post-1968 rule, and every one has a developer, a general contractor and a future managing agent who all need to agree on where the intercom cable runs before the walls close.

Same-Day Repair in Pelham Bay

Need Repair in Pelham Bay? Lobby Panels That Freeze, Upper Floors That Never Ring, or a Release That Clicks While the Door Stays Locked

Repair calls from Pelham Bay buildings tend to follow a few patterns. In the elevator co-ops on the parkway and Middletown Road, the classic complaint is a lobby panel that calls some apartments and not others. When the dead apartments are all on one line or one floor, the fault is usually in the riser: a splice in a pull box that corroded, a pair that was cut during an apartment renovation, or a junction where an old repair was twisted together and taped. When the dead apartments are scattered, the panel or its power supply is the likelier suspect. A second pattern is a panel that restarts itself, freezes on its screen or drops a call halfway through. Tired power supplies, a failed battery backup, a lost network or cellular link, or a summer storm surge that damaged the electronics each produce that symptom, and the fix depends on which one it is.

In the brick walk-ups along Westchester Avenue and the side streets, the most common failure is the top floor going quiet while the lower floors still work, which almost always means the riser has a break above the last working apartment. In houses, it is the old pattern of a bell that rings the wrong floor, a button that does nothing because the small transformer in the cellar has died, or a release that hums upstairs while the street door stays shut because the latch no longer lines up with the strike. That last one is rarely an intercom fault at all. A door that has sagged, a frame that shifted, or a new lockset fitted by someone who never checked the strike will hold the latch too tightly to let go.

One review of a Bronx intercom company that we read online described a technician breaking a resident's working bell while fixing someone else's. That is the failure every building fears most during a repair, and our routine is built to prevent it: before we touch anything, we call a sample of working apartments and note which ones answer; after the repair, we call the same apartments again, so the building knows the fix didn't trade one dead station for another. Until we arrive, keep the entrance door locking with its key and resist the urge to prop it open for visitors, since that turns a broken intercom into an unlocked building. If a power supply is hot to the touch, smells burnt or is buzzing loudly, switch off its breaker and leave it off. Then call (646) 490-0629. Many single faults, such as a dead supply, a broken riser pair, a strike that needs moving, or a panel that lost its programming, are found and fixed on one visit. Service calls and extended tracing are billed at $195 per hour with a three-hour minimum, and we estimate the tracing time before starting. If the repair would cost a large share of a replacement, or parts are no longer made, we write both options down so the board or owner can decide with numbers in front of them.

The 1970 co-op: a lobby panel that has to answer for every apartment

A building like The Pelham, finished in 1970 with 95 apartments, sits on the side of the 1968 line where the Multiple Dwelling Law expects a working intercom at the self-locking main entrance. Parker Custom Security describes a non-working intercom in a covered building as a Class B hazardous violation under the Housing Maintenance Code, enforceable when a tenant or shareholder complains, and Connextivity notes that a system where some apartments work and others don't still counts as a violation. For a board, that turns the lobby panel from a convenience item into a maintenance obligation, and it changes how we scope the job. The survey covers the panel, its power supply and backup, the riser closets on each floor, and a sample of apartments on every line, top floor included.

Replacement in a building this size is usually a two-stage plan. The lobby panel and its head-end equipment go in first, wired so the old apartment stations keep working where the existing riser allows, and then the apartment stations are changed floor by floor on scheduled days. Where shareholders bought their own monitors years ago, we test whether those can stay on the new system or need replacing, and the board decides policy before anyone is told what they're getting. Throughout the changeover, the lobby door keeps locking with its key, and at the end every apartment is called from the lobby and released while someone watches the door relatch. The super gets a one-page note covering the power supply's location and breaker, the directory, who can change it, and what to check first when a shareholder reports a dead station.

The early-1960s elevator co-ops: older risers and a rule that needs reading

1870 Pelham Parkway South went up in 1960 and 1910 in 1964. Both are elevator buildings of about fifty apartments, and both predate the 1968 line. That does not mean they can ignore the intercom. ButterflyMX says pre-1968 buildings need a careful review, and the Department of Buildings rule 1 RCNY 42-01 addresses buildings erected or converted before January 1, 1968 that contain eight or more apartments and lists an intercommunication system among their entrance requirements. We set out what each source says later on this page. In practice, most of these boards are not asking whether they may let the intercom die; they're asking how to replace a panel installed decades ago without spending money the reserve fund doesn't have.

The engineering question in these buildings is the riser. Cable pulled in the early 1960s may still be sound, or it may have been patched so many times that a new panel would inherit every weak splice. We test each line's pairs from the panel position to a sample of apartments before quoting, and the quote says plainly which lines can carry the new system and which need new cable. If the building's internet or cellular service will carry app calls, that connection is written into the plan with a battery backup, and the board, not the installer, holds the administrator login.

The tower on Middletown Road: twelve floors, two elevators and a long directory

Pelham Bay Towers at 3121 Middletown Road was completed in 1963, has twelve stories and 157 units, and Homes.com lists it both as a condominium building and under co-op status, which is why the first conversation there is about who actually decides. A building of that size needs a directory panel visitors can search, not a wall of buttons, and the riser distance from the lobby to the twelfth floor is long enough that the cable type and the power arrangement matter. We look at where the risers run relative to the elevator shafts, how the floors are fed, whether there are secondary entrances to the lot or the side, and what the building currently uses to connect any app or phone calls.

Directory programming is a job in itself in a building with 157 households. We load names or apartment numbers as the board decides, set call rollover rules for unanswered calls where the system allows, and test calls to apartments at the far end of every line. Changes after move-outs are handed to the managing agent or the super with written steps, so the directory stays current without a service call every time someone sells.

The brick walk-up: one riser, one stair hall, one hard-working door

Pelham Bay's walk-ups are typically brick, three to six stories, with a vestibule at the street and a stair hall climbing past every apartment. The buzzer riser usually runs up that stair hall, sometimes inside old pipe, sometimes stapled behind molding, sometimes in a chase beside the plumbing. A Bronx installer's published advice for pre-war walk-ups is to use existing risers where possible to avoid major wall work, and we agree with the intention, but only after the riser has been tested. A riser with one bad splice can usually be repaired; a riser that fails on several floors is cheaper to replace than to chase forever.

Walk-up street doors take more punishment than lobby doors in elevator buildings, because every resident, visitor and courier uses the same one. Before a new strike goes in, we check that the closer brings the door shut from fully open and that the latch catches without anyone pulling it. In a building of three or more households, the door has to be self-closing under HPD's rules for all multiple dwellings, and an electric strike must never become the reason it stops latching.

The store with apartments above: two entrances that must never be confused

Westchester Avenue is lined with buildings like 3211, built in 1927 with two stories and four units, where the apartments' door sits a few feet from the shop's. Under the el, that residential door needs its own station, its own release and cable that stays out of the shop. The residential panel goes on the side of the door away from the shop entrance, labeled clearly, so a customer doesn't ring an apartment and a resident's visitor doesn't wander into the store. The store, if it wants a system, gets its own, wired separately and powered from its own circuit. Nothing the store controls opens the residents' door, and nothing upstairs opens the shop.

The house: one family, two families, or three and a cellar door

In a detached or semi-detached house, the question is reach and privacy rather than law. A family upstairs in a Colonial Revival on Wilkinson Avenue often can't hear the hall chime at the back of the house, and a video station on the floor where they spend their evenings solves that better than a louder bell. In a two-family or three-family house, like the listings describing three large units with a cellar entrance in back, each household gets its own button, its own station and a release that opens only the door that household uses. The cellar entrance, if a household uses it, gets its own small station so their visitors stop ringing upstairs first. We test the existing bell wire before reusing it, run new cable along the cellar ceiling and up through a shared stair hall or closet where we can, and keep the power supply off the cellar floor on any block that has taken water.

The new building near the terminal: planned on the drawings

The buildings permitted along Westchester Avenue, Crosby Avenue, Gillespie Avenue and Colonial Avenue, from 11 homes to a 17-story tower, are all new multiple dwellings, and those with eight or more homes fall under the post-1968 requirement. We work with the general contractor on the panel location and height, the cable path from the panel to an equipment closet and up to every apartment, strikes and closers on each entrance, and a dedicated circuit for the power supply, installed by a licensed electrician. Where the building has covered parking, as 3217 Westchester Avenue's lottery listing describes, the garage entrance is part of the same plan. Before move-in, every apartment is called from the lobby, every release is tested while someone watches the door relatch, and the owner holds the administrator account.

Field Notes

Field Notes: How a Pelham Bay Survey Runs Before Any Quote Is Written

Start with the paperwork question. The first thing the technician writes down is the number of apartments and the year the building was erected or converted. In a neighborhood where co-ops built in 1960, 1963, 1964 and 1970 sit within a short walk of each other, that one line on the survey sheet decides which rules the design must satisfy.

Call a sample of apartments first. Before anything is opened, we call apartments on every line and every few floors from the panel and note which ones ring, which ones talk and which ones release the door. That record protects the building: it shows what worked before we touched it.

Open the riser closets and the cellar. The power supply, its breaker, any battery backup and the way the riser leaves the panel tell us how many repairs the system has already absorbed. Twisted splices, mismatched cable and taped junctions each say something about what can stay.

Let the door close on its own. We open the entrance door fully and let go. If it doesn't latch without help, no intercom will fix the building's real problem, and the closer or the frame goes on the quote before the strike does.

Walk every entrance, not just the front. Side doors to parking, rear doors to yards, cellar entrances and garage people doors are where buildings in this part of the Bronx have had trouble. We note each one and ask the board or owner whether it should be part of the system.

Risers, Doors and Power

Risers, Lobby Doors and Power Supplies: Wiring a Pelham Bay Building So the Intercom Lasts

The panel is the part everyone sees, but most of an intercom's working life is decided in the riser closets, the cellar and the door frame. This section walks through what we check in Pelham Bay buildings and houses and what we do about each finding, so the written quote reads clearly line by line.

Testing riser pairs before trusting them. In the elevator co-ops and walk-ups, each apartment's station connects back to the panel over a pair or a few pairs of a multi-pair riser cable. We test each pair end to end for continuity, look for shorts and leakage between conductors, and check for splices in pull boxes on each floor. A pair that tests clean can carry a two-wire audio or video system to that apartment. A pair that has been spliced several times, or that fails under test, gets replaced. We would rather tell a board that three lines need new cable than install a new panel that inherits three lines' worth of dropped calls.

Finding a route when the riser has to be replaced. Where a line of apartments needs new cable, the cleanest route is usually the existing riser path: the chase beside the plumbing stack, the closet stack that the original installer used, or the stair hall in a walk-up. Where that path is full or blocked, we look at the corridor ceiling in elevator buildings and, as a last resort, a painted surface raceway in a common hall. We show the board or owner the route before drilling, and nothing goes through an occupied apartment without the shareholder's or tenant's agreement and an appointment.

Making the lobby door relatch every time. Lobby doors in the parkway co-ops are usually heavy, metal-framed and glazed, and they are opened hundreds of times a day. A strike fitted to such a frame has to sit exactly where the latch lands with the door at rest, and the closer has to carry the door past the strike firmly without slamming. We test by releasing the door from several apartments and letting it close on its own each time. HPD requires building entrance doors in all multiple dwellings to be self-closing, and an owner who fails to keep them in good repair faces a class C immediately hazardous violation, according to HPD's self-closing door rule. A strike that holds a door open is worse than no strike at all.

Fitting old house doors. Many Pelham Bay houses keep their original wooden doors and frames. A strike lives in the jamb, so we look at the jamb's thickness, the latch position with the door pushed shut, and whether the door has sagged so the latch now rides low. A sound frame takes a mortised strike exactly where the latch lands. A patched or thin frame may need building up first, or a surface-mounted strike. A door the family won't let anyone cut can get a talk-and-see system without a release, which is a reasonable choice in a one- or two-family house no rule covers.

Power that stays on. The power supply should sit on its own labeled circuit, mounted where the super or owner can find it, with a battery backup where the system calls for one and surge protection on the line. Summer thunderstorms are a common time for panels to fail, and a surge suppressor costs far less than a panel. In houses on lower blocks, and in any cellar that has taken water, the supply goes high on a wall or upstairs. NYC Parks has identified eight areas of Pelham Bay Park as vulnerable to flooding, and while that study concerns the park, it is a reminder that cellars near the water deserve the same caution. Any new line-voltage circuit is installed by a licensed electrician; the low-voltage intercom work is done under our New York State license #12000287431.

Connections for app calls. Networked panels that ring residents' phones depend on an internet or cellular link. We document which one the system uses, put the network equipment on battery backup, and write the account details into the handover record in the board's or owner's name. If the link drops, the panel should still ring wall stations where the building has them, and we test that before we leave.

Leaving a record. Every job ends with a written note: which pair serves which apartment line, where the power supply and its breaker are, which strike and closer models were fitted, and the administrator details for networked systems. The Rules at the Door

The 1968 Line, Pre-War Walk-Ups and Houses: What New York Rules Mean for a Pelham Bay Intercom

Within a short walk in Pelham Bay you can pass a house no intercom rule touches, a pre-1968 co-op whose obligations need careful reading, and a post-1968 co-op that must keep a working intercom for every apartment. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. This is what the public sources say, and where they disagree, so boards, owners, developers and residents can ask the right questions of counsel, HPD or the Department of Buildings.

Buildings of eight or more apartments erected or converted after January 1, 1968. Parker Custom Security summarizes Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a this way: every Class A multiple dwelling erected or converted after that date with eight or more apartments must have an intercommunication system at its self-locking main entrance door. The Pelham at 1874 Pelham Parkway South, built in 1970 with 95 units, and the new buildings of eight or more homes near the terminal fall into this group.

Buildings of eight or more apartments from before 1968. The Department of Buildings rule 1 RCNY 42-01 addresses buildings erected or converted before January 1, 1968 containing eight or more apartments and lists self-closing, self-locking doors and an intercommunication system among their entrance requirements. ButterflyMX describes these older buildings as needing a more careful review. The early-1960s co-ops on the parkway and the tower on Middletown Road fall into this group, and a board should have counsel read the rule against its own building rather than assume the building is exempt.

Buildings of three to seven apartments, where the sources disagree. Parker Custom Security ties the intercom requirement to eight or more units. Seneca Security says the maintenance rule applies to most residential buildings with three or more units. Both cannot be right, and the gap covers many of Pelham Bay's walk-ups and three-family houses. We haven't relied on either claim. What all the sources agree on is the door: HPD's ABCs of Housing says building entrance doors and unit entrance doors in all multiple dwelling types must be self-closing.

One- and two-family houses. A house with one or two households is not a multiple dwelling, and none of the sources we read require it to have an intercom. Whatever goes on the door of a detached house on Hobart Avenue or a semi-detached duplex on the Hutchinson River Parkway is the owner's choice.

Broken intercoms and how complaints become violations. Parker Custom Security calls a non-working intercom or buzzer in a covered building a Class B hazardous violation under the Housing Maintenance Code, enforceable on tenant complaint. Connextivity says complaints filed through 311 trigger HPD inspections and that a system where some apartments work and others don't is a violation. HPD's own guidance tells owners, managers and supers to repair broken locks or intercoms immediately. We couldn't confirm the violation class from HPD directly, so we report what the secondary sources say.

Audio is enough. Impera Security says a basic audio intercom meets the requirement. Video is an upgrade boards choose for security and convenience, not a legal standard.

Mounting heights. 1 RCNY 42-01 says no push button may be more than six feet from the floor and the speaking and listening device must be between four and five feet from the floor. On the steps of a parkway co-op or a stoop on a side street, we measure from where a visitor actually stands.

Permits and licensing after December 2025. UpCodes summarizes Administrative Code section 28-105.4.7, effective December 21, 2025: no electrical permit is required for signaling, communication, alarm and data circuits under 50 volts, provided the work is performed by a licensed master electrician, special electrician or qualified person. A Bronx low-voltage resource adds that General Business Law Article 6-D requires a Department of State license to install or service security systems. Another source, Low Voltage Nation, says low-voltage work requires permits through DOB. We note the disagreement rather than resolve it, and any new line-voltage circuit for a power supply still needs a licensed electrician.

Landmarks. The Orchard Beach bathhouse and promenade became a city landmark in 2006, and the Bartow-Pell Mansion is a designated city landmark and National Historic Landmark. Both are inside Pelham Bay Park. We found no historic district covering Pelham Bay's residential blocks, so Landmarks Preservation Commission facade rules probably don't reach these entrances, but a search that found nothing isn't proof, so we check each address.

Getting out. Every door fitted with an electric strike or lock must open freely from inside without a key, code or button. Every system we install controls the way in only.

Co-ops, condos and the rules

Two co-ops near each other on Pelham Parkway South were finished six years apart, one in 1964 and one in 1970. Why would the intercom law treat them differently?

Because the state drew its line at January 1, 1968. Parker Custom Security summarizes Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a as requiring every Class A multiple dwelling erected or converted after that date with eight or more apartments to have an intercommunication system at its self-locking main entrance. A 1970 building with 95 apartments, like The Pelham at 1874, is clearly inside that rule. A 1964 building with 52 apartments, like 1910 next door, was built before the line, and its obligations come from other provisions. The Department of Buildings rule 1 RCNY 42-01 addresses pre-1968 buildings of eight or more apartments and lists an intercommunication system among their entrance requirements, while ButterflyMX describes these buildings as needing careful review. So the older co-op shouldn't assume it is free to let its intercom fail; it should have counsel read the rules against its own history, including whether the building was ever converted. Practically, both buildings face the same reality: shareholders expect a working lobby panel, a partly working system invites complaints, and the self-closing door requirement applies to both.

Listings describe our Middletown Road building as a condominium in one place and a co-op in another. Who actually decides on a new intercom, and who signs your contract?

The governing documents decide, not the listing sites. Homes.com describes Pelham Bay Towers as a condominium building and lists it with co-op status in the same entry, and buildings in that position sometimes have a history, such as a conversion or a mixed ownership structure, that explains the confusion. Before we quote, we ask the managing agent who holds authority over common elements like the lobby, the entrance doors and the risers: a co-op board acting for the corporation, or a condo board acting for the unit owners. That body approves the scope and signs. What happens inside individual apartments can be different. In many buildings the station inside a home belongs to that home's owner or shareholder, and the bylaws or proprietary lease say who pays for it. We write the quote so the common-element work and the in-apartment work are separate lines, which lets the board apply whatever its documents say without us guessing. If nobody can find the answer, we'll wait while the board's attorney finds it; a signature from the wrong body helps nobody.

The risers in our early-1960s building run beside the elevator shafts. How do you find out which old pairs are still good before the board commits to a new panel?

We test them from both ends before quoting. With the board's permission, the technician works from the panel position or the head-end location and tones each line's pairs up to the riser closets, then tests a sample of apartments on every line, including the top floor, where long runs and old splices show their weaknesses first. Each pair is checked for continuity, shorts between conductors and leakage, and each pull box we can open is inspected for twisted or taped splices. Where the riser runs beside the elevator shaft, we also note how the cable is supported and whether earlier repairs added runs that bypass the riser altogether. The result is a line-by-line table in the quote: these lines carry a new two-wire system as they are, these need one splice repaired, and these need new cable. A Bronx installer's published advice is to use existing risers where possible to avoid major wall work, and testing is how we tell the board where that advice holds and where it doesn't.

Sources disagree on whether low-voltage work needs a filing with the Department of Buildings since the December 2025 code change. What should our board ask an intercom contractor to show?

Ask for the contractor's credentials and a clear statement of who will do the line-voltage work, rather than a promise about permits. UpCodes summarizes Administrative Code section 28-105.4.7, effective December 21, 2025, as saying no electrical permit is needed for signaling and communication circuits under 50 volts when the work is performed by a licensed master electrician, special electrician or qualified person. A Bronx low-voltage resource notes that General Business Law Article 6-D separately requires a Department of State license to install or service security systems. Low Voltage Nation says low-voltage work requires DOB permits. Given that disagreement, a board should ask to see the installer's state license, its insurance certificate naming the corporation, and the name and license of the electrician who will install any new circuit for the power supply. We work under New York State license #12000287431, provide a certificate of insurance on request, and coordinate any new circuit with a licensed electrician who handles whatever filing that work requires.

Garages, cars and footage

After the break-in at a Pelham Parkway co-op's garage, our board wants to know whether visitors should ever be buzzed into our garage, or whether it should be residents only.

For most co-op garages we recommend residents only, with visitors directed to the lobby. ABC7 reported that the thief in the January 2025 case lifted the garage door before breaking into cars inside, which shows that a garage is a building entrance in its own right, not just a parking space. If the garage door can be buzzed open from an apartment, every resident becomes a possible weak point, because a convincing voice at a call station can get a car or a person into a space with no doorman and many vehicles. Our usual layout is a credential reader or fob for residents on the vehicle door and the people door, a station on the people door that rings the super or management rather than individual apartments, and a door position contact so the board knows when the overhead door is left open. If the board does want visitors to reach the garage, perhaps for a guest parking arrangement, we can route those calls to the super's station during set hours only. Either way, the overhead door's own hardware should close and lock fully, and we check that before connecting anything.

Our block has had overnight car break-ins. Is a door intercom any use against that, or is it purely a camera problem?

A door intercom does almost nothing for a car parked on the street, and we would rather say so than sell you something for the wrong reason. News 12 reported the May 2026 break-ins and airbag thefts near Pilgrim, St. Theresa and Edison Avenues as happening on the street overnight, and an intercom only acts when someone comes to a door and presses a button. Where an intercom helps is the next step people worry about: someone trying a building door, a side gate or a garage entrance at night. A video station records who rings, a release that relatches stops a door from being left open behind a visitor, and stations or readers on side and garage doors bring those entrances into the same plan as the front. For the street itself, a camera aimed at the curb, good lighting and an off-street spot are the tools. If a camera is what the building really needs, we will tell you so and keep the intercom quote separate, so the two decisions don't get mixed together.

Our co-op's new lobby panel will record video of everyone who rings. Who should be allowed to see that footage, and what do we do when the 45th Precinct asks for it?

Decide that in writing before the panel goes live. Most video panels can store clips or snapshots on the panel, a recorder or a maker's cloud account, and the board chooses who holds access. We suggest a short policy: the managing agent and one or two named board members can view and export footage, the super can view live video but not export it, and access is logged. Retention should be long enough to be useful after an incident and short enough that the building isn't keeping months of shareholders' comings and goings. When the 45th Precinct asks for footage, the request goes to the named person, who exports the specific clip covering the specific time rather than handing over the system login. We set the system up so exporting a clip is simple and doesn't require us. Shareholders should be told the panel records, which a sign at the entrance and a note in the building newsletter take care of. The board's attorney should review the policy, since we are installers, not lawyers.

Money, shops and restaurants

A vendor offered our 52-unit co-op a lobby panel with no upfront cost and a monthly lease. How does that compare with buying the system outright?

The two models spread the same costs differently, and the lease usually adds the vendor's financing and service margin on top. With a purchase, the co-op pays for the panel, the apartment stations, the door hardware and the labor at installation, owns the equipment, and pays only for any maker's software or cellular plan the system requires plus repairs when needed. With a lease, the vendor owns the equipment, the co-op pays monthly for a set term, and the contract decides what happens to service, upgrades and removal at the end. Before signing a lease, a board should ask what the total of all payments over the term comes to, whether the equipment can stay or must be returned, what happens if the vendor is sold or stops answering, and who holds the administrator login. We sell and install systems the co-op owns, and our quote lists any recurring software or connectivity charges from the maker separately, shown before the board chooses, so the comparison with a lease is a straight one.

Our Westchester Avenue shop wants two doors that work as a pair, so the inner door won't release until the outer one has closed behind a customer. Can an intercom do that?

Yes. That arrangement is usually called an interlock, and it can be built with two electric strikes or locks, door position contacts on both doors and a controller that refuses to release one door while the other is open. The customer rings at the outer door, the staff member at the counter sees them on a video station and releases the outer door, the customer steps into the vestibule, and only after the outer door has closed and latched can the inner door be released. It fits shops that keep the door locked and buzz customers in. Before installing it, we check three things: that both doors and frames are strong enough for strikes, since a glass door broken by a rock, as happened at a hardware store on the avenue, is a glazing problem a strike cannot fix; that the vestibule is large enough for someone with a stroller or wheelchair; and that both doors open freely from inside for exit at all times, which is non-negotiable.

FAQ

Pelham Bay Intercom Installation FAQ

Our lobby panel went dark after a summer thunderstorm. Is a power surge a likely cause, and can the new panel be protected?
+

Surges from storms are a common reason panels and power supplies fail, especially when the supply plugs into an ordinary outlet or shares a circuit with other equipment. A replacement should sit on its own labeled circuit with a surge suppressor, and network or cellular equipment should be on a battery backup that also filters power. Those parts cost far less than a panel. We also check whether the old panel's supply, rather than the panel itself, is what actually failed.

Afternoon sun hits our co-op's entrance directly. Will glare wash out the panel's screen or the camera picture?
+

Direct sun can make some screens hard to read and can flatten a camera image, especially when the visitor stands with bright sky behind them. During the survey we note where the sun falls at the entrance and choose a panel position, a small hood or a model with a brighter screen and wide dynamic range to match. Sometimes moving the panel a short distance along the wall, into the shade of a pier or canopy, solves it entirely.

The intercom cable in our building passes through the boiler room. Can heat damage it, and should it be rerouted?
+

Cable lying near hot pipes or the boiler can degrade over years, and splices in a hot room are a frequent source of intermittent faults. When we find intercom cable in a boiler room, we check its condition and how close it runs to heat sources, and we usually reroute new cable along a cooler path or keep it well clear of pipes and flues. The power supply should never be mounted in a boiler room.

Insects keep nesting behind our outdoor station. What stops that?
+

Insects use the gap between a surface box and the wall and any open cable entry. A properly installed outdoor station has its back box sealed to the masonry, the cable entry plugged, and the station's gasket seated evenly. When we replace a station, we seal those gaps and check the old box for nests and moisture. Spraying insecticide into a station can damage the electronics, so it is better to have the seals fixed.

Some shareholders don't want neighbors' phones showing them walking into the lobby. Can the camera image go only to the apartment being called?
+

On most current systems, yes. The panel's video is sent to an apartment only when a visitor calls that apartment, and residents can't browse the lobby camera unless the board grants that permission. We set the permissions during programming and show the board exactly who can see what. Recorded footage access is a separate setting, held by the people the board names in its written policy.

Can the lobby panel show a short message to visitors, such as sending couriers to a different door?
+

Many directory panels can display a welcome or instruction line on the screen, and some can show it only at certain hours. It works best for one short, stable instruction rather than frequent announcements. We can program it at installation and show the managing agent how to change it. A small printed sign beside the panel is still worth having for visitors who don't read the screen.

What should the notice to residents say before installation day in our building?
+

It should give the dates and the floor schedule, explain that the lobby door will stay locked and work with keys throughout, say which apartments need someone home and for roughly how long, and name a contact at management for reschedules. It should also say what residents will receive afterward, such as a new station and a short instruction sheet. We give the managing agent a draft schedule to base the notice on.

Our new apartment monitors are a different shape from the old handsets. Who deals with the marks and holes left on the walls?
+

New stations rarely cover an old handset's footprint exactly. We choose mounting plates that cover as much of the old mark as possible, fill the old screw holes we create or uncover, and leave the wall clean. Painting to match a resident's wall color is usually left to the resident or the building's painter, and the quote states this so nobody is surprised.

The apartment beside our lobby hears the panel's beeps and calls late at night. Can its volume be lowered after hours?
+

Many panels allow speaker volume and key tones to be adjusted, and some allow a quieter schedule overnight. We can also mount the panel on the side of the entrance away from the shared wall, or on the outer face of the vestibule rather than inside it. Turning the speaker too low hurts visitors' ability to hear residents, so we test with the board before settling on a level.

Our building is about to renovate its lobby with new flooring and paint. Should the intercom work come before or after?
+

Plan them together and do the cable and rough work first. New cable, back boxes, the strike and any new power circuit should go in before walls are painted and floors laid, and the finished panel and trim go on at the end.
