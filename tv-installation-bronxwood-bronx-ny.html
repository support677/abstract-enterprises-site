The Neighborhood

Why Bronxwood Properties Need TV Installation

Bronxwood is a place where the same street can hold a 1950 brick apartment building and a wood-frame two-family house, and a TV goes onto those two walls in completely different ways. The name itself is used loosely. Wikipedia describes Bronxwood as the southern part of Allerton, the half that includes the Pelham Parkway Houses and Boston Road Plaza, with Laconia as the other half. CityNeighborhoods.nyc draws a tighter box, bounded by Pelham Parkway on the south, Bronx Park East on the west, Waring Avenue on the north and Williamsbridge Road on the east. And Forgotten New York uses "Bronxwood Park" for a tiny enclave of two curving roads, North and South Oak Drives, between Wallace and Cruger Avenues just north of Burke Avenue, close to the White Plains Road el. We work in all three, at the same Bronx rate, so this page covers all of them. What matters for a TV is not which map you trust but which building you live in.

Start with the biggest one. According to Wikipedia, the Pelham Parkway Houses are a NYCHA development of 23 six-story buildings completed in June 1950, with buildings sited on the blocks around Mace and Waring Avenues between Bronxwood Avenue and Williamsbridge Road. The Manhattan Institute describes the development as 1,266 apartments scattered over a campus-style setting in 23 six-story brick buildings erected in 1950. That one development holds more homes than most of the surrounding blocks put together, so a large share of the TV calls we expect from Bronxwood come from brick apartments of that age. Brick buildings of the period call for masonry tools; one New York mounting outfit puts it simply, that you need a hammer drill with carbide-tipped masonry bits. A drywall anchor from a hardware store kit, pushed into a wall like that, either refuses to go in or crumbles the plaster around it and holds nothing.

The same development also sets the rules. NYCHA's House Rules say that TV, cable and dish antennas may only be installed inside the apartment, and that video doorbells and cameras must be wireless, with tenants not allowed to make holes in or alterations to the door, the frame or the walls above and around the door. Those rules are written about antennas and doorbells, not TV brackets, and we did not find a line in them about mounting a TV. But they tell you the direction of travel: the landlord cares about holes, and a tenant should have permission in writing before anything is drilled or opened. That is why our first question for a Pelham Parkway Houses apartment is not "what size is the TV" but "what has management said."

Then there are the people who live there. When DNAinfo reported in July 2012 on new security work at the development, it noted that senior citizens made up nearly half of all households in its 23 buildings. Just to the east, on Boston Road, the Bronx Times describes Boston Road Plaza at 2440 Boston Road as a single 20-story tower with 235 apartments, and NYCHA's community plan for it calls it a senior public housing development built in 1972. NYCHA Real Talk records that it converted to private management under the PACT program on September 26, 2024, and lists it as a senior development exclusively. For TV work that combination produces a very particular kind of call: an older resident who wants a bigger screen at a comfortable height, a remote they can actually use, and an installer who will work within a building's rules. Often the person booking is a son, daughter or neighbor rather than the person who will watch the TV.

Off the projects, the housing is lower and older. CityNeighborhoods.nyc describes the internal grid, anchored by Bronxwood Avenue, Paulding Avenue and Barnes Avenue, as made up mainly of low-rise brick homes, two-family houses and small apartment buildings, and says the name "Bronxwood" came into use in the early twentieth century as developers marketed new tracts east of Bronx Park, with the area turning from farmland into a leafy neighborhood between the early 1900s and the 1930s. Wikipedia adds that tenements are scattered through the area, mostly within about five blocks of the el on White Plains Road. A tenant on the third floor of one of those walk-ups, an owner in a brick two-family on a side street and a family in a Pelham Parkway Houses apartment will each be drilling into a different material, and each will need a different conversation about who has to say yes.

Bronxwood Park is the oldest piece. Forgotten New York traces it to the late 1880s and early 1890s, when John T. Adee, who owned considerable property in the northeast Bronx, sponsored a development there and laid out curving roads lined mainly with chestnut and oak trees. Its houses are, in Forgotten New York's words, original multiroom buildings that typically housed two families and have long since been aluminum-sided. WFUV, visiting in 2014, described gated driveways and large porches, and noted that many of the houses are as old as the neighborhood. Aluminum siding on the outside of a house that old is a strong hint that the walls inside are wood framing covered with plaster, or plaster that has since been covered with drywall. Old plaster is where TV mounts go wrong most often: stud finders give false readings, pilot holes crack the finish if they are rushed, and a bolt that misses the stud and catches only lath will hold for a while and then let go.

The people in the side streets add another layer. Wikipedia notes that the area south of Allerton Avenue and east of Bronxwood Avenue has a large Italian-American and Albanian-American population. Many of the two-family houses are shared between an owner on one floor and a tenant on the other, and the TV calls from those houses split roughly two ways: an owner fitting a big screen into a living room they control completely, and a renter on the other floor who needs a TV that looks good without putting holes into a wall that is not theirs. Mounting NYC, writing about rental leases in the city, points out that most standard residential leases prohibit permanent alterations without the landlord's written consent. In a two-family house, the landlord may be the person living downstairs, which usually makes that consent easy to get, but it still has to be asked for.

We also want to be honest about the gaps. We looked for Reddit threads, local news and community posts about TV mounting in Bronxwood, Allerton or the Pelham Parkway Houses, and found none. Yelp's Bronx TV-mounting listings returned only praise in the snippets we could see, so we have no record of local complaints to build on. We did not retrieve HPD violations, 311 complaints or DOB permits for the area. And we have not surveyed the inside of any Bronxwood building; where this page talks about brick behind plaster in a 1950 apartment or plaster on wood lath in a sided house, it is reasoning from the building types and ages in the public record and from what we find in buildings like them, not from a study of these particular buildings. The test hole on the day settles it.

Bronxwood facts that change a TV job

  • One development dominates: the Pelham Parkway Houses, 23 six-story brick buildings completed in 1950, with about 1,260 apartments (the Manhattan Institute gives 1,266), all under NYCHA's House Rules.
  • Antennas stay indoors: NYCHA's House Rules allow TV, cable and dish antennas only inside the apartment, which decides where an antenna or cable line can go in those buildings.
  • A senior tower under new management: Boston Road Plaza, 20 stories and 235 apartments built in 1972, moved to private management under PACT on September 26, 2024, so its rules may no longer match NYCHA's.
  • A low-rise grid: the streets anchored by Bronxwood, Paulding and Barnes Avenues are mainly low-rise brick homes, two-family houses and small apartment buildings, with tenements within about five blocks of the White Plains Road el.
  • A frame-house enclave: Bronxwood Park's houses on North and South Oak Drives date from a development begun in the late 1880s and early 1890s and are mostly sided two-families.
  • Taller buildings next door: Wikipedia describes the neighboring Pelham Parkway area as dominated by six- and seven-story elevator apartment and co-op buildings, so a short move across the parkway can mean another set of building rules.

What usually comes next. Once the TV is on the wall, most Bronxwood households ask about sound. In a brick apartment the easiest answer is a soundbar placed on the cabinet under the screen, at $79, because it needs no more holes. In a house the owner controls, a wall-mounted soundbar at $129 lines up with the TV and leaves the cabinet free. If you think you will want one, tell us when you book, so the soundbar goes on during the same visit rather than needing a second set of holes later.

What the walls are made of

The Manhattan Institute describes the 23 buildings as brick, and that is the starting assumption we bring: an outer wall of masonry, finished inside with a coat of plaster. When the wall you want is an outside wall, the one with the windows in it or the long wall at the end of the living room, we expect the bit to pass through a thin skin of plaster and then meet solid masonry. That is good news for holding a TV. Properly set sleeve anchors in sound masonry hold far more than any TV in a living room weighs. The work is in placing them: in the face of the brick or block rather than in the mortar joint, deep enough to bite, and far enough from the edge of a window opening that the masonry around the hole does not crack. We drill a single test hole where the bracket plate will cover it and look at the dust that comes out. Red dust means brick, gray dust means block or concrete, and white dust that keeps coming means the plaster is thicker than expected or there is a gap behind it.

Interior walls are less predictable. Apartment buildings of that era can have partitions of masonry block, plaster on metal lath, or later gypsum board, and the wall between a living room and a bedroom may not be the same as the wall between two neighbors' apartments. We have seen claims online that the big Bronx complexes all have poured concrete walls; we deal with that claim in the reality check further down, but the short version is that we test every partition rather than guess, and we choose between masonry anchors, toggles rated for the load, or a stand, depending on what the test hole shows.

What the house rules decide

NYCHA's House Rules say antennas for TV, cable and dish may only be installed inside the apartment, and that video doorbells and cameras must be wireless, with no holes in or alterations to the door, the frame or the walls around it. We read that as two practical limits for a TV job. First, any antenna we set up goes indoors, near a window if that helps reception, never on the outside ledge or the fire escape. Second, nothing we do touches the entry door or the wall around it. Beyond that, the rules we read do not mention TV brackets or in-wall cable kits. So we keep it simple: if you want the TV on the wall, ask management and get the answer in writing. If you want the cables inside the wall, ask specifically about that, because opening a wall is a bigger step than four bolt holes. Until you have an answer, a TV on its stand at $137.99, or a wall mount with a paintable cord cover at $59, keeps the job modest.

Getting to the right door

The Manhattan Institute's description of the development is blunt about how hard it can be to find an address: the buildings are set on a diagonal away from the street, and only by wandering around the courtyard can one find individual addresses. The same report describes buzzer panels where the apartment listings had been burned and could not be read. For a technician carrying a 65-inch screen in a padded carrier, that is not a small detail. When you book, give us the building number, say which street or path the entrance faces, tell us if there is a landmark such as a playground or a parking area beside it, and give a phone number that will be answered when we arrive. If you can, come down and meet us at the entrance; it saves time and it means your neighbors see who is coming in with you.

Elevators, stairs and big boxes

Six-story buildings mean an elevator for most apartments, and a resident-run website, pelhamparkwayhouses.us, says elevator modernization was among the upgrades completed at the development in 2025, along with facade and heating work. That is not an official NYCHA source and we have not confirmed it, but either way we plan for a day when a car is out of service. Large screens travel upright in their box or a padded carrier, never flat, because flexing the panel can crack it. If we have to take the stairs, two people carry. If you know an elevator has been unreliable in your building, tell us when booking, and we will plan the visit with that in mind.

Older tenants and the people booking for them

With seniors making up such a large share of the households in 2012 and Boston Road Plaza reserved for seniors, many of our Bronxwood calls are from a family member booking for a parent. We are glad to work that way. The person booking can send the photos and the measurements, pay, and stay on the phone during the visit, and the technician spends the last part of the job with the person who will actually watch the TV, showing them the power button, the input they use and the volume. We also pay attention to height. For someone who watches from a recliner, or from a wheelchair, the right height is lower than the usual living-room advice, and we tape the outline at their eye level, not ours.

Measure from the seat that matters

The best height for a TV depends on whose eyes it is for. Sit in the chair or on the sofa you actually use, look straight ahead, and have someone measure from the floor to your eye level. Then measure from the front of the seat to the wall. In the living rooms of the 1950 apartment buildings and the front rooms of two-family houses, distances are rarely long, and a very large screen in a short room can be tiring to watch. If you are deciding between two sizes, send both measurements and we will tell you how rooms of that size usually work out. For a TV that will be watched from a bed, measure from the pillow instead, because a screen at the usual living-room height ends up far too high for someone lying down.

Look at both sides of the wall

A kitchen, bathroom or radiator on the other side of the TV wall means pipes may run through it. An outlet or light switch on either side means wiring close by. In a two-family house, look at the floor above as well, because upstairs radiators and bathrooms often drop their pipes through downstairs walls. In an apartment building, the neighbor's layout is a mystery, which is one more reason we scan before we drill and keep our holes shallow until the test hole has told us what is there. Your notes tell us where to look hardest.

Know what stays and what goes

Decide before the day whether the old TV is coming down, and whether it stays with you or should be taken away. Taking down an old TV is $85 on the list. If there is an old bracket already on the wall, photograph it, including the label if you can see one, because sometimes it can carry the new TV and sometimes it cannot. And tell us if the cable box or router has to stay where it is, because that decides how far the cables have to travel and whether a cord cover or a cabinet run is the tidier answer.

Brick with plaster on it, and brick with a gap behind it

In older brick buildings, the plaster on an outside or party wall is either laid straight onto the brick or held away from it on wooden strips, which leaves a hollow space between the finish and the masonry. You can often hear the difference: plaster straight on brick sounds flat and solid all the way across, while plaster on strips sounds slightly hollow between the strips. The difference matters because a bracket bolted tight against a hollow finish crushes it and cracks the plaster around the plate. Where we find a gap, we use fixings long enough to reach well into the brick behind it and, where needed, spacers so the bracket bears on the masonry rather than on the plaster. Where the plaster is tight to the brick, we drill straight through into the masonry and set the anchors there. Either way, the brick line of $65 applies, because the work is masonry work.

Old plaster on wood, and the stud finder problem

In a frame house like those on the Oak Drives, the plaster is usually laid over thin horizontal strips of wood called lath, which are nailed across the studs. A stud finder designed for drywall reads every strip of lath as wood, so it can beep along the whole wall. One New York mounting outfit advises that on plaster you either find the studs inside it or use toggle bolts through the wooden lath. We prefer the studs for anything heavier than a soundbar, because lag bolts in solid old framing are the strongest fixing a frame house offers. We use the stud finder to suggest where the studs are, confirm one with a small probe hole that the bracket plate will cover, then measure across to the next. We drill pilot holes slowly, score the paint first so it does not chip, and never force a bolt. That extra care is why the plaster line is $45 on our list.

Partitions we do not trust until we have tested them

The walls between rooms inside an apartment building can be anything from solid masonry to hollow block to plaster on metal lath to modern gypsum on metal studs. Each wants a different fixing. Solid masonry takes sleeve anchors; hollow block takes anchors designed to grip the inside of the block's face; plaster on metal lath and gypsum on metal studs take toggles rated for the load, or a backer that spreads the weight across more than one point. We do not treat any of them as interchangeable, and we will not put a large TV on a full-motion arm onto a thin partition just because the room layout would like it there. If the test hole shows a weak wall, we say so, and suggest another wall or a stand.

On cables in masonry. Hiding cables inside a brick wall is a much bigger job than hiding them inside a hollow frame wall, because there is no empty space between studs to fish them through. That is why, on brick and block, our usual answer is a paintable cord cover, $59, which runs down from the TV to the outlet and can be painted to match the wall. The code-compliant in-wall kit at $169 is the right answer where the wall is hollow, the owner agrees, and there is a clear bay between two studs from the TV down to the outlet height. In a frame house in Bronxwood Park that is often the case; in a 1950 brick apartment it usually is not.

A counter screen is not a living-room TV

A TV in a shop is looked at in quick glances by people standing at a counter or walking in from the street, often from a few steps away and at an angle. That changes where it goes. A menu screen needs to sit above head height so it is not blocked by the people in front, tilted down toward the line, and placed so the light from the shop window does not wash it out at the time of day the shop is busiest. A waiting-room TV needs to be visible from the chairs, not from the door, and quiet enough that the front desk can still hear the phone. In a common room in a senior building, the screen should be at a height that residents in chairs and wheelchairs can watch comfortably, with the controls somewhere a staff member or a resident can reach without climbing. We plan each screen with the owner or manager in the room before we price it.

Masonry, shop hours and the tenants upstairs

Most of the storefronts in Bronxwood are in low brick buildings, and many have apartments above them. Hammer drilling into brick carries through a building, and a tenant who works nights or an older resident upstairs will hear it. The noisy part of a mount is short, a few minutes per bracket once the position is agreed, and the rest of the job is quiet. We schedule the drilling for an hour that suits the shop and, where we can, the people above it, and we ask the shop owner to give the upstairs tenants a word of warning. A shop's own landlord may also want to approve the work and see a certificate of insurance, which we provide on request.

Rules and limits for business screens

  • The TV's own cord stays out of the wall. National Electrical Code §400.12 does not allow flexible cords to be concealed behind walls or run through holes in walls. We use a code-compliant in-wall power and cable kit, or a surface cord cover, never the TV's cord pushed through plaster.
  • New circuits belong to an electrician. Our work is mounting and low-voltage. If a shop wants a new outlet high on the wall behind a screen, that circuit is work for a licensed electrician, and the quote says so.
  • Screens must not stick out into a walkway. The 2010 ADA Standards limit how far wall-mounted objects can project into a circulation path at certain heights, so that people with low vision do not walk into them. A screen beside the path to a counter or a restroom has to be high enough or flat enough to keep clear.
  • Exit signs stay visible. We check sight lines before fixing a bracket, so no screen covers an exit sign or narrows the way out.
  • Opening walls can need a filing. Hoppler Design Build notes that mounting alone usually needs no DOB permit, but opening a wall, building a recessed AV chase or moving electrical circuits does. If a business wants a screen recessed into a wall, that part goes through the proper filing before we mount.
  • Insurance papers. Landlords and managing agents of commercial spaces often want a certificate of insurance before a contractor works. Send the request with the booking and it will be ready.
Field Notes

Field Notes: The Test Hole Before the Bracket in a Brick Apartment

We do not publish stories about a particular customer's apartment unless we can document them, so this is not a tale about one address. It is the routine we follow at the start of every job in a brick apartment of the kind Bronxwood has so many of: a 1950s building, plaster on the walls, a TV still in its box, and often an older resident watching from the doorway.

We introduce ourselves and ask who will watch the TV and from where. We lay blankets down and leave the TV boxed. We knock along the wall and listen for the change from flat to hollow. We run a scanner for pipes and live wiring, then pick one spot that the bracket plate will cover and drill a single small hole, slowly. The dust tells us most of what we need: red for brick, gray for block, white that keeps coming for thick plaster or a gap. If the bit suddenly drops forward, there is a void behind the finish, and we change the fixing plan. Only then do we tape the outline of the screen on the wall, ask the person in the chair to look at it, and adjust it to their eyes. When they agree, the real holes go in. That small hole at the start is the difference between a bracket that holds for years and one that has to be fixed in a month.

Living in the Pelham Parkway Houses

Which anchors actually hold a TV on the outside wall of a 1950 Pelham Parkway Houses apartment?

On masonry, sleeve anchors set into solid brick or block, sized for the bracket and the TV's weight. The buildings are described as six-story brick buildings, so we expect a skin of plaster over masonry, but we confirm it with one test hole before choosing. We place anchors in the face of the brick or block rather than in the mortar joints, which hold far less, and keep them back from the edges of window openings so the masonry does not crack. Plastic plugs and drywall anchors from a bracket's box do not belong in a wall like this. The brick line of $65 is added to the base mount, because the work needs a hammer drill, carbide bits and slower drilling.

The only good TV spot in our apartment is the wall between the living room and the bedroom. Is an inside wall like that strong enough?

It depends entirely on what the partition turns out to be, which is why we test it first. Inside walls in apartment buildings of that era can be solid masonry, hollow block, plaster on metal lath or later gypsum board. Solid masonry carries a TV as well as an outside wall. Hollow block needs anchors made to grip the inside of the block. Plaster on metal lath or gypsum needs rated toggles, and a heavy TV on a moving arm may be too much for it. If the test hole shows a weak partition, we tell you before any bracket goes up and suggest either a fixed low-profile bracket, which puts the least leverage on the wall, or the TV on its stand against that wall instead.

Can the TV's cables go inside the wall in a NYCHA apartment, or does it have to be a cover on the surface?

We only open a wall in a NYCHA apartment if you have management's approval in writing for that specific work. The House Rules we read cover antennas and doorbells, not in-wall kits, so we do not assume either way, and opening a wall is a bigger change than drilling bolt holes. In practice, the outer walls of the 1950 buildings are masonry, where in-wall runs are rarely practical anyway. The usual answer is a paintable cord cover at $59, run straight down from the TV to the outlet and painted to match. It is tidy, it comes off when you move out, and it does not need anyone to cut into the wall.

We would rather watch free channels with an indoor antenna than pay for cable. Where can it go under the house rules?

Inside the apartment. NYCHA's House Rules allow TV, cable and dish antennas to be installed only inside the apartment, so nothing goes on the outside window ledge, the fire escape or the facade. Within that rule, an indoor antenna usually does best near a window that faces open sky, kept off metal surfaces, with its cable run neatly to the TV. We place it, connect it and run a channel scan, and if the reception is better in another spot, we try that before we leave. We cannot promise which channels a particular apartment will receive, because that depends on the direction it faces and the buildings around it, but a scan on the day shows you exactly what you have.

Our building sits back on a diagonal in the courtyard. What should we tell you so the technician finds the right door?

Tell us the building number, which street or path the entrance faces, anything next to it that is easy to spot, and a phone number that will be answered on the day. The Manhattan Institute described these buildings as set on a diagonal away from the street, so that only by wandering around the courtyard can one find individual addresses, and it also reported buzzer listings that had been burned and could not be read. A technician carrying a large TV should not be wandering. If you can come down to meet us when we call from the van, it saves time and lets your neighbors see who is coming in with you.

Our building had heating and facade work recently. Does new work behind the walls change where you can drill?

It can, so we look rather than assume. A resident-run website for the development, not an official NYCHA source, says facade renovations, elevator modernization and heating improvements were completed in 2025. Facade work is outside and does not usually change the inside face of a wall, but new heating pipes or risers may run in places the old ones did not. Before we drill, we scan for metal and live wiring, look for any new pipes, valves or access panels near the TV wall, and keep our test hole shallow until we know what is behind it. If you saw workers open a wall in your apartment, tell us which one, because that is exactly where we will look hardest.

Houses on the grid and in Bronxwood Park

Our house on one of the Oak Drives has aluminum siding outside. What does that tell you about the walls inside?

Mostly that the house is a wood-frame building. Forgotten New York describes Bronxwood Park's houses as original two-family buildings that have long since been aluminum-sided, and siding of that kind is a covering added over the original exterior, not a sign of masonry. Inside a frame house of that age we expect wood studs with plaster on lath, or plaster that a later owner covered with drywall. Both carry a TV well once lag bolts are in the studs. The work is finding the studs reliably and drilling without cracking the plaster, so we confirm the framing with a probe hole behind the bracket plate. The plaster line of $45 applies where the finish is plaster rather than drywall.

Our two-family is brick, but when I knock on the inside of the outer wall it sounds hollow. Which is it?

Probably both. In many older brick houses the plaster inside an outer wall is not laid straight onto the brick but on wooden strips fastened to it, which leaves a narrow gap between the finish and the masonry. That gap is what sounds hollow. It matters for the mount because a bracket tightened against a hollow finish can crush it and crack the plaster. Where we find a gap, we use fixings long enough to reach well into the brick behind and, if needed, spacers so the bracket bears on the masonry. The test hole tells us how deep the gap is. The job is priced on the brick line, $65, because the anchors go into brick.

I rent and my lease bans alterations without written consent. Does a TV on its stand with a cord cover count as an alteration?

A TV set on its own stand on furniture involves no change to the wall at all, so it is the safe choice while you wait for an answer. A cord cover is different: it is fixed to the wall, usually with adhesive or small screws, so we treat it as something to ask about. Mounting NYC notes that most standard residential leases in New York prohibit permanent alterations without the landlord's written consent, and Brick Underground has written about landlords who object to anything hung on walls. We are not giving legal advice, and your lease is the document that counts. Ask your landlord in writing, name the wall and what will be fixed to it, and book the stand or the mount depending on the answer.

We just moved into a Housing Connect lottery apartment. Are newer affordable buildings stricter about mounting a large TV?

Some are, and it is worth asking before the TV arrives. A City-Data forum thread from someone in a Housing Connect apartment asked exactly this question about large TVs, which tells you how common the uncertainty is. Newer buildings are managed by private companies with their own lease terms, and many are built with gypsum walls on metal studs, which hold a TV well with the right fixings but not with the plastic anchors in a bracket box. Ask the management office whether wall mounting is allowed and on which walls, and whether they want a certificate of insurance. Then send us the answer with a photo of the wall, and we will tell you which lines of the list apply.

Could our TV be set flush into a niche cut into the living room wall instead of hanging in front of it?

It can be done, but it is a construction job before it is a TV job. Hoppler Design Build points out that mounting a TV alone usually needs no Department of Buildings permit, while opening a wall, building a recessed AV chase or moving electrical circuits does. A recessed niche involves all three, and in a brick house it also means cutting masonry. That work belongs to a general contractor and a licensed electrician, with the proper filing. Once the niche is built and finished, we mount the TV in it, run the low-voltage cables and set up the devices. If you are planning a renovation anyway, tell us early so the niche is sized for the TV and its bracket.

Our Installation Process

Every Bronxwood TV job follows the same eight steps, whether it is a small TV on a bedroom partition in a 1950 apartment, a large screen on the plaster wall of a sided frame house, or a menu screen over a counter near the el. The order matters most in masonry and old plaster, where a wall read too quickly is what leads to a sagging bracket.

1. Send the address, building and photos

Street, building number and entrance, floor, the TV model and size, and a photo of the wall. We match the job to the Bronx price list before you book; TV jobs are booked and paid as set out on our policies page.

2. Settle permission and access

Who controls the wall (NYCHA, private management, a co-op board or the house owner), any certificate of insurance, the elevator or stairs, and quiet hours for a shop or a sleeping household.

3. Read the wall

Knock test, a scan for pipes and wiring, and one small probe hole where the bracket plate will cover it, to confirm brick, block, plaster on lath, plaster on furring or drywall.

We tape the screen's outline on the wall and check it from the chair, wheelchair or bed it is for, and agree it with you before drilling.

Inside the wall with a code-compliant kit where permitted and practical, under a paintable cord cover, or down to the cabinet, exactly as booked.

7. Hang the TV and connect devices

Two people lift the screen onto the bracket; then the soundbar, cable box, indoor antenna or streaming device is connected and checked.

8. Test, clean up and walk through

Every input tested, the remote shown to the person who will use it, dust vacuumed, and the bracket and fixing details left in writing.

FAQ

Bronxwood TV Installation FAQ

If a setup-only visit takes twenty minutes, is the full first hour still charged?
+

Yes. A setup-only visit is $225 for the first hour, whether the work takes twenty minutes or the full hour, because that covers the trip and the technician's time on the day. Anything past the first hour is billed in 30-minute blocks. To make the most of the hour, have the TV already in place, the cable box or antenna nearby, and any streaming account passwords written down before we arrive.

Should our soundbar sit on the cabinet or go on the wall under the TV, and how do the prices compare?
+

A soundbar placed on the cabinet is $79 and a wall-mounted soundbar is $129. On the cabinet it needs no extra holes, which suits rentals and NYCHA apartments. On the wall it lines up neatly under the screen and frees the cabinet top, which suits a house where the owner is happy with the drilling. The sound is much the same either way, as long as nothing blocks the front of the bar.

When the old TV comes down and the new one goes up on the same wall, how is that priced?
+

Taking down the old TV is $85, and the new TV is priced from the usual mount lines for its size and the wall. If the old bracket is rated for the new TV and fits its mounting pattern, we may be able to reuse it and save you buying a new one. If not, we fit a suitable bracket in the same spot, using the existing solid fixing points where they are sound.

Why does a plaster wall cost more than drywall when plaster feels harder?
+

Because plaster is hard on the surface but brittle behind it, and the wood lath under it fools stud finders. Finding the studs takes a probe hole, pilot holes have to be drilled slowly with the paint scored first, and a rushed bit can crack a whole section. That extra time and care is the $45 plaster line on our list.

I own our two-family and the tenant upstairs wants a TV mounted. Can I book it and decide how it is done?
+

Yes. As the owner you can book and pay, and tell us your conditions, for example a wall mount with a cord cover but no in-wall work, or a specific wall only. We follow those conditions and tell the tenant what was agreed. If the tenant books instead, we ask them to show your written agreement before we drill.

My father watches from a wheelchair. Can the TV be hung lower than usual for him?
+

Yes. We measure his eye height while he sits in the wheelchair where he watches, and tape the outline of the screen at that height before drilling, so he can look at it and say if it feels right. A slight tilt can help if the screen has to sit a little higher than his eyes. We also make sure the power and input buttons, or the remote's usual spot, are within his reach.

Someone in our home is recovering in bed for a few months. Can the TV be angled toward the bed?
+

Often, using a full-motion arm that lets the screen turn toward the bed and back toward the room later. An arm puts more pull on its fixings than a flat bracket, so we only fit one on a wall that tests strong, such as solid masonry or wood studs. We measure from the pillow rather than from a chair, because a screen at living-room height is too high for someone lying down.

What happens if the elevator in our building is out of service on the day of the install?
+

We call before we come, so tell us if you know the elevator is down. For smaller TVs and upper floors that are not too high, two technicians can usually carry the screen up the stairs in its box or a padded carrier. For very large screens or the top of a six-story building, it may make more sense to move the visit, and rescheduling terms are set out on our TV installation policies page.

Drilling brick makes a lot of red dust. Do you clean it up?
+

Yes. We lay blankets under the work area before drilling, hold a vacuum nozzle at the bit when we drill into masonry, and vacuum the floor and the baseboard before we leave. Brick dust is fine and travels, so we also ask you to move anything delicate away from the wall, and to close the door of the next room if someone is sleeping or the windows are open.

Our building's common room needs a TV for residents' programs. Who should book it?
+

Whoever is responsible for the room, usually building management or the tenant association with management's agreement. They know which walls can be drilled, whether a certificate of insurance is needed and who will look after the remote afterwards. We plan the height so residents in chairs and wheelchairs can watch comfortably and put the controls somewhere staff can reach.

Can the cable box be tucked behind the wall-mounted TV so the cabinet stays clear?
+

Sometimes. It needs a bracket with enough space behind the screen, a box small and cool-running enough to sit there, and a remote that works without line of sight, because the TV blocks the box from view. Many cable box remotes need to see the box, so we check your provider's box and remote first. If it will not work, a short run down to the cabinet is the tidier answer.

Do I have to stay in the room for the whole visit?
+

No, but we ask that someone who can make decisions about the wall is there at two moments: at the start, when we agree the exact height and position from the taped outline, and at the end, when we test every input and show how the TV works. In between, you can carry on with your day while we fix the bracket and route the cables.
