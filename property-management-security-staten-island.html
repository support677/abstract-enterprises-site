The Neighborhood

Why Staten Island Property Managers Need a Security Contractor Who Works in Houses, Not Just Lobbies

Ask a Manhattan manager to describe a portfolio and you hear about lobbies, risers and doormen. Ask a Staten Island manager and you hear about driveways. The city's own tax-lot file makes the point. When we pulled the current MapPLUTO records for the borough, two-family buildings accounted for 31,505 lots holding 63,010 apartments, while the Island's 83 elevator apartment lots held 13,585 units between them (NYC Open Data, Primary Land Use Tax Lot Output, Staten Island lots grouped by building class, queried November 27, 2026). In other words, the rental stock a manager handles here is mostly stairs, side doors and porches, with a tower or two near the water.

That shape changes what security work looks like. A two-family house has no lobby panel and no super. It has a doorbell for each floor, a basement door, maybe a separate entrance cut into the side for a lower apartment, a driveway, a detached garage and a backyard that ends at a fence. Multiply that by a dozen addresses scattered from Port Richmond to Tottenville and the manager's real problem is not choosing a flagship product. It is keeping a few hundred small devices, installed by a dozen different people over thirty years, working and accounted for. Owners who self-manage two or three houses have the same problem on a smaller scale and less time to deal with it.

The rules also change at a line most Staten Island portfolios straddle. State law defines a multiple dwelling as a building "occupied as the residence or home of three or more families living independently of each other" (NYS Multiple Dwelling Law §4). Two-family houses sit below that line, so the building-wide intercom and smart-access rules written for apartment houses mostly do not reach them, while the three- and four-family buildings in the same portfolio are inside it. HPD's registration duty bridges the two: owners register every building with three or more units, and one- and two-family homes too whenever neither the owner nor the owner's family lives there (HPD, ABCs of Housing for owners). A management office here has to know which side of the line each address falls on before deciding what to install.

What does not drive the work on Staten Island is the inspector. In 2025 HPD wrote 24 Housing Maintenance Code violations in the borough whose description mentions an intercom, against 1,637 in the Bronx (NYC Open Data, HPD Housing Maintenance Code Violations, 2025 records filtered for "intercom"). Calls here come from tenants who cannot hear the bell, owners who watched a car leave the lot on someone else's phone, and storm damage in the cellar. That is why this page spends more time on doorbells, lots, garages and weather than on lobby panels, and why each section below points to the Staten Island service page that goes deeper into one system.

The Staten Island rental stock in numbers

  • Two-family houses dominate: 31,505 two-family lots with 63,010 apartments, against 873 three-family lots (2,619 units) and 531 four- to six-family lots (2,333 units), by Department of Finance building class (NYC DOF building classifications; MapPLUTO counts above).
  • Garden apartments are a real segment: 86 lots classed as garden apartments hold 3,032 units, the walled-off complexes with lots and courtyards that need gates, cameras and many small entrances.
  • Elevator buildings are few but large: 83 elevator apartment lots hold 13,585 units, an average of about 160 apartments each.
  • Stores with apartments above: roughly 1,270 lots are classed as primarily residential with mixed use, the corner buildings along the commercial avenues.
  • Who registers: HPD registration covers buildings with three or more units and non-owner-occupied one- and two-family homes (HPD property registration).
  • Flood exposure is concentrated here: New York City Emergency Management counts 67,600 buildings in the 1 percent annual chance floodplain citywide and says high-risk areas are concentrated on Staten Island's East Shore (NYC's Risk Landscape 2.0).

Rented Houses

Two- and Three-Family Rentals: Security Sized for a House

The most common call we get from a Staten Island landlord is not about a building at all. It is about a house with two front doors, or one front door and a stairway, where the doorbell for the upstairs apartment stopped ringing and the tenant now waits on the porch for deliveries. The fix is small, but the decisions around it set the pattern for every other house on the list, so it pays to make them once.

Start with who answers. In a house where each apartment has its own door, the cleanest setup is a wired video doorbell at each door, a chime inside each unit and the tenant's phone ringing as well. Nothing runs between floors, nothing is shared, and when one tenant moves out only that doorbell's account needs to change. Where both apartments share one front door and an inside stair, a two-button video station on the door frame does the same job and releases the door from either floor. We size it to the house rather than borrowing an apartment-building panel, which would cost more and give a tenant more to break.

Then decide who owns the accounts. Doorbells and cameras are registered to an email address, and that address controls everything later: who gets alerts, who can see recordings, who can hand access to a new tenant. For a house your office manages, the owner account should sit with the management company or the owner, never with a tenant or the installer, and the tenant should be added as a user who can be removed. That one choice prevents most of the lockouts we are later called to undo. An owner with one rental building rather than a managed portfolio can start with apartment building security systems in Staten Island.

Finally, decide what the owner sees. Most owners want the outside of the property covered: the driveway, the side path, the garage door and the backyard gate. Most tenants do not want the owner watching their own front door. Current camera apps let us split the two, so the owner or manager sees the exterior cameras and each tenant sees only the doorbell for their own apartment. Building it that way from the start avoids the uncomfortable conversation that follows when a tenant discovers the landlord can see who visits them.

Three- and four-family houses are where the shape begins to change. Once three families live independently in a building, it is a multiple dwelling under state law, the shared front door becomes the building entrance, and the city's rules for entrance doors and smart access start to matter. Those buildings usually move up to a small video entry panel with a button per apartment and a release for the street door, which is the territory of our intercom installation work on Staten Island. The same office login can still run the houses and the small buildings side by side.

What a typical rented house needs, and what it does not

  • Usually needed: a doorbell per apartment entrance, a chime or second ringer where the bell is hard to hear, one or two exterior cameras, and a recorder or hub that keeps footage inside the house.
  • Often needed: a camera or motion light on a separate basement entrance, a contact on the detached garage, a keypad deadbolt on a shared door so keys stop changing hands.
  • Rarely needed: a lobby-style entry panel, fob readers, a rack or a monthly platform subscription.
  • Never ours: new electrical circuits, outlets or light fixtures. Where a camera or doorbell needs line power that is not there, your electrician runs it first.
Intercoms & Buzzers

Buzzers and Intercoms in Staten Island Walk-Ups and Garden Buildings

Above the house level, Staten Island's apartment stock is mostly low and spread out. A garden complex can have eight or ten small buildings around a lot, each with its own front door, its own handful of apartments and its own aging buzzer. The equipment is rarely exotic. What wears it out is the setting: doors that face open lots and take the wind, frames that shift on settled foundations, and cable runs between buildings that were buried or stapled along a fence decades ago.

Most repairs start the same way. A tenant reports that the buzzer does nothing, or the door clicks but does not open, or one floor can hear the visitor and cannot answer. The $250 service call finds the failed piece, and the published Staten Island ranges cover nearly everything we find: an apartment station from $250 to $525, a door release relay or button from $250 to $475, a 24-volt transformer from $325 to $650, an electric strike replaced and aligned from $450 to $950, lobby panel work from $650 to $1,800, and riser repair from $850 to $2,400 (door buzzer repair on Staten Island). Older audio systems that keep failing are handled on our Staten Island intercom repair page, which also explains when a repair stops making sense.

When the answer is replacement, the published starting point for a building-wide intercom in a five- to twenty-unit Staten Island building is $1,200 to $4,500 and up, depending on the number of apartments and the system chosen. Garden complexes change the arithmetic because the count that matters is entrances, not apartments: ten small buildings with six apartments each need ten panels, not one. We price each entrance on the walk-through and show where a single platform across all of them saves the office time, since one login then covers every door on the property.

Some managers we work with also run co-op or condo buildings for boards. Those buildings bring board approval, shareholder or owner notices and alteration paperwork into the job, so we have written separate guides for each: the Staten Island co-op security systems guide, the Staten Island condo security systems guide, a decision guide for boards weighing a co-op board intercom upgrade, and the companion piece on condo intercom replacement. A rental owner can skip all of that and decide in one conversation.

Garden-complex entrances: what we look at first

  • Whether each building's door closes and latches on its own before any electronics are touched.
  • Where each building's wiring comes from: its own cellar, a neighboring building, or a cable buried across the lot.
  • Which entrances tenants actually use, since a rear door facing the parking area often carries more traffic than the front.
  • Whether apartments still have working stations, or whether tenants have been answering by phone for years.
Access Control

Keypads, Fobs and Tenant Turnover Across a Staten Island Portfolio

Keys are the hidden cost in a house-heavy portfolio. Every move-out means a locksmith or a lost afternoon, and every set of keys handed to a contractor, an aide or a dog walker is a set you never fully get back. Electronic entry ends most of that. A keypad deadbolt on a shared door or a reader on a laundry room lets the office issue and cancel access from a screen, and the record shows which code or fob opened which door and when.

Choosing between codes and fobs depends on the door. Codes suit doors that many people use occasionally: the side door of a two-family where a cleaner comes weekly, a storage room, a rental office back door. Fobs suit doors that residents use daily, because a fob cannot be shared by shouting it across a parking lot. For the gates and side doors of garden complexes we often combine them, with fobs for tenants and timed codes for vendors. Pricing on the Staten Island access control page runs from $1,500 to $2,000 for a pedestrian gate keypad and $4,000 to $7,000 for a combined vehicle and pedestrian gate system, with larger projects quoted after a survey (access control installation on Staten Island).

The law draws its line at the same place it did for intercoms. New York City's Tenant Data Privacy Law covers "smart access systems" such as fobs, apps and biometric readers in class A multiple dwellings. It requires a written privacy policy and tenants' consent before their data is used, bars selling it, sets deadlines for deleting it, and bars owners from requiring tenants to use smart access to get into their own apartment (Local Law 63 of 2021; HPD summary). A rented two-family house is not a multiple dwelling, but the three-, four- and six-family buildings in the same portfolio usually are. We set up every system so the office can produce a clear record of what is collected, and we keep a physical key option on apartment doors. Whether a given building is covered, and what its policy should say, is a question for the owner's counsel.

The Island's elevator buildings are few, mostly postwar, with a small number of new rentals going up near the ferry. Where an owner wants the cars restricted to residents, a reader in the cab starts from $1,650 per car for card, fob or code on Staten Island, and from $2,750 per car for face, fingerprint or iris readers, with the elevator contractor's connection billed by that contractor (elevator access control on Staten Island). We do the reader, the cable and the programming; only a licensed elevator agency touches the controller.

Lots & Garages

Parking Lots, Driveways and Detached Garages

On Staten Island the car is part of the lease. Garden complexes rent parking rows, two-family houses come with a shared driveway, and many older houses have a detached garage at the back of the lot that the owner keeps for storage. That makes vehicle crime a landlord's problem in a way it rarely is in Manhattan. SILive reported in July 2026 that vehicle thefts on the Island were up roughly 47 percent for the year, "with 163 incidents this year through July 19 compared to 111 incidents during the same time period last year," citing NYPD CompStat (SILive, Maura Grunlund, July 24, 2026).

Cameras do not stop a determined thief, and we say so. What they do well on a rental lot is narrower and still worth the money: they show which car came in and when, they give the police and the insurer something to work with, they push opportunists toward the lot next door, and they settle the daily disputes over who scraped whose bumper or who dumped a mattress by the dumpster. Getting that value depends on placement. A lot camera needs to see faces and plates at the entrance, where cars slow down, not a wide panorama of parked roofs from forty feet up.

Detached garages are the hard case. They usually have no network connection and sometimes no reliable power, and trenching a cable across a yard can cost more than the cameras. Three options cover most of them. A wireless bridge links the garage to the house's network with a pair of small antennas. A solar-charged camera covers the garage door with no cable at all, at the cost of shorter recordings and dependence on the sun. Where there is no internet anywhere on the property, a cellular camera sends alerts over a data plan the owner pays. We put each option in writing with its limits so the owner can choose.

Boards that hire a manager have their own versions in co-op security camera installation on Staten Island and condo security camera installation on Staten Island, and general camera work for any property is on our Staten Island security camera installation page. Installed four-camera systems on the Island start at $999 on the borough's camera price list.

Storms & Flooding

Storm and Flood Exposure: Where the Equipment Goes Matters

Staten Island remembers Sandy for good reason. The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, describing the seawall now being built from Fort Wadsworth to Oakwood Beach, notes that "Sandy resulted in 24 deaths on Staten Island, more than any other borough," and that the project will protect "nearly 7,300 residential and commercial properties" (USACE North Atlantic Division, October 7, 2024). For a property manager the lesson is not dramatic. It is about where the transformers, recorders and network switches end up.

In older houses and walk-ups, the low-voltage equipment tends to live in the basement because that is where the electrical panel and the phone line come in. In a flood-prone block that is the worst place for it. When we replace or add equipment in an East or South Shore property, we move the recorder, the intercom power supply and the network switch above the likely water line where the building allows: a first-floor closet, a utility room shelf, a locked box high on a garage wall. The cable path changes a little; the risk changes a lot.

Wind and salt do slower damage. Doorbells and panels on the side of a house that faces the bay take rain driven sideways into every seam, and cheap outdoor connectors corrode within a few seasons. We use weather-rated boxes for every outdoor splice, mount cameras under eaves where the house allows, and choose stainless or sealed hardware on the most exposed walls. Battery backup on the entry system and the recorder keeps doors working and footage recording through the short outages that follow most storms; anything longer depends on the building's own generator, which is your electrician's work.

Many houses on the East Shore were raised after the storm, which leaves a long stair between the ground and the front door. On those houses the doorbell often belongs at the bottom of the stair or the gate, with the release and the chime up at the door, and cable has to cross the gap between the old ground level and the new one. We plan that on the walk-through rather than discovering it on install day.

North Shore

North Shore Apartment Buildings and the Bay Street Corridor

The North Shore is where Staten Island looks most like the rest of the city. St. George, Tompkinsville and Stapleton hold older apartment houses near the ferry, large Victorian houses split into several apartments, and a growing number of new rental buildings. The City Council approved the Bay Street Corridor rezoning in June 2019, covering about 20 blocks of the North Shore and projected to bring around 1,800 new homes, about 1,300 of them income-targeted (City Limits, June 26, 2019).

That mix gives North Shore managers two kinds of work that rarely meet elsewhere on the Island. The converted Victorians behave like houses with too many doorbells: wiring snakes through original plaster, and the entry is a porch that was never designed for a panel. The newer buildings arrive with cloud-managed entry, a package room, elevator readers and a manufacturer subscription someone has to keep paying. A manager who runs both needs a vendor comfortable inside a 120-year-old staircase and in front of a programming screen on the same afternoon.

Foot traffic is the other North Shore difference. Buildings within a few blocks of the ferry and Bay Street see a steady stream of people who are not visiting anyone inside, which makes the street door the weak point. A self-closing door that actually latches, an entry panel set inside a vestibule rather than flush on the sidewalk, and a camera looking down at the door rather than out at the street do more here than any extra device.

Alarms & Infrastructure

Alarms, Cabling and Where Fire Alarm Work Fits

Burglar alarms matter more in a house-based portfolio than in a doorman building. A vacant two-family between tenants, a rental office with files and keys, or a garage full of an owner's tools are each a better fit for an alarm than for more cameras. We install wired and wireless intrusion systems and connect them to central-station monitoring, which costs $30 a month with a keypad only or $45 a month with the phone app (alarm installation on Staten Island). Owners who inherited an alarm on someone else's contract can have it taken over, reprogrammed or replaced after a service call shows what is in the panel.

Every camera, reader and modern panel rides on cable, and cable is where Staten Island jobs differ most from apartment towers. Runs go between a house and a detached garage, between garden buildings across a lot, and up the outside of raised houses. Inside, we fish through frame walls rather than chasing masonry. Network cabling for these systems is published at $150 to $300 per drop on the Island (structured cabling installation on Staten Island). The city does not require an electrical permit for signaling and data wiring under 50 volts, outside certain life-safety systems (NYC Admin Code §28-105.4.7), which is why that side is ours and the 120-volt side is the electrician's.

Fire alarm systems are a separate trade with separate approvals. In multifamily and mixed-use buildings where code requires one, we handle the work only within our fire alarm scope, starting from engineered drawings and the approvals they need, and we quote it per building. The process is laid out on our Staten Island fire alarm installation page. Locks and readers on doors that are part of an exit path also have to release correctly when the alarm sounds or the power fails, which is why fire alarm and access control get planned together even when different contractors do them.

Building Specialists

Property Management Security for Staten Island's Architecture

Before any product is chosen, the building type settles most of the scope. These are the property types that fill Staten Island management portfolios, and what each one asks of a security system.

Detached two-family frame houses

Up-and-down apartments with separate doors, vinyl or aluminum siding and a driveway to a garage. Doorbells per door, exterior cameras on the siding and a recorder on the first floor.

Semi-attached and high-ranch two-families

Shared party walls and a garage under the upper unit. The lower apartment's entrance is often at the side or rear, which is where cameras and lighting are thinnest.

North Shore Victorians and conversions

Large old houses cut into several apartments, with plaster walls, deep porches and doorbells added one tenant at a time. Small entry panels and careful wire fishing.

Garden apartment complexes

Several low buildings around a parking lot, each with its own entrance. Many small doors, a gate or two, and cameras that have to reach across open asphalt.

Postwar elevator buildings

The few large rental buildings, mostly on the North and East Shores: a lobby, a mailroom, elevator cars and a staffed or semi-staffed front door.

Stores with apartments above

Corner and avenue buildings with retail at street level. Separate store and residential systems, with the shared rear door or cellar as the line between them.

How the method changes with the building

Frame houses are the easiest to wire and the easiest to wire badly. Cable fishes through stud bays without opening plaster, but every hole in the siding is a path for water, so exterior penetrations get a sealed box or a drip loop, and cameras mount on blocks made for the siding profile rather than straight through a vinyl panel that will crack in the first cold snap. Semi-attached houses add a party wall we do not cross; each side of the building gets its own equipment, even when one owner holds both.

Victorian conversions reward patience. Original plaster on lath cracks if it is drilled carelessly, porch ceilings are often beadboard over a void, and the best cable path is usually an old chase or the back of a closet. A small surface-mount panel on the porch post, with stations or phones answering in each apartment, keeps the facade intact. Garden complexes are the opposite problem: the walls are simple, but the distances are long. Cable between buildings runs in conduit or overhead on a messenger wire, or the link goes wireless, and the recorder sits in the rental office or a central building rather than in each basement.

Elevator buildings look like their counterparts in the other boroughs, with a lobby panel, a package area and readers in the cars, but they are often the only large building in the portfolio, and the office staff who run the houses may have little experience with a building-wide platform. We set those up with simpler permissions and a short walk-through for whoever answers the phones. In mixed-use buildings, the store keeps its own alarm and cameras, the residential entrance gets its own, and the shared doors are wired so neither side can lock the other out.

Commercial Property Management Security

Commercial and Mixed-Use Property Management Security in Staten Island

Many Staten Island management firms also run storefront strips and small office buildings alongside their rentals, and the same vendor file can cover them. The commercial stock here follows the main avenues rather than clustering downtown, so a commercial portfolio is spread out much like a residential one.

  • Bay Street and Victory Boulevard near St. George: ground-floor shops, offices for nonprofits and professionals, and apartments above, close to the ferry and the courthouses.
  • Forest Avenue through West Brighton and Port Richmond: long runs of small retail with apartments upstairs and parking behind.
  • Richmond Avenue around the mall: strip centers and pad buildings with large lots, where cameras and parking-lot coverage dominate.
  • Hylan Boulevard and New Dorp Lane: doctors' offices, banks, restaurants and older mixed-use buildings along the East Shore spine.
  • Richmond Road and the Mid-Island corridors: small office buildings and professional suites with shared entrances and after-hours access needs.

Commercial tenants usually want their own alarm and their own cameras inside their space, while the landlord is responsible for the shared entrance, the lot and the exterior. We draw that line in writing on every job so there is no argument later about whose camera should have caught what. Commercial camera work in depth, including retail and office layouts, is on our Staten Island camera pages linked above.

Rules that come up on Staten Island commercial and mixed-use jobs

  • Installer licensing: New York requires a license to install, service or maintain security or fire alarm systems for others (General Business Law §69-m). Ours is NYS license #12000287431.
  • Locks on exit doors: sensor-released electric locks on egress doors need a push-to-exit button and must unlock on loss of power and when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate, and magnetic locks have to release from the door's own hardware (NYC Building Code §1010.1.9).
  • Entrance doors in multiple dwellings: buildings of eight or more apartments erected or converted after 1968 need self-closing, self-locking entrance doors and an intercom from each apartment that releases the door; older buildings owe one when a majority of tenants ask in writing (MDL §50-a).
  • Audio recording: recording conversations raises eavesdropping questions under New York's Penal Law article 250, so we install cameras with audio switched off unless the owner's counsel says otherwise (The Cooperator, camera placement Q&A).
Vendor Setup

Seasonal Checklist

Before Hurricane Season and Before Winter: A Staten Island Checklist

Two times of year account for most of the equipment trouble we see on Staten Island: late summer into fall, when coastal storms arrive, and the first hard freeze, when doors shrink and batteries fade. A short list for each lets a manager schedule checks before the calls start.

Late summer, before storm season

Your Questions Answered

Property Management Security in Staten Island: Questions Answered

Rented two- and three-family houses

Which entry rules apply to a two-family house, and which only begin at three apartments?

A two-family house is not a multiple dwelling, because state law starts that category at three families living independently. That keeps it outside the Multiple Dwelling Law's entrance-door and intercom section and outside the city's Tenant Data Privacy Law, which covers class A multiple dwellings. The city's Housing Maintenance Code still applies to rented houses, the apartment doors still need working locks, and HPD registration applies when the owner and the owner's family do not live there. A three-family building crosses into multiple-dwelling territory, which is why we treat the three-family houses in a portfolio as small buildings rather than as houses. For any specific property, the owner's attorney has the final word.

Once the owner moves out of a two-family and both floors are rented, what should change?

Three things, usually. The owner's personal doorbell and camera accounts should move to the management company or a business email the owner controls, so the outgoing household is not still receiving alerts from the new tenants' door. The downstairs apartment, which the owner may have treated as part of their own home, needs its own doorbell or button and its own lock on any interior door to the basement or the stair. And the owner should decide what exterior coverage they keep, since cameras that once watched the owner's own driveway now watch a tenant's comings and goings. A single visit usually covers all three.

The basement apartment has its own side door. Should it get its own doorbell and camera?

Yes on the doorbell, usually yes on the camera. A side or rear entrance down a few steps is where visitors get lost, deliveries get left and lighting is poorest. A wired doorbell at that door gives the tenant their own ring and video. A camera covering the path to the door, angled so it sees the walkway rather than into the apartment's windows, helps the owner without intruding. Whether that apartment is a legal unit is a separate question about the building's certificate of occupancy, which the owner should settle before we start, not something security equipment can resolve.

Rekeying every time a tenant leaves one of our houses is getting expensive. What are the options?

On shared doors, the most common answer is a keypad deadbolt or a keypad with a fob reader: the office issues a code at move-in and deletes it at move-out, and the log shows when each code was used. On a tenant's own apartment door, we keep a key cylinder as well, and in multiple dwellings the city bars owners from making smart access the only way into an apartment. For houses where the owner wants to stay entirely with keys, a lock with interchangeable cores lets the office swap the core at turnover in minutes instead of calling a locksmith.

Tenants keep putting up their own doorbell cameras on our houses. Should we allow it?

That is the owner's policy decision, but there are good reasons to provide one instead. Tenant-installed doorbells are often battery units stuck to the trim, they leave holes and adhesive when the tenant moves, and they sometimes look straight at the neighbor's door or the shared driveway. A wired doorbell that the owner provides and the tenant uses gives the tenant what they wanted, stays with the house, and lets the office remove the old tenant's access on move-out. Many owners write that into the lease.

Does a camera aimed down a driveway shared with the neighbor's house cause problems?

It can, so we aim and mask it carefully. Most cameras let us black out parts of the frame, so the camera records the shared driveway and the owner's garage door while the neighbor's windows and yard are hidden in the recording. Pointing a camera into a neighbor's home invites complaints and, depending on the facts, legal trouble, and audio recording raises separate issues under New York law. We show the owner the actual view on a phone before we leave.

Garden complexes, lots and garages

Our complex has a row of detached garages with no power or internet. Can they be covered?

Yes, with a trade-off for each method. A point-to-point wireless bridge carries the network from the nearest building to the garage row over a pair of small antennas, and a camera there can run on power over Ethernet if there is any outlet at the garage, or on a solar kit if there is none. Solar cameras record on motion rather than all the time and depend on daylight, which is short in December. Cellular cameras work anywhere with a signal but need a monthly data plan. On the walk-through we test the signal and the line of sight before recommending one.

Car thefts are up on Staten Island this year. What can cameras on a rental lot really do?

They can show when a car arrived and left, capture the faces and plates of people entering through the lot's one or two entrances, and give the police and insurers usable video. They also make the lot a less attractive target than one with nothing on the poles. They cannot stop a thief who is already inside a car. The best results come from cameras placed where vehicles slow down at the entrance, good lighting, and a recorder kept long enough that a tenant who notices damage on Monday can still find Friday night.

Keypad codes or fobs: which suits a garden building with several small entrances?

For tenants, fobs. A fob cannot be passed around by text, it is cancelled in one click when someone moves, and readers handle cold and wet weather well. Codes are better for the people who come occasionally: the cleaning service, the exterminator, a home health aide. Many garden complexes end up with both on the same reader, fobs for residents and time-limited codes for vendors, so the office never has to hand out a fob that might not come back.

Battery cameras on our frame houses keep dying in winter. Should they be wired instead?

If someone is not reliably charging them, yes. Batteries lose capacity in the cold, and a camera that records on every passing car drains faster still. Wiring a frame house is usually straightforward because cable fishes through the stud bays without opening walls, and a wired camera records continuously and never needs a ladder visit to recharge. Where wiring truly is not possible, a small solar panel on the battery camera solves most of the problem on a wall that gets sun.

Inherited systems and changes of hands

We bought a building that came with the old owner's alarm still under contract. Can we take it over or replace it?

Usually either. The monitoring contract is between the previous owner and their alarm company, so the cancellation or transfer is theirs or yours to handle with that company. The panel itself often can be reprogrammed once someone has the installer code, and if no one does, many panels can be reset by a technician. A service call tells you whether the panel, sensors and siren are worth keeping. If they are, we connect it to our central station; if not, we replace it.

One of our North Shore buildings faces a busy sidewalk. Should the entry panel sit at the street or inside the vestibule?

Inside the vestibule, if the building has one and the outer door can stay unlocked during the day. A panel set back from the sidewalk is out of the weather, harder to damage and less inviting to people passing by. Where the outer door has to stay locked, the panel goes on the frame of that door instead, with a camera above it looking down at the step. Flush sidewalk-facing panels near the ferry and busy avenues take more abuse and cost more to keep running.

We just took over management of a property from its owner. Which security items should be checked first?

Start with access, not hardware. Find out who has keys, codes and fobs, and who holds the logins to the doorbells, cameras and alarm, because those are the items that let a former tenant or contractor back in. Next, confirm that every exterior door closes and latches on its own. Then check whether the cameras are actually recording and how far back the footage goes. Our first visit to a newly managed property covers all three and leaves the office with a written list of what is installed and who controls it.

Should each apartment in a two-family get its own alarm, or should the house share one?

Separate, in most rentals. A shared alarm means one tenant's code arms and disarms the other's home, and a false alarm downstairs wakes the household upstairs. Two small systems, each with its own keypad or app and its own monitoring if the tenant wants it, avoid that. The owner can still have a contact on the shared basement or garage door reporting to their own phone. When the house is vacant between tenants, one system covering the whole building is often the better choice.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Property Management Security in Staten Island

Ask a search assistant how to secure rental property and you tend to get an answer written for an apartment tower or for a single homeowner. Staten Island portfolios are neither. Here are the claims we hear repeated most often, and how they hold up on the Island.

"Every rental property needs a building intercom system."

Guides written for multifamily owners treat a lobby intercom as the starting point for every property. On Staten Island, where tens of thousands of rentals are two-family houses with a separate door for each apartment, that advice sells hardware nobody needs.

A house with two front doors is served better by two wired doorbells, each ringing its own apartment. A lobby-style panel there adds cost, a shared device that fails for both tenants at once, and often a subscription.

The building intercom becomes the right tool when three or more apartments share one entrance, which is also where state law starts treating the building as a multiple dwelling. We draw that line per address on the walk-through.

"Wireless battery cameras are the easy answer for landlords."

Consumer reviews love battery cameras because they install in minutes. For one homeowner who charges them, they are fine. For a management office with thirty houses, every battery is a future site visit.

Cold weather shortens battery life, busy driveways drain them faster, and nobody on the tenant's side is responsible for recharging a landlord's camera. The result is a camera that was dead the night something happened.

We wire cameras wherever a frame house allows, which is most of the time, and keep battery or solar units for the spots where cable truly cannot go.

"Landlords are required to install security cameras."

Some articles imply that New York City requires cameras in rental buildings. The requirements we work with are about doors and entry: self-closing, self-locking entrance doors and intercoms in many multiple dwellings, working locks on apartment doors, and rules about data from smart access systems.

Cameras are a choice the owner makes for safety, evidence and insurance reasons. Once installed, they raise their own questions about placement in common areas, audio and who may see the footage.

We help owners make that choice for each property and keep cameras out of places where tenants expect privacy. Legal questions about a specific building go to the owner's counsel.

"Cameras will lower your insurance premium."

Insurance marketing and some AI answers suggest a security system pays for itself through lower premiums. Whether an insurer offers any credit, and for what equipment, is up to that insurer and that policy.

Monitored burglar alarms are more often recognized by insurers than cameras are, and some carriers ask for a monitoring certificate. Cameras are valued mainly for the evidence they produce after a loss.

We will not promise a discount. If an owner's carrier lists specific requirements, send them to us and we will tell you what meets them and what it costs.

"Modern systems come with professional monitoring included."

Many search answers blur the difference between an app that sends alerts and a staffed monitoring center. They are not the same, and neither comes free.

Our burglar alarms can be connected to a central station for $30 a month with a keypad only or $45 a month with the phone app. Camera alerts go to the people the office chooses; nobody watches the video live unless a separate monitoring service is hired.

For most rental houses, an alarm on central-station monitoring plus cameras for evidence is the honest combination. We do not offer monitored video and will not imply that we do.

"Outdoor-rated equipment is flood-proof."

An outdoor rating on a camera or doorbell means it resists rain and dust. It does not mean the recorder, the transformer or the network switch in the basement will survive standing water.

When surge water reaches a basement, a camera on the outside wall can come through untouched while the equipment that powers and records it does not. The weak point is where the support gear sits, not the cameras.

Moving that gear above the likely water line, adding battery backup and sealing outdoor splices costs far less than replacing a building's system after the next storm.

"Any handyman can install a doorbell or a keypad."

For a homeowner's own door, that is often true. For a landlord's property, the work touches tenants' doors, egress hardware and, in multiple dwellings, entry rules and tenant data.

New York requires a license to install, service or maintain security systems for others, and an unlicensed installer leaves the owner with no recourse and often no record of what was done or who holds the accounts.

A licensed contractor who documents each device and hands the logins to the office is the difference between a doorbell that works and a portfolio the owner actually controls.
