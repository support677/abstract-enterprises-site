The County

Why Putnam Rentals Need a Security Contractor Who Works Small

Putnam is a county of owners, and anyone who manages rentals here knows it. The Census Bureau's American Community Survey for 2019 through 2023 counts 35,054 occupied homes in the county, and only 5,949 of them are rented, about one household in six. The same survey puts 29,826 of the county's 38,369 housing units in detached single-family houses. So the rental stock that a property manager or a landlord actually handles is scattered: a few dozen apartments in one village, a handful of units over stores on a hamlet main street, a garden building near the train, a cottage by a lake, an apartment carved out of a house on a back road. Very little of it looks like the lobby-and-riser buildings our crews service in the city.

That shapes what good property management security means in Putnam County. A firm that manages forty rentals here may hold forty different arrangements: a buzzer at a Brewster walk-up, a keypad on the side door of a Carmel accessory apartment, a camera on a garage at a Kent cottage, an alarm at a vacant house waiting for a tenant. Selling each of them an enterprise access platform would be a waste of the owner's money. What the office needs instead is a contractor who can fix the failed piece on the first visit, keep a record of what is installed at every address, and recommend the smallest system that solves each property's actual problem.

The numbers inside the county explain where the work clusters. The same Census tables show the Village of Brewster as the exception to everything above: 775 of its 977 occupied homes are rented, roughly four in five. The Carmel hamlet counts 695 renter households, Mahopac 709, Lake Carmel 385 and the Village of Cold Spring 301. Town by town, Carmel holds 1,879 renter households and Southeast 1,548, which together account for more than half of all Putnam rentals. Structure type tells the rest of the story: the county has 1,684 homes in two-unit buildings, 1,024 in buildings of three or four units, 987 in buildings of five to nine, and only 372 in buildings of fifty or more. Most of the multifamily rental work here is small-building work.

Seasonal housing is the other half of the picture. The Census survey counts 1,757 Putnam homes held for seasonal, recreational or occasional use, led by Putnam Valley with 451, Carmel with 403, Philipstown with 384 and Kent with 259. Many of those sit in the lake communities that developers laid out beginning in 1928, when, as the Historic Patterson exhibit on Putnam Lake describes it, Lake Carmel in Kent, Lake Peekskill in Putnam Valley and Putnam Lake in Patterson were planned as communities of summer bungalows around man-made lakes. Those cottages were later expanded and winterized, and today they are a large share of the county's year-round and seasonal rentals. A security plan for one of them looks nothing like a plan for an apartment building, and we write it accordingly.

The legal setting is also its own. Putnam's rental buildings fall under New York's Multiple Residence Law, which section 3 applies to every town and village and to cities of fewer than 325,000 people, rather than the city's Multiple Dwelling Law; complaints go to the town or village building department, not to a city housing agency. Accessory apartments carry their own rules, which we cover below, because the Town of Carmel rewrote them in 2025. We are a security contractor, not a law firm, and nothing here is legal advice, but we plan every job with the right rules in view and say where each one comes from so the owner's attorney can check it.

As for who arrives at the door: the company is owned by a technician who has worked on entry and camera systems since 2000 and has run the business since 2002. What fails most often at Putnam rentals

Across the calls we take for rentals in this county, the failures sort into a short list, and it is not the same list as the city's. First comes power. A single summer thunderstorm or a winter ice event can knock out a whole road for days, and when the lights flicker back on, the equipment without protection dies: a recorder that lost its drive, a doorbell transformer that cooked, a network switch in a garage that never came back. Second is the door itself: a strike or a keypad lock on a side entrance that has been through a few winters of frost heave and no longer lines up. Third is connectivity. Rentals on back roads lose their internet more often than city buildings, and a camera or a keypad that depends on the cloud goes quiet with it. Fourth is the alarm that calls the police for no reason, which in Carmel and Patterson can start costing the owner money. Fifth is the login nobody can find, usually because the previous owner or tenant set the system up on a personal account. Every one of those starts with the $250 service call, and most are finished on the first visit once the technician knows what is installed.

The people we answer to on a Putnam rental

The owner or the managing office authorizes the work. Every quote, appointment and invoice runs through whoever hired us, whether that is a management firm in Carmel or a landlord who owns two houses in Kent. A tenant who calls us directly is pointed back to the owner or the office, and nothing is scheduled with a tenant on our own.

The owner who lives on the property is a common figure here, because Carmel's accessory apartment rules require the owner to live in the main house or the apartment. That owner is both client and neighbor, so the plan has to keep the tenant's entry, codes and camera views separate from the owner's while still letting the owner manage the system.

The caretaker takes the place of the superintendent at many Putnam rentals: a neighbor, a handyman or a cleaning contractor who holds a key and meets our technician. We set caretakers up with their own codes and app access, limited to what they actually need, and leave a one-page guide by the equipment.

Tenants and guests experience the system in a few moments a day: getting in, letting someone in, and knowing the place is watched while they are away. When their complaints reach us, they go back to the owner or the office, which keeps the work authorized and the paperwork in one place.

Door entry, buzzers and small-building intercoms

The classic Putnam entry call comes from a walk-up near Brewster's Main Street or a two-story building in a Carmel or Mahopac hamlet: the buzzer from upstairs no longer releases the street door, or one apartment cannot hear the caller. In buildings this size the cause is usually local and cheap to fix. A transformer in the basement has failed, a call button at the street door has corroded through a few winters of road salt, a tenant pulled an apartment station off the wall, or the strike no longer meets the latch. We test before we recommend anything, and if the old wires test clean, the fix stays small. Where a building wants video, a two-wire system can often reuse the same pairs, which keeps the work at the door and in the basement rather than in every apartment.

Repairs to existing entry systems are covered on our door buzzer repair page for Putnam County and our Putnam County intercom repair page. The buzzer page publishes $250–$525 to replace an apartment station, $250–$475 for a door release relay or button, $325–$650 for a transformer, $450–$950 for a strike with alignment, $650–$1,800 for panel or faceplate work and $850–$2,400 for riser pair repair. New systems are on our intercom installation page for Putnam County, which lists $1,200–$3,500 for a video intercom in a four- to eight-unit building, $1,200–$5,000 for multi-unit video systems generally, and $2,500–$8,000 and up for app-connected systems in larger buildings. A house with an accessory apartment rarely needs any of that; it usually needs a second doorbell, which we come to below.

One habit of ours matters in older rental buildings. We never move a building to phone-only entry without keeping a way to reach tenants who don't use a smartphone or who live where the cell signal is poor, which describes a fair number of Putnam addresses. An app-based panel suits a building of commuters on the Harlem Line; a building of long-term tenants may need in-unit stations, and the owner should hear that before choosing.

Strikes, keypad locks and the door itself

Many Putnam rentals have no lobby at all. The controlled door is a side entrance to an accessory apartment, a rear door to a basement unit, or the front door of a cottage that changes hands every week in summer. On those doors the hardware is the whole system, and it lives outside through freeze and thaw. A strike set in a wood frame that has moved, a keypad deadbolt whose batteries died in January, or a closer that slams the door hard enough to knock the latch out of line accounts for a large share of our calls. We set the electrified hardware true to the latch, choose keypad locks rated for outdoor cold, and tell the owner when a frame or a closer needs a carpenter or a locksmith; mechanical locksets on apartment doors stay with the locksmith.

Exit matters as much as entry. Any locked door on a way out must open from inside without a key, code or card, and a magnetic lock on an exit route needs a release that works when power fails and when the fire alarm sounds. In an accessory apartment where the side door may be the tenant's main way out, we keep that rule front and center, and if a property's existing hardware does not meet it, we say so in writing.

Keypads, fobs and access control for rentals

For a Putnam landlord, access control usually means stopping the cost of rekeying and keys that walk off with former tenants. On a single door, a keypad lock or a small reader lets the owner issue a code to each tenant, the cleaner and the plumber, and delete it the day the lease ends. On a small building, a controller in the basement can run the street door, the laundry room and the storage room from one list. On a complex in Southeast or Carmel, a networked system lets the office manage every entrance from a desk in Mahopac. We size the system to the property: a house with one apartment should not be paying a monthly platform fee per door that it does not need.

Our access control installation page for Putnam County publishes $1,900–$6,000 for a single door or gate with a keypad, fob reader or gate controller, $5,000–$18,000 for multi-door properties, and $18,000–$50,000 and up for a full property. Those are installed figures; any recurring platform fee is shown on its own line so the owner can compare the long-term cost. Whatever the size, every credential belongs to a named person, every code can be deleted on its own, and the log shows who opened which door and when.

Driveway gates and shared private roads

Some Putnam rentals sit behind a driveway gate, and a few share a private road with neighbors. We add keypads, readers and video call boxes at the gate and connect them to the operator through a dry contact, so tenants use the same code as the house and visitors can call the tenant or the owner. The gate company keeps responsibility for the motor, the arm and the safety loops. When a call box sits a long way from the house, we look at wireless links or a cellular call box before anyone digs a trench across a lawn, a ledge or a wetland buffer.

Alarms for rentals, vacant units and town permits

An intrusion alarm earns its keep at a Putnam rental during the weeks nobody lives there: between tenants, over the winter at a lake cottage, or while an apartment is being renovated. Central-station monitoring through our provider is $30 a month keypad-only or $45 a month with the phone app, and basic residential alarm installation starts at $600, both as published on our alarm installation page for Putnam County. Local rules matter more here than most owners expect. The Town of Carmel's alarm chapter, Chapter 37, requires an alarm permit and says any alarm with an outside siren or strobe must have an automatic cutoff that ends it within 15 minutes, and it fines owners for each false alarm under a schedule the Town Board sets. Patterson's alarm law fines properties with more than two false alarms in a twelve-month period, Putnam Valley requires a permit before an alarm that calls the fire department is installed, and Southeast requires an annual permit. We set entry delays, sensor placement and user codes so that a tenant's late arrival or a cleaner's early one does not become a police call.

Fire alarm systems for rental buildings

Fire alarm work for larger rental buildings is in our scope. Outside New York City, approval runs through the town or village code enforcement office under the state Uniform Code: drawings from an engineer or architect, approval, installation and an acceptance test witnessed by the authority. Our fire alarm installation page for Putnam County walks an owner through it. We do not publish fire alarm prices; each system is priced from the approved drawings. Where a rental building has electric locks, the fire alarm must release them, so we plan the two together.

Cabling, wireless links and outbuildings

Putnam properties spread out. A converted garage apartment can sit well back from the main house, a cottage colony may have five small buildings on one lot, and a rental office may be across a parking area from the units. Everything above depends on getting a signal between those buildings. Where conduit exists, we pull Cat6 through it. Where it does not, a point-to-point wireless link between two buildings is often faster and kinder to the lawn than a trench, and a fiber run makes sense where distance or lightning exposure is high. Inside, we run Cat6 to each device, set a PoE switch with battery backup in a locked spot, and keep the security equipment off the tenants' Wi-Fi. Our structured cabling page for Putnam County lists Cat6 data drops at $175 and up per drop, and our network installation page for Putnam County covers switches and separate networks. We do not do electrical work; a new circuit for a gate operator or an outbuilding goes to the property's licensed electrician, and we coordinate with them.

Power, storms and surge protection

Most of Putnam is served by NYSEG, and the utility's own restoration numbers show what a storm does here. Mahopac News reported that after the July 4, 2026 storm, NYSEG's Brewster Division, which serves parts of Dutchess, Putnam and Westchester, peaked at about 25,000 outages with 234 downed power lines and 31 broken poles, and that Patterson and Southeast were among the hardest-hit communities. Daily Voice reported that during Tropical Storm Isaias in August 2020, 34,150 of NYSEG's 38,693 Putnam customers lost power. When an outage lasts days rather than hours, the plan for a rental has to say what keeps working: we put battery backup on the access controller, the network switch and the recorder, add surge protection on long outdoor runs, and leave a note at the equipment that tells the caretaker what to restart and in what order. Fail-secure strikes keep a door locked from outside and openable from inside through an outage; magnetic locks release, which is why buildings that depend on them need backup sized for more than a few minutes.

Doorbells, smart locks and the two-unit house

A large part of the Putnam rental market is houses with two units, and for those a wired video doorbell at each entrance usually beats any panel. Each tenant gets a doorbell in their own account; the owner keeps the driveway and yard cameras in theirs; both are reset at turnover. Our Putnam doorbell camera installation page covers that work, and our smart lock installation page for Putnam County covers code locks for side entrances, furnished rentals and short-term stays.

Who owns the logins

The quiet failure at many Putnam rentals is not hardware at all. A previous owner set the doorbell up on a personal email, a tenant registered the camera app to their own phone, or the alarm company that installed the panel twenty years ago still holds the installer code. When that happens, nothing can be changed without recovering access or replacing working equipment. We put administrator rights in the owner's name, give the managing office its own administrator account, and give caretakers and cleaners limited accounts, so a change of tenant, caretaker or management firm never means rebuilding the system.

Staffed services we do not sell

We do not supply guards, patrols or a staffed service that watches cameras or answers a rental's door. Those are sold by companies that put people on the job. What we do is install and maintain the entry hardware, cameras, alarms and network a property needs, and connect to a staffed provider the owner already uses. That contract stays between the owner and the provider.

Main Street walk-ups: one stair door between two shops

Putnam County's own Main Street study describes Brewster as a long-established mixed-use Main Street with a Metro-North station at its end, and the same study pictures the typical arrangement: a doorway to a staircase leading up to an apartment above street-level businesses. That doorway is the whole security problem. It is narrow, it opens straight onto the sidewalk, it takes deliveries, and it sits between two businesses whose staff, customers and alarm companies have nothing to do with the apartments. We put a small call panel or a row of buttons at the doorway, a strike that holds even when the door is slammed, and a camera inside the stair looking at the door rather than at the street. The shops keep their own cameras and alarms on their own accounts, and any shared basement door between the stores and the apartments is controlled from the residential side.

These buildings also have the most crowded basements in the county. The intercom transformer, the shops' alarm panels, the internet equipment for every tenant and sometimes a boiler share one cellar, and the wire that runs up to the apartments often shares a chase with the stores' lines. Our first visit at a building like this labels what belongs to the apartments, so the next technician, or the next manager, does not have to guess.

Hamlet apartment buildings in Carmel and Mahopac

The two- and three-story apartment buildings in Carmel, Mahopac and along Route 6 are closest to what a city manager would recognize: a shared front door, a hallway, a basement with the meters, a laundry room and a gravel or paved lot behind. The security plan is equally familiar. A door entry panel or call buttons at the front, a strike or a keypad on the rear door from the lot, a camera on each entrance and one on the lot, and a credential on the laundry and storage rooms cover most of the incidents an owner hears about. Because these buildings are small, the work usually takes a day and fits a single scope that the owner can approve in one look.

Accessory apartments: two households, one house

The Town of Carmel rewrote its accessory apartment rules in 2025, and they shape how security goes into these units. Under §156-15 of the town's zoning code, a single-family house in the low- and medium-density residential districts may have one accessory apartment, either inside the existing house or in an accessory building that already existed; the owner must live in the main house or the apartment; the apartment may not be used as a short-term rental; and the house must keep a single-family appearance with one front entrance, so any additional entrance goes on the side. The apartment may sit in an outbuilding up to 500 feet from the house. Putnam County's Health Department has to approve the water supply and sewage disposal before the town's building permit is issued, and the town permit is renewed with a certification that the owner still lives there.

For security, that adds up to a few clear rules of thumb. The tenant's entrance is usually the side door, so the side door gets its own doorbell or call button, its own keypad or reader, and a light and camera that see that door without looking into the owner's living space. The owner's cameras on the driveway and the front door stay in the owner's account; the tenant's doorbell stays in the tenant's. An apartment in a converted garage or carriage house needs a reliable link back to the main house if it shares a recorder or a network, which is where a wireless bridge or a buried conduit earns its cost. And because Carmel's law requires the apartment to be removed if the owner moves out, the tenant-side equipment should be easy to take down or reassign without disturbing the rest of the house.

Lake-colony cottages around Lake Carmel, Putnam Lake and Lake Peekskill

The lake communities were built for summer. Historic Patterson's exhibit on Putnam Lake describes lots of 20 by 100 feet, typical New York City lot sizes, that had room only for a summer bungalow and an outhouse; Wikipedia's article on Lake Carmel says its cottages were later expanded and winterized as more people lived there year-round. The result is a rental stock of small houses close together, on hilly lanes, often with old wiring and an electrical service that was extended over the decades. Security here is about restraint: cameras that cover the owner's door, steps and parking without staring into the house six feet away, motion lights that do not wake the neighbors, and a modest alarm for the months a cottage sits empty.

Vacant periods are the main risk. The Putnam County Sheriff's Office offers a Dark House Check for residents away for an extended period, in which deputies may patrol the area and check a house's exterior for signs of forced entry. That service is for homeowners and is the Sheriff's to give; what we add is the record of what happened while nobody was there. Rules a Putnam landlord should keep in view

  • Multiple Residence Law. Putnam's apartment buildings fall under the Multiple Residence Law (§3), which covers all towns and villages, not the city's Multiple Dwelling Law.
  • Accessory apartments in Carmel. Zoning code §156-15 (2025): one per qualifying house, owner on site, no short-term rental, any extra entrance on the side, Health Department approval first, and removal if the owner leaves.
  • Short-term rentals. Cold Spring adopted a law in 2021 and is revising it; Philipstown's draft rules would add permits, inspections and a 20-minute response requirement.
  • Alarm permits. Carmel (Chapter 37) requires a permit, a 15-minute cutoff on outside sirens and strobes, and fines false alarms; Patterson fines properties with more than two false alarms in twelve months; Putnam Valley and Southeast have their own permit rules.

Your Questions Answered

Property Management Security in Putnam County: Questions Answered

Accessory apartments and two-household houses

A tenant rents the accessory apartment in a Carmel house, and the town's rules keep a single front entrance. How does the tenant get an entry of their own?

Through the side. Carmel's 2025 rules keep one front entrance and put any additional entrance on the side of the house, so the tenant's door is usually a side or rear door. That door gets its own doorbell or call button, a keypad lock or a small reader with codes only the tenant and the owner control, an outdoor light, and a camera that sees the door and the path to it without looking into the owner's rooms. Mail and deliveries are the one thing to settle with the tenant up front, because drivers will go to the front door unless the address or the listing tells them otherwise.

Can visitors at a shared front door ring the accessory apartment without also ringing the owner?

Yes. The simplest version is two labeled buttons at the door, each wired to its own chime, or a two-button video station that rings each household separately. If the house has a single video doorbell, some models can send the call to one account only; others ring everyone, which is a poor fit when the owner and the tenant are separate households. We look at what the house has, then choose between a second button, a two-button station or a doorbell for each entrance.

The converted garage apartment behind our Mahopac house is out of Wi-Fi range. How should it connect to the main house's system?

With a dedicated link, not a stronger router. A pair of point-to-point wireless radios, one on each building, carries the camera and keypad traffic reliably over a few hundred feet with a clear line of sight. Where trees or the house itself block the view, a cable in conduit is the dependable option, and fiber avoids carrying a lightning surge from one building to the other. The tenant's own internet stays separate from the link either way, so a tenant who changes providers does not knock the owner's cameras offline.

Lake cottages and seasonal rentals

The bungalows we rent around Putnam Lake sit on narrow lots, close to the neighbors. Where can cameras go without looking into the house next door?

Mount them high on your own wall and aim them along your own property: down at the entrance steps, across the parking spot, along the path to the lake. Most current cameras also let us black out parts of the picture, so a neighbor's window that falls inside the frame is masked and never recorded. Narrow-angle lenses help on tight lots, and motion lights should be aimed at your door, not across the lane. Before anything goes up we stand where the camera will be and check the view with the owner.

A lake cottage alternates between weekly summer guests and a winter tenant on a lease. What has to change on the system when the lease starts?

Three things. The keypad switches from per-booking codes to the tenant's own permanent code, and the cleaner's and the guests' codes are deleted. Any camera that looks at areas the winter tenant now treats as private, such as a porch or a side yard, is either turned away or masked, and the tenant is told in writing which cameras remain and what they see. And the alarm's user list changes, so the tenant can arm and disarm it while the owner keeps the administrator role. We write the switch down as a checklist so it is the same every year.

Philipstown's draft short-term rental rules would require an owner or representative to respond within 20 minutes. What can the equipment handle, and what still needs a person?

The equipment can tell the host fast and accurately what is going on: who is at the door, when the guests checked in, whether a door was left open, whether the owner's locked closet was opened, and it can let a locked-out guest in remotely. It cannot answer a neighbor's complaint, talk to a guest about noise or show up at the house. Plan for a named local person within reach, and use the equipment so that person arrives knowing what they are walking into.

Our Putnam Valley rental sits in a hollow with a weak cell signal. How do we find out before buying whether a cellular camera will connect?

Test it at the exact spot where the camera will go, on the carrier the camera would use, before anything is ordered. A phone's signal bars are only a rough guide; we check the actual data connection on site. If it is marginal, an external antenna on the roofline or a camera placed a few feet higher can make the difference. If there is no usable signal, the camera records locally to a recorder, and remote viewing waits for whatever internet service the property has.

Village and Main Street buildings

Our Brewster building's stair door opens straight onto Main Street between two shops. Where does the call panel go when the entry is only a doorway wide?

On the frame or the wall beside the door, at a height a visitor can reach, using a slim surface-mount panel or a narrow row of call buttons. If the doorway is recessed, the panel goes inside the recess where it is out of the weather and the plows' spray. The camera goes inside the stair, looking down at the door, so it sees everyone who comes in without recording the sidewalk or the shops' customers. A strike sized for a door that gets slammed all day finishes the job.

Do wireless cameras work through the thick stone walls of older Cold Spring buildings?

Poorly. Stone and brick absorb Wi-Fi, so a camera on the outside of a masonry wall often loses its connection to a router inside. We either run cable through the wall to a wired camera, put a small access point or a wired switch near the outside wall, or place the router where it has a clear path to the camera. In a building inside the historic district, cable routes that avoid the street-facing facade are worth the extra effort.

Power, alarms and licensing

Our Southeast complex has a standby generator. Which security devices belong on it?

The ones that keep doors controlled and record what happens: the access controller and its lock power supply, the network switch that powers the cameras, the recorder, and the alarm panel. Each of those should also have its own battery backup, because a generator takes a few seconds to start and that gap is enough to reboot a recorder or drop a camera. The electrician connects the circuits to the generator; we tell them which outlets our equipment is on and test everything after the first transfer.

A rental we manage in Patterson has had three false alarms this year. What does the town's alarm law mean for us, and how do we stop the next one?

Patterson's alarm law fines properties with more than two false alarms in a twelve-month period, so the next one is likely to cost money. Start with the alarm history to see which sensor or which user is setting it off. The usual causes at rentals are a tenant who never learned the code, a motion sensor that sees a pet or a heat source, a door contact on a door that warps in humid weather, and a low battery in a wireless sensor. We fix the cause, retrain the users, and lengthen the entry delay if people simply need more time to reach the keypad.

Is a Putnam County home improvement registration enough for the company that installs our cameras and alarms?

No. The county registration shows that a contractor working on residential property is insured, bonded and covered by workers' compensation. Installing or servicing security and fire alarm systems needs a state license from the Department of State under Article 6-D, and the county's own low-voltage license application says alarms, CCTV and access control are licensed by the state, not the county. Ask for both the county registration and the state license number.

Mail, tenants and their equipment

Our rental houses share a row of mailboxes at the end of the road. Can a camera watch them without running power all the way down the hill?

Often, yes. A solar-powered camera with a battery can sit on a post near the boxes and send video back over a wireless link to the nearest house, or over a cellular connection where the signal allows. The camera should be aimed so it sees hands at the boxes and the vehicle that pulled up, not the whole road. If the boxes are on a town right-of-way, we mount on the owner's side of the line and check with the owner before anything goes up.

A tenant bought their own camera for the apartment they rent from us. Will you install it?

With the owner's approval, yes, for a labor-only price given by phone or text once we know the make and model, and the date is held once the deposit is in. The camera stays the tenant's property and in the tenant's account, so it comes down when they leave, and how it performs is between the tenant and its manufacturer. We mount it where it sees the tenant's own entrance and nothing else, and the owner should put the arrangement in writing.

What AI Answers Get Wrong About Rental Security in Putnam County

Landlords and managers increasingly arrive with an answer from a chatbot or a search summary. Most of those answers were written for big-city apartment buildings or for one house anywhere in the country. These are the ones we correct most often in Putnam.

1. "Property management security means an access control platform"

In a county where 1,684 homes sit in two-unit buildings and only 372 in buildings of fifty or more, most rentals need a working door, a keypad or a doorbell per entrance, a few cameras and an alarm for vacant weeks. A platform makes sense for a complex or a portfolio; it is overkill for a house with an accessory apartment.

2. "Any accessory apartment can be rented by the night"

Not in Carmel. The town's 2025 rules bar short-term rental of an accessory apartment and require the owner to live on the property. Owners elsewhere in the county should check their own town's code before setting up per-booking codes for an apartment.

3. "A camera that works on Wi-Fi works anywhere"

Stone walls in Cold Spring, distances between a house and a garage apartment, and dead spots in the hollows of Putnam Valley all defeat ordinary Wi-Fi. A camera is only as good as its link back to the recorder, which is why we test the link before choosing the camera.

4. "Your county license covers alarm and camera work"

Putnam's Board of Electrical Examiners says its low-voltage license does not include fire and burglar alarms, CCTV or access control, which the state licenses under Article 6-D. County Consumer Affairs registration is a separate requirement about insurance, bonding and workers' compensation.

5. "Battery backup is a luxury"

Not when NYSEG's Brewster Division counted about 25,000 outages at the peak of a single July 2026 storm, or when 34,150 of its 38,693 Putnam customers lost power in Tropical Storm Isaias. Backup decides whether the doors stay controlled and the cameras keep recording while the lights are out.

6. "A short-term rental host can meet a response rule with technology alone"

The response requirement in Philipstown's draft rules, as reported, is about an owner or a representative responding. Cameras, keypads and alerts help that person respond quickly and well; they do not replace the person.

What moves a Putnam quote up or down

Five things explain most of the gap between two quotes for similar-looking rentals. The first is distance between buildings: a garage apartment or a cottage across the lot needs a link, and whether that link is a pair of radios or a trench changes the number. The fourth is recurring fees, which range from nothing after installation to a monthly charge per door or per unit. The fifth is the wiring already in the walls: if an old buzzer pair or a doorbell run tests clean, it can often be reused, and if not, new cable has to reach the door.

FAQ

Putnam County Property Management Security FAQ

Can cameras be mounted on cedar shingles or fieldstone without damaging them?
+

Yes. On cedar shingles we mount to a backing block or a mounting box fastened through to the sheathing, seal every penetration and keep the cable under the shingle line where it can drip clear. On fieldstone or brick we anchor into the mortar joints rather than the stone, so a camera moved later leaves a repairable hole. Either way the owner sees the spot before anything is drilled.

Do you cover the hamlets along the Connecticut line, such as Putnam Lake and Brewster Hill?
+

Yes. We cover all six Putnam towns and the villages of Brewster, Cold Spring and Nelsonville, including the hamlets near the state line. Rentals on private lake roads are fine too; send the directions you give tenants, and the visit is booked into the same early-day arrival windows as everywhere else in the county.

Can the accessory apartment's side door take a keypad lock that the owner can reset between tenants?
+

Yes. An outdoor-rated keypad deadbolt or a keypad with a strike gives the tenant a personal code and lets the owner delete it and issue a new one at turnover. We set the owner up as the administrator, give the tenant a user code, and show the owner how to change codes without calling us.

Should a rental with outdoor cameras tell tenants and visitors that it records?
+

We recommend it. Tell tenants in writing which cameras exist and what each one sees, ideally in the lease, and a small notice at the entrances is a common courtesy that also discourages trouble. Whether any specific notice is legally required is a question for the owner's attorney; we keep cameras off tenants' windows and private space either way.

Is financing available for a landlord with only one or two Putnam rentals?
+

Financing is available through Wisetack, subject to approval. It can be used for a single property's work as well as a larger project, and the owner sees the terms before deciding.

What happens to the apartment's keypad and doorbell if a Carmel accessory apartment stops being a rental?
+

Carmel's rules require the accessory apartment to be removed if the owner stops living on the property, and an owner may also simply decide to take the unit back. Either way, the tenant's codes are deleted, the tenant's doorbell is reset or moved into the owner's account, and any device the house no longer needs comes down, with the holes patched by the owner's contractor.

If a rental's internet goes out, does the recorder keep saving video?
+

A local recorder does. It keeps recording to its own hard drive whether or not the internet is up; what stops is remote viewing and phone alerts until the connection returns. Cloud-only cameras without local storage stop recording during the outage, which is one reason we favor a recorder or a camera with a memory card at rentals on back roads.

Does a homeowners association have to approve a doorbell camera on a rented townhouse?
+

Often, yes. Many associations treat anything on a unit's exterior as subject to their architectural rules, and the declaration and bylaws decide. The unit owner, not the tenant, should get the approval in writing before we mount anything outside, and we follow whatever the association sets for placement and color.

How quickly is a visit to a Putnam rental booked after we call?
+

The visit is booked within 2 hours of the request into the next open early-day, two-hour arrival window, seven days a week. The technician arrives within that window, never earlier, and the owner or the office is told who is coming.

How long can an outdoor alarm siren sound at a rental in the Town of Carmel?
+

No more than 15 minutes. Carmel's alarm chapter requires any alarm with an outside siren or strobe to have an automatic cutoff that ends it within 15 minutes of activation. We program the cutoff when we install or take over an alarm in the town, and the town's alarm permit stays in the owner's name.
