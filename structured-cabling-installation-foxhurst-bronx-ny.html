The Neighborhood

Why Foxhurst Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

In Foxhurst, the hardest part of getting a building connected is rarely the street outside. It is the forty or fifty feet of wire between the cellar and the apartment, and who owns it. The public numbers make the point. Point2Homes puts the median construction year of Foxhurst homes at 1981, with about 23.7 percent built before the 1940s and another 5 percent added by 1949. At the other end, 16.1 percent went up between 2000 and 2009 and 12.3 percent between 2010 and 2019. So roughly three in ten homes stand in buildings old enough to predate television, and more than one in four in buildings young enough to have had a network in mind when they were drawn. Homes.com and Apartments.com describe the older half the same way: midrise brick buildings from the 1910s, with brick apartments stacked over street-level storefronts. Each of those buildings has had telephone, then cable television, then broadband threaded into it after the fact, by whichever company was selling service at the time. Very few of them have a clean, documented path from the provider's line to every apartment door.

The neighborhood's history explains why there are so many layers. CityNeighborhoods.nyc traces the name to Fox Street, which first appeared on nineteenth-century maps of the old Town of West Farms, and Hatching Cat NYC tells how the area came to be called Foxhurst after the Fox and Tiffany family estate at Fox's Corners, where a mansion stood from around 1840 until about 1914; a 1913 view shows it roughly a year before it came down. By the 1890s the grid of Prospect Avenue, Intervale Avenue and Fox Street was surveyed. Crotona Park opened in 1888 and the White Plains Road subway line in 1904, and by the 1910s and 1920s Foxhurst was fully built up. That is the generation of brick midrises we still work in. In the 1950s, Puerto Rican families settled in Foxhurst and neighboring Longwood, and then came the 1970s, which CityNeighborhoods.nyc calls Foxhurst's crucible. The Mott Haven Herald reports that nearly 70 percent of the housing stock in Community District 2, Hunts Point and Longwood, was destroyed by fire.

The rebuilding left its own kind of wiring. Banana Kelly's history records residents "liberating" three buildings on Kelly Street in 1978 to rehabilitate them through sweat equity, and CityNeighborhoods.nyc notes that affordable apartments have since replaced many of the abandoned houses and vacant lots that once marked the area. Hetty Fox's play street, begun in 1977 on Lyman Place, belongs to the same story, though some sources place Lyman Place in Morrisania rather than Foxhurst. Buildings gut-renovated in the 1980s and 1990s were usually wired for the services of that moment: a telephone pair or two to each unit, a coaxial drop for cable TV, maybe a single conduit in the stair hall. Thirty years and several providers later, those buildings often hold three or four generations of cable, most of it abandoned in place and none of it mapped. When an owner wants every apartment on a modern, tested connection, the first job is figuring out what is already in the walls.

Then there is the question of who buys the work. Homes.com reports that 93.4 percent of Foxhurst residents rent and 6.6 percent own, and RentCafe's breakdown shows 71 percent of rentals in buildings with fewer than 50 units, 28 percent in buildings of more than 50 units and just 1 percent in single-family rentals. Homes.com also notes HDFC cooperative buildings here, which carry income restrictions and are partly subsidized. Put together, the person deciding on structured cabling in Foxhurst is almost always a landlord, a managing agent, an HDFC board or a nonprofit housing operator, and the people living with the result are tenants or shareholders who did not choose the contractor. That is why our quotes are written so a board can read them at a meeting and a managing agent can file them, and why we do not drill anything a tenant asks for unless the building has agreed.

Connectivity here is not a luxury question. The New York City Council's internet access data lists Longwood–Hunts Point, Bronx Community District 2, among the districts where households lack broadband, at 41 percent; that dataset is several years old, and we cite it as a sign of the gap rather than a current count. On the supply side, ISPReports says Verizon offers fiber to 97.0 percent of homes in Hunts Point, just south and east, and BroadbandNow puts fiber availability at 84.7 percent across the Bronx. Those numbers look reassuring until you read BuildingHealthX's Bronx guide, which describes Fios availability in NYCHA-heavy parts of the South Bronx and Hunts Point as patchy building by building, and explains that the landlord or building management has to give Verizon access to install conduit, risers and the in-unit boxes. A fiber line on the street does not reach apartment 4C on its own. The inside path does, and that path is structured cabling.

HPD has also changed the rules about that inside path. Its residential broadband installation guidance states that exclusive revenue share agreements between a landlord and an internet provider are now banned, and that sale-and-leaseback arrangements, in which a provider sells the inside wiring to the landlord and leases it back, are banned too. For a Foxhurst owner, that turns cabling into a property decision. When the building pays to install and owns a tested cable plant from the cellar to every unit, it can let more than one provider use it, it can replace a provider without rewiring, and it is not tangled in the kind of arrangement HPD now prohibits. We write that ownership into every building-wide plan.

And the neighborhood is still building. New York YIMBY reported permits filed in January 2026 for 840 Freeman Street, a 74-foot building with 39 residences and a concrete-based structure. The Bronx Times reported permits for a six-story, 20-unit residential building at 870 Home Street. YIMBY's Foxhurst page also lists a seven-story building at 1166 Fox Street and a housing lottery at 1230 Prospect Avenue. The DOE Fund describes Tiffany Court Plaza at 980 Westchester Avenue as bringing 151 new affordable and supportive units to Foxhurst with 24-hour front desk security, and Governor Hochul announced the completion in October 2023 of a 72-unit affordable and supportive development, HELP Home Simpson, in the Foxhurst section. Each of these needs a telecom room, risers, cable to every unit, and cable to the desks, offices, cameras and entry points the operator runs. The cheapest time to get all of that right is before the concrete is poured and before the drywall closes.

We want to be clear about what we could not find. We searched for Reddit threads, neighborhood forum posts and local news about network wiring, internet installs or wiring disputes in Foxhurst and came back with nothing specific. The Bronx Times' Foxhurst feed, when we read it, was mostly gun-violence and housing coverage. Boundaries also vary by source: CityNeighborhoods.nyc draws Foxhurst from Sheridan Boulevard and the Bruckner Expressway to East 163rd Street, Prospect Avenue, Crotona Park, Louis Niñé Boulevard and Freeman Street, while Wikipedia places the Simpson Street station in Longwood and the Freeman Street station in Crotona Park East. Where this page describes walls, risers and closets, it draws on the building ages and types in the public record and on what we find in buildings of those kinds, not on inspections of any particular address.

What Foxhurst owners tend to add next. Once a building has a real riser and a mapped closet, the next conversation is usually about the front door, the lobby and the cellar: cameras, a video entry panel, a card reader for the side door. Inside Wiring and HPD

Who Owns the Cable Behind a Foxhurst Wall, and Why HPD's Broadband Rules Care

Every apartment building in Foxhurst has inside wiring of some kind, and most owners have never been asked who owns it. For decades it did not seem to matter: a provider ran its line in, the tenant paid the bill, and when the next provider came along it ran another line beside the first. HPD's broadband guidance now says plainly that two arrangements are off the table: exclusive revenue share agreements between the landlord and an internet provider, and sale-and-leaseback deals in which a provider sells inside wiring to the landlord and leases it back. That makes it worth understanding the other ways a building's wiring can be owned and used, because the choice affects what a tenant can buy, what the owner controls and what the next contractor has to deal with. The table below is our practical guide, written from a cabling contractor's point of view. It is not legal advice, and for any agreement with a provider, the owner's attorney should read the paper.

A building-owned cable plant is the one that ages well

When the owner pays for the riser, the closet and the cable to each apartment, the building ends up with something that behaves like plumbing: it belongs to the property, it is documented, and anyone with permission can use it. A provider who wins a tenant's business connects at the closet rather than drilling a new hole through the front wall. A tenant who switches providers does not leave a dead cable behind. A managing agent who takes over the building inherits a port map rather than a mystery. In a 1910s brick midrise, where each new hole through masonry is slow, noisy and a fire separation to seal, the savings over the years are real. In a new building like the ones permitted on Freeman Street and Home Street, it is simply the cleanest way to deliver every unit ready for service on day one.

When providers have each run their own lines

Many older Foxhurst buildings look like this today: a coaxial cable from one company, a fiber drop from another, an abandoned telephone block, and a few runs from an installer nobody remembers. That arrangement is not illegal and it often works, but it accumulates. Each line enters at a different point, follows a different route and ends at a different box. When we are asked to bring order to a building like this, we trace each cable, confirm which are live, photograph everything as it stands, and then propose a single path forward that the owner controls. Removing dead cable is always offered as its own line on the quote, because some owners want it gone and others would rather leave it until a larger renovation.

HDFC boards and the long view

An HDFC co-op board spends shareholders' money and answers to them at every meeting. That makes a building-owned cable plant an easy thing to explain: the co-op pays once, owns the result, and every shareholder can pick a provider without the board negotiating with anyone. It also makes documentation essential. Board officers change, and the next board needs to know what was installed, what it cost, what was tested and where the records are kept. We hand over a port map, test results and photos of every sealed penetration as a single file the board can keep with its building records, and we write the scope so it can be attached to the minutes that approved it.

Supportive housing operators

Buildings like Tiffany Court Plaza at 980 Westchester Avenue, which the DOE Fund describes as having 24-hour front desk security, and HELP Home Simpson combine homes with on-site staff. That means at least two networks with very different owners: the operator's network for the front desk, staff offices, cameras and entry, and the residents' own internet service in each apartment. Both can travel through the same riser and closet, but they need separate cables, separate panel ports and separate network segments. We plan those buildings so the operator's systems never depend on a resident's account and no resident device can reach the operator's equipment.

For new buildings, drawings beat guesses

On a building that is still on paper or still in its foundation, we would rather read the architect's set than walk the site. The drawings tell us where the stair and elevator cores sit, how tall each floor is, where mechanical shafts rise, and which walls carry a fire rating. From that we can mark the telecom room, the sleeves through each slab and the path to each unit and each ground-floor space, and send it back to the architect and general contractor before the concrete crew needs it. A walk-through still happens, but it happens with a plan in hand.

For older buildings, photograph the outside first

On a brick walk-up, the outside walls often tell us more than the inside. Provider cables clipped down the facade, coax bundles disappearing into a cellar window, a gray box beside the stoop: each one is a clue to where service enters and how many companies have served the building. Stand across the street and take a photo of the front, then do the same at the rear yard if you can reach it. Add a photo of the cellar wall where the cables come through. Those three or four pictures save us half an hour on site and let us arrive with the right tools for the route we expect.

Count connections by apartment and by system

For a building-wide job, the count is usually one or two jacks per apartment plus whatever the building itself runs: the super's office, the entry panel, cameras, a laundry room reader, a community room. For a store or office, walk the space and list what plugs in today and what you expect to add. In both cases, write it down by location, not just as a total, because the price follows the routes, and a jack at the far end of the top floor costs more to reach than one beside the closet.

1910s brick midrises: plan the vertical route before anything else

The horizontal part of a walk-up job is usually manageable. Cable can travel along the cellar ceiling to a point below each apartment line, and inside an apartment it can follow a closet or a baseboard to the room that needs it. The vertical part is where these buildings resist. Brick walls between apartments and stairs leave no hollow stud bay to fish through, and the stair hall is a shared space that every resident walks past. So before we price anything we look for the building's own vertical path: closets stacked directly above one another on each floor, a closed and disused shaft that can be used safely, or a corner of the stair hall where a painted surface raceway would be least noticed. Each floor crossing is a fire separation and gets a listed firestop seal sized to the cable bundle. We show the owner the proposed route on every floor, because a raceway that looks fine on paper can look very different on a hallway wall residents have lived with for decades.

Buildings rebuilt after the fires: read the renovation, not the date stone

A building whose cornerstone says 1912 but which was gutted and rebuilt in the 1980s or 1990s is, for cabling purposes, a building from the decade it was rebuilt. The inside walls may be block or drywall on studs rather than plaster on brick; the halls may have dropped ceilings; there may be a telephone conduit or two running up from the cellar. That is good news, because a rehab often left pathways a new cable can follow. It is also where we find the most abandoned cable. Thirty years of providers coming and going can leave the cellar ceiling and the stair conduit stuffed with dead coax and unused telephone pairs. We trace and identify before proposing removal, and we never cut a cable we have not confirmed is dead.

Concrete and block new builds: the pour decides the price

In a concrete-frame building, every vertical route has to exist before the slab is poured, or it has to be cut afterward. Sleeves set in the formwork at the right spot cost very little; a core drilled later through a finished slab costs much more, needs the structural engineer's agreement, and puts dust and noise into a building that may already be occupied. So on projects like 840 Freeman Street, our first deliverable is a sleeve and pathway plan the concrete crew can follow, with spare sleeves at each core for systems nobody has chosen yet. Block walls around stairs, elevators and shafts are the next consideration: where cable has to pass through them into a telecom room, the opening is drilled to size and sealed with a listed firestop product once the cable is through.

Attached houses: short runs, two households

The attached houses built in the 1990s rebuilding are the simplest Foxhurst buildings to cable. Wood framing allows walls to be fished, runs are short, and the basement ceiling usually offers a direct route to each interior wall. The complication is the second household. When an owner lives on one floor and rents the other, we give each unit its own route from where service enters, so each can have its own provider and neither can see the other's network, and we confirm with both households before drilling through a shared floor.

A counter that has to work every hour the store is open

For a takeout counter on Westchester Avenue or a phone shop on Southern Boulevard, the network is judged by one thing: whether the payment goes through when the line is out the door. We design storefront networks backward from that moment. The card reader and register get wired drops wherever the payment equipment supports it, because a cable does not care how many phones are in the store. The modem, router, switch and any camera recorder sit together in a small locked cabinet, ideally in the back office or the store's part of the cellar rather than on a shelf behind the counter. A battery backup keeps that cabinet alive through a brief power dip. Guest Wi-Fi, if the store offers it, runs on its own network segment so customers' phones never share a network with the register. And everything is labeled so the owner can tell a provider's technician, on the phone, exactly which cable to unplug and which to leave alone.

Working under the elevated tracks

Stores along Westchester Avenue and the stretch of Southern Boulevard where the el runs have a structure overhead and constant traffic below. For cabling, that mostly affects how we handle the outside of the building and how we secure what is inside it. We do not attach anything to the transit structure; a store's cable stays on and inside its own building. We keep cable off the facade wherever an inside route exists, because exterior runs on a busy avenue get damaged and look untidy. Indoors, cables are supported along their whole length, strain-relieved at the jack and the panel, and dressed so nothing hangs loose to be knocked. Where a store's sign band or awning area has to be opened to reach a camera or a sign, the work is planned for quiet hours and kept within the building's own frontage.

The store and the apartments share one building

Most Foxhurst storefronts sit under apartments, and many share a cellar with the residential side. A store's cable leaving through that cellar crosses space the store does not control, and the building's own systems may run through the store's ceiling. We sort out with the building owner, before quoting, which spaces each party may use, where each party's equipment will be locked, and whether the store's cable can use the building's riser or must have its own path. That keeps a store's network from depending on the building's closet and keeps tenants' and the building's equipment out of the store's back room.

Rules and limits for business cabling in Foxhurst

  • The low-voltage permit exemption. NYC Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, added by Local Law 128 of 2024, provides that an electrical permit is not required for wiring designed to operate below 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data. A Bronx hiring guide we read says the exemption applies when the work is done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person, and that work tied into life safety systems is reserved for licensed master or special electricians. On commercial scopes we confirm the filing requirement for the specific job before starting.
  • Fire alarm is its own trade. Fire alarm wiring sits outside the data exemption and carries separate licensing and filings. We keep it out of data jobs entirely.
  • Power is the electrician's. Where a rack, recorder or access point needs a new receptacle or circuit, that work goes to a licensed electrician and our quote says so.
  • Jackets match the space. Plenum-rated cable in air-handling ceilings, at least riser-rated cable between floors, outdoor-rated cable anywhere a run leaves the building.
  • Seal what you open. Every hole through a rated wall or floor is sealed with a listed firestop product, left visible and photographed for the owner's records.
  • Approval in writing. In a leased store, the lease governs alterations. We ask for the landlord's or managing agent's written agreement, and for any insurance certificate they require, before the visit is booked.
Field Notes

Field Notes: What We Check on the Sidewalk Before Entering a Foxhurst Building

We do not tell stories about a specific customer's building unless we can document them, so this is not about one address. It is the routine a technician follows outside a brick building with a store on the ground floor, which in Foxhurst is where many jobs begin.

We stand across the street first. We count the cables coming down the front wall and note where each one ends: a cellar window, a hole beside the stoop, a box at the store's sign band. Each cable is usually a provider, and the count tells us roughly how many companies have served the building. We look at the rear if the yard is reachable, because some providers came in from the back. We note whether the building has an elevator bulkhead on the roof. We read the storefront: what the business is, what hours are posted, whether there is a separate residential entrance. Then we go in with the super or owner, and the first thing we ask is where every one of those outside cables lands inside. Only when every line we saw outside is accounted for in the cellar do we start talking about new routes, and only after the owner has agreed to a route does anyone pick up a drill.

Your Questions Answered

Structured Cabling Installation in Foxhurst: Questions Answered

Owners, providers and who controls the inside wire

An internet company offered to buy the wiring inside our building and lease it back to us. Is that still allowed?

According to HPD's residential broadband guidance, no. It lists sale-and-leaseback arrangements, where an internet provider sells inside wiring to the landlord and leases it back, as banned. If someone has offered you that kind of deal, do not sign, and if your building already has one from years ago, ask your attorney what it means for you now. From the cabling side, the cleaner path is for the building to own its riser, closet and apartment cable outright, installed and tested by a contractor the owner hires. Once the building owns a documented cable plant, any provider can connect at the closet with the owner's permission, and no one is leasing wire back to anyone. We are happy to walk the building and describe what that plant would look like, so you have something concrete to compare against whatever the provider proposed.

A provider wants to be the only service in our building and offered the landlord a share of its revenue. What has changed?

HPD's guidance says exclusive revenue share agreements between a landlord and an internet provider are now banned. In practical terms, that means a Foxhurst landlord should not be trading tenants' choice of provider for a cut of one company's income. That has a cabling consequence: if tenants may choose among providers, the building needs a way for each provider to reach each apartment without drilling a new path every time a tenant switches. A riser and a cable from the closet to each unit, owned by the building, is the simplest way to do that. Providers connect at the closet, tenants choose freely, and the brick on the front of the building stops collecting new holes. Ask your attorney about any agreement you already have; ask us about the wiring.

We are about to sign an access agreement so a provider can wire our building. What should we check from the cabling side?

Four things, in writing. First, where the provider's equipment will go and who will hold the key to that space. Second, whether the provider will run its own cable to each unit or connect to a building-owned riser, and who owns any cable it installs. Third, how every hole through a wall or floor will be sealed, and whether the provider will photograph and document the seals. Fourth, what happens to the provider's equipment and cable if it stops serving the building. BuildingHealthX's Bronx guide notes that for Fios to come in, the landlord has to grant access for conduit, risers and in-unit boxes, so the agreement really is about your building's interior. We can review the physical plan with you before you sign, and point out where it would leave the building with cable it cannot use or holes nobody sealed. The legal terms are for your attorney.

Is apartment turnover a good time to add data jacks to a unit?

It is the best time short of a full renovation. A vacant apartment can be worked without scheduling around a household, furniture is out of the way, and any patching and painting can happen alongside the turnover work the unit is getting anyway. In a Foxhurst walk-up, where most units are rented, an owner who adds a jack or two to each apartment as it turns over can bring a whole building onto a modern, mapped cable plant over a few years, without ever disturbing a sitting tenant. The condition is that the riser and closet exist first, so each new unit has somewhere to connect. We often recommend building the riser and closet in one visit, cabling the vacant units immediately, and leaving pull strings at each occupied apartment line so those units can be finished quickly when they turn over.

New buildings and supportive housing

Our new building on Freeman Street has a concrete frame. Should data conduit be cast into the slabs or run above the ceilings?

Usually a combination, decided by the drawings. Vertical paths, the sleeves that carry cable from floor to floor at the telecom room or a shaft, should be cast in place, because cutting them later means coring finished concrete with the engineer's agreement. Horizontal distribution along each floor is often better kept out of the slab and run above the corridor ceiling or inside walls, where it can be reached and changed later. Conduit embedded across a slab is permanent and cannot be enlarged, so we reserve it for places with no other option. New York YIMBY describes 840 Freeman Street as a 74-foot building with 39 residences and a concrete-based structure, which is exactly the kind of project where a sleeve plan delivered before the first pour saves real money. We send that plan to the architect and general contractor for their approval.

What should the low-voltage section of our bid documents say so contractors price the same thing?

It should name the cable category for each kind of drop, the number and location of drops per unit and per common space, the telecom room's size and contents, the riser capacity including spare sleeves, the termination standard, the labeling scheme, the testing method and what results are handed over, and the firestopping approach for every rated penetration. It should also state who owns the finished cable plant and that it will be open to any provider the owner permits, given HPD's rules. Without that detail, one bidder prices a minimum and another prices a complete plant, and the numbers cannot be compared. We can draft that section for a developer or architect from the drawings, and we write it so another contractor could bid against it.

Our supportive housing building has a 24-hour front desk. What does the desk need cabled?

More than most people expect. A typical desk needs wired drops for the staff computer, a phone if it uses the network, the station that releases the entry door, a monitor or computer for the cameras, and often a printer. Behind it, cable has to reach every camera, the entry panel and any door readers, all landing in a closet the operator controls. The DOE Fund says Tiffany Court Plaza at 980 Westchester Avenue will have 24-hour front desk security, and buildings like that work best when the desk's systems run on the operator's own network segment, separate from residents' internet, on a switch and battery backup sized for the cameras it powers. We plan the desk, the closet and every device route together so nothing at the desk depends on a resident's account or a provider's router.

Our building wants a small computer room for residents. What does that room need?

Given that the Council's data shows a large share of households in this part of the Bronx without home broadband, a shared computer room can matter a great deal. It needs a wired drop for each computer and printer, a ceiling or high-wall drop for a wireless access point, and its own network segment so residents' traffic stays away from the building's cameras and staff systems. It should connect back to the main closet on a cable that tests clean, with a battery backup in the closet so a brief power flicker does not drop everyone mid-session. Plan a couple of spare drops for the day the room adds a screen or another workstation. If the room will also host classes, a drop behind the wall where a display will hang saves a second visit.

Brick buildings, storefronts and older routes

Our store is under the elevated tracks on Westchester Avenue. Does the train passing overhead cause problems for cable?

We have no measurements for this neighborhood, so we will not claim the trains damage anything. What we do in practice, in any building beside heavy transit or traffic, is install so that nothing depends on luck. Cables are supported along their full length rather than hanging between a few clips. Every jack and patch-panel termination is strain-relieved, so a cable that moves slightly over the years does not pull on the connection. The rack or wall cabinet is fixed firmly to the structure, not standing loose on a shelf. And every drop is certified on an analyzer when we finish, so if a problem appears later, there is a clean baseline to test against. We also keep the store's cable off the facade on the avenue side wherever an inside route exists, which avoids most outdoor damage.

Can cable run up the back wall of our building or across the rear yard instead of inside?

Sometimes, and in a brick walk-up with no usable interior route it can be the least disruptive option. An exterior run on the rear wall uses outdoor-rated cable inside protective conduit, enters the building through a sealed, sloped penetration so water does not follow the cable in, and is fastened into the mortar joints rather than the brick face where possible. A run across a yard to a rear structure goes in conduit, buried or supported, and is sealed at both ends. The drawbacks are weather exposure, appearance and access for future repairs. We compare the rear-wall option against interior routes at the walk-through and let the owner choose. For buildings next to the Longwood Historic District, which is in Longwood rather than Foxhurst, exterior work there would need Landmarks review; we check the address before proposing anything on the outside.

Some apartments in our 1910s building were gut-renovated and others were not. Does the cable plan change from floor to floor?

It often does, and that is normal in Foxhurst, where buildings have been repaired in pieces over decades. A renovated apartment may have new drywall on studs that can be fished easily, while the unit next door still has plaster on brick. A renovated stair may have a new chase; an original one may not. We walk every apartment line, not just one floor, and the drop plan records the route and method for each unit individually. The price follows that: a unit with open framing costs less to reach than one with original masonry. Where the renovated units left a usable path, such as a chase behind a new kitchen, we use it to serve the units above and below where the structure allows.

Broadband access for residents

Many households around here don't have home internet. Can we cable every apartment so tenants can pick any provider?

Yes, and that is one of the most useful things a Foxhurst owner can do. The Council's dataset listed 41 percent of households in Longwood–Hunts Point without broadband, while ISPReports says Verizon offers fiber to 97 percent of Hunts Point homes, which suggests the gap is often inside buildings rather than on the street. We build a riser from the cellar or telecom room to each floor, run a tested cable from the closet to a jack in each apartment, and label it all on a port map. Providers then connect at the closet with the owner's permission. That arrangement fits HPD's rules, which ban exclusive revenue share and leaseback deals, and it removes one practical barrier for a tenant who wants service: no drilling, no waiting for permission to run a new line, just a connection at a jack that already exists.

If 5G home internet or a sidewalk kiosk serves our block, does the building still need cable inside?

Usually, yes. Community Board 2's September 2023 agenda shows the founder of Wired broadband updating the committee on Link5G, and wireless home service is a real option for some households. But a wireless signal still has to get into the building and reach each room, and brick walls and concrete slabs weaken it. A building's own systems, such as the front desk, cameras, entry panel and staff offices, need steady wired connections regardless of which provider serves the residents. And if a wireless provider puts a receiver on the roof or in a window, cable still has to run from that point to the closet and out to each unit. Wireless can change where service enters a building; it rarely removes the need for a cable plant inside.

Can the lobby or a community room offer residents free Wi-Fi without opening the building's network to everyone?

Yes. The access points serving the lobby or community room get their own cables back to the closet and their own network segment, separate from the building's cameras, entry panel and staff systems. The switch enforces that separation, so a phone on the free network can reach the internet but not the camera recorder or the front desk computer. The access points draw power over the same cable, so no outlet is needed on the ceiling. Where the free Wi-Fi uses a separate internet line, the building's own systems are not slowed when the community room is full. We place the access points after the walk-through, taking into account how block walls and long hallways behave, and we label each one on the port map so the operator knows exactly which cable feeds which area.

Our Installation Process for Foxhurst Buildings

Whether it is a single storefront drop on Prospect Avenue or a riser to every apartment in a six-story walk-up, the same eight steps apply. In Foxhurst, the second step carries more weight than usual, because who owns the finished cable is decided there.

1. Send the address and who signs for the building

Street, building type and age, and whether it is privately owned, an HDFC co-op, a nonprofit operator's or a developer's, with photos of where lines enter and where connections are needed.

2. Settle approvals and the inside-wire question

Written approval from the owner, agent or board, and agreement in writing that the new cable belongs to the building, not to any provider.

3. Walk from the provider's entry to every outlet

Facade, cellar, risers and each apartment line or room, noting brick, concrete, renovated units and every fire separation crossed.

4. Write the drop plan and price

Each drop with its cable, route, termination, firestopping and price, plus the closet or rack, agreed before any drilling.

5. Build pathways and pull the cable

Sleeves, conduit or J-hooks along the agreed routes, and every cable pulled in one organized session from a single staging point.

6. Seal, terminate and label

Listed firestop at every rated penetration, jacks and panels terminated to one standard, and labels at both ends matching the port map.

7. Certify every drop

Copper drops tested on a cable analyzer and fiber strands checked; anything that fails is fixed and retested before we leave.

8. Hand over maps, results and photos

The port map, test results and penetration photos as one file, and a walk-through of the closet for the super, agent or board.

Reading a Foxhurst drop plan

FAQ

Foxhurst Structured Cabling Installation FAQ

How should we tell tenants before a building-wide cabling job?
+

In writing, a week or more ahead, in the languages your tenants read. The notice should give the dates, the hours work will happen, which apartments need access and roughly how long each visit takes, what noise to expect, and who to call with questions. We provide a plain description of the work and the schedule that the owner or managing agent can put on letterhead and post in the lobby, and we stick to the hours it promises.

Our HDFC co-op has a tight budget. Which cabling work should come first?
+

The riser and the closet. They are the backbone every later drop connects to, and building them once stops the cycle of each provider drilling its own way in. After that, cable the vacant units and the building's own systems, then finish occupied apartments as they turn over or as the budget allows. Each piece is a separate line on the quote so the board can fund the work in stages without paying twice.

Does any of your work touch the elevated train structure on Westchester Avenue?
+

No. We never attach anything to the transit structure. A store's or building's cable stays on and inside its own property, and we prefer interior routes so nothing runs down the avenue-side facade at all. If a job needs a ladder on the sidewalk, it is planned for quiet hours and kept within the building's frontage.

Our street gets closed for events like the Bronx Halloween Parade. Does that affect scheduling?
+

It can, mostly for parking and carrying in equipment. Bronx Buzz reported the 2026 parade starting at Simpson Street and 163rd Street, and street fairs and closures happen through the year. Tell us about any closure you know of when you book, and we will choose a different day or plan to carry materials in from the nearest open corner.

Can our managing agent check the building network from their office?
+

Yes, if the building's switch and router are set up for remote management on a secured account. The agent can then see whether the closet is online and whether a particular port has gone dark without sending someone to the cellar. We set the access up in the owner's name, not ours, and document the logins in the handover file so they survive a change of agent.

Do you leave extra cable at each end in case a jack or the rack has to move?
+

Yes. We leave a neat service loop at the closet and a shorter one behind each jack, dressed so it does not tangle. That slack lets a jack be moved a short distance, or a panel re-terminated, without pulling a new cable. In tight brick-wall boxes where space is limited, we keep the loop where the cable enters the wall instead.

Does the building's front desk and camera system need its own internet account?
+

It should. When the building's systems share a resident's or an office's account, a billing change or a provider swap can knock the cameras and door station offline. A separate account for the building, connected at the closet and kept on its own network segment, means the operator's systems never depend on anyone else's service.

Will tenants' own routers work with the new apartment jacks?
+

Yes. The jack in each apartment is a standard network connection. Once a provider connects that apartment's cable at the closet, the tenant plugs in the provider's equipment or their own router as usual. We label each jack with its apartment and port number so a provider's technician can find the right cable at the closet without guessing.

Where should the port map live so it survives a change of super or management?
+

In two places. A printed copy goes inside the closet door, sealed in a sleeve, so anyone working at the rack has it in hand. A digital copy, with the test results and penetration photos, goes to the owner or board, not just the super, and should be kept with the deed or building records. We can send updated copies whenever the cable plant changes.

We own a 1990s attached two-family off Kelly Street. Where does cable usually travel in a house like that?
+

Usually along the basement ceiling and up through interior walls, because houses from that rebuilding era are often wood-framed inside and can be fished cleanly. Where a wall is blocked, a stacked closet or a run behind base trim does the job. We confirm the framing on the walk-through and give each household its own path from where service enters.

If our internet provider has an outage, will the building's cameras and door release still work?
+

Inside the building, generally yes. The cameras keep recording to the recorder in the closet and the door station keeps talking to the desk over the building's own cable and switch, because none of that needs the internet. What stops is remote viewing from a phone or an off-site office until service returns. We set systems up to work locally first so an outage is an inconvenience, not a blackout.
