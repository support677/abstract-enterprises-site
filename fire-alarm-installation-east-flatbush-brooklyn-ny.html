The Neighborhood

Why East Flatbush Properties Need Fire Alarm Installation

East Flatbush is a neighborhood of small landlords. Community Board 17's budget statement for fiscal year 2027 describes the district's housing as predominantly one- to four-family homes, usually managed by smaller landlords rather than large developers, and counts 62,636 housing units. That matters for fire detection more than any building code chapter. In a two- or three-family house there is no super walking the halls, no lobby panel and no fire safety director. Whether a fire is caught early depends on the detectors inside each apartment, on whether a detector on one floor can be heard on another, and on whether the owner, who may live on the parlor floor or in another borough, has kept the devices current. The board itself put smoke and carbon monoxide detectors on its list of city funding requests, beside a fire prevention inspection team.

The rental side carries most of the risk. The NYU Furman Center puts homeownership at 34.1 percent, so roughly two households in three rent, and it recorded 229.5 serious housing code violations for every 1,000 private rental units in 2025. Those cover everything from heat to mold, but they describe housing where repairs often wait. When we survey a rented building, we pull its HPD record first, because a detector violation, a door that does not latch or a run of electrical complaints changes the plan before we arrive.

Recent East Flatbush fire reports have mostly involved apartments, and several turn on warning rather than flames. After an April 2026 fire on Kingston Avenue that killed a mother and her son, neighbors on the same floor told News 12 they had not heard any alarms, and one said, "It would have been nice to know something about it rather than just coming out here." Then there is the date on the calendar. HPD requires natural gas detectors in multiple dwellings and in one- and two-family homes the owner does not occupy, wherever there is gas piping, starting January 1, 2027. In October 2026 the Department of Buildings, the FDNY, HPD, National Grid and Con Edison ran a joint reminder, and HPD Commissioner Dina Levy said: "Installing detectors will save lives. Starting next year, HPD code enforcement staff will inspect for them to ensure compliance and keep New Yorkers safe." For an owner with tenants, the sensible move is to handle gas, smoke and CO in one visit per apartment, with one notice and one record, rather than opening every door twice.

Row houses with a cellar boiler: smoke rises along one stair to the people asleep

The Landmarks Preservation Commission's designation of the East 25th Street Historic District, the neighborhood's first, describes 56 row houses built between 1909 and 1912 with limestone or brownstone fronts and rounded or angled bays. Plainer attached houses across East Flatbush share the same layout: a cellar with the boiler, water heater and often a gas dryer; a parlor floor with the kitchen at the back; and bedrooms above, all joined by one interior stair. A fire in the cellar or the kitchen has a short, direct path up that stair.

So in a row house we start at the bottom. A smoke alarm goes at the head of the cellar stair, where smoke from below arrives first, and CO detection goes on the bedroom floor. In the cellar we place CO detection where it is useful without sitting on top of the boiler, since the FDNY lists a detector mounted too close to a fuel-burning appliance among the common causes of nuisance alarms. Smoke alarms go on every level and inside bedrooms where doors stay closed, and linking them so the cellar alarm sounds in the top-floor bedroom does the most good. Exterior work on a designated house needs Landmarks review, but our devices and cable go inside and nothing changes on the street front.

Houses split into two or three apartments: separate households, one way out

The two- and three-family house defines East Flatbush rentals. Each floor is its own household, the owner may live on one floor or far away, everyone shares the front door and the stair, and the cellar holds the boiler and a gas meter for each apartment. Under HPD's rules, a house with one or two units and an owner who lives elsewhere needs CO detection wherever there is a fossil-fuel burning device and natural gas detection from January 1, 2027 where there is gas piping. At three units it is a multiple dwelling, and every apartment needs a working smoke detector within fifteen feet of the primary entrance to each room lawfully used for sleeping, plus posted notices and kept records.

We plan these houses in two layers. Inside each apartment go the detectors HPD requires. In the stair, front hall and cellar we add linked alarms so a fire on one floor sounds on every floor, because the household asleep upstairs cannot see a fire starting by the parlor-floor door. Then we write down whose device is whose: which alarms the owner maintains, which ones the tenant tests monthly, and where each expiration date is recorded. When the owner lives elsewhere, that written split is often what keeps a dead alarm from going unnoticed for a year.

Rooms and apartments below grade: protect who is sleeping there, and say plainly what an alarm cannot fix

Basements in East Flatbush are lived in, some as legal apartments, many as finished rooms for a relative, a tenant or a boarder. In March 2026, News 12 reported a fire behind a basement wall in a home on New York Avenue near Avenue D that had allegedly been converted into a rooming house; the Department of Buildings said it had closed earlier complaints after inspectors were denied access twice. In October 2025, a man drowned in a basement unit near Rutland Road and Kingston Avenue during record rainfall, and the cellar got a partial vacate order.

We do not decide whether a room is legal, and an alarm never makes an illegal room safe; a room below grade with one way out and no window big enough to climb through is dangerous to sleep in regardless. What we do is protect whoever sleeps there tonight: smoke and CO detection in the room, linked to the floors above in both directions. On blocks where cellars take water, sealed-battery and wireless devices mounted high do better than anything near the floor, and any panel or power supply goes well above the last water line. If the room is not legal, we say in writing that the alarm is not the fix.

Walk-ups of four to six floors: the apartments, the halls and the cellar are three separate questions

Walk-ups on the side streets between Church Avenue and Linden Boulevard usually hold a dozen to a few dozen apartments with nobody living on site to notice a problem. Their protection comes in layers added at different times: battery alarms in the apartments, a few hall detectors or bells, and sometimes a small cellar panel nobody has tested in years. A 2014 fire in a four-apartment building on East 49th Street at Church Avenue, reported by amNY, shows why halls matter: smoke from a third-floor kitchen fire killed the man who lived there and injured three others, aged 41, 69 and 86, as it spread.

We treat the three layers separately: HPD devices and records in each apartment; the existing hall and stair devices, tested and repaired like for like or flagged for upgrade; and heat detection rather than smoke in a hot, dusty boiler room where the code allows it. Repairing with identical parts is service work. Adding a building system where none existed, or changing the panel, is a design change for a registered design professional and goes to the FDNY before installation. We tell the owner in writing which one the building needs before pricing anything.

Garden complexes built after the war: many entrances, one management office, one record

East Flatbush has some of Brooklyn's largest privately owned complexes. Flatbush Gardens, built in 1949, has 59 six-story brick buildings and about 2,500 apartments, and the Brooklyn Eagle reported more than 4,000 open violations there in October 2022, before a 40-year regulatory agreement with HPD in July 2023. Rutland Plaza on East 93rd Street has five buildings and 428 units. In complexes like these, the fire alarm questions are about records and consistency across dozens of stairs: which apartments got new detectors and when, which buildings have hall detection, and who tests the boiler rooms. When we are brought in, it is usually to replace detectors on a schedule or fix a specific panel problem, with a building-by-building device list the office can keep after the staff who ordered it move on.

New towers for seniors and supportive housing: engineered systems, and the all-electric exception

The newest buildings are going up on former hospital land. Brownstoner reported that Utica Crescent, at 832 Rutland Road, is two 12-story buildings with 322 apartments, 89 of them supportive units for older residents. 6sqft reported that Sparrow Square's first phase at 681 Clarkson Avenue will be two 10-story buildings with 261 apartments, built as an all-electric Passive House project. Neither report describes fire alarm equipment, so we make no claims about it.

In buildings like these, the base system was designed by an engineer, filed with the FDNY and accepted, and it belongs to the owner and its fire alarm company of record; anything that touches it goes through the managing agent. One detail matters for all-electric buildings: HPD says a building with no fossil-fuel burning devices and no enclosed parking is exempt from CO devices and notices, and the gas rule applies where there is gas piping. Residents there should not be surprised to find no gas detector in the kitchen.

Shops with families overhead: the store closes, the apartments do not

On Church, Utica, Linden and the Nostrand edge, the typical commercial building is two or three stories with a store below and apartments above. ABC7 reported a June 2025 fire in a three-story building at Church Avenue and East 31st Street that damaged Lenny's Pizza, the two floors above it and the roof; the FDNY secured lithium-ion batteries and recovered a charred e-bike, and the outlet said it was unknown whether it played a role. That is the shape of the risk: a business full of heat or batteries, closed overnight, under people who are asleep.

We plan the store and the apartments together. The store gets detection suited to the business, such as heat near ovens and smoke away from the cooking line, plus a way to warn the families upstairs or call for help when the shop is empty. The apartments keep their own HPD devices. Where a store with gas ovens or a loading area is protected by a building fire alarm, the FDNY's commercial CO rule applies too, as covered in the business section below.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Read the Ceiling, Count the Doorbells, Then Shut Every Door

Look up before anything else. In an older East Flatbush walk-up or two-family, the first thing the technician checks in each apartment is the ceiling around the existing detector. A brown ring, bubbled paint or a light fixture with rust on the trim means water has been coming through from above. A detector that has been wet is not one we trust, and the source of the leak may also be sitting near wiring. We note it, photograph it and tell the owner before we put a new device in the same spot.

Follow the gas from the meter. Next stop is the cellar, at the meters. From there we trace every gas line to what it feeds: the boiler, the water heaters, the dryer, each kitchen range, and anything somebody added later, such as a second range in a basement kitchen. Each appliance goes on the survey sheet by room and apartment, because from January 1, 2027 those are the rooms where rented units need a natural gas detector, and because carbon monoxide planning starts at the same appliances.

In a two- or three-family, we compare the households the owner describes with what we find: names on mailboxes, locks on interior doors, a hot plate in a basement room. When the count does not match, we say so, because detection planned for two households will miss the third.

Close every door and listen. Last, with every bedroom door shut and any window air conditioner or fan running, a second person sounds an alarm at the far end of the building while the technician stands in the farthest bedroom. If the alarm is not clearly audible there, the plan needs linking or another device, whatever the minimum would allow. In a brick row house with a cellar boiler and a third-floor bedroom, this test changes the plan more often than any other.

January 1, 2027

Gas Detectors in East Flatbush Rentals by January 1, 2027: What the City Has Said, and How to Get It Done in One Visit

The Department of Buildings' October 13, 2026 release on natural gas detectors says that under Local Law 102 of 2025, covered owners must have code-compliant natural gas alarms installed in every dwelling unit by January 1, 2027, and placed in each dwelling unit near gas appliances. It lists the building types it means as apartment buildings, private dwellings including one- and two-family homes, hotels and rooming houses, and single room occupancy buildings, where the building has gas piping. HPD's own detector page describes the requirement for multiple dwellings and for one- and two-family homes the owner does not occupy. As of this page's review, the deadline is about seven weeks away.

What counts. The release says hardwired, plug-in and battery-powered detectors are all permitted, that the city does not endorse brands, and that detectors must be listed and labeled to UL 1484 or UL 2075. It names a partial list of battery models identified as UL 1484 listed and compliant, and says buyers should confirm the listing. Smoke and CO alarms do not satisfy the gas requirement on their own; a combined device counts only if gas detection is one of its listed functions. HPD allows combination units, and caps what a Class A tenant can be charged for them.

Where it goes. HPD says the gas detector belongs near the fuel-gas appliance, and where a gas appliance is in a room used for sleeping, a detector is needed in that room. In an East Flatbush two-family, that usually means each apartment's kitchen, plus any room with a gas water heater or dryer. In a studio apartment, the kitchen and the sleeping room are often the same room, which we deal with in the questions below.

The release says DOB and National Grid are handing out a limited number of free detectors to small owners at outreach events, first come, first served, and that HPD will inspect for the detectors starting in 2027. A free detector still has to be mounted in the right place and recorded with its dates, which is the part that takes time in a building with tenants.

Getting into every apartment is the slowest part of detector work. If a unit's smoke alarm is near the end of its life or its CO alarm is missing, replace it in the same visit as the gas detector, with one notice and one record. We price smoke, CO and gas together, apartment by apartment, on our per-unit landlord rate of $400 to $800 per unit, devices, installation, testing and the dated list included.

Older Households

Planning Fire Detection Around East Flatbush's Older Residents and the Families Who Look After Them

Community Board 17's statement says the district "has a large senior population," served by only one senior center, at 3304 Clarendon Road, and asks for outreach so homebound older adults can age in place. Many houses we work in have a parent or grandparent who has lived there for decades, often up a long stair, sometimes alone, with family living elsewhere.

Three things change in the plan. First, reach: an alarm outside a bedroom must be clearly heard with the door shut, and for a deep sleeper or someone who takes out a hearing aid at night, that often means a linked alarm inside the bedroom too. Second, the route: we note which floor the person sleeps on and put a smoke alarm at the top of that stair. Third, who else finds out. A linked alarm warns the people in the house, not a son in Queens; where the family wants that, monitoring through our partners, $30 a month with a keypad or $45 with the phone app, sends the signal out of the house.

We also keep devices simple. New York State has required new and replacement residential smoke alarms sold since April 1, 2019 to have a sealed battery of at least ten years or be hardwired, and for an older resident living alone we would rather fit a sealed alarm than leave a battery change for someone to forget.

Compliance notes for East Flatbush businesses and buildings

  • The design comes first. The FDNY's tips guide for fire alarm approval says a New York State registered professional engineer or registered architect prepares the plans, which are filed with a TM-1 application and a valid Certificate of Occupancy, an approved Schedule A, or a Letter of No Objection from the Department of Buildings.
  • Plan on months, not weeks. The same guide says the initial review can take up to 8 weeks and estimates about twenty to twenty-four weeks from submission to final approval. Resubmitting within 6 months of an objection avoids a new filing fee.
  • Inspection has prerequisites. Before an inspection is requested on form B-45, the guide says the central station connection must be active, the monitoring agreement and an executed TB-60 must be on file, and the installation must be 100 percent complete. The Fire Alarm Inspection Unit is at 9 MetroTech Center in Brooklyn, and a technician holding an S97 or S98 Certificate of Fitness has to be present.
  • CO in the occupancies the FDNY lists. The FDNY's smoke and CO guidance says occupancies protected by a fire alarm system needed CO detectors by July 1, 2021 when they contain fuel-burning equipment, an enclosed parking garage or a loading dock. The groups include restaurants and bars, places of worship, barber and beauty shops, clinics, drug stores and markets, which covers much of what lines Church and Utica Avenues.
  • Detectors stay detectors. The same guidance says detectors in commercial properties must remain detectors, may not be converted to single-station alarms, are installed by DOB-licensed electrical contractors and are programmed by an S-97 or S-98 holder employed by an approved fire alarm company. Smoke detectors may also be maintained by F-78 or S-78 holders.
  • Nuisance alarms have consequences. The FDNY says it is unlawful for a monitored system to transmit two or more unwarranted alarms in any three-month period, and lists cooking particles, dust, heavy steam, ceiling paint, CO detectors mounted too close to appliances and vapors from paints, aerosols and strong cleaners as common causes.

Your Questions Answered

Fire Alarm Installation in East Flatbush: Questions Answered

These are the situations East Flatbush owners, tenants, heirs, pastors and shopkeepers bring to us. Each is answered in full here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Apartment buildings on the side streets

Water from the apartment upstairs has been coming through our ceiling for months, right beside the hallway smoke detector and a light fixture. Can a detector that has been wet still be trusted?

No. A smoke detector that has taken water may still sound on the test button, which checks the horn and circuit, while its sensing chamber no longer works properly. Replace it, and do not mount the new one under the same wet patch until the leak is fixed. The larger issue is what the water is near. The family whose Lenox Road apartment burned in April 2025 told News 12 they had reported water damage from the unit above for months and believed it may have contributed; no official cause was reported, but water around a light fixture or outlet is a reason to call an electrician. Tenants should report the leak and the wet detector to the owner in writing and, if nothing happens, through 311, which JustFix notes is how HPD learns about a condition. Owners should fix the leak, have the fixture checked, then replace the detector and record the date. We replace the device; plumbing and wiring belong to the plumber and electrician.

A fire gutted one apartment in our six-story building, but every other tenant stayed. What should be checked in the apartments that did not burn?

More than most owners expect. Start with the fire floor and the floors above it, since smoke travels up stairs and shafts. In each apartment there, check that the smoke and CO detectors are present, working and free of soot; a detector that sat in heavy smoke can pass the test button with a fouled sensor, so replace any that were exposed. Then ask the question the Kingston Avenue fire raised, where some neighbors told News 12 they heard no alarms: what hall and stair detection does the building have, is it part of a filed system, and did it sound? A panel's history log may show what happened. If there is no hall detection, that is a design question for a registered engineer. We can survey every apartment and common area, replace in-unit devices, test the existing hall system and give you a written list, apartment by apartment.

Our studio apartments have the gas range in the same room the tenant sleeps in. Where do the smoke, CO and gas detectors go when it is all one room?

In that room, spaced so each device stays useful. HPD says that where a fuel-gas appliance is in a room used for sleeping, a natural gas detector is needed in that room, so a studio's gas detector goes there, near the range as its instructions direct but not over the burners. The smoke detector still sits within fifteen feet of the entrance to the sleeping area, usually on the ceiling between the bed and the door and as far from the range as the layout allows. CO detection goes in every unit of a building with a fossil-fuel burning device, so the studio needs it too, away from the stove. A listed combination device can cover several functions in one spot only if every function belongs in that spot. In a building of studios we draw one plan per layout and repeat it, which keeps notices and records consistent.

Houses, heirs and new owners

We inherited our parents' two-family off Church Avenue. The estate is still in probate, and a tenant lives upstairs. Who carries the detector duties until it is settled?

In practice, whoever manages the house for the estate, usually the executor or administrator. HPD's rules attach to the building's owner and do not pause while ownership is sorted out. For a rented two-family with no owner on site, that means CO detection wherever there is a fossil-fuel burning device, gas detection from January 1, 2027 where there is gas piping, a notice to an adult in the tenant's apartment, dated records, and replacement of missing or dead devices before any new tenant moves in. Who signs for the estate and whether costs come from estate funds are questions for the estate's lawyer. We can make the house safe and documented now: survey both floors and the cellar, replace what is dead or missing, link the alarms so the tenant upstairs hears a fire below, and hand over a dated device list the eventual owner or a buyer can rely on.

We think our tenant has been renting rooms in the basement to other people without telling us. What are we responsible for while we sort it out?

For the safety of whoever sleeps in the building, starting now. The New York Avenue fire in March 2026 was in a home News 12 said had allegedly been converted into a rooming house, and the Department of Buildings said earlier complaints there were closed after inspectors were denied access twice. Whether the arrangement breaches the lease, whether the rooms can lawfully be occupied, and how to end it are questions for a housing lawyer and the Department of Buildings. The fire question cannot wait. Every room someone sleeps in needs smoke detection that would wake them, CO detection if the house has a boiler or gas appliances, and a link to the alarms upstairs so a fire on either level reaches the other. We will install that and tell you in writing that it does not make the rooms legal. If a room has one way out and no window large enough to escape through, nobody should sleep there.

We bought a two-family at a foreclosure sale without seeing inside. What alarm work should happen in the first week we own it?

Assume nothing about what is on the ceilings. Community Board 17 says East Flatbush leads Brooklyn in foreclosures, at 10.5 per 1,000 family and condo properties, and reports vacant houses that attract squatting, so houses here often change hands with no records and sometimes with people still inside. In the first week, walk every floor and the cellar with a technician: which detectors exist, their dates, whether any were taken down or painted over, and whether gas and electric service are on. If anyone is living there, install working smoke and CO alarms in every sleeping area right away, before renovation starts, because removing occupants can take months. If the house is empty, plan detection with the renovation so linked or hardwired devices go in while ceilings are open, and get gas detection into each rented unit by January 1, 2027 if it will be rented by then. Keep the dated device list from day one.

Our landlord is replacing the detectors in our apartment, and we have a gas stove and a two-year-old. Is there anything else the owner owes us for fire safety in that kitchen?

Yes. JustFix notes that owners must provide stove knob covers to households with a gas stove and a child under six, and lists the owner's duty to provide and install smoke and CO detectors. From January 1, 2027, HPD adds a natural gas detector near the stove in rented apartments with gas piping. Ask the technician to place the smoke detector within fifteen feet of the bedroom doors and far enough from the stove that cooking does not set it off, because the detector that sounds at every meal is the one that gets taken down. Ask for the notice HPD requires owners to give an adult in each unit, which covers testing, maintenance and carbon monoxide. If the knob covers are still missing afterward, request them from the owner in writing.

The 2027 gas rule

The Department of Buildings' reminder lists one- and two-family homes, but HPD's page says homes the owner does not occupy. We live downstairs in our two-family and rent the top floor. Which apartments need a gas detector?

Plan for both. HPD's page describes the gas requirement for multiple dwellings and for one- and two-family homes the owner does not occupy, while the DOB release of October 13, 2026 lists private dwellings, including one- and two-family homes, among the covered types. The two do not read the same way for an owner-occupied two-family, and the release is a reminder, not the rule text. The rented apartment upstairs is the clear case: it needs a gas detector near its gas appliances by January 1, 2027, plus CO detection, the notice and the records. For your own floor, the cautious and safe reading is to install one as well; it costs little next to a gas leak in a house with a range on each floor and a boiler below. For the exact answer for your house, ask HPD. Either way, both apartments in one visit costs less than coming back.

The gas detector a store sold us is labeled UL 2075, not UL 1484. Does it count?

On the city's own guidance, yes. The Department of Buildings' October 2026 release says natural gas detectors must be listed and labeled to UL 1484 or UL 2075, that hardwired, plug-in and battery models are all permitted, and that the city endorses no brands. Its sample list of battery models names UL 1484 units, which is why many owners think that is the only listing, but the release names both. Before mounting, check that the device is actually a natural gas or methane detector, that the listing mark is printed on the device or its label, and what the manufacturer says about mounting height and distance from the appliance, since natural gas is lighter than air. Record its install and end-of-life dates. If you bought several, we can install them as a customer-supplied kit at our labor-only rate.

Stores, churches and clinics

A handyman replaced the dead detectors on our store's fire alarm with battery smoke alarms from the hardware store. Is that allowed?

No, and it can bring enforcement. The FDNY's smoke and CO guidance says detectors in commercial properties must remain detectors, and converting them to single-station alarms without connecting them to the required fire alarm system may result in FDNY enforcement. A detector on the panel sounds the building's notification and, if monitored, calls for help; a battery alarm on the ceiling only beeps in its own room. The same guidance says required detectors are installed by DOB-licensed electrical contractors and programmed by an S-97 or S-98 holder employed by an approved fire alarm company. To put the store right, the battery alarms come down, compatible detectors go back on the existing circuits as a like-for-like repair, and the panel is tested so every device reports. Anything that changes the system's design goes through a design professional and the FDNY.

Our church hall on Church Avenue hosts repasts after funerals, with trays kept warm over chafing-dish burners. Will that set off the detectors, and what keeps protection on?

It can, and the answer is planning the room, not covering the devices. The FDNY lists cooking particles and heavy steam among common causes of unwarranted smoke detector alarms, and a table of chafing dishes puts out both near the ceiling of a low church hall. If the hall's detection is on a monitored system, the FDNY says two or more unwarranted alarms in any three-month period are unlawful. Start with where the warming happens: a serving area at one end of the hall, away from the nearest smoke detector and out of the return air path. A heat detector may suit a spot over a serving area better, but changing a device type on a filed system is a question for the system's engineer. What should never happen is a volunteer taping a bag over a detector for the afternoon. A hall full of mourners is exactly when detection matters.

Our medical practice is moving into a storefront near Kings County Hospital that used to be a shop. Does the change of use mean the fire alarm has to be redesigned?

Probably, and it starts with paperwork rather than the ceiling. The FDNY's tips guide says a fire alarm filing needs a valid Certificate of Occupancy, an approved Schedule A or a Letter of No Objection from the Department of Buildings, so the building's legal use has to line up with a medical office before the FDNY reviews alarm plans. A design professional decides whether the space needs a system and of what type. If it has one, the FDNY's CO rule reaches clinics protected by a fire alarm system that have fuel-burning equipment, an enclosed garage or a loading dock, and those CO detectors connect to the panel. The guide estimates twenty to twenty-four weeks from submission to final approval, so the alarm belongs at the start of the build-out. Our part is installing, labeling and pre-testing to the approved drawings.

What AI Gets Wrong About Fire Alarm Installation in East Flatbush

AI answers and national guides from Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr are written for an average American house or office. These are the shortcuts that mislead East Flatbush owners most often.

"A fire alarm system costs $1 to $7 per square foot"

That range comes from commercial cost guides. Safe and Sound Security's 2026 guide, for example, gives $1 to $7 per square foot for a standard commercial system and $4 to $12 for retrofits.

Most East Flatbush jobs are not priced by area at all. A rented two-family needs a set of household detectors per apartment, and the count of bedrooms, kitchens and gas appliances drives the cost.

That is why our list shows per-unit and whole-house prices, from $400 to $800 per apartment for landlords and $2,500 for a whole house. A square-foot figure applied to a two-family would mislead in both directions, and the same guide's per-device prices assume a commercial panel that most houses here do not have.

"Smoke alarms are the tenant's responsibility"

Tenants have duties: HPD says they test detectors monthly and replace any that go missing during their occupancy.

But the owner provides and installs the devices, gives the notice, keeps the dates, and replaces devices before a new tenant moves in. JustFix adds that the owner stays responsible for installation whoever pays.

In a neighborhood where roughly two households in three rent, an owner who leaves detectors to tenants is the one who answers to HPD, and from 2027 HPD says its inspectors will be checking for gas detectors as well.

"A combination smoke and CO alarm covers the 2027 rule"

It does not. Carbon monoxide comes from fuel burning; natural gas is the fuel leaking before it burns, and a CO sensor is not built to catch it.

The DOB release says gas detectors must be listed to UL 1484 or UL 2075. A combined device counts only if gas is one of its listed functions.

East Flatbush two-families usually have a gas range on each floor and a gas boiler below, so this is not a technicality here.

"Any building with apartments needs a full fire alarm panel"

Many small buildings do not. The 2022 NYC Building Code, as published by UpCodes, does not require a manual fire alarm system in buildings of two stories or fewer where each unit is separated by one-hour fire partitions and has a direct exit.

What every rented home does need are HPD's detectors. Selling a panel to a two-family that does not need one wastes money.

Missing the cases that do need a system, such as a clinic or church moving into a storefront, is worse. We sort that out before pricing.

"Swap a dead store detector for any smoke alarm"

Not on a commercial fire alarm. The FDNY says commercial detectors must remain detectors and may not be converted to single-station alarms.

A battery alarm on a store ceiling beeps in one room. A detector on the panel sounds the building and, if monitored, calls for help.

For a shop with apartments above it on Church or Utica Avenue, that difference decides whether the families upstairs are warned. The FDNY says such conversions may bring enforcement action, so the shortcut can cost more than the repair.

"FDNY approval takes a couple of weeks"

The FDNY's tips guide says the initial plan review alone can take up to 8 weeks and estimates twenty to twenty-four weeks from submission to final approval.

Monitoring must be active and the installation complete before the inspection can be requested.

A clinic or congregation planning to open in a converted storefront near the hospitals or on Church Avenue should put the alarm design at the front of the schedule, months before the planned opening, not weeks.

"Old battery alarms must all be torn out under the new law"

Not exactly. New York State requires new and replacement residential smoke alarms sold since April 1, 2019 to be sealed ten-year or hardwired, but existing code-compliant replaceable-battery alarms do not have to be removed.

In multiple dwellings, the city's Local Law 112 of 2013 requires owners replacing expired battery alarms to use sealed ten-year alarms with an end-of-life signal.

So the real rule is replacement by date and condition, not a blanket swap. Our device list shows which alarms are due, and we replace the expired ones first rather than charging to tear out alarms that still meet the code.

FAQ

East Flatbush Fire Alarm Installation FAQ

We rent out the upstairs of our two-family. What can we charge the tenant for the new CO and gas detectors?
+

HPD's page says tenants of private one- and two-family dwellings reimburse the owner $25 only for each carbon monoxide or natural gas detector, and the $50 and $75 combined-device amounts are written for Class A multiple dwellings. Charging is optional, the owner stays responsible for installing the devices either way, and JustFix notes that unpaid detector charges cannot lead to eviction.

How do you count how many detectors a rented two-family needs before pricing it?
+

We count by apartment: a smoke detector within fifteen feet of each bedroom entrance, CO detection in each unit when the house has a boiler or other fuel-burning device, and a gas detector near each gas appliance. Then we add the shared stair, the front hall and the cellar. A typical count comes from the bedrooms and gas appliances, not the square footage.

What decides whether an apartment lands near $400 or near $800 on your landlord rate, and are the detectors included?
+

The devices, installation, testing and dated record are included. The price within the range depends on how many bedrooms the apartment has, how many gas appliances need their own detector, whether combination units fit the layout, and whether any device has to be hardwired or linked to the shared stair. A one-bedroom with one gas range sits low; a large apartment with several sleeping rooms sits higher.

Our carbon monoxide alarm sounded right after we cleaned the kitchen with a strong spray. Could the cleaner have set it off?
+

It is possible. The FDNY lists vapors from paints, adhesives, aerosols and strong-odor cleaners among the common causes of CO detector nuisance alarms. Treat every CO alarm as real anyway: get everyone out, call 911 from outside, and let the FDNY check the air. If it keeps happening after cleaning, we can move the detector away from where sprays are used.

Should the gas detector in a tenant's kitchen be battery, plug-in or hardwired?
+

The Department of Buildings says all three are permitted if the device is listed to UL 1484 or UL 2075. Battery models are the easiest to place in the right spot. Plug-in models depend on an outlet in the right place and can be unplugged. Hardwired models suit a renovation, when an electrician is already working in the kitchen.

The boiler room in our walk-up stays locked, and only the super has a key. How does the detector in there get tested?
+

We schedule the boiler room with the super on the same visit as the apartments, test the device in place and record the date on the device list. A locked room is a good place for a sealed or panel-connected device, since nobody passes it daily. If the super changes, the next one should get the key and the record together.

Our community board asked the city to fund smoke and CO detectors. Should we wait for a giveaway before replacing ours?
+

No. Community Board 17's budget statement lists detectors among its funding requests, but a request is not a program, and HPD's duties apply now. The Department of Buildings and National Grid have handed out a limited number of free gas detectors at outreach events, first come, first served. If you get free devices, we can install them at our labor-only rate.

Our cousins live in our second house and pay no rent. Is it still a home the owner does not occupy under HPD's rules?
+

HPD's page frames the CO and gas rules around whether the owner occupies the home, not whether rent is paid. If you do not live in the house, plan it as a home the owner does not occupy, with CO and gas detection where the rules call for them. For a firm answer on your situation, ask HPD directly.

HPD's rules talk about a fossil-fuel burning device. Which things in an East Flatbush house count?
+

Anything that burns gas or oil: the boiler or furnace, a gas or oil water heater, a gas range, a gas dryer and a gas fireplace. One such device anywhere in a multiple dwelling or rented house brings CO detection into every unit. HPD exempts a building with no fossil-fuel devices and no enclosed parking from CO devices and notices.

Our barbershop on Utica Avenue has a building fire alarm and a gas water heater in the back. Do we need CO detectors on that system?
+

Very likely. The FDNY lists barber and beauty shops among the Group B occupancies whose fire alarm-protected spaces needed CO detectors by July 1, 2021 when they contain fuel-burning equipment, and a gas water heater counts. Those detectors connect to the fire alarm system and are programmed by an S-97 or S-98 holder. Without a fire alarm system, that rule does not apply as written, but a CO alarm near the heater is still sensible.
