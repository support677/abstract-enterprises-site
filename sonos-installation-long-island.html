The Neighborhood

Why Long Island Homes Need Sonos Installation

Sonos was designed for houses with more than one room worth listening in, and Long Island is mostly houses. Across Nassau and Suffolk, roughly eight in ten homes are lived in by the people who own them, according to the Census Bureau, and the typical household is close to three people. That means a family room that doubles as a movie theater, a kitchen where someone always wants the radio on, kids' rooms, a home office, a finished basement and, from May to September, a yard. One speaker can't serve all of that. A properly planned Sonos system can, and it is the reason Long Islanders ask us about Sonos more than any other audio brand.

The catch is that a Sonos system is only as good as the house it sits in and the network it rides on. A single Era 100 on the kitchen counter works out of the box. Eight zones across two floors, a basement and a pool deck is a different job: it needs a plan for which rooms get their own volume, where the wired speakers go, where the amplifiers live, how power reaches the surrounds behind a sectional, and, most of all, whether the Wi-Fi actually covers the far end of a 3,000-square-foot colonial and the back fence. Most of the calls we get about Sonos on Long Island aren't about the speakers at all. They are about speakers that go silent in the basement, a sub that drops off when the microwave runs, or a patio pair that works until someone closes the slider.

The houses shape the answer. A center-hall colonial with a full basement makes wire easy to run: drop it through the floor behind the TV and you are in the joists. A high ranch puts the family room half below grade beside the garage, so the theater and the living room sit on different floors. A Cape or a ranch that grew a dormer, a rear extension or a second storey over the years hides older framing behind newer drywall, and sometimes a set of ceiling speakers a previous owner installed and never connected. And the new construction going up across both counties is the one moment when speaker wire, network cable and the equipment closet cost a fraction of what they will after the drywall is taped.

Then there is outdoors. Decks, patios, pergolas and in-ground pools are part of how Long Island lives in summer, and a Bluetooth speaker on the table is how most of them are served. Sonos's own outdoor speakers, designed to be powered by Sonos Amp, are built for weather; the work is in getting wire from the house to the speaker, keeping power where the electrical code wants it near water, and making sure the network reaches the yard.

That is the work we do across Nassau and Suffolk: planning zones for the way a family moves through the house, installing the speakers and wiring cleanly, fixing the network the system depends on, connecting Sonos to the TV, the alarm app and the smart-home controls already in the house, and pricing every step up front.

Building Specialists

Sonos Installation for Long Island's Architecture

The same set of Sonos speakers goes into a high ranch, a colonial and a new build in three very different ways. What matters for Sonos is not the style on the outside but four things on the inside: where wire can travel, where the router sits, how far the farthest zone is from it, and which rooms are over, under or beside each other.

Center-Hall Colonials

Two floors over a full basement, family room off the kitchen, bedrooms upstairs. The basement is a wiring highway to the first floor; the second floor is reached through closets stacked above each other or the attic. The router is often in a corner office, far from the primary suite.

High Ranches and Splits

Living room, kitchen and bedrooms upstairs; family room, laundry and garage on the lower level, partly below grade. The theater usually lives downstairs, the music upstairs, and the slab-on-grade lower floor limits which walls wire can run through.

Expanded Capes and Ranches

Post-war houses that gained dormers, rear extensions and second storeys over the decades. Old and new framing meet behind the drywall, attic access is often over the original section only, and Wi-Fi has to cross what used to be an exterior wall.

Newer Colonials and Custom Builds

Two-storey foyers and great rooms, open kitchens, mudrooms and bonus rooms over the garage. Large footprints that one router can't cover, and ceilings that suit in-ceiling speakers better than anything on a shelf.

New Construction in Progress

Framing open, no insulation yet: the window to run speaker wire, Ethernet and a home run to the equipment closet, and to set rough-in brackets for ceiling speakers so the drywall crew cuts around them.

Houses With Pools and Big Yards

Decks off the kitchen, patios below, pools beyond. Outdoor zones that need wire through a rim joist or along the foundation, and a network that reaches past the back wall of the house.

Methods by house type and floor

Basement to first floor. In a house with an unfinished or drop-ceiling basement, wire for a family-room theater or kitchen ceiling speakers comes up through the floor plate from below. We drill the plate inside the wall cavity, not the floor in the room, and support cable on hooks or straps across the joists rather than leaving it loose on the ceiling tiles. Where the basement ceiling is finished drywall, a few small access holes at the joists let us fish across, and we tell you before we make them.

First floor to second floor. Second-floor rooms are reached through a stacked closet, a chase beside the chimney flue's framing, a plumbing wall or the attic. In a colonial the attic over the second floor is often the easiest route to bedroom and primary-suite ceilings; we come down from above, which avoids opening finished walls downstairs.

Attic and bonus rooms. Converted attics and rooms over the garage tend to have knee walls with accessible space behind them. Wire runs behind the knee wall and across, and insulation is put back the way we found it. These rooms often sit at the edge of Wi-Fi coverage, so an Ethernet run to a speaker or a mesh node is part of the plan.

Slab floors and lower levels. A high ranch's lower level and many ranch additions sit on a slab, so wire can't come up from below. Routes go through the wall from the floor above, along the ceiling in a soffit, or in a paintable raceway along the trim where nothing else works.

New construction. Before insulation we run 16-gauge or heavier speaker wire to each speaker location, Ethernet to the TV walls and the farthest rooms, and everything back to one closet or basement wall where the Amps and network switch will sit. Each run is labelled at both ends and tested before the walls close.

When Sonos Is the Answer

Signs a Long Island House Is Ready for Whole-Home Sound

Nobody wakes up wanting an amplifier. They want the music to follow them, the film to sound like a film and the app to stop arguing with them. These are the situations that most often bring a Long Island family to a planned Sonos system rather than another speaker on a shelf.

  • Four speakers, four apps. A smart speaker in the kitchen, a Bluetooth box on the deck, a soundbar from a different brand and a radio in the garage. None of them play together, and each one needs its own phone pairing. One system with zones replaces all of it.
  • The kitchen is louder than the music. Range hood, dishwasher and an open plan that carries every conversation. A pair of ceiling speakers spreads sound across the room instead of blasting it from one corner of the counter.
  • The basement is where everyone actually watches. The family room upstairs has the nicer TV, but the kids, the game console and the sectional are downstairs. That room deserves the theater.
  • Summer means a speaker on the patio table. It needs charging, it gets knocked into the pool, and nobody can hear it past the second row of chairs. Fixed outdoor speakers on an Amp are louder, weather-rated and always there.
  • Speakers in the ceiling that nobody uses. The builder or the last owner put them in, and the wire disappears into a wall. They can almost always be brought back to life.
  • A new build or a gut renovation on the calendar. The walls are about to be open. This is the one time wire is cheap, and the most common regret we hear is not running it.

If one of these sounds like your house, a list of rooms and the products you already own is enough for us to start. If a room is better served by a single portable speaker than by wire in the ceiling, we'll say so.

Your Questions Answered

Sonos Installation on Long Island: Questions Answered

These are the questions Long Island homeowners ask when they move from one Sonos speaker to a system. They are grouped by the decision behind them; short questions about price, timing and policies are in the FAQ further down.

Zoning a whole house

How do we decide which rooms of a colonial get their own Sonos zone and which ones share?

Start from what happens at the same time. A zone is any area that needs its own source and volume, so the test is simple: will two rooms ever want different things playing at once? The kitchen and the family room in an open plan almost never do, so they share. The primary suite and the kitchen often do on a Saturday morning, so they're separate. The basement nearly always wants its own, because that's where the game or the film is on while the rest of the house has music.

In a typical four-bedroom colonial that usually lands at five to seven zones: family room theater, kitchen and breakfast area, dining or living room, primary suite, basement, and one outdoor zone, with kids' rooms and the office as single speakers that can join any group. Sonos lets you group and ungroup zones from the app at any time, so the plan doesn't have to predict every weekend; it just has to put the boundaries in sensible places so no zone fights another.

Can one Sonos Amp run the kitchen and the dining room as separate zones?

No. One Amp is one zone: everything wired to it plays the same thing at the same volume. Sonos's own guide to ceiling speakers says a single Amp can reliably power up to three pairs of Sonos Architectural speakers wired in parallel, so an Amp can cover a long kitchen and breakfast area, or a great room, as one space. If the dining room needs its own volume, it needs its own Amp or its own self-powered speaker.

There is one more rule worth knowing before you buy: to get Sonos's speaker tuning and Trueplay on an Amp, every speaker connected to it must be the same size. Mixing a large and a small in-ceiling model on one Amp works, but loses the optimisation. When we plan a floor, we group speakers by room and by model so the Amp count matches the zone count.

Will music stay in sync when the whole first floor is grouped for a party?

Yes, when the network is healthy. Sonos is built to play the same stream in time across rooms, and grouping the kitchen, family room, dining room and deck is the normal party setup. What breaks sync is a weak link: one speaker on the edge of the Wi-Fi drops and rejoins, and the group stutters.

So the party test is part of our hand-over. We group every zone, play the same track, walk the house and the yard, and watch for any room that lags or drops. If one does, we fix the network path to that room before we leave rather than leaving you to discover it with forty guests in the backyard.

Finished basements and attic rooms

Should the Arc and Sub go in the finished basement or the main-floor family room?

Whichever room gets used for films, and in a lot of Long Island houses that is the basement. It's darker, it's away from the bedrooms on the second floor, the sectional is bigger and the game consoles already live there. It's also the easiest room to wire, because the ceiling over it is the floor of the house and the walls were framed recently.

The upstairs family room often has the fireplace, the windows and the open kitchen, which make it a better music room than a cinema. A common split is an Arc Ultra or Beam with a Sub and surrounds in the basement, and a Beam or a pair of ceiling speakers on an Amp in the family room. Both join the rest of the house for music, and the theater gets the room where it sounds best.

Our basement has a drop ceiling — can Sonos in-ceiling speakers go into the tiles?

They can go into the ceiling, but not supported by the tile alone. A suspended ceiling tile won't hold a speaker's weight for long, so the speaker is fitted with a support bracket or tile bridge that spreads the load onto the grid, and in some cases a safety wire to the structure above. The cut-out is made in a new tile, which is cheaper than patching drywall.

Two other things matter in a drop ceiling. The space above is open to the rest of the basement ceiling, so speakers sound thinner without a back box or enclosure; we'll tell you when one is worth it. And the cable is supported above the grid on its own hangers rather than lying on the tiles, so the next person who lifts a tile to reach a pipe doesn't pull a speaker wire with it.

The converted attic bedroom never gets Wi-Fi — what's the fix before adding a speaker there?

First, find out how bad it is. We measure the signal in the room from the speaker's actual position, because the bed corner and the dormer window can differ a lot. Attic rooms are often separated from the router by two floors, a run of ductwork and a knee wall packed with insulation with a foil facing, any of which can eat the signal.

Then pick the cheapest fix that works. Sometimes moving a mesh node to the hallway below is enough. Sometimes an Ethernet run up through a closet to the attic, to a node or straight to the Sonos speaker, is the reliable answer. What Sonos itself says not to use is a plug-in wireless range extender; its system requirements list those as unsupported, and they cause exactly the dropouts people are trying to cure.

Patios, decks and pools

What actually survives a Long Island summer and winter outdoors — Sonos's outdoor pair or a Move?

For speakers that stay up all year, the wired outdoor pair. Sonos's outdoor speakers by Sonance are rated IP66 and tested for humidity, salt spray, temperature and UV, with an operating range Sonos lists as minus 13 to 149°F. They are passive speakers driven by a Sonos Amp kept indoors, which means no electronics outside to fail in a January freeze.

A portable Sonos speaker is the right answer for a table you carry outside on a nice evening and bring in afterwards. It isn't a fixed installation, and it shouldn't live on the deck from Memorial Day to Labor Day. Many families end up with both: fixed speakers for the deck and pool area, and a portable one for the end of the dock or the beach.

How far from the pool can the outdoor speakers and their power go?

The speakers themselves carry low-voltage speaker wire from an Amp inside the house, so the question is really about electricity. The residential electrical code's pool section keeps general receptacles at least six feet from the inside wall of a pool, and requires a protected receptacle within a set distance for in-ground pools. That governs where an electrician can put an outlet, not where we can put a passive speaker.

Because the Amp lives indoors, outdoor speakers around a pool need no outlet at all near the water. We mount them on the house, a pergola post or a fence line aimed back at the seating, run the wire through the rim joist or along the foundation, and keep every powered device inside or on a properly protected outdoor receptacle installed by an electrician.

Will patio speakers bother the neighbors on a quarter-acre lot?

They don't have to. Sound carries further than people expect on a still summer evening across open yards, so outdoor speakers are aimed at the seating and away from the property line, mounted under an eave where possible, and set with a sensible volume ceiling. Several smaller speakers at moderate volume cover a patio far better than two loud ones, and they are much kinder to the house next door.

Sonos also lets you cap the maximum volume of a zone in the app. On an outdoor zone we set that cap with you during hand-over, so a teenager's playlist at 11 PM can't go past the level you agreed was fine with the neighbors.

New construction and renovation pre-wire

Building on Long Island this year — what should the electrician's walkthrough include for Sonos?

Bring the speaker plan to the pre-drywall walkthrough, not the electrician's alone. Decide where each pair of ceiling speakers goes, where every TV will hang, where the outdoor speakers will mount and where the equipment will live. Then make sure the low-voltage contractor runs speaker wire to each location, Ethernet to each TV and to the far rooms, and a home run for each zone back to the equipment spot.

The electrician's part is power: a dedicated outlet at the equipment shelf or rack, outlets behind the TVs and wherever a Sub or surrounds will sit, and protected outdoor receptacles for anything powered outside. With both done before insulation, finishing the system later is mostly fitting speakers and plugging in Amps.

Where should the Sonos Amps live in a new house — a closet, the basement or a rack?

Somewhere central, ventilated and near the network. The Sonos Amp's documentation asks for at least three inches of clearance on all sides when it sits on a shelf, and says two Amps fit side by side in a 2U rack space and four in 3U. A basement wall with a small rack, or a ventilated closet near the router, works well for most houses.

Avoid a shut closet with no airflow, the attic and the garage. Each Amp also needs a network path: the Amp has two Ethernet ports, so a small switch in the same spot lets every Amp be wired, which is the most reliable way to run a multiroom system with several Amps.

The family-room home theater

Under a 65-inch TV in an open-plan family room, does an Arc Ultra beat a Beam plus a Sub?

It comes down to the room and the TV. Arc Ultra is about 46.4 inches wide and 13 lb, with 14 drivers and Dolby Atmos; Sonos describes it as built for large rooms and TVs. Under a 65-inch or larger screen in an open family room it is the natural choice, and it can pair with two Subs. A Beam is shorter and suits a smaller den, a bedroom or a TV under 55 inches, and it pairs with a Sub and surrounds just the same.

Before choosing, measure the TV's width and the wall or console below it, and check how the room opens to the kitchen. In an open plan, the Sub matters as much as the bar, because it lets the bar run cleanly without straining. We'll look at the room and tell you honestly when the smaller bar plus a Sub sounds better than the bigger bar alone.

Where do the Sub and surrounds go when the sectional floats in the middle of the room?

Sonos's surround guidance is to place the speakers behind and to the left and right of the usual listening position, and both surrounds must be the same model in the same orientation. With a sectional in the middle of the room, that means stands or wall mounts behind it, each with power nearby, since Sonos surrounds are wireless for audio but still plug in.

The Sub 4 can stand upright or lie on its side under a sofa, according to Sonos, which helps in a family room with no spare corner. We try two or three positions, run Trueplay and leave it where the bass is even across the seats, not where it's loudest in one. Where floor outlets don't exist, we route cords neatly to the nearest wall outlet or recommend an electrician add one.

Can ceiling speakers on an Amp be the rear surrounds instead of two Era speakers?

Yes, with a second Amp. Sonos's surround guidelines describe using one Amp for front speakers and a second Amp to drive the surrounds, and note that the surround Amp can't be networked on its own; it works alongside the main home-theater product. In a family room where the ceiling over the sofa is flat and accessible from the floor above, in-ceiling surrounds keep the floor clear.

If the room above is finished and the ceiling can't be reached, Era 100s on stands or wall mounts are the simpler route. We'll price both and show you where each would go before you decide.

Wi-Fi across a larger footprint

Our mesh system covers the house — why does Sonos still drop in the far rooms?

Usually because of how the mesh is arranged, not because Sonos and mesh don't mix. Sonos recommends a Wi-Fi mesh router when you need to extend coverage, and when a Sonos product is wired it says that product must connect to the primary mesh node. A wired speaker plugged into a satellite node, or a satellite placed at the edge of the old router's range instead of halfway, are the two layouts we fix most often.

The other common cause is a setting. Sonos lists guest networks, networks behind a login page and 5 GHz-only routers as unsupported, and all products need to be on the same subnet as the phone running the app. A new router from the cable company with band steering or client isolation switched on can quietly break things that worked the week before.

In a five-to-eight-zone house, which Sonos products should be plugged into the network by cable?

Sonos's own system requirements recommend one or more Sonos products wired to the router by Ethernet at all times for a wired setup, and require the router to support Spanning Tree Protocol when that's done. In a house with several Amps, a Port or a Sub near the network equipment, wiring them is simple and makes the whole system steadier.

It isn't mandatory for a small system on good Wi-Fi. For a five-to-eight-zone Long Island house with a basement and an outdoor zone, we wire whatever can sensibly be wired at the equipment location, and let the room speakers run on Wi-Fi from properly placed mesh nodes.

Can the backyard zone run on Wi-Fi from a router at the front of the house?

Sometimes, but plan to check it. A router in a front office has to send its signal through the whole house, often including a kitchen full of appliances and an exterior wall, before it reaches a speaker by the pool. We measure at the deck and at the far edge of the patio before mounting anything outside.

The good news with fixed outdoor speakers is that the Wi-Fi only has to reach the Amp, and the Amp sits indoors near the back of the house or in the basement. Put the Amp on Ethernet or near a mesh node and the outdoor zone is as reliable as any room inside.

Alarm and smart-home integration

Can the alarm system's app control the Sonos speakers?

If the alarm runs on Alarm.com, yes. Alarm.com's installation guide says Sonos speakers can be added to an Alarm.com account so that users can play audio from their Sonos favorites, play and pause, change the volume and mute from the Alarm.com app, and include audio in Scenes. Fuller control, such as grouping speakers and choosing sources, still opens the Sonos app from inside the Alarm.com app.

Two limits are worth knowing: an Alarm.com account supports one Sonos household at a time, and music has to be saved to My Sonos before it can be played from Alarm.com. We set the favorites up with you so the Scenes you want, such as music on in the kitchen when the system is disarmed in the morning, actually have something to play.

Which voice assistants work with a Sonos system in our house?

On current Sonos products such as Arc Ultra, Sonos lists Amazon Alexa and its own Sonos Voice Control alongside the app and the touch controls, plus Apple AirPlay 2 for anyone on an iPhone. We set up voice per room, so asking the kitchen speaker for music plays in the kitchen and not in the basement.

If your household is invested in another ecosystem, check the specific Sonos model before you buy, because voice assistant support has changed over time and isn't the same on every product. We'll confirm compatibility for the exact models on your list during the quote.

Our house was pre-wired with ceiling speakers that were never connected — can they join Sonos?

In most cases, yes. Houses that change hands sometimes come with ceiling speakers and a bundle of unlabelled wire in a closet or the basement; one Long Island homeowner on Reddit's r/Ubiquiti described powering a set of in-ceiling speakers the original owners had installed and never hooked up. We use a tone generator and probe to match each wire to its speaker, test each run for shorts and continuity, and check the speakers' impedance before connecting anything.

The Sonos Amp drives a minimum of 4 ohms, according to its product guide, so we confirm the speakers' rating first; two pairs of 8-ohm speakers can be wired in parallel on one Amp. Once each run is identified, the old speakers become Sonos zones on an Amp at the equipment location, often for far less than new speakers would cost.

Plain-English Glossary

Sonos Terms You Will See on a Box, an App or a Quote

  • Zone (room). Any area with its own source and volume. In the Sonos app each room is a zone; zones can be grouped to play together.
  • Group. Two or more zones playing the same thing in sync, each with its own volume. The usual party setup.
  • Household. Every Sonos product on one system and one account. Sonos says up to 32 products can be added to a single system.
  • Home theater product. The soundbar or Amp connected to the TV, which the Sub and surrounds are bonded to.
  • Surrounds. A matched pair of speakers behind the seats, bonded to the home theater product. AirPlay, Bluetooth and line-in are disabled on speakers used as surrounds.
  • Passive vs active. Passive speakers (in-ceiling, outdoor) need an amplifier such as Sonos Amp. Active speakers (Era, Arc, Sub) have their own amplifiers and plug into the wall.
  • Wired in parallel. How several speakers share one Amp channel. The total load must stay at or above the Amp's 4-ohm minimum.
  • Trueplay. Sonos's room tuning, using a phone's microphone (advanced, iOS) or the speaker's own microphone (quick tuning on supported models).
  • Mesh router. A Wi-Fi system with several nodes that share one network name. Sonos recommends mesh for extending coverage; wired Sonos products connect to the primary node.
  • Range extender. A plug-in repeater that creates a second network. Sonos lists these as unsupported.
  • Spanning Tree Protocol (STP). A router feature that stops network loops. Sonos requires it when products are wired.

Network First

Getting Wi-Fi to the Far Corners of a Long Island House

Long Island is almost fully connected — the Census Bureau puts broadband subscriptions at 94.8% of Nassau households and 93.2% in Suffolk — yet the most common Sonos complaint we hear is dropouts. The internet line is fine; the problem is getting the signal from a router in the front office to the back deck, the basement and the bedroom over the garage. These are the rules we work to.

  • Find where the router actually is. In many houses it sits where the cable or fiber line enters, which is rarely the middle of the house. Moving it, or wiring a mesh node to a central spot, often helps more than any new product.
  • Use mesh, not extenders. Sonos recommends a mesh router to extend coverage and lists wireless range extenders and Ethernet-over-power adapters as unsupported. If either is in the house, it's the first suspect.
  • Wire the anchors. Amps, a Port and a Sub near the equipment location can be wired to a switch. Sonos recommends at least one product wired to the router for a wired setup, connected to the primary mesh node in a mesh network.
  • Check the router's settings. Guest networks, sign-in portals, WPA Enterprise and 5 GHz-only setups are all on Sonos's unsupported list, and the phones and speakers must be on the same subnet. STP has to be supported when Sonos products are wired.
  • Measure, don't guess. We test signal at the position each speaker will sit — including the deck and the far end of the patio — before anything is mounted.

Network fixes are billed as a setup-only visit, $225 for the first hour in Nassau and $250 in Suffolk. New Ethernet runs to a node or a speaker are quoted after we see the route; our network cable repair service on Long Island handles existing runs that have failed.

Before the Drywall

A Pre-Wire Checklist for Long Island New Builds and Renovations

Use this at the pre-drywall walkthrough with your builder or general contractor. Everything on it is far cheaper with the framing open than after the walls are finished.

  • Speaker wire to every ceiling location you might want, including rooms you aren't ready to fit yet. Label both ends and leave the far end coiled in a rough-in bracket or low-voltage box.
  • Outdoor runs to the deck, patio and pool area, brought through the rim joist or foundation and sealed. Sonos's outdoor speakers call for 16-gauge or heavier wire.
  • Ethernet to every TV wall, to the likely mesh-node spots on each floor, and to the equipment location.
  • A single equipment location — a basement wall or ventilated closet — where every speaker home run and network cable lands. Allow shelf or rack space for one Amp per wired zone with three inches of clearance around each.
  • Power from the electrician: a dedicated outlet at the equipment location, outlets behind each TV and where the Sub and surrounds will sit, and protected outdoor receptacles where anything outside will be powered, placed per the pool distances in the code.
  • Conduit or a pull string from the equipment location to the attic and to the TV walls, so future cables can be added without opening anything.

If your project is already past drywall, all is not lost. Basements, attics and stacked closets still give access to most rooms; the walkthrough tells us which.

Fixing Earlier Installs

Common Problems in Sonos Systems Someone Else Set Up

Plenty of Long Island houses already have Sonos — installed by a builder, a previous owner or a weekend of YouTube. When those systems misbehave, the cause is usually one of the eight below, and a single setup-only visit sorts most of them.

  • A range extender or powerline kit holding the system together. Both are on Sonos's unsupported list. Replacing them with properly placed mesh or an Ethernet run usually ends the dropouts.
  • Wired speaker on a satellite node. In a mesh, the wired Sonos product belongs on the primary node. Moving one cable fixes a surprising number of houses.
  • Mixed speakers on one Amp. Different sizes or models sharing an Amp, so Sonos's tuning is off. We re-group them by model or add an Amp.
  • Speaker load below 4 ohms. Too many speakers paralleled on one Amp. The Amp's guide sets 4 ohms as the minimum; we re-balance the zones.
  • Soundbar on the wrong HDMI port. Plugged into an ordinary input, not eARC, so sound drops or the TV remote can't control volume.
  • Surrounds that don't match. Sonos surrounds must be the same model and orientation. We pair them correctly or swap one.
  • Trueplay never run. Common in Android-only households, since advanced tuning needs iOS. We tune every supported zone.
  • An Amp shut in a closet with no air. Sonos asks for clearance on all sides. We move it to a ventilated shelf or rack.

Setup-only visits are $225 for the first hour in Nassau and $250 in Suffolk; rewiring or adding speakers is quoted in writing.

Field Notes

Field Notes From Long Island Sonos Walkthroughs

The order our technicians work in on a multiroom walkthrough in a Nassau or Suffolk house (general practice, not a single customer's job)

We start at the router, not the family room. Where the internet line comes in decides almost everything else: how far the farthest zone is, where mesh nodes have to go, and whether the equipment can sit near the network or needs its own run. A quick signal reading in the basement, the primary suite and at the back fence tells us more about how the system will behave than any product list.

Next we go down to the basement and up into the attic. An open basement ceiling or an accessible attic over the second floor turns a hard wiring job into a routine one, and a finished basement with no access panel turns a routine one into a longer conversation. We look for old wire too; a coil of speaker cable in the utility room usually means there are speakers somewhere waiting to be found.

Only then do we talk about speakers. By that point the zones almost draw themselves: which rooms can be wired cheaply, which should get a self-powered speaker instead, and where the Amps will live. The family sees a plan with a price per zone, and can start with two rooms knowing the rest will slot in later.

Long Island Sonos Installation FAQ

What does a Sonos family-room setup cost on Long Island, start to finish?
+

For a wall-mounted Sonos soundbar on drywall over wood studs, it is $129 to mount the bar plus $125 for the Sonos setup, so $254 in labour. Hiding power and HDMI inside the wall adds $169, bringing it to $423. If we hang the TV too, add the mounting base for your county: $209.99 in Nassau or $229.99 in Suffolk. Brick, block or tile walls add the published surcharge.

Do you check that the Amps, surrounds and mounts we picked actually match before install day?
+

Either works. Most families buy their Sonos products from a retailer they like and we install them; if you would rather we supply them, we quote them separately. Either way, tell us the exact models before the visit so we can confirm Amps, surrounds and mounts match each other and the rooms.

Can a multi-room wired system be split into a wiring day and a setup day?
+

A family-room theater or a few self-powered speakers usually fit in one appointment. A wired system with ceiling speakers in several rooms is scheduled in stages we agree in the written quote, typically wiring first and fitting and setup afterwards, so the house is never left with open ceilings longer than necessary.

Will you have to open our ceilings to wire in-ceiling speakers?
+

Each speaker needs its own cut-out, which is covered by the speaker grille. Beyond that, it depends on access: with an open basement below or an attic above, wire is fished in with no other openings. Under a finished floor we may need a few small access holes, and we show you where before quoting. Patching and painting are not included.

Can you take over a Sonos system another installer put in?
+

Yes. We start with a setup-only visit to see what is there: which products, how the Amps are wired, where the network is weak and whether the account is in your name. That first hour is $225 in Nassau or $250 in Suffolk, and we tell you what needs fixing before doing more.

What happens to our Sonos system if we switch internet providers or routers?
+

The speakers stay; the network connection has to be re-established. A new router means updating the Wi-Fi details on the system and checking that settings such as guest mode or a sign-in page are not enabled, since Sonos does not support them. If you book us, a setup-only visit covers it.

Can everyone in the family control the system from their own phone?
+

Yes. Anyone on the home Wi-Fi can install the Sonos app and control the speakers, and iPhone users can also use AirPlay. During hand-over we install and check the app on every phone you want included and show each person how to find their rooms and groups.

Is there a monthly fee for a Sonos system you install?
+

Not from us. You pay for the installation and own the equipment outright. Streaming services such as music subscriptions bill you separately, and an Alarm.com integration depends on your existing alarm service plan.

Do you install in both counties, including evenings and weekends?
+

Yes. We work across Nassau and Suffolk in two-hour arrival windows from 8 AM to 8 PM, seven days a week. Same-day service depends on a window being open, and for the North Fork and the far East End booking a few days ahead gives the most choice of times.

What should we have ready before the technician arrives?
+

The Sonos products on site and unopened if you want us to check them first, your Wi-Fi name and password, the login for your Sonos account and streaming services, clear access to the TV wall, the basement ceiling and the attic hatch, and the house cleared of furniture where speakers will be fitted.

If we sell the house, do in-ceiling speakers stay or go?
+

That is a matter for your sale contract, not for us, but plan for it. Ceiling and outdoor speakers are fixed to the house and are often treated as staying; the Amps, soundbar and portable speakers are usually considered yours. We label every Amp and wire run, which makes either choice simple.

How do you catch a dead-on-arrival Sonos unit before it goes into a ceiling?
+

We power up and test products before fitting them, so a dead unit is usually caught before it is in a wall. A faulty Sonos product is a matter for the retailer or Sonos under its own warranty, and mounts and kits carry their manufacturer warranty.
