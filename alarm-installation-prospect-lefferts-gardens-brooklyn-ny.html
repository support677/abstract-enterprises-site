The Neighborhood

Why Prospect Lefferts Gardens Properties Need Alarm Installation

Prospect Lefferts Gardens is a small neighborhood with an unusually wide mix of buildings. It runs from Empire Boulevard down to Clarkson Avenue and from Ocean Avenue and the edge of Prospect Park east to New York Avenue, all of it in the 71st Precinct and most of it in ZIP code 11225. Inside that rectangle you find the Lefferts Manor blocks of single-family brick and stone houses laid out after 1893, six-story apartment houses from the 1920s along Ocean Avenue and Flatbush Avenue, the 1960s Patio Gardens complex, small condos from the 2000s, and towers such as The Parkline at 626 Flatbush Avenue. An alarm that suits one of these buildings is often wrong for the one next door, which is why we do not sell a single package here.

What the buildings share is a quiet back. The front of a Manor house faces the street, where neighbors and passers-by can see the stoop. The back faces a yard, and that yard sits beside other yards and behind the yards of the next block, out of sight of anyone on the sidewalk. The apartment houses have rear courts, fire escapes and cellar doors that few tenants ever look at. The stores on the avenues have a gate and glass at the front and a steel door or a small window at the back, often off a service passage. In the cases we found in local news, the people who broke in chose those back and side openings far more often than the front door.

Two stories show the pattern. In August 2018, Patch reported that two men climbed through the window of a home on Nostrand Avenue at Maple Street at about three in the afternoon and left with roughly $3,500 in electronics. It was daylight, on a busy avenue, and the way in was a window. In early 2016, DNAinfo reported that four businesses on Flatbush Avenue in Prospect Lefferts Gardens were burglarized in less than a month: one through its front gate and door, one through its back door after closing, one through a back door at about 2:50 in the morning, and a restaurant through a back window at about four in the morning, where roughly $20,000 was taken from a safe. At the pharmacy, the burglars took the hard drive that held the store's own camera footage.

Those details decide what an alarm here has to do. It has to cover the openings at the back and on the garden floor first, not only the front door. It has to be armed during the day, when people are at work, and not just at night. And it has to report somewhere other than a box in the building, so that the signal is already out by the time anyone reaches the equipment. Everything else on this page, from the sensors we choose for a landmarked house to the way we set up a renter's system in a prewar building, follows from those three points.

The numbers do not call for alarm, and we would not pretend they do. The NYPD's CompStat report for the 71st Precinct, for the week ending November 1, 2026, shows 64 burglaries so far this year against 71 at the same point in 2025, a drop of 9.9 percent, and only three in the most recent 28 days. Over sixteen years, burglary in the precinct is down 75.8 percent, and over thirty-three years it is down 95.9 percent. What has grown is theft without a break-in: grand larceny stands at 338 for the year against 295 last year, up 14.6 percent. An alarm does little about a stolen phone on the train, but it can tell you when a package room, a bike room, a cellar storage cage or a shed in the yard is opened while you are away.

Need Repair in Prospect Lefferts Gardens?

A Keypad Beeping Since the Last Owner Moved Out, a Siren That Rang Until a Neighbor Called, or a Panel That Stopped Reporting?

Many of the alarm calls we take from this neighborhood are about systems someone else installed. Houses in Lefferts Manor change hands with alarms already in the walls, and the new owners often receive a keypad, a box in the cellar and no codes. Apartments in the big rental buildings are handed over with a wireless kit the last tenant left behind. A shop on Flatbush or Nostrand Avenue takes over a storefront with a panel still dialing a monitoring company the new owner has never heard of. In each case the first job is the same: find out what the equipment is, whether it still reports anywhere, and whether it can be adopted safely or should be replaced.

Older hardwired panels are the most common surprise. Systems installed in the Manor houses years ago were often wired with care, with contacts hidden in the door frames and cable fished through plaster, and that wiring is frequently still good. What has usually failed is the communicator, the part that sends the signal out. Panels that reported over a landline stop working when the phone service is dropped for internet or mobile, and panels built for older cellular networks went silent when those networks were switched off. Replacing the panel and communicator on sound existing wiring is often the best value in alarm work, and it sits on its own line of our price list.

If your alarm is beeping, showing a trouble message or sounding for no reason, call or text (347) 934-8335 with the brand name on the keypad and a photo of the screen. We will tell you honestly whether the fix is a battery, a sensor, a reset or a new panel. Repairs and takeovers on systems we did not install are billed as a service visit, which is shown with every other price further down this page, and many simple problems can be sorted out over the phone first.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Prospect Lefferts Gardens' Architecture

An alarm plan starts with the building, and Prospect Lefferts Gardens has at least six kinds that each change the plan. The single-family houses of Lefferts Manor are big, old and handsome, and most of their openings sit at the back. The apartment houses of the 1920s are large, rented and controlled by a landlord. The newer buildings have doormen, package rooms and keycard elevators, which take care of the lobby but not the apartment. The landmarked enclaves of Chester Court and Ocean on the Park add Commission rules on what can be seen from the street. And the mixed buildings on the avenues put stores, offices and apartments under one roof. Here is what each one asks of an alarm.

The Lefferts Manor house

Single-family brick or stone, at least two stories by the 1893 deed rules, many with a garden floor, a parlor floor, bedrooms above and a cellar below. The stoop is in public view; the yard is not.

The row house off the Manor blocks

Attached houses on the streets outside the covenant, where a house may hold an owner and a tenant, which turns one alarm into two areas with their own codes.

The prewar elevator building

Six-story apartment houses from the 1920s, such as 115 Ocean Avenue with its 90 units, where an alarm belongs to one apartment and the landlord controls everything outside its door.

The small new condo

Buildings such as 185 Ocean Avenue, eight floors and twenty units built in 2008 with no doorman, where owners control their own units and the board controls the rest.

The tower with a desk

The Parkline at 626 Flatbush Avenue, with a doorman, concierge and package room, or Hello Lenox at 271 Lenox Road with keyed elevator access to each floor. The lobby is staffed; the apartment is yours to protect.

The landmarked enclave

The eighteen Tudor-revival townhouses of Chester Court off Flatbush Avenue near Rutland Road, and the twelve townhouses of the Ocean on the Park district, where anything mounted outside has to satisfy the Landmarks Commission.

The Lefferts Manor house: one family, many floors, and the back half nobody sees

The houses of Lefferts Manor were built under deed restrictions that required a single-family house of brick or stone, at least two stories tall, and the Lefferts Manor Association, founded in 1919, still enforces that covenant. For an alarm, that has a useful consequence: most Manor houses hold one household, so there is no need to divide the panel between an owner and a tenant the way we do in two-family houses elsewhere in Brooklyn. The challenge is size. A Manor house can have four levels, two or three doors to the outside, a dozen or more windows that a person could reach from the ground or from a rear extension, and a cellar with its own way in.

We plan these houses from the back forward. The garden floor gets contacts on the yard door and every window that faces the yard, and usually a glass-break detector in the main rear room so that a broken pane is caught even if the window stays latched. A motion detector on that floor covers the space between the back door and the stairs. The parlor floor gets contacts on the front door and the tall windows if they can be reached, and the cellar door, inside or out, gets its own zone. The upper floors usually get contacts only on windows near a rear extension roof, a fire escape or a neighbor's lower roof. Most families here arm the garden floor and cellar at night while they sleep upstairs, then arm the whole house when everyone leaves in the morning.

The wiring choice depends on the walls. Many Manor houses still have their original plaster on wood lath, which is solid, old and full of surprises, and many have been renovated floor by floor over the decades. In a house that is not being opened up, we use wireless sensors on a cellular panel, which avoids patching plaster entirely. In a house that is being gutted or renovated, we can run cable while the walls are open and install a hardwired system, which costs more but has no sensor batteries to replace. Both kinds report the same way and use the same app.

The row house off the Manor blocks: owner downstairs, tenant upstairs

Outside the covenant, on the blocks toward Nostrand Avenue and New York Avenue, there are attached houses that hold more than one household. Where an owner lives on one floor and rents another, the alarm becomes two areas on one panel. Each household arms and disarms its own floor with its own code, the shared front door and hallway sit in a common area that is armed only when both households are armed or away, and the cellar is assigned to whoever controls it. Nobody's code opens anyone else's floor, and the tenant's alerts go to the tenant's phone.

Where a house has a rental unit and a gas range, boiler or water heater, HPD expects carbon monoxide detectors as well as smoke detectors, within fifteen feet of the entrance to each sleeping room. Those detectors can be stand-alone, or they can be wireless detectors on the alarm panel so that a warning reaches the owner's phone as well as the tenant's ears. We fit both kinds and explain the difference before you choose.

The prewar elevator building: an alarm for one apartment in a building you do not control

The six-story apartment houses on Ocean Avenue, Flatbush Avenue and the side streets near the park are the largest homes in the neighborhood by count of people. A building like 115 Ocean Avenue, built in 1925 with 90 apartments on six floors and an elevator, is a small town. Buildings of that age and size typically have a lobby, stairs, a cellar used for storage or laundry, and fire escapes at the rear or on a court. An apartment alarm in such a building protects the apartment door, any window on a fire escape or reachable from a lower roof, and anything inside worth an extra sensor. It does not protect the lobby, which belongs to the landlord.

The landlord question matters more than the hardware. Brick Underground has pointed out that a landlord in New York only has to give you a working lock; an alarm is yours to buy. It also noted that standard leases say alterations and installations become the landlord's property. So in a rented apartment we use a wireless panel, contacts held on with adhesive or a single small screw where you have permission, a plug-in or tabletop siren, and a keypad that stands on a shelf. When you move, the system comes down and leaves at most a couple of tiny holes, and nothing the landlord could claim as a fixture.

The small new condo and the tower with a desk

The buildings that went up after the long gap in construction between Patio Gardens in the early 1960s and the mid-2000s are a different job. In a condo such as 185 Ocean Avenue, the owner controls the unit and the board controls the halls, so an alarm for the unit is straightforward once the building's rules on contractors and hours are checked. In a tower such as The Parkline or Hello Lenox, the lobby is staffed or the elevator is keyed, and people sometimes ask whether an alarm is needed at all.

Our honest answer is that in a doorman building, the alarm is less about burglars at the front door and more about the hours you are away and the things a desk never sees: a water leak under the kitchen sink that runs into the unit below, a smoke alarm sounding behind a closed door while you are at work, a terrace door left unlatched, or a dog walker or cleaner who comes and goes with a key. A small system with a door contact, two or three water sensors, a smoke and heat sensor and a motion detector covers most of that, and it costs less than many people expect.

Chester Court and Ocean on the Park: protecting a landmarked house without changing its face

Chester Court, a cul-de-sac off Flatbush Avenue near Rutland Road, holds eighteen Tudor-revival townhouses and became a historic district in 2014. Ocean on the Park, a row of twelve townhouses, was designated in 2009. Both sit alongside the much larger Prospect Lefferts Gardens Historic District, designated in 1979. In all three, anything that changes the outside of a house needs the Commission's approval before the work begins, as the Lefferts Manor Association reminds its members. We cover what that means for sirens, sensors and wiring in its own section below. The short version is that we keep the alarm inside wherever possible, so that most jobs never touch the facade at all.

Back Yards and Daylight

The Way In Is Behind the House: Planning a Prospect Lefferts Gardens Alarm Around Yards, Garden Floors and Daytime Hours

The blocks of Lefferts Manor form long rectangles of houses with their yards meeting in the middle. From the street you see stoops and front doors. From any one yard you can see the backs of many houses, and a fence between two yards is rarely much of an obstacle. A burglar who gets into the middle of the block has time and cover. That is why the first question we ask about a Manor house is not what the front door looks like, but how someone in the yard would get into the garden floor.

The answer is usually one of three openings. The first is the garden-floor door, which on many houses is a wood or glazed door under the rear of the parlor floor. The second is a rear window on the garden floor, often an old double-hung sash at a height a person can reach standing on the ground or on a garden chair. The third is a window on the parlor floor or above that can be reached from the roof of a one-story rear extension. We put a contact on each of these, a glass-break detector in the room behind the largest pane, and a motion detector that sees anyone who gets past them. If the house has a cellar door from the yard, that gets its own zone too.

Time of day is the second half of the plan. The Patch report on the burglary at Nostrand Avenue and Maple Street described a window entry at about three in the afternoon. Daytime break-ins happen when a house is empty and the street is busy enough that nobody pays attention to one more person. For an alarm, the lesson is simple: it only helps if it is armed when the house is empty. We set up every system so that arming on the way out takes one button on a keypad or one tap in the app, and we can add a reminder that alerts your phone if the system is still disarmed when everyone's phone has left the house. A modest system that is armed every time you leave does far more than an elaborate one that is armed only at night.

There is also the sound question. On a quiet Manor block a siren on the garden floor can be heard by neighbors in the yards behind. That is useful, but it is not the same as help. The siren tells the person inside that they have been noticed; the report that leaves the house over cellular, to your phone and, if you choose, to a monitoring station, is what gets someone to act. City law also limits how long the siren may sound, which we explain in the compliance notes below, so we set the siren to run its full permitted time and the report to go out the moment the alarm trips.

Landmarked Blocks

Sirens, Sensors and Wire on a Landmarked House: What the Commission's Equipment Rules Mean for a Prospect Lefferts Gardens Alarm

A large part of Prospect Lefferts Gardens lies inside the historic district the Landmarks Preservation Commission designated in 1979, and the Lefferts Manor Association tells members plainly that landmark status "applies to the exterior of the buildings" and that "any proposed alterations to the outside of homes must receive approval from the Commission before work begins." People sometimes take this to mean that any alarm on a landmarked house needs a permit. It does not. The Commission regulates the outside of the building. An alarm that lives entirely indoors, with its sensors on the inside of doors and windows, its keypad in the hall and its siren in a closet or on an interior wall, changes nothing the Commission looks at.

Where an alarm does reach the outside, the Commission's own rules set the standard. Section 2-20 of its rules covers health, safety and utility equipment, and it names security cameras and lighting as examples of equipment that is "not required by law but is commonly used for safety reasons." The staff-level criteria in that section say the equipment should not go on or in front of a primary facade "unless there is no feasible alternative," should be attached "at mortar joints or through plain brick, stone or wood to the greatest extent possible," should have a finish that matches the surrounding building material, and should be "either not visible from a public thoroughfare or as minimally visible as possible." On wiring, the rule allows no visible conduit or, if that is not feasible, about ten inches of it, painted to match the wall. And it closes with a line we take as our own standard on these blocks: the installation "will not call undue attention to itself."

In practice that shapes four decisions. First, the siren: we put the main sounder inside, and if a client wants an outdoor siren or strobe, we look for a place on the rear wall rather than the front, where it can be heard in the yards without being seen from the sidewalk. Second, the cellular antenna: modern panels have the radio built in, so there is no need for an antenna on the front, and where signal in a cellar is weak, we move the panel upstairs rather than mount anything outside. Third, the wiring: old alarm systems sometimes ran a phone line or a siren cable down the front of the house, and when we replace them we remove what is no longer used rather than add to it. Fourth, cameras: if you want a camera with the alarm, the rule for cameras asks that they be "as small and as limited in number as feasible," so we suggest a doorbell camera or a small unit tucked into the door opening for the front, and save larger cameras for the back.

When something must go on the outside, the owner is the one who applies to the Commission, and we provide the information the application needs: the product, its size and color, the exact mounting point, the route of any cable, and photographs. We would rather design the job so that no application is needed. On almost every house in Lefferts Manor, Chester Court and Ocean on the Park we have looked at, that is possible.

Renters

An Alarm for a Rented Apartment on Ocean, Flatbush or Lenox: What the Landlord Owes, What You Own, and How to Leave Without a Trace

A great many people in Prospect Lefferts Gardens rent, often in the big prewar buildings along Ocean Avenue and Flatbush Avenue and on streets such as Lenox Road, Lincoln Road, Midwood Street and Hawthorne Street. A renter who wants an alarm usually has three worries: whether the landlord has to provide one, whether installing one breaks the lease, and whether the money is lost when they move. We can answer the first and third directly and help with the second.

On the first, the answer is no. In an article on what renters pay for, Brick Underground summed up New York practice: the landlord must give you a working lock, and items such as alarms are the tenant's responsibility. On the third, the answer depends on how the alarm is installed. The same article pointed out that standard leases say alterations and installations become the landlord's property. A hardwired system with contacts in the door frame and a panel screwed to the wall could, on that reading, stay behind. A wireless system that stands on furniture, sticks to the frame with adhesive and plugs into an outlet is your property and goes with you.

So for renters we build almost everything to be removable. The panel sits on a shelf or a table near an outlet, with a battery inside for power cuts. Contacts on the apartment door and the windows are held on with adhesive pads, which we test for a firm hold on painted wood and metal frames, and which come off cleanly when you leave. Motion detectors sit on a shelf or a corner bracket. The siren is in the panel or plugs in. The keypad can be a small wireless unit by the door or simply your phone. If the lease or the landlord allows small screws for a more secure hold, we can use them, but we ask first and note it in the work order.

On the second worry, we suggest you tell the landlord or managing agent before installation, in writing, that you are adding a wireless alarm to your own apartment with no wiring and no alterations to the building. Most landlords do not object, and a note on file avoids arguments later. What we will not do in a rented apartment is wire into the building's intercom, the door buzzer or the fire alarm system, because those belong to the owner. Your system protects your apartment and reports to you.

Lefferts Manor houses

Our Lefferts Manor house is single-family under the old deed rules. Does that let us skip the separate areas you set up in other Brooklyn houses?

Usually, yes. Separate areas, or partitions, exist so that two households can each arm their own floor. A house with one family needs one area for the whole house, with different arming modes instead: one for night that protects the garden floor and cellar while you sleep upstairs, one for leaving that arms everything, and perhaps one for the hours a nanny or cleaner is in. That makes the panel simpler to program and simpler to use. We only add a second area if part of the house, such as a home office with its own street door, needs to be armed on a different schedule from the rest.

The garden-floor back room has a pair of old wooden French doors onto the yard. Is one sensor enough for a double door?

No. Each leaf of a pair of doors can usually be opened on its own, so each gets its own contact. On many old pairs, one leaf is held shut by bolts at the top and bottom and the other leaf latches to it; we put a contact on the active leaf and another on the fixed one, so that pulling the bolts is caught too. Because French doors are mostly glass, we also add a glass-break detector in the room, which catches a pane broken without either door being opened. On a pair of doors this old we check how much the leaves move in their frame before choosing the contacts, so that a gust of wind in the yard does not trip the alarm.

We sleep on the top floor of a four-story Manor house. If the alarm goes off at night, can we see which room tripped before anyone goes downstairs?

Yes, and we set every house up so you can. Each sensor is named for its location when we program it, such as "garden floor yard door" or "parlor rear window", and the keypad and the app both show that name the moment it trips. We also suggest a second keypad on the bedroom floor so that you can read it, silence the siren or trigger a panic alarm without going down the stairs. If you have a camera on the yard or the garden floor, the app can open that view from the alert, so you can see what set it off.

The parlor-floor rear window is high off the yard, but there is a one-story kitchen extension right below it. Does that window need a sensor?

Yes. A window above a low roof is one of the easier ways into a row house, because the roof makes a platform at exactly the right height and is hidden from the street. We treat any window that can be reached from an extension roof, a neighbor's roof, a fire escape or a garden wall as a ground-floor window and give it a contact. If the extension has a skylight, that gets a contact too. Windows on the upper floors with no way to reach them usually do not need sensors, which keeps the price down.

Landmarked houses

Our Chester Court house has leaded-glass casement windows. Will a glass-break detector hear a leaded pane break, and where do the contacts go?

Leaded glass is made of small panes held in metal strips, and a small pane breaking makes a different, quieter sound from a large sheet of plate glass. Glass-break detectors are rated for particular glass types and distances, so in a room with leaded windows we choose a detector rated for that kind of glass, place it closer than we would for a modern window, and test it with a simulator before we leave. The contacts go on the opening edge of each casement frame and the fixed frame beside it, never on the glass or the leading, and they are small enough to sit out of sight when the window is shut.

We would like the keypad to be visible from the stoop, so people know the house has an alarm. Can it go in the vestibule of a landmarked house?

Yes, because the vestibule is inside the front door, and the Commission's rules apply to the exterior. Many Manor houses have an outer door and an inner door with a small vestibule between them, and a keypad on the vestibule wall is both convenient and visible through the glass of the outer door. We would still avoid putting it where a person on the stoop can watch you type the code. A small window decal, which goes on the inside of the glass, also signals the alarm without any change to the facade.

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Prospect Lefferts Gardens

"Crime is high in Prospect Lefferts Gardens, so get the biggest package available."

The precinct's own figures do not support that framing. "In a historic district you need a Landmarks permit to install an alarm."

This mixes up the inside and the outside of a building. The Commission regulates exteriors, and the Lefferts Manor Association's own guidance says landmark status "applies to the exterior of the buildings." An alarm whose panel, sensors, keypad and siren are all inside the house does not change anything the Commission reviews.

Where something does go outside, such as a siren, a camera or a run of cable, the Commission's rules in § 2-20 set the standard: avoid the primary facade where there is a feasible alternative, attach at mortar joints or plain masonry, match the finish to the wall, keep visible conduit to about ten inches or none, and do not "call undue attention." That is a design brief, not a ban.

Our approach on landmarked blocks is to keep the whole alarm indoors whenever we can and, when a client wants something outside, to put it at the rear and give them the details an application would need. On most houses no application is ever needed.

"Renters can't really have an alarm without the landlord installing it."

The landlord's legal duty is narrower than people think. As Brick Underground put it in its piece on renters' security costs, a New York landlord has to provide a working lock, and alarms are the tenant's own responsibility. That cuts both ways: the landlord does not have to install one for you, and you do not need the landlord to install one.

What a renter does need to think about is the lease, because standard leases say that alterations and installations become the landlord's property. A system wired into the walls could be argued to be a fixture. A wireless system that stands on a shelf, sticks to the frames and plugs into an outlet is personal property.

So in rentals we install removable systems, we suggest you give the landlord a short written note before the visit, and we never connect your alarm to the building's intercom, door release or fire alarm. That way the alarm is clearly yours and comes with you when you leave.

A doorman or concierge, like the staff at The Parkline, watches the lobby and takes in packages. They do not see inside your apartment, they do not hear a smoke alarm behind your closed door on the twentieth floor, and they cannot tell that the dishwasher hose has split while you are on vacation.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Open the Back Door Before You Look at the Front

The habit we bring to a survey in Prospect Lefferts Gardens is simple: before we talk about the front door, we ask to go out the back. On a Manor house that means standing in the yard and looking at the house the way someone climbing the fence would see it. Which door is glass, which windows are low, what sits under the parlor-floor windows, whether the cellar has its own door, and how much of all that the neighbors' houses can see. On a store it means walking out the service door and looking at the alley or the passage beside it, and seeing how long a person could work on that door without being noticed from the avenue.

In an apartment, the same habit means going to the fire escape window first. The front windows on a high floor almost never matter. The one onto the fire escape always does, and so does any window beside it. That walk usually changes the plan more than anything a client tells us on the phone. It is also why our quotes list every sensor with the opening it protects. You should be able to stand at each door and window in the quote and see why it is there.

The second habit is to stand where the panel will go and check the signal on a meter before we fix anything to the wall. Brick and stone walls and cellars can make a big difference. A few minutes spent moving the panel to a better spot is worth more than any feature on the box.

FAQ

Prospect Lefferts Gardens Alarm Installation FAQ

Does a whole Lefferts Manor house always land on the $2,500 complete-protection line, or can a smaller plan cover just the garden floor and cellar?
+

A smaller plan is possible. If the upper floors have no reachable windows and you mainly want the yard door, the garden-floor windows and the cellar covered, the job can fall in the $600 to $1,200 wireless range instead. Most owners who start that way add the parlor floor and life-safety sensors later on the same panel, so nothing bought at the start is wasted.

Besides burglary sensors, which alarm devices can count toward a homeowner's insurance credit in New York?
+

Insurers commonly credit monitored fire alarms and low-temperature alarms as well as burglar alarms, and NYCM, for example, lists water and temperature alert systems alongside burglar and fire alarms in its protective device discount. Brick Underground reported that most insurers want the monitoring done by a third party and a certificate of installation from the installer. Ask your agent which devices your policy credits before we finalize the sensor list.

Should I get my landlord's written permission before putting a wireless alarm in my rent-stabilized apartment?
+

We recommend telling the landlord in writing, even though a removable wireless system with no wiring is your own property and does not alter the building. A short note that says what is being installed, that nothing is wired into the building and that it will leave with you avoids disputes later. If your lease restricts any screws or fixtures, we use adhesive mounts only.

Will cats, squirrels or other animals in the yard set off the alarm at night?
+

Not with the way we install. Sensors that protect the yard side are on the doors and windows themselves and inside the house, so an animal outside does nothing. We do not use outdoor motion detectors as alarm zones on Manor houses. If you want to know when something moves in the yard, a camera or floodlight camera can send a notification without ever sounding the siren.

Our apartment building already has cameras in the lobby and the halls. Is an apartment alarm still worth adding?
+

Often, yes. Building cameras belong to the landlord, are usually reviewed only after something has happened, and do not cover the inside of your apartment or your fire escape window. An apartment alarm tells you the moment your own door or window opens and can sound a siren while the person is still there. The two do different jobs and work well together.

We are buying a house in Lefferts Manor that has an alarm. What should we ask the sellers before closing?
+

Ask for the master code and installer code, the name of the monitoring company and whether the contract transfers or ends with the sale, the age of the panel and its communicator, a list of zones, and any manuals. Ask whether the system still reports and when it was last tested. With that list we can tell you in one visit whether to keep, adopt or replace the system.

What happens to the old panel, sirens and batteries you take out?
+

Unless you want to keep them, we remove them and take them away, including old lead-acid backup batteries, which should not go in household trash. Old wiring that is still sound and reused stays in the walls; wiring that is no longer needed and is visible, such as an old siren cable on a facade, is removed where we can reach it.

Can the alarm switch lamps on and off on a schedule so the house looks lived in while we are away?
+

Yes, with smart plugs or switches added to the system. They can follow a schedule or switch at random times in the evening, and they are controlled from the same app as the alarm. On a landmarked house this is all indoors, so it does not raise any exterior questions.

Is it better to buy a kit online and pay you $200 to $400 to install it, or to buy the whole system from you?
+

A kit makes sense for a small rented apartment where you want a few sensors and phone alerts. A system from us makes sense where you want cellular reporting as the main path, glass-break and safe sensors, life-safety sensors and our warranty on the equipment. We will tell you honestly which fits your home before you buy anything.

How many keypads does a four-floor house need?
+

Usually two: one by the door you come and go through, which in many Manor houses is the garden-floor door rather than the stoop, and one on the bedroom floor. Some families add a third by the stoop door. The phone app does everything a keypad does, so extra keypads are about convenience, not coverage.

Can the sensors be matched to the dark wood trim of an old house instead of bright white plastic?
+

Partly. Many contacts come in white and brown, and some can be recessed into the frame so that almost nothing shows. Where a contact must sit on the surface of dark trim, we use the brown version and place it on the hinge-side edge or the top of the frame, where it is least visible.

Can the alarm send a spoken phone call when it trips, not just an app notification?
+

Yes. With central station monitoring, an operator calls the people on your list in order. Without monitoring, the app can send notifications, texts and emails, and we can set up the alerts so that the most urgent ones, such as a break-in or smoke, stand out from routine ones like a door opening during the day.
