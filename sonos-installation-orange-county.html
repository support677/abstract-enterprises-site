The Neighborhood

Why Orange County Homes Need Sonos Installation

Orange County is the Hudson Valley county where a Sonos installer is most likely to be working in a house a builder handed over in the last twenty-five years. The Census Bureau’s 2019–2023 American Community Survey counts 148,460 housing units in the county, and 30,131 of them — about one in five — were built in 2000 or later. In the Town of Wallkill the share is 26.4 percent, in New Windsor 25.0 percent and in the Town of Newburgh 22.8 percent. Chester, Monroe and Montgomery all sit between roughly 18 and 20 percent. These are the subdivisions and townhouse developments strung along Route 17 and Interstate 84, and they are where a Sonos plan in this county most often has to start from builder wiring.

Newer does not mean simpler. A house built in 2005 or 2020 was wired by a builder’s low-voltage subcontractor to a budget, usually with coaxial cable to several rooms, a few network jacks, and a small enclosure in the basement or a closet where everything meets. The internet provider then dropped its modem and router wherever the line came in, often in that basement. Nobody planned the house for music. So the first part of a Sonos job here is working out what the builder left behind the drywall, which parts of it are usable, and where a new cable or a ceiling access point is the cheaper answer.

The second part is respecting what the newer house relies on. Top-floor ceilings sit under an insulated attic whose air barrier the energy code expects to be continuous; a careless speaker hole lets attic air into a bedroom. Floors are often framed with engineered I-joists that tolerate holes in the web but not cuts through the flanges. Townhouses share a fire-rated wall with the house next door. And a house only a few years old may still be inside its builder’s warranty period. Each of those changes where, and how, a speaker can go.

The third part is the household. Orange County keeps growing — USAFacts, working from Census estimates, reports that the population rose by about 2,600 between 2024 and 2025 and by 11.1 percent over the decade to 2025 — and the homes are big enough to fill. The ACS puts the median house at six rooms, and nearly three in ten homes have four bedrooms or more. A Sonos system here is often planned for a family that will add a nursery, finish the basement for teenagers, put a theater in the bonus room over the garage and spend summer evenings by a pool. We design for the house as it will be in five years, not only the room you want playing this weekend.

The older river cities are the contrast. In the City of Newburgh, 49.4 percent of homes were built in 1939 or earlier and only about 4 percent since 2000; in Port Jervis the pre-1940 share is 54.2 percent. Those houses bring plaster, brick party walls and balloon framing rather than coax runs and I-joists, and we plan them on their own terms, as the section on older homes below explains.

Builder-Grade Basics

Behind the Drywall of a Newer Orange County House

The most useful hour on a newer-house job is the one spent with the media panel door open. Builders in Orange County, as across most of the country, offer low-voltage packages at the design-center stage: a few coax outlets, a few network jacks, sometimes a pair of patio or kitchen ceiling speakers, all running back to one enclosure. National builders also sell connected-home packages; Ring’s page for Lennar homeowners, for instance, lists a wired video doorbell, a smart lock, a thermostat and an alarm kit. What those packages rarely include is a plan for Sonos.

That leaves owners with wiring of uncertain value. A coax run that tests clean from the panel to the bonus room is worth a lot: with a pair of coax network adapters it becomes a wired connection without a new hole, and Sonos community members describe running speakers and Sonos home theaters exactly that way. A network jack in the kitchen desk that was never connected at the panel end is worth nothing until someone terminates it. Ceiling speakers the builder fitted with a wall volume knob may be perfectly usable, or may present a load a Sonos Amp shouldn’t drive. None of it can be judged from the faceplate.

So the audit comes first. We open the panel, label every cable we can identify, put a tone on the ones we can’t, and test each run end to end. You get a short written list: which jacks and coax runs are live, which are dead, which carry signal well enough for a network link, and where a new cable or an access point will be needed anyway. That list becomes the network part of the quote, and it is yours to keep for the next contractor.

Five things to check in the first month after closing

  • Where the internet landed. If the provider put the router on the basement floor or inside the media panel, Wi-Fi to the upper floor will be weaker than it needs to be. Sonos lists metal among the materials that block wireless signal most, and a closed metal enclosure is exactly that.
  • The router’s default password. Change it before any Sonos product joins the network, so you don’t have to rejoin every speaker later.
  • Which jacks are live. A cheap tester or our audit will tell you; it is easier to find out before the furniture is in front of them.
  • What the builder’s walkthrough list says about low-voltage. If something in the package was promised and not delivered, raise it with the builder in writing while the house is new.
  • Whether the basement ceiling is still open. An unfinished basement is the best cable route in the house. Plan the speaker cable for the floor above before the basement is drywalled.

If the builder’s wiring turns out to be thin, the fix is usually modest: one new cable from the panel to a central ceiling access point, or a wired link to the family room where the Sonos home theater lives. Sonos’s own system requirements describe products wired to the router at all times as one supported setup, and say that in a mesh system with a wired setup, the wired Sonos product must connect to the primary node. In a newer house, getting that one connection right does more for reliability than any number of extra speakers.

Your Questions Answered

Sonos Installation in Orange County: Questions Answered

These are the questions Orange County owners actually put to us on walkthroughs, grouped by the part of the house they concern. Each answer is the one we give on site, with the source named where a rule or a specification is involved.

What the builder left behind the walls

Our builder ran coax to every bedroom — can that cable give the upstairs Sonos a wired connection?

Often, yes, and it is one of the most useful things a newer house offers. A pair of coax network adapters — one at the media panel next to the router, one at the far outlet — turns a clean coax run into a wired network connection, and the Sonos product or an access point plugs into the far adapter by Ethernet. Sonos’s support pages rule out powerline adapters and plug-in range extenders, but they do not list coax adapters among unsupported setups, and Sonos community members describe wiring Sonos speakers and home theaters over coax this way successfully.

The catch is the run itself. Builder coax passes through splitters in the media panel, and a run that is fine for cable TV may lose too much signal for a network link. We test each run, replace old splitters with ones rated for the adapters, and fit a filter where the provider’s line enters so the signal stays inside your house. Then we test the Sonos product over the link before we rely on it. If a run fails, we say so and price a network cable or a ceiling access point instead.

The builder put a volume knob on the wall beside the kitchen ceiling speakers — will it work with a Sonos Amp?

Usually it is better removed or set out of the way. Builder wall knobs are often impedance-matching volume controls, made for receivers that drive several pairs of speakers at once, and they change the load the amplifier sees. Sonos’s Amp guide says not to connect speakers rated below 4 ohms, supports up to two pairs of 8-ohm speakers in parallel, and says speakers should not be wired in series — so whatever is on the wall has to leave the Amp seeing a load inside those limits.

With a Sonos Amp the volume control moves to the app, the voice assistant or a button on the Amp, so the knob mostly becomes something to trip over. We test the speakers behind it, and if they are sound we bypass or replace the knob with a blank plate, wire the speakers straight to the Amp and set a volume limit so the kitchen can’t be turned up past what you choose. If the speakers themselves are weak or damaged, we say so before you spend money on an Amp for them.

Will cutting speaker holes in a house that’s two years old affect the builder’s warranty?

It shouldn’t touch the warranty on the parts of the house we don’t work on, but read your builder’s written warranty before we cut. New York’s housing merchant implied warranty, in General Business Law section 777-a, runs one year for skillful construction, two years for the plumbing, electrical, heating, cooling and ventilation systems, and six years for material defects, and it survives the passing of title. The same section says it does not extend to defects that aren’t the builder’s own workmanship, materials or design.

In practice that means a crack or leak around an opening we cut is ours to answer for, not the builder’s, and we keep our work well away from the builder’s ducts, pipes and electrical so there is no argument about who caused what. Builders often sell their own limited warranty on top of the statutory one, and some say how owner alterations are treated. We photograph the ceilings before we start, and if your warranty has a claim open on a room, we leave that room until the claim is settled. This is practical guidance, not legal advice.

Joists, insulation and the wall you share

The basement ceiling shows engineered I-joists — can speaker wire be drilled through them?

Yes, in the right places. Engineered I-joists, common in floors built since the 1990s, are a thin web panel between a top and a bottom flange, and the makers publish where holes may go. Weyerhaeuser’s technical support, for example, explains that its chart for uniformly loaded TJI joists allows holes of 1½ inches and smaller anywhere in the web outside the marked zone near a bearing, and that larger holes must be at least 12 inches from the bearing. Speaker cable needs a hole well under an inch.

What we don’t do is cut or notch a flange, drill close to where the joist sits on a wall or beam, or cluster holes beside the holes the plumber and electrician already made. We read the stamp on the joist to identify the maker and series, follow that maker’s chart, and keep a printout in the van. Where the joists run the wrong way for the cable route, we use the open basement ceiling and a single bundled path rather than drilling across every joist.

Will speaker openings in the top-floor ceiling let attic air into the bedrooms?

They can if they are done carelessly, which is why we treat each one as a hole in the house’s air barrier. The model energy code’s air-barrier table (IECC 2021, table R402.4.1.1) says gaps in the air barrier at the ceiling and attic must be sealed, and that recessed light fixtures in the building envelope must be airtight, IC-rated and sealed to the drywall. A speaker isn’t a light, but an 8.25-inch cut-out for Sonos’s 6-inch in-ceiling speaker is at least as large as a typical recessed-light opening, and it deserves the same care.

Before cutting a top-floor ceiling we look from the attic side if there is a hatch. We clear the insulation from the spot, fit a back enclosure or build a sealed box over the speaker so attic air can’t pass around it, seal the cable entry, and put the insulation back over the top. In a room where the attic is inaccessible, we either choose a wall position instead or quote the extra time to do the sealing from below.

Can a speaker be recessed into the wall we share with the townhouse next door?

No, and we won’t try. In a townhouse the shared wall is a fire separation. The International Residential Code, the model code New York’s residential code builds on, requires townhouse units to be separated either by two 1-hour fire-resistance-rated walls or by a common 2-hour wall, and it limits openings in those walls tightly: the outlet-box exception covers steel boxes of no more than 16 square inches each, with a small total per 100 square feet of wall. A Sonos in-ceiling or in-wall speaker cut-out is several times that size.

The good news is that the other walls and the ceilings are usually ordinary construction. We put recessed speakers in the ceiling or on interior walls, use surface-mounted Era speakers on brackets near the shared wall if the room layout needs them there, and keep the Sub off the shared wall so bass doesn’t travel into the neighbor’s living room. If you are unsure which wall is the separation, the builder’s plans or your association will show it, and we check before drilling anything.

Tall rooms, bonus rooms and echoes

Our family room is open to the second floor — where do speakers go when the ceiling is eighteen feet up?

Usually not in that ceiling. Sonos’s own guide to in-ceiling speakers explains that speakers project sound outward in a V shape, so a speaker in a 14-foot ceiling covers more floor than one in an 8-foot ceiling. At great-room heights the cone spreads so wide that the sound reaches you thinner and later, bouncing off the glass and the balcony first. Sonos’s installation manual gives spacing by ceiling height for exactly this reason.

In most two-story rooms we build the system at ear level instead: an Arc Ultra under the TV, a Sub on the floor, and either in-wall speakers or Era 300s on stud-mounted brackets at the sides or rear. If you want ceiling speakers for background music, the flat ceiling of the kitchen or the area under the balcony is the better place, and the great room plays as part of the same group.

Is the bonus room over the garage a good place for the Arc and Sub?

It can be a very good theater room, with three things to plan for. The first is temperature: rooms over garages run warmer in summer and cooler in winter than the rest of the house, and Sonos lists its Amp’s operating range as 32° to 104°F, so equipment should not sit in a closet that gets hotter than the room. The second is the signal: the bonus room is often the farthest point from a basement router, so it usually needs a wired link or its own access point.

The third is the neighbors inside your own house. A Sub on a framed floor over the garage won’t bother the cars, but the bedroom that shares the bonus room’s wall will hear it. We place the Sub against an outside wall, set the bass with Trueplay, and agree a night setting before we leave. And because the garage ceiling below is part of the house’s fire separation from the garage, cable reaches the bonus room from the house side, never through a hole cut in that ceiling.

Our two-story foyer and hardwood floors make every sound echo — will Sonos sound harsh in there?

It can, if the speakers fire straight into the hard surfaces. Newer Orange County houses often pair a two-story entry with hardwood or tile floors, painted drywall and a stair with no soft furnishings, and that combination reflects sound back and forth before it reaches you. Sonos’s Trueplay tuning exists for exactly this: Sonos says it measures how sound reflects off walls, furnishings and other surfaces and adjusts each speaker to suit the room.

We still start with placement. The foyer rarely needs its own speaker; music from the family room and kitchen, aimed into furnished space, carries into the entry at a comfortable level. Where a foyer or stair hall does get a speaker, we keep it low on the wall or under the landing rather than high in the open volume, tune it with Trueplay, and suggest a runner or a rug, which does more for echo than any setting.

Finishing the basement

We’re finishing the basement this winter — at what point does the speaker wire need to be in?

After the framing and the electrician’s rough-in, and before insulation and drywall. That window is the cheapest moment in the whole life of the house to run speaker and network cable, and it is short. Finishing a basement is building work, and local building departments treat it that way — the Village of Chester’s list of work requiring a permit includes additions, alterations and conversions — and inspections of framing and rough wiring usually come before the walls are closed. Ask your building department which inspections apply to you.

We run our cable once the electrician has finished, keep it apart from the electrical cable and boxes, label every end, and leave a coil at each speaker spot and at the equipment location. The ceiling speaker openings are marked on the joists so the drywall crew can cut them, or we cut them after the ceiling is up. Tell your contractor early: one phone call before the insulation goes in saves opening a new ceiling later.

The patio, the pool and the association

We have a saltwater pool — will the salt and chlorine damage the outdoor speakers?

Not if they are the right speakers in the right place. Sonos says its outdoor architectural speakers, made with Sonance, are rated IP66 and tested to the Mil Spec 810 standard for humidity, salt spray, temperature and UV, with an operating range of −13° to 149°F. Those are the speakers we mount around a pool. Indoor speakers carried outside are a different story: Sonos warns that speakers that aren’t waterproof may be damaged if exposed to water or left outside for long periods.

Placement matters as much as rating. We mount the speakers under the eaves or on the deck framing, out of the direct splash zone and pointed at the loungers rather than across the water, run the cable to an indoor Amp through a sealed entry, and keep any outdoor power and its distance from the water for your electrician and the inspector, since pools carry their own permit — the Village of Chester, for one, lists pools, hot tubs and decks around pools among the work that needs one.

Our homeowners’ association must approve exterior changes — what should we bring them for patio speakers?

A short, specific package. We give you a sketch of the back of the house with each speaker marked and its height noted, the Sonos product sheet showing its size and color, a note of how the cable enters the house and how the entry is sealed, and a photo of a similar installation. Most associations care about what can be seen from the street and the neighbors’ yards, so speakers tucked under the eaves in white or black usually raise fewer questions than anything on a fence or a post.

Your association’s declaration and design guidelines decide what is allowed, not us, so read them first and submit before the speakers are bought. If the association asks for changes — a different color, a position under the overhang instead of the wall — we redraw the plan and resend it at no charge.

As the family grows

Can the nursery speaker play white noise all night without the rest of the house hearing it?

Yes. Each Sonos speaker can play its own source, so the nursery speaker plays white noise or lullabies while the kitchen plays something else, as long as nobody adds the nursery to a whole-house group. We name the room clearly, leave it out of the groups we set up for parties, and show you how to set a sleep timer from the Now Playing screen, which Sonos says turns the music off automatically after the time you choose — or how to leave it playing all night if that’s what helps.

We also set a volume limit on that speaker, so an accidental tap in the app can’t blast the room, and place it on a high shelf or wall bracket with the power cord out of reach of a crib or a curious toddler. When the nursery becomes a big kid’s room, the same speaker gets a morning alarm instead.

Can we stop explicit lyrics playing in the kids’ rooms?

Partly, and it is worth knowing the limits before you rely on it. Sonos’s Parental Controls include a filter for explicit content, but Sonos says it applies to your entire system, not to one room, and it is password-protected through your Sonos account. It also only works with services that support filtering through Sonos — Sonos currently lists Amazon Music (US only), Apple Music and YouTube Music.

So in practice we switch the system-wide filter on if you want it, and for other services we set up the service’s own family or child settings on the accounts the kids use. Parents keep their own accounts on their own phones. We explain the arrangement at handover so nobody is surprised when a song plays in the kitchen but not in the bedroom.

The older house in the river city

We moved from a new Wallkill subdivision to an 1890s Newburgh row house — what changes for Sonos?

Almost everything about how the wiring is done, and very little about the speakers. A newer subdivision house gives you open basement ceilings, drywall, coax and an attic; a brick row house in Newburgh’s East End gives you plaster walls and ceilings, brick party walls and no builder wiring at all. Sonos’s own interference table rates brick as a medium obstacle to wireless signal and plaster as high, so a router in the front parlor may barely reach the back bedroom.

The plan therefore turns wireless-first: free-standing Era speakers and a soundbar rather than cut-in ceilings, a wired access point on each floor fed through a closet or the cellar, and in-ceiling speakers only where a ceiling is being replaced anyway. The street side of a house in the historic district is also subject to the city’s Architectural Review Commission, so outdoor speakers belong at the back. Pricing reflects the walls: our plaster surcharge is $45 per bracket and brick or stone is $65.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Sonos Installation in Orange County

We could not view Google’s AI Overview for these searches from our build environment, so this section answers claims that cost guides, forums and answer engines commonly repeat, and corrects each one with what we find in Orange County houses and what Sonos and the codes actually say.

“A new house comes ready for Sonos”

The assumption is understandable: a house built in the last few years has network jacks, coax outlets and a media panel, so surely it is wired for whole-house audio. Builder low-voltage packages are generally designed around TV and internet, so speaker cable to the rooms where you actually want music is usually there only if it was ordered as an option.

Even the wiring that exists is uneven. Jacks are sometimes never terminated at the panel end, coax runs pass through splitters that weaken them, and the provider’s router sits in a metal enclosure that Sonos’s interference table would rate as the worst place for a wireless signal.

The fix is not to rewire the house. It is an hour with a tester and the panel door open, followed by a short plan that uses what passes and adds only what is missing.

“A mesh system solves Wi-Fi in a big suburban house”

Mesh routers are a real improvement over a single router in the basement, and Sonos supports them. But Sonos’s system requirements add conditions: if you use a wired Sonos setup with a mesh system, the wired Sonos product has to connect to the primary mesh node, and range extenders are unsupported altogether.

In a 3,000-square-foot colonial with a bonus room over the garage, a mesh satellite placed in the hallway may be relaying a weak signal from the basement, and every Sonos product behind it inherits the weakness.

Mesh works best in these houses when at least one satellite is wired back to the router, which is often exactly what a tested coax run or one new cable makes possible.

“In-ceiling speakers are a weekend DIY job”

Sonos itself says architectural speakers often require a professional installer. In a newer Orange County house the reasons are specific: engineered I-joists that tolerate holes only in the web, a top-floor ceiling that forms part of the attic air barrier, and in townhouses a fire-rated wall that must not be cut.

None of that makes the job difficult for someone who knows it. It does mean a hole in the wrong place costs more than the speakers: a damaged joist flange, a draft from the attic, or a breach in a separation wall that an inspector or a future buyer will notice.

We scan, pilot-drill, follow the joist maker’s chart, seal top-floor openings and keep away from the shared wall. That is most of what a professional installation is buying.

“Whole-home audio costs $500 to $1,000 per room”

That range appears in Angi’s cost guide for additional zones or multi-room audio, and it is a national average across every kind of house. It can’t tell you what a kitchen pair costs in a house where the basement ceiling is still open, compared with the same pair in a finished house with no attic access.

In Orange County the biggest price swing comes from timing. Cable run during a basement finish or before drywall in a new build costs a fraction of the same run fished through finished walls later.

So we publish the lines we can fix in advance — $125 for Sonos setup, $129 to wall-mount a soundbar, $99 for a cable run of up to 25 feet — and quote ceiling and outdoor zones in writing after seeing the house.

“Any weatherproof speaker will do by the pool”

Many portable speakers are splash-resistant, and many owners carry one out to the pool. Sonos is clear about the difference: speakers that aren’t waterproof may be damaged if exposed to water or left outside for extended periods.

The speakers that stay outside year-round around an Orange County pool face chlorinated or salt spray in summer and freezing winters. Sonos rates its outdoor architectural pair IP66 and tests it for humidity, salt spray, temperature and UV, from −13° to 149°F.

Those are the speakers we bolt up for good. A portable can still come out for the afternoon, as long as it goes back inside.

“Parental controls block explicit songs in the kids’ rooms”

Sonos does have a filter for explicit content. What answer engines tend to leave out is that Sonos says it applies to the entire system, not one room, and that it works only with services that support filtering through Sonos — currently Amazon Music in the US, Apple Music and YouTube Music.

For a family that streams on another service, or wants different rules in the kitchen and the kids’ rooms, the Sonos filter alone won’t do it.

We combine the Sonos setting with the music service’s own child or family settings, give the kids their own accounts where the service allows it, and explain the arrangement so it is clear which control does what.

“The soundbar can go anywhere the TV goes, even over the fireplace”

Over-the-fireplace TVs are common in Orange County family rooms, and so are questions about putting the soundbar on the mantel. Sonos’s Arc Ultra guide asks for the bar not to be enclosed in a cabinet or corner, for 4 inches below the TV and for a foot of space at each end.

Heat matters too. Sonos community answers cite the Arc’s operating range as 32° to 104°F, and air above a running gas fireplace can exceed that.

So we measure with the fire on before mounting, put the bar on the wall rather than the mantel when we do mount there, and often recommend another wall for the TV and soundbar altogether.

Field Notes

Field Notes From Orange County Sonos Jobs

General installer practice: the order in which we look at a recently built Orange County house on a first visit. This describes our routine, not a particular customer’s home.

We start at the media panel. The door comes off, every cable gets a temporary label, and the ones nobody can identify get a tone. Within half an hour we know which coax runs are clean enough for a network link, which jacks were never connected and where the router really ought to be. That list decides the network plan before anyone talks about speakers.

Next is the basement ceiling, if it is still open. We read the stamp on the joists, note which way they run under the rooms that want ceiling speakers, and photograph the plumber’s and electrician’s holes so ours stay clear of them. Then, if there is a hatch, the attic: insulation depth, where the trusses land, and whether a top-floor speaker can be sealed from above.

Last is the kitchen table, where we ask how the house will change — a baby, a basement finish, a pool — and sketch the floors with the rooms, the Amps and the cable runs marked. That sketch becomes the written quote, with the stages you can do now and the ones that can wait.

FAQ

Orange County Sonos Installation FAQ

Can the ceiling speakers line up with the recessed lights the builder installed?
+

Usually, and it is worth asking for. Builders lay recessed lights out on a grid, so we measure that grid and place the speakers on the same lines or centered between lights, which makes the ceiling look planned rather than added to. Sonos's guidance allows the left and right speakers to sit anywhere from six to ten feet apart, which leaves room to follow the lighting layout without hurting the sound.

Will a ceiling speaker interfere with the smoke alarms nearby?
+

Not electrically, but it should not crowd them. Newer Orange County houses usually have hardwired, interconnected smoke alarms, and their makers publish placement guidance. We keep speakers clear of the alarms so nothing blocks them, never move, cover or disconnect a hardwired alarm, and leave any change to an alarm's position to a licensed electrician.

Is the router our internet provider installed good enough for Sonos?
+

Often it is, once it is in the right place. Sonos's system requirements ask for a router that offers 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi rather than 5 GHz only, WPA or WPA2 Personal security rather than Enterprise, and every product and the app on the same network, not a guest network. Most provider routers meet that. The usual problem is where the provider put it, in a basement or a closed media panel, so we move it or add a wired access point before suggesting a new router.

Do you need our Wi-Fi password, and what happens to it after the visit?
+

We need it while we work, because every Sonos product has to join your network. You can type it in yourself on our tablet or phone, and we do not write it down or keep it after the visit. If the builder or the internet provider's installer left the default password on the router, we suggest changing it before we start and joining the speakers to the new one.

Can you come while the house is still under construction, if the builder allows it?
+

Yes, when the builder agrees in writing and the site is safe to work in. Speaker and network cable is easiest to run after framing and before insulation and drywall, so that is the window we aim for. We coordinate with the site supervisor, label every cable end, and come back to fit speakers and set up Sonos after you close.

What happens if a planned ceiling speaker spot lands on a joist or a duct?
+

We move the speaker before we cut, not after. Every opening starts with a scan and a small pilot hole, and if a joist, duct or pipe sits where the speaker was drawn we shift it to the nearest clear bay and show you the new spot. Moving a speaker over by a joist bay rarely changes how the pair sounds, and it is far better than cutting into framing or ductwork.

Do you install Sonos in model homes and sales offices for builders?
+

Yes. A model home or sales center usually wants background music in the kitchen and living areas, a TV wall with a soundbar, and a system that sales staff can run from one tablet without logging into personal accounts. We set it up that way, document it, and can return to move or reset the system when the model is sold.

Can the ceiling speakers you wire be used with a different amplifier by a future owner?
+

Yes. In-ceiling speakers on a Sonos Amp are ordinary passive speakers on ordinary speaker cable, so a future owner can connect them to another amplifier or receiver. We label each cable at the equipment end by room and left or right, and leave that labeling written down so it survives a sale of the house.
