The Neighborhood

Why Astoria Properties Need Smart Lock Installation Planned Around the People, Not Just the Door

The numbers explain the job before we ever see a door. The NYU Furman Center’s profile of the Astoria community district — Queens Community District 1, which also takes in part of Long Island City — puts homeownership at 18.5 percent in 2024, against 32.7 percent citywide. Roughly four households in five rent. Its 2025 sales figures add the other half of the picture: 204 two-to-four-family buildings changed hands that year against just 47 one-family houses. Put those together and you get the Astoria most of our customers live in: a rented floor of a two- or three-family house, an apartment in a walk-up of a dozen units, or a share in either, with an owner who may live downstairs, around the corner or nowhere nearby.

A lock in that setting has a different job from a lock on a suburban house. It is not guarding one family for twenty years. It is guarding a changing list of people: the leaseholder, the roommate who arrived last spring, the friend who sublet a room for the summer, the partner who moved in and may move out, the owner’s spare key, the super’s. Furman’s profile also counts 38.5 percent of Astoria households as single-person, and the district’s rental vacancy rate at 3.4 percent; small homes, tight rentals and people sharing to afford them mean the cast behind a door changes again and again. Every change used to mean copying keys and hoping they came back. A smart lock turns it into editing a list.

The law has already drawn the outline of that list, and it is not the outline most people expect. New York’s Real Property Law section 235-f says a landlord may not limit an apartment to the people named on the lease: a single tenant may share with immediate family, one additional occupant and that occupant’s dependent children, and must tell the landlord the occupant’s name within thirty days. New York City’s Administrative Code section 26-521 makes it unlawful to change or disable an entrance lock without giving a key to anyone who has lived there thirty consecutive days or more. And the Multiple Dwelling Law’s right for a tenant to add a lock of their own applies to multiple dwellings, which the same law defines as buildings with three or more families — not to the two-family houses that make up so much of Astoria’s side streets. We plan each lock inside those lines.

The doors that get people into the building matter as much as the one on the apartment. Patch reported in March 2021 that a single burglar worked his way through five Astoria apartment buildings in three days: he pried open a lobby door near 33rd Street and 28th Avenue, got into a building near 31st Street and 28th Road through a side door, walked out of another near 29th Street and 31st Avenue with a rent check a tenant had left in front of her apartment door for the landlord, and tried a fourth near 18th Street and 27th Avenue. CBS New York reported in 2019 that a burglar hit two apartments in the same building near 27th Street and Hoyt Avenue South, with a neighbour saying his lock had been picked; when CBS2 returned the following week, the owner was outside helping to change the locks. Neither story is about smart locks. Both are about shared doors that let strangers in, and old keyed cylinders that someone could get past.

Then there is everything that moves around the door: the elevated N and W along 31st Street, which has run above Astoria since 1917; the restaurants and cafés along Ditmars Boulevard, 30th Avenue, Broadway and Steinway Street, where staff come and go by the season; the production offices clustered around Kaufman Astoria Studios; and the new waterfront towers at Halletts Point, where the building may supply its own entry app. A good Astoria lock plan starts by asking who uses the door and who decides, and only then by choosing hardware.

What we look at on an Astoria door before we quote

The cylinder nobody can account for. In a rented floor or a share, the honest answer to “who has a key?” is usually “we’re not sure.” Keys get copied for roommates, partners, cleaners and friends, and they do not all come back when people leave. A smart lock that keeps the old cylinder — including the interior retrofit units that are so popular with renters — still opens for every one of those keys. So on most Astoria installs we fit the smart deadbolt with a new cylinder, give the household its keys, and arrange the owner’s duplicate the way the lease or the law requires. Where the owner insists the original lock stays, we say plainly that a retrofit adds convenience but does nothing about old keys.

Who may decide on which door. In a building of three or more apartments, the Multiple Dwelling Law lets a tenant add a lock of their own, no more than three inches in circumference, alongside the owner’s, provided the owner gets a duplicate key on request. The city’s Housing Maintenance Code separately obliges the owner to supply a working key lock on every apartment entrance door. In a one- or two-family house neither the add-a-lock right nor a multiple-dwelling rule reaches the tenant’s door, so the lease and the owner’s written agreement govern. We ask which kind of building you are in before we quote, because the answer changes the paperwork.

Doors that have moved. Older Astoria houses settle, and doors on the top floor of a house often drop on their hinges or bind in summer. A motorised bolt that rubs on the strike drains its batteries and throws jam warnings, so we check the reveal around the door, tighten or shim the hinges, and move the strike before the lock goes on. If the door needs more than adjustment, we say so before you buy anything.

Leaving needs nothing but a hand. Whatever goes on the outside, the inside of a home door we fit has a plain thumb-turn or lever, so a guest, a child or a half-asleep roommate can get out without knowing a code or finding a phone. HPD’s fire-safety page states that double-cylinder locks are illegal on apartment doors, and we will not fit a model that needs a key or code from the inside.

Your Questions Answered

Smart Lock Installation in Astoria: Questions Answered

These are the questions Astoria renters, roommates, house owners and business owners bring us, grouped by the kind of door and the kind of household behind it. Prices, timing and coverage are in the FAQ further down. Nothing here is legal advice: where we mention a law, we name it so you can read it yourself or take it to a lawyer, a tenant organisation or the owner of your building.

Sharing an apartment

We added a roommate who isn’t on the lease — do we have to tell the landlord before giving them a code?

The code is a household decision; the notice is a legal duty that exists whether or not you have a smart lock. New York’s Real Property Law section 235-f says a lease signed by one tenant must be read to allow the tenant, their immediate family, one additional occupant and that occupant’s dependent children, as long as the tenant or the tenant’s spouse lives there as a primary residence. Where two or more people signed the lease, the total of tenants and occupants may not exceed the number of tenants on the lease. Section 235-f also requires the tenant to tell the landlord an occupant’s name within thirty days after that person moves in, or within thirty days of the landlord asking.

Nothing in that section mentions locks, so giving the new roommate their own code does not need the landlord’s sign-off; giving them a code instead of a copied key is simply the tidier way to do what you would have done anyway. What a code does add is a date: the lock’s app records when the person was enrolled, which is a useful note to keep beside the name you send the landlord. In a building of three or more apartments, the Multiple Dwelling Law also lets the owner ask for a duplicate key to any lock a tenant adds, which is a key, not a code, and should be handled exactly as before.

One more line of 235-f matters for the lock’s settings: an occupant does not gain a right to stay on if the tenant leaves, unless the landlord agrees in writing. That is a good reason to make the leaseholder one of the lock’s administrators and the roommate an ordinary user, and to write down who would take over the account if the leaseholder moved out first.

Our roommate’s last day is the 31st — can we switch their code off now, before they’ve actually gone?

Not while they still live there. New York City Administrative Code section 26-521 makes it unlawful for any person to change or disable the lock on an entrance door without giving a key to an occupant who has lawfully lived in the unit for thirty consecutive days or more, and section 26-523 makes a violation a misdemeanour with civil penalties of $1,000 to $10,000 per violation. Deleting the only credential a current resident has is the electronic version of changing the lock on them. We will not do it, and we would rather you didn’t either.

What works is setting the end in advance. Agree the move-out date in writing — a text exchange is better than nothing — and put that date on the departing roommate’s code so it stops working at the end of the agreed day without anyone pressing a button in anger. Ask for their physical key back the same day, remove their phone access once they have carried out the last box, and, if a key does not come back, have the cylinder changed rather than hoping. If a new roommate is arriving, their code can start on the morning of the 1st.

If someone refuses to leave, that is a matter for the courts and, in a share, for a lawyer or a tenant organisation; it is not something a locksmith or an installer can or should settle at the door. A lock with a clear history of who was enrolled and when can help everyone remember what was agreed, which is about as far as hardware should go.

Can each roommate keep their own comings and goings private from the others?

Partly, and it is worth deciding before the lock goes on rather than after the first argument. Smart lock apps sort people into roles. Administrators can add and remove people and usually see the full entry history; ordinary users can open the door and, depending on the app, may see little or nothing of anyone else’s activity. In a share, that means whoever holds the administrator role can, in principle, see when everyone else comes home.

The fairest set-ups we see in Astoria shares give the administrator role to two people openly agreed by the household, switch off per-person unlock notifications that nobody actually needs, and keep the notifications that protect everyone — low battery, jams, the door left unlocked, wrong codes tried. Some apps let administrators choose which events send alerts, so the history exists for when something goes wrong without anyone being pinged every time a roommate walks in.

Write the house rule down in a sentence or two: who the administrators are, what the history is for and what it is not for. A lock that records entries is a security tool; it should not quietly turn into a way for one roommate to watch another.

Rented floors in two-family houses

The owner of our two-family says the last tenants’ keys “probably” all came back and won’t change the cylinder — what can we do?

Start with what kind of building it is. The Multiple Dwelling Law calls a building with no more than two families a private dwelling, and the tenant’s right under section 51-c to add a lock of one’s own applies to multiple dwellings, which start at three families. In a two-family house, then, the lease and the owner’s agreement decide what goes on your door. The city’s Housing Maintenance Code separately requires the owner of a dwelling to provide a key lock on each apartment’s entrance door and at least one key, so the conversation is about which lock and which keys, not whether you get one.

Owners usually say yes when the request is cheap, reversible and leaves them a key. Ask in writing, offer to pay for a new cylinder in the existing lock or for a smart deadbolt with a new cylinder, and offer the owner a duplicate key the same day. Make the point that an interior retrofit unit — the kind that turns your existing thumb-turn — would not help, because every old key would still open the door. One r/astoria thread from a tenant whose apartment between the 36th Avenue and Broadway stations was broken into two weeks after move-in drew a reply asking, in effect, whether the landlord had been there long enough to recognise previous tenants and whether a locksmith had been called yet. That is exactly the question to settle on day one.

If the owner still says no, do not change the lock yourself on a two-family door; keep the written request, which shows you raised it, and talk to a tenant organisation about your options. We will happily send the owner a short scope explaining what we would do, what it costs and what the owner keeps, because that letter often settles it.

The owner downstairs offered to put our apartment lock on the same app as the front door — should we accept?

It is convenient, and it hands the owner more than a key. Whoever holds the administrator role on an app account can usually add and delete codes and read the entry history for every lock on that account. On a shared account, that means the owner could see each time you or your guests came home and could change your codes without asking. The city’s Tenant Data Privacy Act limits what owners of multiple dwellings may collect through smart access systems; in a two-family house, your protection is mostly whatever you and the owner agree.

Our usual recommendation in an Astoria two-family is two accounts. The owner’s front or side door stays on the owner’s account, with a code for each person on your floor. Your apartment door goes on your household’s own account, with the owner holding a mechanical key or a separate code if the lease calls for access. Each side keeps control of its own door, and neither can read the other’s history.

If you do decide to share an account, settle it in writing: who the administrators are, whether the owner may add or delete codes on your door, and that the history is not checked unless something has gone wrong. Clear expectations matter more in a house where you share a stair and a mailbox with the person holding the account.

Street doors, side doors and small buildings

What the 2021 five-building pattern says about street and side doors

The March 2021 pattern Patch reported is a tidy summary of how strangers reach apartment doors in Astoria. On the first day, the man pried open a lobby door near 33rd Street and 28th Avenue and took a parcel, then robbed an apartment near 31st Street and 28th Road after coming in through the building’s side door. Later the same day he walked into a building near 29th Street and 31st Avenue and took a rent check a tenant had left outside her apartment door for the landlord. Two days later cameras caught him inside a building near 18th Street and 27th Avenue, where he failed to get into any apartment, and hours after that he took $150 from an apartment near 31st Drive and 23rd Street.

The lessons are mostly the owner’s, not the tenant’s. A street door that can be pried needs a reinforced strike and frame before it needs electronics. A side door that is supposed to be locked needs to close and latch on its own, and a door-position contact that reports when it is propped. Where an owner wants to know who comes through, a keypad or fob reader with an electric strike gives every resident their own credential and the owner a log, at $1,575–$2,625 per door at the Queens rate.

For residents, the lesson is simpler: the hall outside your door is not a safe place for rent checks, spare keys or parcels, and your own apartment door is the one you control. A smart deadbolt with a fresh cylinder that relocks itself behind you is the part of this you can fix this week.

Picked locks and changed locks: the 2019 Hoyt Avenue South break-ins

CBS New York reported in September 2019 on two burglaries on August 29 in the same building near 27th Street and Hoyt Avenue South. Police said the burglar took $13,000 in jewellery from one apartment; a neighbour said his own lock had been picked and that passports and a safe were among the things taken. When CBS2 returned the following week, the owner was outside helping to change the locks and arranging for more cameras. The same report said burglaries in the 114th Precinct were up 12 percent year to date, from 117 to 131.

A smart deadbolt does not make a door unpickable: most keep a key cylinder for emergencies, and that cylinder is only as good as its grade. What it changes is how often anyone needs a key at all. When the household uses codes or fingerprints day to day, the mechanical key can live in a drawer rather than on a ring that gets lent and copied, and a lock that auto-locks behind each person removes the commonest weakness of all, the door that someone forgot to lock.

When a building has had a break-in, owners tend to change every cylinder at once. That is the right moment for tenants to ask for the apartment door to take a smart deadbolt with a new cylinder rather than another plain lock with a new batch of keys to copy.

Fobs instead of keys on the street door

In an r/astoria thread about package theft, one resident wrote that their building had replaced the front-door key with a key fob to deter porch pirates and that it seemed to have helped. That matches what we see: a credential that can be cancelled is worth more than one that can be copied. A lost fob or a departing tenant’s code is removed in a minute; a lost street-door key means either re-keying the building or living with a key in a stranger’s pocket.

For a small building, the trade-offs are cost, power and who runs it. A keypad or fob reader with an electric strike needs low-voltage wiring and a power supply, and someone — usually the owner or the managing agent — has to add and remove people promptly. If tenants’ data is involved, the Tenant Data Privacy Act sets rules on consent, retention and what may be collected.

Mail carriers and buzzers still have to work after the change, and residents who prefer a code to a fob should be able to have one. We plan both before the strike is ordered.

Restaurants, studios and small businesses

Our production office near the studios is rented for one season — can everyone’s door code run off the crew list and stop on wrap day?

Yes, and it is one of the tidiest uses of a smart lock we see in Astoria. Productions working out of offices around Kaufman Astoria Studios and the blocks nearby tend to take space for a fixed run, staff it from a list that changes week by week, and hand it back at the end. A keypad lever on the office door, with the production office coordinator and one other person as administrators, lets each crew member have a personal code with an end date set to wrap day. Nobody is handed a key that walks off to the next job.

We enrol from your written list in one sitting, label each code with the person’s name and department, and show the coordinator how to add day-players or remove someone who leaves early. Rooms that hold equipment or petty cash get their own lock with a much shorter list. Two things to settle with the landlord first: whether you may change the hardware at all, and whether the landlord needs a key or a code during your tenancy. We write both into the scope so the paperwork is done before the first call time.

We rent our rehearsal room by the hour — can each booking get a code that works only for its slot?

Most current smart locks can hold codes limited to a date and a time window, so a renter’s code can begin a few minutes before the booking and end a few minutes after it. Doing that by hand for every booking works for a handful a week. For more than that, some lock platforms can create codes from a booking system automatically, and some cannot; whether yours can depends on both the lock and the booking tool, so we check that pairing before you buy rather than after.

The room itself needs a few house rules built into the hardware: the door opens from inside without a code, the lock relocks when the door shuts, and storage cupboards or the office stay on separate locks with separate codes. A door-position contact will tell you if the last renter left the door propped for the next one.

Keep a fallback for the night the internet drops at 9 p.m.: a staff member with an administrator code that works at the keypad without any connection, and the mechanical key somewhere it can be reached.

When northwest Queens loses power

Astoria has lived through a long outage before. Baruch College’s NYC data archive records that the 2006 Queens blackout began on July 17 and continued for eight days until power was restored on July 25; a Pace law review article notes that on the worst days Con Edison lost twelve of the twenty-two feeders in its Long Island City network. A household that went keyless needs to know what keeps working in a week like that.

Battery-powered smart deadbolts keep working on their own batteries: codes, fingerprints and the inside thumb-turn carry on as normal. What stops is anything that needs the apartment’s power or internet — the Wi-Fi bridge, the router, and therefore remote unlocking and phone alerts. On a building’s street door, a powered electric strike does what it was specified to do: fail-safe strikes release, fail-secure strikes stay locked and rely on the key. Buzzers go silent either way.

The plan is simple and worth writing down: fresh batteries when the lock asks for them, one mechanical key held by someone who does not live in the apartment, a written note of which street doors are fail-safe and which are fail-secure, and an understanding that remote access is a convenience, not the only way in.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Smart Lock Installation in Astoria

How this section was written: the tools used to build this page had no access to Google’s AI Overview, so nothing below quotes it directly. Instead, each heading is a claim that keeps appearing in lock makers’ marketing, DIY articles and cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr — the sources answer engines draw on — followed by how it holds up on Astoria doors.

“Renters should pick a lock that fits over the deadbolt so the landlord’s key still works”

Retrofit locks are sold to renters on exactly that promise; one maker describes its retrofit as leaving the outside of the door untouched. That is a real advantage where an owner forbids any change to the door.

In Astoria, though, the commonest worry on move-in is not the landlord’s key. It is the keys nobody can account for: copies made by earlier tenants, their roommates and their friends. A retrofit leaves every one of those keys working.

Where the owner agrees, a smart deadbolt with a new cylinder, plus a duplicate key for the owner, solves both problems at once. Where the owner does not agree, a cylinder change in the existing lock is the cheaper fix to ask for first.

“A smart lock lets you decide exactly who lives with you”

A lock controls who can open a door. It does not decide who has a right to be behind it. In New York those rights come from the lease and from statutes such as Real Property Law 235-f, which lets a single tenant share with one additional occupant, and the city’s unlawful-eviction rule, which protects anyone who has lived in a unit for thirty days or more from having the lock changed on them.

In a roommate apartment, that means a code cannot be used to settle a dispute. Deleting a current resident’s only credential can amount to the same thing as changing the lock on them.

Use the lock to carry out what the household has agreed: a dated end on a departing roommate’s code, a dated start for the next one. Use a lawyer or a tenant organisation for anything the household has not agreed.

“Tenants in New York can always add their own lock”

That is true in a lot of New York apartments, and it is the rule most articles quote. Multiple Dwelling Law section 51-c lets a tenant add a lock of their own, no more than three inches in circumference, provided the owner gets a duplicate key on request.

But section 51-c applies to multiple dwellings, which the same law defines as buildings housing three or more families, and it excludes buildings run by a municipal housing authority. Much of Astoria’s housing is two-family houses, where the right does not apply, and the Astoria Houses campus is a NYCHA development.

So the first question we ask a renter is how many apartments the building has. In a two-family, we write a short scope for the owner; in NYCHA, residents go to the Housing Authority first.

“Smart locks stop break-ins”

A smart lock is good at one thing: making sure a door is locked and that only enrolled people open it. Astoria’s own burglary reports show how much else matters. When the 114th Precinct warned residents in 2014 about a string of break-ins between 28th and 31st Avenues, the Queens Gazette reported that entry came through open fire-escape windows, unlocked doors, or air conditioners removed from fire-escape windows.

The four restaurant break-ins QNS reported in July 2025 came through windows too. A locked door is necessary; it is not sufficient.

The precinct’s own advice in 2014 was to fit deadbolts on all doors and locks on all windows. We fit the door side; window gates and window contacts are a separate job, and NYC rules forbid key-locked gates on fire-escape windows.

“The hardware is the whole job”

Testers at one home-improvement site needed little more than a Phillips screwdriver and about half an hour, on a door made for the job. Astoria doors are often older than that, re-drilled for several earlier locks, and hung in houses that have settled.

The hardware is the quick part. Swapping the cylinder, moving a strike, adjusting hinges so the bolt does not drag, testing the latch and the inside release, and then enrolling four roommates and two administrators properly takes longer.

A straightforward Astoria door with three or four people to enrol is usually a visit of an hour or two; a door that needs work or a long list of users takes longer, and the walkthrough tells you which.

“You can open your door from anywhere with the app”

Only while the lock can reach the internet. Bluetooth locks need a bridge close to the door — August asks for 10 to 15 feet — and the bridge needs power and Wi-Fi. In a long floor-through with the router in the back room, that link is the first thing we test.

It is also the first thing to fail in an outage. During the eight-day 2006 Queens blackout, any bridge or router in an affected apartment would have been dark, and remote access and alerts with it.

Codes, fingerprints and the inside thumb-turn keep working on battery power. Treat remote access as a convenience and make sure every person on the roster can open the door at the door.

Three of us share the apartment — can each roommate pay a share of the invoice directly?
+

We issue one invoice per job, made out to whoever books it, and the deposit is taken from that person. Roommates usually settle shares among themselves; if you want the scope letter to list who contributed toward the lock, tell us before the visit and we will add a line saying so.

Does the landlord need to be at the apartment while you install?
+

No, unless your lease or the owner asks for it. Someone over 18 from the household must let us in and stay until the lock is tested. If the owner has agreed to the work in writing, bring that email to the visit; if the owner wants to see the finished door or collect a duplicate key, we can time the end of the visit for that.

Could taking the smart lock off at the end of our lease cost us part of the security deposit?
+

It can if the door is left with extra holes or a different cylinder than the owner expects. That is why we bag and label the original deadbolt at installation, reuse the existing bore where we can, and put the original hardware back on a short return visit when you move out. Photographs of both faces of the door on day one help settle any argument later.

Can the low-battery warning go to everyone in the apartment, not just the person who set it up?
+

On most connected locks, yes. Notifications are set per user in the app, so every roommate with app access can switch on battery and jam alerts for themselves. People who only use a code get no app alerts, so we also show everyone what the keypad's own low-battery signal looks like and where the spare batteries are kept.

Half of us use iPhones and half use Android phones — is there a lock that works for both?
+

Yes. Most major lock brands have their own app on both platforms, and locks that support Matter can be shared with more than one smart-home system at a time; one manufacturer's guide says up to five. A Thread-based Matter lock also needs a compatible border router in the apartment. People without a smartphone simply use a code or a fingerprint.

Do we get to try every code and the key before the balance is due?
+

Yes.
