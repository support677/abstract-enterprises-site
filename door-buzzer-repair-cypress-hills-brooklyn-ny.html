The Neighborhood

Why Cypress Hills Properties Need Door Buzzer Repair

Cypress Hills is one of the few Brooklyn neighborhoods where a buzzer technician can work on a 1928 riser in the morning and a 2025 cloud panel in the afternoon without leaving the zip code, and that spread is the main reason repair calls from here are so varied. The older half of the housing stock is large. openigloo's index of pre-war buildings in Cypress Hills runs to 1,372 addresses, from walk-ups over the stores on Fulton Street to houses on Etna, Ridgewood and Sunnyside and the six-story buildings that climb toward Highland Park. Two of those six-story buildings show how the entry systems in this group have aged. The building at 2 Elton Street, built in 1928 with 53 apartments, listed 25 open violations in its openigloo record, 13 of them Class C. The 87-apartment building on Highland Boulevard from 1940 listed 197 open violations, which openigloo works out to 2.26 per apartment against a city average of 0.81. Neither number is about the intercom alone, but both describe buildings where repairs pile up, and the entry system is the one repair every resident and every visitor touches every day.

The houses are the second group. Cypress Hills grew as a late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century suburb, and the side streets still hold detached and semi-detached frame houses, brick rows and two- to four-family houses with a cellar under each one. Their bells were simple when they were wired: a transformer, a button per household, a chime or buzzer upstairs and, in the multi-family houses, a release that fires a strike in the shared front door. A century of owners has added to those circuits, re-sided the facades and finished the cellars, and most of the faults we find in the houses are where somebody's renovation met the bell wire.

The third group is new, and it is growing fast. The East New York Neighborhood Plan, passed in 2016, rezoned hundreds of parcels on the Cypress Hills and East New York border around Atlantic Avenue, Conduit Boulevard and Pennsylvania Avenue, and New York YIMBY reported that it is expected to create nearly 6,500 new apartments by 2030. Chestnut Commons at 110 Dinsmore Place opened in October 2022 with 275 apartments. Phipps Houses' 14-story building at 254 Euclid Avenue offered 369 rent-stabilized apartments in its 2025 lottery. An eight-story building with 51 apartments at 2795 Fulton Street opened its lottery that September. Buildings like these have video or app-based panels, card or fob readers, package rooms and a management office that controls the admin account, and their first generation of entry hardware is now reaching the age when the first parts wear out and the installer's warranty runs out.

Tenants notice all of it. A former tenant's review of 2 Elton Street on openigloo says plainly, "The Intercom system for the entrance door does not work." A review of the Highland Boulevard building lists being "charged for keys" among its complaints. Those are the everyday costs of a failing entry system in any building: residents walk down to let visitors in, couriers leave packages outside, keys multiply, and sooner or later someone wedges the street door open. Owners who want the entrance covered while the buzzer is being fixed often pair the repair with a camera over the door, and we keep the two systems separate in every report.

Pre-War Elevator Buildings

Decades of Patches on One Riser: Repairing Intercoms in Cypress Hills' Six-Story Buildings Below Highland Park

The six-story buildings on the streets that rise toward Highland Park and the cemetery edge were built for a few dozen to almost a hundred households each, and almost none of them still has the entry system it opened with. A building from 1928 or 1940 may have gone through a bell-and-buzzer plate, an audio intercom, a second audio intercom and a directory panel, each one hung on the cable left by the one before. That history is what a repair in one of these buildings is really up against. The cable in the walls usually holds up well, but every generation added splices, swapped stations and moved the supply, and nobody wrote any of it down.

The symptoms sort the fault before we open anything. If the whole building went quiet together, the cause is shared: the supply or its fuse, the panel's own electronics or the common audio pair. If one vertical line of apartments stopped ringing, the fault is in that line's riser or in a junction box on one floor where it was spliced during an apartment renovation. If one apartment is dead, the station or the short run from the riser to it is the place to look. And if every handset in the building hums or crackles, a single shorted or water-damaged station is often loading the shared pair for everyone, so pulling one bad handset off the line can bring the rest back. Brick Underground's 2019 guide to intercom repair in New York quoted Daniel Vasquez of Paragon Security and Locksmith summing up where most of these failures sit: "It's all in the wire connections."

Access decides how quickly that can be done. A six-story building has a lobby, a cellar and a lot of occupied apartments, and the break can be in any of them. Before the visit, we ask the super or managing agent for three things: a list of which apartments fail and which work, someone with the cellar and meter-room keys, and two or three residents on each failing line who will be home during the arrival window. With those, a technician works from the lobby to the supply and then up the riser floor by floor, testing at each junction instead of knocking on every door. Without them, the same repair can stretch across two visits, and we would rather say so when booking than after.

When the directory is newer than everything behind it

Many of these buildings replaced their button plates with a directory panel at some point, where a visitor dials a code or scrolls a list of names. The panel is newer, but the stations upstairs are often a mix: original handsets in some apartments, replacements bought by tenants in others, and a few apartments with no working station at all. A newer panel calling a riser of mixed stations is one of the most common reasons "some apartments work and some don't" in Cypress Hills' older buildings. The answer is rarely another panel. It is usually matching stations to the system, remaking a splice or two on the riser, and checking that each code dials the pair it is supposed to, since codes drift out of line every time an apartment turns over and a new name goes in without anyone checking the pair.

Houses on the Hill Streets

A Century of Owners on One Bell Circuit: Buzzer and Doorbell Repair in Cypress Hills Houses

The houses between Jamaica Avenue and Atlantic Avenue are where Cypress Hills looks most like the suburb it started as. Brownstoner's architecture writers have profiled Queen Anne frame houses from around 1900 on Arlington Avenue and the developer-built brick rows of Sunnyside Avenue with their tile roofs and deep porches, and listings in the neighborhood describe detached wood-frame houses with side driveways and rear garages and solid two-family houses on streets such as Nichols Avenue. What they share, for a buzzer technician, is a cellar under the house, a front door or porch door at the end of a run of steps, and a bell circuit that has been touched by every owner since it was put in.

The typical fault list in these houses is short and practical. The transformer in the cellar is old, small, or both, and it has been asked to run a newer chime, a wired video doorbell and, in a two- or three-family house, a strike in the shared front door. It reads normal at rest and collapses when the strike pulls. A button on the porch post or door frame has let water in for years and corroded at the contacts. A splice on the cellar ceiling was disturbed when the cellar was finished, the boiler was replaced or a plumber worked along the joists. And in houses that were re-sided, the button may have been remounted on vinyl or aluminum siding over the original shingles or clapboard, where it works loose and pulls on the wire every time someone presses it hard.

Multi-family houses add a question of who rings where. Over the decades a second or third button was added for a new household, its wire run to whatever transformer was handy, and its release spliced into a strike that already served the first floor. The result shows up as a button that rings two apartments, a chime that rings in the wrong household, or a release that works from the parlor floor and not from the top floor. Repairing that starts with a map: someone in each apartment, every button pressed and noted, every release tested and every pair traced from the plate to the cellar. Then we measure the transformer with the strike held, because an undersized one gives itself away only under load.

Steps, porches and the door at the top

Many Cypress Hills houses sit above the sidewalk, some with long flights of steps up the slope, and that changes how a bell circuit wears. The button and the strike are often at the top of the steps on a porch that takes rain, snow and salt, while the transformer is a full story or more below in the cellar, so the wire between them is long and passes through a porch floor, a door frame or an outside wall. We check the run where it enters the house first, because that is where water and movement work on it, and we look at whether the porch door's latch still meets the strike squarely. A frame house shifts with the seasons, and a strike that lined up in August can bind in February.

Newer Rentals

The Post-Rezoning Buildings: Repairing Networked Panels, Readers and Steel Doors on Atlantic Avenue, Fulton Street and Euclid Avenue

The buildings that went up after the 2016 rezoning are a different kind of repair. Chestnut Commons at 110 Dinsmore Place, completed in October 2022, holds 275 apartments and was developed by MHANY Management with the Cypress Hills Local Development Corporation and Urban Builders Collaborative. The 14-story Phipps Houses building at 254 Euclid Avenue is part of a three-building development that Brick Underground reported will add 1,200 apartments to the neighborhood, and its listing names package lockers among its amenities. The eight-story building at 2795 Fulton Street lists a doorman among its amenities. None of these is a building where one transformer and a riser of handsets run the door. They have networked entry panels, card or fob readers, cameras, sometimes elevator controls, and a management office or vendor that controls the software.

That changes what fails. In the first years a new system's cable is rarely the problem. What we see is a panel that dropped off the network after the internet provider changed a router or the building's switch lost power, a power-over-Ethernet port or injector that quit and took the panel or a reader with it, a reader that stopped talking to its controller, a strike or electrified lever on a heavy steel door that has been slammed out of line, and directory or app settings that went wrong when apartments turned over. On these jobs the first thing we need is not a ladder but access: the equipment closet or IDF, the network switch, and the admin login or a contact at the vendor who has it. A technician who arrives without those can only confirm what is broken; one who has them can usually fix it on the same visit.

The doors themselves deserve attention in the newest buildings. Chestnut Commons is certified Passive House, a standard New York YIMBY describes in terms of "greater air tightness." In any tightly sealed building, the air pressure between the lobby, the vestibule and the outside can push back on a street door as it closes, and a door that stops an inch short of latching defeats the strike no matter how well the panel works. We don't assume that is the cause in any particular building, but we test for it: we watch the door close with the inner doors open and shut, check the closer's speed and latch settings, and adjust the closer or the strike so the door locks on its own every time.

Warranty, vendor or AESS: who should fix a newer system

A building that is only a few years old may still have an installer's warranty, a manufacturer's warranty on the panel or a software contract with the entry-system vendor, and it is worth checking all three before paying for a repair. We ask for the make, model, installer and any service agreement, and say plainly when a fault looks like a warranty claim. Where the warranty has run out, the installer can't be reached or the fault is in the door, the power or the wiring rather than the software, we repair it under our own Brooklyn list and leave a record that names every part and setting we touched, so the next vendor or technician starts from facts.

Above the Stores

One Street Wall, Two Front Doors: Residents' Buzzer Repair Beside Fulton Street and Atlantic Avenue Shops

Fulton Street runs the length of Cypress Hills under the J and Z, and Atlantic Avenue marks its southern side. On both, the common building is a storefront at street level with a narrow residents' door to one side and one to a few apartments above. In those buildings the residents' bell system usually has no room of its own. The transformer may hang in a cellar the store uses for stock, the wire may run through the store's ceiling, and the residents' door may share a frame or a wall with the shop's own door and lock.

Repairs there have a pattern. The residents' bell died during a store renovation because the wire in the ceiling was cut and taped to the nearest pair. The release works only when the shop's lights are on because someone powered the bell transformer from the store's lighting circuit. The strike in the residents' door was painted over or knocked out of line by deliveries and no longer moves. Each of those has a physical cause, and finding it usually means getting into the store's cellar or back room during business hours, so we arrange the visit with the building owner and the shop together.

Who owns which wire decides the repair. Where the owner runs the store and lives upstairs, there is one decision maker. Where the store is leased, the residents' bell is normally the building owner's system even when it draws power from the store's side, and a fix that changes the power source needs the owner's approval and sometimes a licensed electrician for the line-voltage side. We name that in the quote and record which circuit the bell transformer is on afterward, so the next renovation downstairs doesn't kill the apartments' bell again.

Power and Cold

Transformer Fires, Teen-Degree Nights and Buzzers That Don't Come Back: Outage Repairs on the East Side of the Borough

An entry system is a low-voltage device fed from ordinary building power, and it is only as dependable as what reaches it. The eastern edge of Brooklyn has had a rough run of outages lately. News 12 Brooklyn reported that on the evening of January 26, 2026, a transformer fire near Shepherd Avenue, Linden Boulevard and Hegeman Avenue in East New York cut power to about 300 customers nearby and more than 1,500 across the borough as temperatures fell into the teens. On February 11, 2026, a manhole fire at 635 New Jersey Avenue knocked out about 43 more, and a car owner there told News 12, "It was here a couple months ago, and I thought they fixed it." Those were in East New York, south of Cypress Hills, but they are the same Con Edison network and the same kind of winter night.

An outage rarely breaks a buzzer outright. It finds the weakest part. When power returns, the surge can finish off a tired transformer or supply, blow a fuse that was already marginal, crack a solder joint on a relay or door-release module, or weld a relay's contacts shut so the street door stays unlocked. Networked panels add their own failure: a panel, switch or power-over-Ethernet injector that doesn't restart on its own, or restarts into default settings and forgets its directory. And older supplies that limped along at the edge of their rating often come back weaker, so the strike buzzes without fully pulling the keeper. This is the "door buzzer not working after a power outage" call, and it usually has a small, specific cause.

Cold does the rest. ButterflyMX's guide to Brooklyn intercom repair warns that "fluctuating humidity and temperature will naturally degrade many of your intercom's parts, including its wires," and the porch doors and stoop doors of Cypress Hills take the full swing of both. A latch and strike that fit in July bind in January, a button plate that lets in water freezes and cracks, and a splice near the door corrodes after a few winters of slush and salt. When we test a winter fault, we measure the supply at rest and with the strike held, check fuses and relays before replacing anything, and release the door both hanging free and with the closer pushing it shut, because a strike that opens on a still afternoon can still bind when the wind is pressing the door against it.

What to check yourself before you call

Owners and supers can save a visit by checking two things safely. First, whether the breaker that feeds the intercom supply or the bell transformer is on, and whether the supply has a reset or fuse indicator that is lit. Second, on a networked panel, whether the building's internet and network switch came back after the outage, which the managing office or the provider can usually confirm. Beyond that, the line-voltage side belongs to an electrician and the low-voltage side to us. Tell us what you checked when you book, and the technician will start where you stopped.

The Rules

Owners, Managing Offices, NYCHA and the 311 Clock: Who Keeps a Cypress Hills Buzzer Working

New York's rules on entry systems depend on when a building went up and how many apartments it holds, and Cypress Hills has buildings on every side of every line. The New York Attorney General's Tenants' Rights Guide says multiple dwellings built after January 1, 1968 must have automatic self-closing and self-locking doors at all entrances, and those with eight or more apartments also need a two-way voice intercom with a buzz-open release. Buildings from before 1968, which includes the six-story buildings below Highland Park and most of the walk-ups on Fulton Street, must have self-locking doors and a two-way intercom if a majority of the apartments request one. Where a buzzer or intercom has been provided, HPD treats keeping it working as part of the owner's maintenance duty and writes broken ones as violations.

The clock matters once a violation is written. JustFix summarizes HPD's correction periods as 90 days for a Class A violation, 30 days for Class B and 24 hours for most Class C conditions. Separately, HPD says a defective self-closing door is a Class C, immediately hazardous violation that must be corrected within 14 days of the owner receiving the notice. Because a buzzer protects nobody if the door doesn't close and latch by itself, we treat the closer, latch and keeper as part of every repair. When one notice covers both the intercom and the door at the same entrance, we tell the owner which repair the order names first, so a certification date isn't missed while waiting for an intercom part.

Public housing is its own system. Cypress Hills Houses, the NYCHA development between Sutter Avenue and Linden Boulevard, and other NYCHA buildings nearby are maintained by NYCHA, and their doors, locks and intercoms are repaired through NYCHA's own process, not by private contractors hired by residents. The scale of that work is real: the NYC Comptroller's September 2022 observations at 262 NYCHA developments found 40.1% of residential entrance door locks broken and 36.8% of entrance doors standing open, and residents told the auditors about intercoms that had been out for years. If you live in a NYCHA building and the door or intercom is broken, the repair request goes to NYCHA. We can't take that job, but we can explain what a technician would look for so your report is specific.

Tenants, managing offices and the people who approve repairs

Tenants in private buildings have a route of their own. NYC 311 takes apartment maintenance complaints for doorbells, buzzers or intercoms that are broken or missing, and Brick Underground's advice is to put the problem to the landlord in writing first, with a repair estimate if possible. We are hired by owners, boards and managing offices for a building's shared system, not by individual tenants, so a tenant who calls us is pointed back to the owner or managing agent. Either way, we need one person who can approve the price and arrange access.

Newer buildings that use app or smart-access entry add one more rule. HPD's guidance on the Tenant Data Privacy Law says owners using smart access systems need tenants' express consent and may not require tenants to use a smart access system to get into their own apartments. A repair that brings back an app-based panel has to leave residents a working way in that doesn't depend on a phone.

Rules and practical limits on Cypress Hills business doors

  • Line voltage is an electrician's work: the Department of Buildings requires a licensed electrician and a permit for electrical work on the line-voltage side. When a bell transformer has to move off a store's circuit, the electrician's part is quoted as theirs, and we do the low-voltage side.
  • People must always be able to leave: Allegion explains that a fail-safe device unlocks when power is lost and a fail-secure one stays locked, and that with an electric strike you can always exit by the lever. A repaired strike on an exit door keeps that free egress.
  • Magnetic locks need their release devices: where a store or office door uses a maglock instead of a strike, the building code requires sensor and push-to-exit releases, and a repair keeps them working.
  • Reach heights matter at clinics and offices: the 2010 ADA standards set the highest forward reach at 48 inches, so a replaced call button or reader at a public entrance goes no higher.
  • Shared street doors still have to close and lock: where the apartments above a store share a street door with the shop, HPD's self-closing door rule covers that door even if the store uses it too.

Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Repair in Cypress Hills: Questions Answered

These are the questions Cypress Hills owners, supers, managing offices and shop owners bring to us, grouped by the kind of building they come from. Each answer describes how we would approach the problem; the written price always comes after the diagnosis.

Pre-war elevator buildings

Our six-story building's intercom supply sits in a cellar room at the back of the lot, about as far from the street door as the building allows. Can the length of that run be why the strike is weak while the apartments still ring?

Yes, it can. Calling and talking draw very little current, so a long, thin pair carries them without trouble, but a strike draws far more, and a long run of small wire loses voltage in proportion to that current. The result is a supply that reads correctly at its own terminals and a strike that gets too little to pull the keeper. We measure the voltage at the supply and at the strike while it is energized; if the difference is large, the fix is a heavier pair for the strike circuit, a relay near the door, or a separate supply mounted closer to the entrance, priced in writing before any of it is done.

Contractors are already opening walls in several apartments to clear other violations in our building. Can the intercom riser be checked while those walls are open?

That is one of the best times to do it. A riser junction hidden behind a closet or a hallway wall is the most expensive part of an intercom repair to reach, and an open wall removes that cost. Send us the list of apartments and dates the other contractors are working, and we will test the lines that pass through those apartments, remake any taped splices into sealed connectors in reachable boxes, and photograph each one before the walls are closed. The super then has a record of where the riser runs, and the next repair starts from a map.

Our intercom has been dead so long that residents stopped reporting it and started propping the street door. Once it is repaired, how do we get people to trust it again?

Tell them, and prove it. After a repair we retest calling, talking and release from every apartment on the affected lines, so the owner can post a dated notice in the lobby saying the system works, which apartments were tested and who to call if a station fails. We also recommend taking out any wedges or kick-down stops at the door the same day and checking that the closer pulls the door shut and latched from fully open. Residents prop doors when the buzzer fails them; when the buzzer and the closer both work, most stop.

Frame, brick and multi-family houses

The home inspection for the house we are buying says the doorbell transformer is "undersized" for the chime and the video doorbell. What does fixing that involve, and can it be done before closing?

Usually it is a straightforward repair: a correctly rated transformer for the chime, the doorbell and, in a multi-family house, any strike on the same circuit, mounted where it can be reached, with the old splices on the cellar ceiling remade. Before closing we need the seller's permission and access to the cellar. We test the whole circuit under load, write down what we found and what the repair costs, and the parties can decide who pays. The line-voltage connection to the transformer is an electrician's work if it has to move or be rewired.

Our porch door is the original wood door, and a strike was cut into its frame decades later. Now the wood around the strike has split. Can it be reset, or does the frame need a carpenter first?

It depends on how much solid wood is left. If the split is shallow, the strike can often be reset on a reinforced mounting or a slightly larger faceplate that reaches sound wood. If the frame is split along its length or rotted where it meets the porch floor, the strike has nothing to hold, and a carpenter should repair the jamb first. We look at both, tell you which it is, and name any carpentry as someone else's work in the quote, so you are not paying us to put a new strike in a frame that can't hold it.

Post-rezoning rentals

The fob reader and the apartment release both stopped opening our new building's street door on the same morning. What do the two share that could fail at once?

Almost always the lock itself or the power and controller behind it. The reader and the intercom are separate inputs, but both normally fire the same strike or electrified lever through the same lock power supply or controller output. If that supply, its fuse, the output relay or the lock fails, every way of opening the door fails together while the panel may still call apartments normally. We check the lock power and the controller output first, then the strike or lever, and only then the reader or the panel.

Couriers at our building enter a separate code that is supposed to open the package room. They say it stopped working, while residents' codes still open the street door. Where is that fault?

Because residents can still get in, the panel and the street door are probably fine, and the problem is in the part of the system that serves only the package room: the code or schedule that grants courier access, the output or relay that fires the package-room door, or that door's own strike and closer. A schedule that was changed by the management office, a code that expired, or a lock that has been forced by heavy traffic are the common causes. We need the admin login or the office's help to check the settings, and access to the package room to test its door.

Since the outage in January, our panel calls the previous residents of several apartments. Did the outage bring back an old directory?

It can look that way. Some panels keep their settings in memory that depends on a clean shutdown, and some restore from a backup or a cloud account that was not updated when apartments turned over. After an unclean power loss, the panel may come back with an older copy. We check which version the panel is running, update the directory from a current list the managing office provides, and confirm each entry by calling the apartment it points to. Where the panel can store its settings elsewhere, we make sure the current version is the one saved.

Our building is only a few years old. The panel's maker says the fault is the door hardware, and the door installer says it is the panel. How do you settle which one it is?

By testing each piece on its own. We measure what the panel or controller sends when it releases, then power the strike or lever directly and see whether it works, and check the door's alignment, closer and latch. If the panel sends the right signal and the hardware fails on direct power, it is the hardware. If the hardware works on direct power and the panel sends nothing, it is the panel. We write the result down with the measurements, which gives the managing office something specific to send either party, especially if a warranty is still in force.

Apartments over Fulton Street and Atlantic Avenue stores

Delivery workers for the shop downstairs use our residents' door all day, and its strike has failed twice in a year. Is the strike the wrong grade for that much use?

It may be. Electric strikes are sold in different duty grades, and one chosen for a quiet residents' door can wear out fast when it is slammed and released dozens of times a day. Before replacing it again, we check whether the door is being forced against a strike that hasn't released, whether the closer is slamming it, and whether the strike's keeper is worn. Then we fit a strike rated for the traffic and the door's weight and adjust the closer. If the shop needs its own way in, a separate door or entry for deliveries is a conversation for the building owner.

The store below us rents its cellar to another business, and the bell transformer for our apartments is behind their locked door. How is a repair arranged when three different parties hold the keys?

Through the building owner, with the visit booked once everyone has agreed on a time. The owner approves the repair and asks both the store and the cellar tenant to give access during the arrival window, and we confirm the details with each of them before the technician leaves. If the transformer has to stay in that cellar, we label it as the apartments' equipment and note in the record where it is and who holds the key. If the owner prefers, moving the apartments' bell supply to a space the residents or the owner control is often worth pricing at the same time.

"Most intercom repairs cost $150 to $400."

"An old building's intercom needs rewiring."

The reasoning sounds sensible: a building from 1928 must have worn-out wire. But cable inside walls, out of the weather, usually outlasts everything attached to it.

Brick Underground's 2019 guide quoted technicians saying the failures happen at the connections, stations, strike and supply. ButterflyMX's advice for small buildings is blunt: "Do not replace a system based on age alone."

What we find in Cypress Hills' older buildings is joints, not cable. We fix those, test every apartment, and only recommend new cable for a run that tests bad end to end.

"Newer systems don't need repairs, only software updates."

Vendor marketing gives the impression that a cloud or app-based entry system is maintained from a laptop. Some faults really are settings, and a vendor can fix those remotely.

But the newer buildings along Atlantic Avenue and Fulton Street still have physical parts that fail: power-over-Ethernet ports and injectors, network switches, readers, controllers, strikes, electrified levers and closers on heavy doors.

"If the buzzer sounds, call a locksmith for the lock."

Summaries often split the job cleanly between an intercom company and a locksmith. In practice the release is where the two meet, and the fault can be on either side.

A strike that hums but won't release may be starved of voltage by a supply that sags under load, which no locksmith will fix; or the voltage may be fine and the door may be sagging onto the keeper, which no new supply will fix.

We test both halves on the same visit. Where a lock body, a split jamb or a door that needs rehanging is the real problem, we say so and name it as a locksmith's or carpenter's work.

"A battery doorbell is an easy fix for a dead house bell."

Battery video doorbells are sold as a quick answer, and on a one-family house with one door they can be. In a Cypress Hills two- or three-family house, they solve only half of it.

A battery doorbell can tell you someone is outside, but it can't release the shared front door for the upstairs household, and batteries struggle on a porch in a cold snap like the one in January 2026.

For a multi-family house, the wired button, release and strike still have to work. A wired video doorbell on a correctly sized transformer is often the sensible upgrade.

"Only post-1968 buildings have to keep an intercom working."

The Attorney General's guide does say post-1968 buildings with eight or more apartments must have a two-way intercom with a buzz-open release, and summaries stop there.

The same guide says older buildings must have self-locking doors and an intercom if a majority of apartments request one, and where a system has been provided, HPD writes broken ones as maintenance violations whatever the building's age.

We don't give legal advice, but we tell owners of 1928 and 1940 buildings to plan for repair rather than argue the year.

"A new intercom costs about $2,500, so repair isn't worth it."

HomeAdvisor's national average for installing an intercom is around $2,500, and summaries use it to argue that any repair over a few hundred dollars is wasted.

That average describes a small installation. Replacing the system in a 53- or 87-apartment building means a panel, a station in every apartment and labor in every unit, and Brick Underground has cited whole-system figures of $2,000 to $50,000 depending on the building. One repaired riser and a few matched stations usually cost a fraction of that.
