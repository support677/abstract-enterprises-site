The Neighborhood

Why a Campus of 46 Look-Alike Towers Changes Fire Alarm Installation in Starrett City

Starrett City is less a neighborhood of separate buildings than one very large building program spread over a peninsula of filled marsh. Wikipedia's article on the complex counts 5,881 apartments in 46 buildings ranging from 11 to 20 stories, designed by Herman Jessor in a towers-in-the-park layout of simple four-square blocks. The land runs from Flatlands Avenue south to Seaview Avenue and the Belt Parkway, with Fresh Creek on the west, Hendrix Creek on the east and Jamaica Bay beyond. Pennsylvania Avenue cuts north to south through the middle; Louisiana Avenue marks the west end and Van Siclen Avenue the east. It opened in 1974. NYSERDA's feasibility study puts the campus at 8.2 million square feet with about 15,000 residents.

The towers are grouped into eight sections, Ardsley, Bethel, Croton, Delmar, Elmira, Freeport, Geneva and Hornell, each named after a New York State municipality and each with its own recreation area and five-story parking garage. That matters for a fire alarm: addresses end in "Loop" (Ardsley Loop, Freeport Loop, Elmira Loop), and an outsider can lose minutes finding the right door among near-identical towers. Panel labels, annunciator text and the address on the central station account all have to match the way the FDNY and the complex's own officers find a building, which is often by section and loop rather than by the avenue.

Ownership and financing shape the work too. The complex was built with Mitchell-Lama loans and federally assisted rents; Wikipedia calls it the largest federally assisted rental property in the United States. In May 2018 the property sold to the Brooksville Company and Rockpoint Group for $905 million, and those two firms took full ownership in September 2021. The Brooklyn Eagle reported in July 2021 that the state extended affordability through 2069 for the 3,569 Section 8 apartments and that the deal committed about $140 million in new upgrades and repairs by 2030, bringing capital commitments since 2018 to $280 million. Capital programs in occupied towers are when fire alarm work gets filed and re-tested, whether or not a line of the budget says "fire alarm."

Then there is power. The Starrett City Cogeneration Facility at 165 Elmira Loop opened in 1973 and, according to Wikipedia, supplies the complex's electricity, heating, cooling and hot water; it kept Starrett City lit during the 1977 blackout. More recently the opposite has made the news. CBS New York reported that on the evening of December 10, 2014, the entire complex went dark around 8:45 p.m., more than 1,000 apartments lost power, and fire crews rescued people stuck in elevators; residents said it was the second outage that week. ABC7 reported that on October 24, 2023, the outage reached the whole 46-building complex, with some buildings dark for at least an hour and the FDNY freeing people from elevators. News 12 reported another call the next day, from 1540 Van Siclen Avenue just after 3:30 p.m., drawing 25 units and more than 100 fire and EMS personnel. "It was 20 minutes long, but longer than that was yesterday," one resident told News 12. For a fire alarm, every one of those events is a test of batteries, booster supplies, elevator recall and the communicator's ability to keep reporting.

What is a commercial fire alarm system in a building like this? It is the panel, usually in a ground-floor service room, the annunciator in the lobby, the detectors in the corridors, compactor and mechanical rooms, the manual stations at the stairs, the horns, strobes or speakers that warn people, the relays that recall elevators and shut fans, the switches on the sprinkler and standpipe valves, and a communicator that reports to an FDNY-approved central station. How does a commercial fire alarm system work across a campus? Each tower normally has its own panel and approval; management and the central station tie them together. When does a building need a fire alarm system at all? Section 907 of the NYC Building Code decides by occupancy, and an apartment building with 16 or more units, or with apartments three or more stories above the lowest level of exit discharge, is inside the requirement. Every Starrett City tower is far past both lines, and the schools, garages and assembly spaces have their own triggers.

Why is my fire alarm panel showing trouble after the lights come back? A trouble means the panel cannot vouch for some part of itself: a wire, a battery, a device, a power supply or the path out to the central station. After a campus-wide outage the list grows quickly. Batteries that were weak but good enough on a normal day are drained and slow to recover; booster power supplies on upper floors may restart in trouble; communicators that rely on a network connection may lose it while the building's own equipment reboots. Staff describe it as a fire alarm panel beeping repair, a fire alarm panel trouble light, a fire alarm trouble signal repair or a fire alarm system trouble condition, and we record them all the same way. Commercial Fire Alarm Systems for Starrett City's Towers, Garages, Schools and Shared Spaces

Starrett City's commercial fire alarm systems and commercial fire detection systems fall into five groups, each answering "who will notice first?" differently.

The towers: 11 to 20 stories, one landlord, thousands of households

Every residential building on the campus is an apartment building fire alarm installation and a multifamily building fire alarm installation by any definition, and the taller ones are high-rise buildings under the code. What makes them unusual is not height but repetition and population: about 15,000 residents, according to NYSERDA, many of them in buildings with the same lobby layout and the same riser locations. A detector that nuisance-trips in one compactor room is probably doing it in the twin tower too.

The Building Code's high-rise section raises the stakes for the tallest towers. For Group R-2 buildings over 125 feet, it lists emergency voice communication systems, including the FDNY's in-building auxiliary radio communication systems, among the loads that need emergency power. And the Department of Buildings' Bulletin 2024-001, which governs work on existing approved fire alarm systems, requires one-way voice communication in any existing building taller than 125 feet that has none, measured from the lowest level of fire department access to the highest occupied floor. Whether a given 20-story tower crosses that line depends on that measurement, and that is the engineer's call, made with the building's own drawings.

Garages and the boiler plants inside them

Each section has a five-story parking garage, and AERCO's case study says the campus heat and hot water now come from 46 boilers grouped into eight modular plants housed in those garages, replacing a central system whose underground distribution ran at 49 percent efficiency. A fuel-burning plant inside an enclosed garage brings three things to the fire alarm conversation: heat detection suited to the space, supervisory monitoring of anything the plant's controls report, and carbon monoxide detection, which the FDNY requires in occupancies protected by a fire alarm system that have fuel-burning equipment or an enclosed parking garage. Garages are also where storage facility fire alarm installation questions come up, because anything stored in a garage cage or room changes the fuel load.

Your Questions Answered

Fire Alarm Questions From Starrett City Management, Supers, Schools and Tenants, Answered

These are the questions the complex's size and history raise. The engineer or architect who seals your drawings has the final word for each address.

Starrett City's electricity comes from its own plant on Elmira Loop, not from Con Edison. Does that change how the fire alarm's power circuit is designed and filed?

Not the rules, only the people involved. The panel and each booster supply still need a dedicated, labeled branch circuit from the tower's electrical system, and the electrician files that work with the Department of Buildings as anywhere else. What differs is coordination: the engineer has to learn from the complex's own electrical staff how each tower is fed and restored after a trip, so the battery calculation and the restart plan match how power actually comes back.

The 2023 outages left people trapped in elevators across the complex. Does the fire alarm have anything to do with how elevators behave when the power fails?

Not directly. A loss of power is an elevator and electrical question; the fire alarm's job with elevators is recall, sending cars to the designated floor when a lobby, machine room or hoistway detector goes into alarm. What the two share is power: the recall signals and the detectors that send them must stay alive on secondary power, and after an outage both the elevator contractor and the fire alarm company should confirm recall still works before the building is called normal.

The heating plants now sit inside the eight section garages. Did moving boilers into a garage change what the fire alarm has to watch?

It can. A fuel-burning plant in an enclosed garage may bring carbon monoxide detection, heat detection suited to a boiler room and supervisory points from the plant's own safety controls into the fire alarm design. Whether those were added when the boilers went in is a question for the filing record. If the garage's fire alarm drawings predate the boiler plant, ask your engineer to compare the two.

Our towers were built to the same plans in the 1970s. Can one engineer's design be used for several Starrett City towers at once?

The approach can be shared; the filings usually cannot. Each building generally has its own address, certificate of occupancy and fire alarm approval, so each gets its own drawings and FDNY application, even when the sheets look almost identical. Repetition still saves real time: one survey method, one device schedule and one set of answers to the FDNY's comments can be carried across a whole section.

The garage boiler plants are watched remotely by SCADA. Can that same monitoring system stand in for the fire alarm's link to a central station?

No. AERCO's case study says each plant is monitored by SCADA and a remote maintenance service, which is useful for keeping heat and hot water running. The fire alarm must report to a central station approved by the FDNY, over the paths its approved design shows. The two can share information where the engineer designs an interface, such as a plant fault appearing as a supervisory point, but neither replaces the other.

FDNY tickets at 185 Ardsley Loop cite failure to prevent unwarranted alarms. What should management ask for after a ticket like that?

A written account of each alarm: date, time, device, floor and probable cause, taken from the panel's history and the central station's records. From that list come the fixes: a different detector type where cooking or steam is the cause, cleaning or replacing old heads, guards on stations that get bumped, and notices to residents where behavior is the cause. Then keep the record, because the next hearing will ask what was done.

P.S. 346 shares its Pennsylvania Avenue building with Frederick Douglass Academy VIII on the fourth floor. How are fire drills and alarm tests handled when two schools share one fire alarm?

Through one schedule and one set of rules for the whole building. Drills and tests are announced to every school in the building, the central station is placed on test for the agreed window and taken off afterward, and any alarm outside an announced window is treated as real. The fire alarm itself does not care which school is on which floor; the people using it do.

The Starrett Early Learning Center received an FDNY fire protection systems ticket in November 2026. What extra planning does a fire alarm need where very young children nap?

Mostly planning for people rather than devices. Notification has to reach staff in every room, including nap rooms where lights are low, and the evacuation plan has to account for children who must be carried or led. The design professional sets the devices; the operator sets the drills and staff assignments. We do not know what that ticket concerned, and the center's own fire alarm company of record is the one to ask.

The 2021 affordability deal committed about $140 million to upgrades and repairs by 2030. If a tower's lobby, corridors or elevators are renovated under that money, does the fire alarm get re-filed?

If the fire alarm is touched, usually yes. Moving devices, adding them, replacing the panel or changing the sequence for new elevators is filed with the FDNY and tested again. Painting around detectors is not. The cheapest moment to settle it is before the renovation drawings are finished, so the fire alarm work is in the same schedule instead of a surprise at the end.

The section garages are five stories. Are the garage levels part of the nearest tower's fire alarm, or a system of their own?

It depends on how the garage was built and filed. A garage that is a separate building usually has its own approval and may have its own panel; a garage connected to a tower may report to that tower's system. Chatbots tend to treat a big Brooklyn complex as a pile of apartments or as public housing. Neither fits Starrett City. Seven common claims, checked:

"Starrett City is NYCHA, so the Housing Authority handles its fire alarms."

Starrett City is not a NYCHA development. It was built with state Mitchell-Lama financing and federally assisted rents, and it is privately owned; the Brooksville Company and Rockpoint Group bought it in 2018 and took full ownership in 2021.

"Apartment buildings only need smoke alarms in each unit."

Smoke and carbon monoxide alarms in apartments are one requirement; a building fire alarm system is another. Under Section 907 of the Building Code, apartment buildings above the 16-unit or three-story lines fall under the fire alarm requirement, and every Starrett City tower is far above both.

The building system covers common areas, service rooms, elevators and the link to the central station. In-unit alarms are usually separate devices that sound only in the apartment.

Confusing the two leads to bad decisions, such as replacing a required system detector with a stand-alone alarm, which the FDNY's guidance specifically says owners may not do.

"A campus with its own power plant never loses power."

The cogeneration plant famously kept the complex lit through the 1977 blackout. It has not kept it lit every day since. CBS New York reported a complex-wide outage in December 2014, and ABC7 and News 12 reported outages on consecutive days in October 2023, with elevator rescues each time.

For a fire alarm, that means secondary power is not a box to check. Batteries, booster supplies and communicators have to ride through real outages and come back cleanly.

After any long outage, we recommend checking every tower's panel for troubles rather than waiting for the next scheduled test.

"A false alarm costs nothing as long as nobody was hurt."

OATH records say otherwise. In the 80 most recent FDNY summonses for ZIP 11239, 39 were for failing to prevent unnecessary unwarranted alarms, and the most common penalty imposed on those was $935. One Ardsley Loop ticket decided by default carried $5,000.

Beyond fines, every unwanted alarm in a 20-story tower sends people into stairwells and teaches residents to ignore the horn.

Unwanted alarms are a maintenance and design problem with a paper trail, and they can be reduced with the right detectors, regular cleaning and resident notices.

"Swapping an old panel for a new one is just a parts change."

The Department of Buildings disagrees. Buildings Bulletin 2024-001 allows repair and in-kind replacement of control panels as repair work only in limited cases; relocating or replacing a panel otherwise needs new plans filed with the FDNY.

If an existing system does not work and cannot be repaired, or listed replacement parts are no longer available, the bulletin requires a full replacement with a system that meets current code.

For towers built around 1974, the age of the equipment can make that last line the deciding one.

"Fire alarm installation takes a couple of days per building."

A crew may spend a short time on site in a single space, but the calendar runs through the FDNY. The FDNY's own guidance puts the average from plan submission to final inspection at roughly twenty to twenty-four weeks, with initial plan review taking up to eight weeks.

Across a campus of 46 buildings, scheduling filings in sequence matters as much as scheduling crews.

Plan the fire alarm portion of any capital program in quarters, not days.

"Any electrician on the maintenance staff can move a detector."

The FDNY draws a firmer line. Work on a building fire alarm belongs to firms holding the FDNY Fire Alarm Company Certificate, each with a principal holding the S-97 Certificate of Fitness. A licensed electrician files electrical work and often works with that firm, but moving devices on an approved system is filed and tested.

A well-meant fix by building staff can leave a tower with an unapproved system and a failed inspection.

Before anyone opens a panel, ask for the certificate holder's name in writing.

The notice tells residents whether the building is combustible or non-combustible, which shapes the advice to stay or leave, and the owner must also provide the Preparedness Guide and post Close the Door notices in stair halls. Explaining what the fire alarm sounds like and what residents should do when it goes off is something management can add in its own notices.

Starrett City's sections are named after towns such as Elmira and Hornell. Should the fire alarm's labels use the section names?
+

Use what responders use first: the building's address, the floor and the room, matching the floor numbers on the certificate of occupancy and the approved drawings. A section name can sit in the panel's building name or the annunciator header, where it helps staff and officers orient, but it should never replace the address. Keep every label consistent from the panel to the central station account.
