The Neighborhood

Why Kensington Properties Need Door Buzzer Repair

Kensington sits between Prospect Park and Green-Wood Cemetery to the north and Midwood to the south, with Borough Park on one side and the Prospect Park South and Ditmas Park parts of Flatbush on the other. Wikipedia's article on the neighborhood describes its housing in one sentence that is also a fair summary of our work there: brick rowhouses, detached one-family Victorians, and apartment buildings, with pre-war brick apartment buildings dominating the frontage of Ocean Parkway and Coney Island Avenue, many of them run as co-ops. The main shopping streets are Church Avenue, McDonald Avenue, Coney Island Avenue and Ditmas Avenue. The ZIP code is 11218 and the 66th Precinct patrols it.

The apartment buildings came in two waves that you can still read on the street. The first was the six-story elevator house, which spread along upper Ocean Parkway and its side streets between the late 1920s and 1941 as the subway reached the area and the suburban villas came down. The second followed the Second World War and the Prospect Expressway: taller buildings on and near the parkway until the mid-1960s, Caton Towers and the Americana Towers among them. The city's lot file shows how much of that second wave arrived at once. Twenty-five of Kensington's elevator buildings of twenty or more homes were completed between 1960 and 1965, with 2,675 homes between them, and Wikipedia's article on the parkway counts twenty-six apartment buildings developed on Ocean Parkway itself in those six years.

Then came the conversions. By the early 1980s, Wikipedia records, the area drew new residents as a source of relatively affordable rentals in the middle of the city's co-op conversion movement, and the parkway article notes that many of Ocean Parkway's apartment buildings were converted to co-ops, particularly toward its northern end. A 1974 New York Times interview quoted in the neighborhood article describes long-time tenants who were reluctant to move because of New York's rent regulation laws. Both facts are still on the ground. Today the city classes 39 of Kensington's elevator buildings as co-ops and lists 46 older elevator buildings as rentals, and the Rent Guidelines Board's rule of thumb is that buildings of six or more apartments built before 1974 are generally rent stabilized.

That mix is the reason this page exists. A dead lobby panel in a co-op on the parkway and a dead lobby panel in the rental next door are the same repair to a technician. They are not the same decision for the people paying for it. In the co-op, the board weighs a repair against a replacement out of the building's reserves. In the rental, the owner pays for a repair as part of running the building, but a full replacement can be filed with the state's Division of Housing and Community Renewal as a Major Capital Improvement, which, if granted, raises the rents of the regulated tenants. And three blocks away, in a two-family house on East 3rd Street, the owner pays, full stop. Kensington is also one of the most varied neighborhoods in Brooklyn: Wikipedia lists Bangladeshi, Pakistani, Orthodox Jewish, Uzbek, Latin American, Polish and Ukrainian communities, and the corner of McDonald and Church Avenues was co-named Little Bangladesh in October 2022. Those are the residents who press the buttons, and very often the owners and board members who pay for them.

Kensington in five numbers

  • 14,410 homes on 2,827 residential lots in the ten census tracts the city counts as Kensington (MapPLUTO, October 2026), roughly from Caton Avenue south to Avenue F and 18th Avenue, and from McDonald Avenue and Dahill Road east to Coney Island Avenue.
  • 133 buildings hold 20 or more homes each, 8,132 homes in all, or 56% of the neighborhood. Sixty-eight of them, with 4,270 homes, have Ocean Parkway addresses. Only nine were built after 1973.
  • 39 elevator buildings are co-ops (3,229 homes) and 46 more elevator buildings from before 1974 are rentals (3,335 homes). The 53 four-story walk-ups add 1,294 homes, built on average around 1924.
  • 168 HPD intercom orders since 1999, 143 of them Class B, at 73 lots; 48 are still listed as open. Over the same years HPD wrote 2,817 orders about self-closing doors.
  • 153 complaints to 311 about a bell, buzzer or intercom at Kensington lots between January 2020 and October 22, 2026, at 51 lots; 39 of them in 2023 alone.
Who Pays

Three Ledgers Behind One Lobby Panel

We sort every Kensington entrance into one of three ledgers before we price it. The sorting is ours, built from the city's building classes, but the money behind each ledger is real and it changes the advice we give.

The rental ledger: about 5,300 homes

Elevator rentals hold 3,512 of Kensington's homes and the walk-up rentals another 1,827: the four-story buildings on Avenue C and the East streets, the five- and six-family walk-ups, and the walk-ups over stores. Almost all of them were built before 1974. In a building like that the owner carries every repair as an operating cost and cannot pass it on to tenants, whether it is a new handset or a rewired riser. A complete new entry system is different. If the building is rent stabilized and meets DHCR's conditions, the owner can apply for a temporary rent increase to recover a verified share of it. That gives owners and tenants opposite reasons to care about where a repair ends and a replacement begins, and it is why we keep the two apart on paper.

The tenants' side of this ledger has its own tools. A rental tenant whose buzzer is dead can call 311, and HPD can write a violation; the record shows those tools in use far more often in rentals than in co-ops. An owner who later applies for a capital-improvement increase must swear that no hazardous or immediately hazardous violations are on file, and tenants are given sixty days to answer the application. So in this ledger the buzzer is not only a convenience. An open intercom violation can sit in the way of an owner's plans for the boiler or the roof, and a replacement can turn up years later in the rent.

The co-op ledger: about 3,300 homes

The 39 elevator co-ops and three walk-up co-ops hold 3,315 homes. Here the people at the door are the owners. A shareholder with a dead handset calls the managing agent or a board member, and the board decides whether to repair the building's system from the operating budget or replace it from reserves or an assessment. There is no rent increase to apply for and nobody to pass the cost to except the shareholders themselves. That changes what a board needs from us: an honest view of how much life is left in the system, and repair prices it can compare against a full replacement. Where a sponsor still rents some apartments in a co-op to rent-stabilized tenants, a small part of the rental ledger sits inside the co-op; we come back to that in the questions below.

The house ledger: about 4,900 homes

Most of Kensington's land is not apartment buildings at all. There are 602 one-family houses, 1,127 two-family houses, 491 three-family houses and 132 four-family houses on the city's lot file, 4,857 homes in all, plus 191 small store-and-apartment buildings along the avenues. In a house the owner pays and no rent rule touches the bell. The repair choice is purely practical: a transformer in the cellar, a button by the porch door, a pair of thin wires along the joists, a release in a wooden frame. The only regulatory edge is at three families and up, where the house becomes a multiple dwelling and HPD can write violations on it.

What changes on a job because of the ledger

  • For a rental owner, we price the repair that keeps the existing system working and, separately, what a replacement would involve, so the owner can decide with DHCR's rules in view. Our invoice says plainly which lines are repairs.
  • For a co-op board, we give the age and condition of each part, the repair prices from our list, and a plain opinion on whether the system is nearer the start or the end of its useful years.
  • For a homeowner, we fix the bell, label the transformer and the wires, and keep the bill to the list line that applies.
The Public Record

What the City's Files Say About Kensington Buzzers

On October 22, 2026 we pulled every HPD intercom violation for the Kensington tabulation area and every 311 bell, buzzer or intercom complaint at a lot in the same ten census tracts, and matched them to the city's lot file. The matching is ours. The pattern suggests that the ledger, more than the age of the wiring, shapes what happens when a buzzer breaks.

Where HPD writes intercom orders

HPD has written 168 intercom orders in Kensington since 1999: 143 Class B, 24 Class A and a single Class C. They landed at 73 lots. Matched to building classes, the four-story walk-ups stand out. Their 53 lots hold 1,294 homes and have drawn 71 orders, about 5.5 for every hundred homes, and 24 of the 53 buildings have been cited at least once. The elevator rentals have drawn 43 orders across 3,512 homes, about 1.2 per hundred. The 39 elevator co-ops, with almost as many homes, have drawn 22, about 0.7 per hundred, at nine buildings. Houses of one and two families have drawn none, because HPD's intercom rules are written for multiple dwellings.

Part of that gap is physical. A four-story walk-up from the early 1920s has had a century of tenants, renovations and repairs on one set of wires, and the lobby panel is often the only thing between the street and forty-six apartments. But the gap between the rentals and the co-ops of the same age and size is hard to explain by wiring alone. Our reading is that it is mostly about who calls whom. A tenant whose buzzer is dead calls 311, and the complaint becomes an inspection. A shareholder calls the managing agent, and the repair, if it happens, never reaches the city's files. That is an interpretation, not something the data proves, but it is consistent with 311: 2.2 complaints per hundred homes in the walk-ups, 1.4 in the elevator rentals and 0.65 in the co-ops.

The orders are getting more frequent

Through the 2000s the neighborhood saw one to four intercom orders a year. Since 2018 it has seen nine to fourteen a year, apart from a single year, 2020, with four. From 2021 through October 22, 2026 HPD wrote 72 of them. The 311 file tells a similar story with a different peak: 39 buzzer complaints in 2023, and 24 so far in 2026. The complaints are concentrated. One elevator rental accounts for 21 of the 153 complaints since 2020, and the five busiest lots for 66. When a building appears in that list, it is usually because one fault has gone unrepaired for a long time, not because the building is unlucky.

Some orders never close

Forty-eight of the 168 orders are still listed as open, 38 of them Class B. The oldest open one was written in December 1999. In our experience an old open order usually means the paperwork was never finished, not that the same fault has lasted for decades: a violation stays on the record until the correction is certified and accepted or an inspector finds it fixed. Open or not, these records matter more in Kensington than in most neighborhoods, for a reason that has nothing to do with the buzzer itself. We explain it in the next section.

For context, the same area has 2,817 HPD orders about self-closing doors, most of them Class C. Doors that fail to close and latch are a far larger part of the city's paperwork than intercoms are, and a front door that does not close defeats any buzzer. When we repair an entry in a Kensington apartment building we look at the closer and the latch as part of the same visit.

Rent-Stabilized Buildings

Repair or Replace in a Rent-Stabilized Building: What DHCR Counts

New York's Division of Housing and Community Renewal lets the owner of a rent-stabilized building apply for a temporary rent increase after a Major Capital Improvement, a building-wide installation such as a boiler, windows, wiring, a roof, or an intercom system. Because so much of Kensington's rental housing is of the age and size that rent stabilization covers, the rules DHCR publishes for these increases belong on a buzzer-repair page here. We summarize them below from DHCR's own fact sheets so owners and tenants can follow a conversation about the entry system. We are an installer, not a law firm or a filing agent, and DHCR decides every application on its own facts.

A repair is never a capital improvement

DHCR's Fact Sheet #24 says it in so many words: to be eligible for a rent increase, an improvement must be a new installation and not a repair to old equipment, and DHCR will not grant an increase for ordinary repairs that only keep a building in working order. It must also benefit the whole building and all its tenants. Replacing a dead handset, a burnt transformer, a stuck strike or a broken riser pair is a repair. A new panel, new controller, new risers and new stations for every apartment is the kind of work that can qualify. Between those two lies most of what we see, which is why we write every Kensington invoice with each line marked as a repair or as new equipment.

The old system has to have lived out its useful life

DHCR publishes a useful-life schedule, Fact Sheet #33, listing how long each kind of building system is expected to last before its replacement can count. An intercom system is listed at 15 years. Lobby and vestibule doors are also 15 years, apartment entrance doors 25, and television security, which is how the schedule describes building cameras, 10. An owner who replaces an intercom sooner must have asked DHCR for a waiver before starting, unless the system was destroyed by an emergency such as a fire or vandalism. Kensington's elevator buildings were mostly finished between the late 1920s and 1965, so their original systems are long past fifteen years; what matters is the age of whatever was put in last.

Hazardous violations stop the application

Since the 2019 rent law, DHCR may not grant a capital-improvement increase while hazardous or immediately hazardous violations are on file with the city, and the owner must affirm that the building is free of them when filing. HPD's hazardous class is Class B, which HPD gives an owner thirty days to correct, and immediately hazardous is Class C. Most intercom violations in Kensington are Class B. That is the practical reason an old, open intercom order matters: it can hold up an increase for a new boiler or a new roof, not just for the intercom. Of the 23 Kensington lots with an open Class B intercom order, 16 are rental buildings of five or more apartments.

Cost is capped, and so is the increase

DHCR does not let an owner recover whatever was spent. It publishes a reasonable-cost schedule, and the version issued on January 9, 2026 for work begun on or after January 1, 2026 allows up to $2,178 per apartment for an audio intercom, $1,955 per apartment for a video intercom and $1,678 for a telephone-entry intercom. Entrance doors have their own lines: $5,307 for a building entry door and $15,741 for a vestibule or lobby door. These are DHCR's ceilings for rent purposes, not prices we charge; our prices are on our Brooklyn list further down this page.

The approved cost is then spread over 144 months in a building of 35 or fewer apartments, or 150 months in a larger one, and divided by the number of rooms. Whatever the result, a tenant pays no more than 2% of the rent in any year on account of capital improvements, and the increase must come off the rent 30 years after it starts. Buildings in which 35% or fewer of the apartments are rent regulated cannot receive these increases at all. Tenants who receive SCRIE or DRIE are exempt. DHCR's fact sheet works through an example in which $227,000 of claimed work becomes ten dollars per room per month.

Intercoms are a small part of what DHCR approves

DHCR posts monthly reports of closed capital-improvement cases. Reading the five reports for May through September 2026, we counted 76 orders citywide, of which two included an intercom: a Bronx building whose order listed pointing, an intercom, cameras and main entrance doors, with $36,450 claimed and allowed for the intercom, and a Manhattan building with a $17,000 video intercom. In September 2026 DHCR issued 14 such orders worth $4,863,961.80 in all. Most entry systems in rent-stabilized Kensington buildings are, and will remain, repaired rather than replaced through this route. The rules matter anyway, because they shape how owners, tenants and contractors talk about every repair.

What this means for a repair we do

  • We read the building's open HPD violations before quoting and tell the owner which intercom orders our repair should allow them to certify as corrected.
  • Each line of the quote and the invoice is marked as a repair or as new equipment, with the parts we take out listed by name.
  • Where a replacement is under discussion, we say what we can see of the existing system's age, and leave the question of whether it qualifies to DHCR and the owner's advisers.
  • We do not prepare or file DHCR applications, and nothing on this page is legal advice. DHCR's fact sheets, its Brooklyn borough office at 55 Hanson Place, and the Rent Guidelines Board answer questions about rents.

DHCR's own pages on these increases are published in English and in Spanish, Chinese, Russian, Yiddish, Bengali, Korean, Haitian Creole, Italian, Arabic, Polish, French and Urdu, which covers most of the languages spoken on Kensington's stoops.

Every service below is a repair to an existing entrance. A new system for a building that has never had one, or a full replacement, is quoted on our installation pages. Each service is listed with the ledger where we meet it most.

Lobby panel repair

Elevator rentals and co-ops on the parkway. Speaker, microphone, call buttons or directory module inside an existing panel replaced or re-terminated, so the rest of the system keeps working and the faceplate stays where it is.

Riser pair tracing and repair

Six-story buildings where one vertical line of apartments has gone quiet. We find the break between floors, splice or re-run that pair, and label the riser at each landing for the next repair.

Power supply and transformer replacement

Every ledger. A tired supply in the back room, or a 16 to 24 volt bell transformer in a house cellar, replaced at the list price and labelled with the date.

Electric strike replacement and alignment

Walk-ups and elevator buildings whose door buzzes but stays shut. New strike, keeper adjusted to the latch, and the door tried from the panel and from apartments on several floors.

Apartment handset and station replacement

A single apartment that cannot hear the lobby or open the door. Replaced with a station that matches the building's system, so one tenant's repair does not become a mixed system.

Release button and relay repair

Apartments, ground-floor stores and offices that buzz visitors in. Buttons and relays replaced without touching the rest of the circuit.

House bell and porch release

One- to four-family houses on the East streets and Albemarle and Beverley Roads. Buttons, chimes, the transformer and any porch-door release, with each button tested from the street.

Door closer and latch adjustment

The other half of most entrance complaints. A closer that slams or never latches is adjusted or replaced at our hourly rate while we are already at the door.

Violation-led repair

Buildings with an open HPD intercom order. We repair the fault the order describes, test it the way an inspector would, and give the owner a dated note to support the certification of correction.

Building Specialists

Door Buzzer Repair for Kensington's Architecture

The same handful of building types repeats across Kensington's blocks. Each has its own entry layout, its own usual faults and its own place in the three ledgers. The figures come from the city's lot file for the study area.

Six-story elevator houses, late 1920s to 1941

Brick or limestone fronts on Ocean Parkway, set back behind front yards, and on Albemarle Road, Caton Avenue and the East streets; typically 40 to 100 apartments. A vestibule with an inner and outer door, a panel in the vestibule, a power supply in the cellar and one riser pair per line of apartments. Some are rentals and some converted to co-ops.

Postwar and early-1960s elevator buildings

The 25 elevator buildings completed between 1960 and 1965, plus a few from the 1950s, with up to 286 apartments in the tallest, the sixteen-story co-op at 135 Ocean Parkway. Wider lobbies with dropped ceilings and, in our experience, aluminum and glass doors and panels that have often been replaced once already. Sixteen of the 25 are co-ops.

Four-story walk-ups, mostly 1915 to 1931

Fifty-three buildings averaging 24 apartments, and as many as 46 in the largest. Dense on Avenue C, East 2nd, 4th, 5th and 8th Streets and parts of Ocean Parkway. One street door, one panel, wiring that has been extended and spliced through generations of renovations. The type with the most HPD intercom orders per home.

Five- and six-family walk-ups and walk-ups over stores

Sixty-two three-story buildings of five or six apartments, mostly from around 1930, and ten four-story walk-ups over shops. Small enough that the owner is often local; old enough that a six-apartment building is usually rent stabilized.

Three- and four-family houses

Four hundred and ninety-one three-family and 132 four-family houses, many from around 1920 to 1930, along the side streets toward McDonald and Coney Island Avenues. A button for each floor at the porch, a transformer in the cellar, and a release in the front door if anyone fitted one. Multiple dwellings in HPD's eyes, but outside rent stabilization.

One- and two-family houses

Six hundred and two one-family and 1,127 two-family houses, including the detached Victorians and American Foursquares toward Ocean Parkway and the brick rows elsewhere. Bells and chimes rather than intercoms, and repairs that never involve the city.

Methods by building type

Front yards on Ocean Parkway. The parkway was laid out with a rule that buildings stand back from the sidewalk, and the city's Special Ocean Parkway District still requires a thirty-foot front yard for new buildings. The result is a long walk from the sidewalk to the vestibule, sometimes past a gate, and a panel that visitors reach only after crossing the yard. When a parkway building's panel is weathered we check the cable route from the cellar to the vestibule before blaming the panel; on older buildings the run is long, and splices in it are where water gets in.

Vestibules with two doors. Most of the six-story houses have an outer door to the street and an inner door to the lobby, with the panel between them and the electric strike on the inner door. A complaint that the door will not open is often a complaint about the inner door's closer or latch, not the buzzer. We try both doors, with the release and with a key, before replacing anything electrical.

Walk-up risers. In a four-story walk-up all apartments usually hang off one cable in the stair hall, spliced at each floor. When several apartments on one floor fail together, the splice on that landing is the first thing we open. Because these buildings have the most HPD orders, we often arrive with a violation in hand, and we test the cited apartment from the street panel exactly as an inspector would before we leave.

1960s lobbies. The early-1960s buildings were finished with dropped ceilings and recessed lights, and their entry cable usually runs above the ceiling to a closet. Many have had a second-generation panel fitted over the original backbox. We note on the invoice which generation of equipment we found and what we replaced, because in these buildings, more than any other, a board or an owner will soon be weighing a repair against a replacement.

House cellars. In the three- and four-family houses the bell transformer is often screwed to a joist beside the electrical panel, with thin bell wire stapled along the beams to each button. A weak or silent bell is usually the transformer, a corroded button at the porch, or a staple driven through the wire during some later job. These are list-price repairs, and none of them involves anyone but the owner.

Rules that apply to commercial and mixed buildings

  • Multiple Dwelling Law § 50-a. The state Attorney General's tenants' rights guide says multiple dwellings built after January 1, 1968 need self-closing, self-locking entrance doors, and those with eight or more apartments a two-way voice intercom with a buzz-open release; older buildings must have self-locking doors and an intercom if a majority of apartments ask for one, with the landlord able to recover the cost. Nearly all of Kensington's apartment buildings fall in the older group.
  • Self-closing doors. HPD treats a defective self-closing entrance door as an immediately hazardous Class C violation, to be corrected within fourteen days of the notice (Housing Maintenance Code § 27-2041.1). A store that props the shared entrance open for deliveries puts the building owner on the hook.
  • Commercial share of capital work. When an owner of a rent-stabilized building with stores applies for a capital-improvement increase, DHCR reduces the approved cost by the commercial share of the building's floor area. An entry system that serves both the shops and the apartments is split the same way.
  • Electric locks on exit doors. A door held by a magnetic lock must release for anyone leaving, by a sensor and a manual button and on loss of power, under the building code's conditions for electrically locked egress doors. On a store's back door we prefer a fail-secure electric strike, which leaves the inside lever working at all times.
  • Reach and access. The 2010 ADA Standards put the highest forward reach for operable parts at 48 inches. We mount replacement buttons and stations within that range where the wall allows.
  • Line-voltage work. Work on the building's power wiring belongs to a licensed electrician with a Department of Buildings permit. Our work stays on the low-voltage side of the transformer or power supply.

Rent-Stabilized Rentals: When a Repair Touches the Rent

Our Ocean Parkway landlord has told tenants that a new intercom will be added to the rent. Can replacing a buzzer system raise a rent-stabilized rent?

It can, but not automatically and not right away. The owner has to apply to DHCR on its form RA-79 within two years of finishing the work, every regulated tenant gets a summary of the application and sixty days to answer, and nothing can be charged until DHCR issues a written order. DHCR grants the increase only for a new building-wide installation, not a repair, and only if the old system had passed its fifteen-year useful life, the building is more than 35% rent regulated, and no hazardous or immediately hazardous violations are on file.

If an increase is granted, the approved cost is capped at DHCR's reasonable cost, $2,178 per apartment for an audio intercom on the 2026 schedule, spread over twelve or twelve and a half years, and divided by rooms. What a tenant actually pays on account of capital work is limited to 2% of the rent a year, and the increase comes off after thirty years. If you want to dispute an application, DHCR's fact sheet lists the grounds, from defective work to violations on file.

The landlord says our old panel "can't be repaired anymore." How can tenants tell whether that is true?

Sometimes it is true. A lobby panel whose maker stopped supplying parts years ago, a riser that has been spliced on every floor until there is nothing left to splice, or a system whose handsets no longer match each other can be cheaper to replace than to keep patching. Equally often, a panel described as finished has one failed speaker, one bad button or a tired power supply, all of which are list-price repairs.

The way to tell is a written diagnosis that names the failed part, says whether a replacement part exists, and gives a repair price. If the owner hires us, that is what our dated note contains, and the owner is free to share it. DHCR's own test is whether the work was necessary and building-wide rather than cosmetic, and tenants who answer an application can say why they think it was not.

For years the owner has swapped parts of the old system one at a time. Does piecemeal work change anything for tenants or for the owner?

For tenants, piecemeal repairs are simply repairs: they are part of running the building and cannot be added to the rent. For owners there is a second consequence. DHCR's fact sheet lists, among the grounds on which tenants may challenge a capital-improvement application, that the work was completed in different stages spread out over many years. An owner who replaces a panel this year, the risers in two years and the stations after that should not expect the sum to be treated as one new system.

From the technical side, staged work is often perfectly sensible; it keeps the door working while money is found. We only ask owners to decide at the start which they are doing, a sequence of repairs or a planned replacement, so the paperwork matches what is actually being done.

We receive SCRIE. If DHCR approves an intercom increase for our building, do we pay it?

According to DHCR's Fact Sheet #24, no. Tenants in New York City who receive the Senior Citizen Rent Increase Exemption or the Disability Rent Increase Exemption do not pay a capital-improvement increase. DHCR's instruction is to call 311 for information about your exemption after an order is issued, and to send a copy of the order to your SCRIE or DRIE office so the exemption can be updated.

The repair itself is unaffected. If the intercom in your apartment is not working, the owner still has to repair it, and you can still call 311 if it is not repaired.

Owners Planning Capital Work

We own a 1920s walk-up off Avenue C and plan to replace the boiler next year. Why does an old open intercom violation matter to that plan?

Because DHCR may not grant a capital-improvement increase for any item, a boiler included, while hazardous or immediately hazardous violations are on file with the city, and the owner must affirm when filing that the building is free of them. Most intercom violations HPD writes in Kensington are Class B, the hazardous class. An intercom order left open from years ago can therefore stand between you and an increase for work that has nothing to do with the front door.

Clearing it means repairing whatever the order describes, if it is still broken, and certifying the correction to HPD. We can do the first part and give you a dated note of what we found and fixed to support the second. Do it well before you file; a correction has to be accepted before the record changes.

The intercom in our six-story rental went in about ten years ago and has failed again. Is it too young to be replaced as a capital improvement?

Under DHCR's useful-life schedule an intercom system is expected to last fifteen years, so a ten-year-old system replaced now would normally not qualify. An owner who believes it must be replaced early can ask DHCR for a waiver of the useful-life requirement, and DHCR's fact sheet says the request has to be made before the work starts, unless the system was destroyed in an emergency such as a fire or vandalism.

On the technical side, a ten-year-old system that keeps failing usually has a cause that can be found: an undersized power supply, water in a splice at the vestibule, a riser damaged during some apartment renovation. We would look for that cause first. Repairing it is a cost you carry, but so is a replacement that DHCR will not count.

Before we spend money on a new entry system, can we find out whether DHCR would count it?

Yes. DHCR has a form for exactly this, RA-86, the Owner's Request for Prior Opinion on Major Capital Improvements, which asks DHCR whether a proposed improvement qualifies before the building is improved. DHCR's Small Building Owners unit also offers technical help before an application, and DHCR encourages small owners to use it.

What we can contribute is the technical description: what the current system is, roughly how old it is, what has failed, and what a replacement would consist of. We do not prepare or file DHCR forms, and DHCR, not a contractor, decides what qualifies.

Co-ops, Condos and Store Buildings

Our Ocean Parkway co-op board wants a long-range budget line for the entrance. What benchmarks make sense?

No rule binds a co-op here, but DHCR's published figures are a sensible public yardstick because they are built for exactly this kind of building. DHCR's useful-life schedule gives an intercom system fifteen years and a lobby or vestibule door fifteen years. Its 2026 reasonable-cost schedule allows $2,178 per apartment for an audio intercom and $1,955 for a video intercom, and $15,741 for a vestibule or lobby door. Those are ceilings for rent purposes, not quotes, but a board that sets aside enough to replace the system and the inner door every fifteen years or so is unlikely to be caught short.

For the years in between, the useful budget line is repairs. Our Brooklyn list puts the common ones in ranges: a panel repair at $650 to $1,800, a riser at $850 to $2,400, a station at $250 to $525. A board that tracks which of those it has paid for, and when, will know when the system is nearing the end of its life rather than guessing.

A handful of apartments in our co-op are still rented by the sponsor to rent-stabilized tenants. Can the building's intercom work raise their rent?

DHCR's fact sheet explicitly addresses owners and managing agents of cooperative and condominium corporations, so capital-improvement applications are not limited to rental buildings. But the same fact sheet prohibits increases in buildings where 35% or fewer of the apartments are rent regulated. In a Kensington co-op where only a few sponsor-held apartments remain regulated, that threshold is usually not met.

Whatever the rent position, the regulated tenants are entitled to a working intercom, and the board and the sponsor need to settle between them who arranges repairs inside those apartments. We can work for either, on the same list prices.

Our building on Church Avenue has stores at street level that use the same entry system after hours. How does that change the numbers?

For a repair, not at all: the owner pays our list price, and how the cost is shared with commercial tenants is a matter for their leases. For a capital-improvement application, DHCR reduces the approved cost by the commercial share of the building. In DHCR's worked example, commercial space that makes up 10% of the building takes 10% off the approved cost before it is spread among the apartments.

On the technical side, a shared entry is worth separating where it can be. A shop's back door on its own release, or a separate panel for the shop's deliveries, means a fault in one does not shut the other out, and makes it clear whose equipment is whose when the next bill arrives.

Houses and Small Buildings on the Side Streets

We own a three-family house on East 4th Street with two tenants. Does any of the rent-stabilization machinery reach our bell?

As a rule, no. The Rent Guidelines Board describes rent stabilization as generally covering buildings of six or more apartments built before 1974, so a three-family house is normally outside it, with no capital-improvement increases to apply for and none to fear. You pay for repairs to the bells as you pay for any other repair.

HPD is a different matter. A house with three or more families is a multiple dwelling under the city's housing rules, so a tenant can complain to 311 and HPD can write a violation if the bell does not work. Keeping each button, the transformer and the door release working is the simplest way to stay out of the record.

We just bought a six-family building near McDonald Avenue. If we put in a new intercom, how would the cost come back to us?

If the building was built before 1974, its apartments are likely rent stabilized, and a new building-wide intercom could be the subject of a capital-improvement application once the old system has passed fifteen years. In a building of 35 or fewer apartments, DHCR spreads the approved cost over 144 months; for six apartments at DHCR's 2026 audio-intercom figure, the most that could count is $13,068, our arithmetic, collectible at no more than 2% of each rent a year and removed after thirty years.

That is a slow return, and many small owners decide a repair is the better use of the money. Our list puts the common repairs between $250 and $2,400. Check the building's open HPD violations first; any open Class B order has to be cleared before an application can succeed.

Types of Door Buzzer Equipment We Repair in Kensington

An entry system is a chain of parts with different ages and different lives. Knowing which link failed is what separates a list-price repair from a replacement.

Lobby and vestibule panels

Audio panels with a speaker, microphone and buttons or a keypad, older directory panels, and newer video panels. Most faults are one module, a button set or the cable behind the faceplate.

Riser cable and splices

The pairs that carry calls and the door release between the panel and each apartment. In walk-ups one cable up the stair hall; in six-story buildings one pair per line, spliced at junction boxes.

Power supplies and transformers

The supply that powers the panel and the strike, usually in a cellar room or closet, and the bell transformer in a house. A tired supply makes every symptom look like something else.

Electric strikes

Fail-secure strikes on the inner lobby door, which stay locked without power while the inside lever still opens the door, and fail-safe strikes, which unlock without power.

Apartment stations and handsets

Audio handsets, hands-free stations and video monitors inside apartments. Replacements are matched to the building's system so one repair does not create a mismatch.

Magnetic locks and exit sensors

Found on some glass lobby doors and store back doors; they need a motion sensor, a manual release and power-loss release to meet egress rules.

Door closers and latches

Not intercom parts, but half of every entry complaint. DHCR's schedule gives lobby and vestibule doors the same fifteen-year life as intercoms.

Telephone and app entry

Panels that dial residents' phones or apps instead of wired handsets, with a monthly service account. A repair here can mean a login, not a wire.

In practice almost every Kensington apartment building already has an intercom, so the question we meet is not whether one is required but who pays to keep it working. That depends on the ledger, not on the 1968 line.

ButterflyMX describes its apartment call box as requiring no building wiring, because calls go to residents' phones instead of to wired stations.

The panel still needs power, and the door still needs a strike or lock, a release circuit and a closer, all wired. In a building with a long front yard on Ocean Parkway, a cellar supply and an inner vestibule door, those are exactly the parts that fail.

A phone-based panel also changes who can be reached. A building should check that every household, including older residents without smartphones, can still be called and can still open the door.

Swiftlane's installation-cost article puts software for app-based entry at that range.

That line comes from a locksmith quoted in Brick Underground's November 2019 guide to intercom repair, and it is true more often than people like to admit: a volume turned down, a handset off its hook, a code changed and not passed on.

In Kensington it is worth ruling out for a further reason. Tenants who want to see what an owner has told DHCR about an intercom replacement can request the application file from DHCR directly, which we explain in the last question below. If a tenants' association and an owner agree that both should have the note, we are happy to send it to both.

Do handsets inside apartments count toward a building-wide replacement?
+

DHCR's fact sheet says work done in individual apartments that is not otherwise an improvement to the entire building is not eligible, and that an improvement must benefit all tenants. A new handset in one apartment is a repair. New stations fitted in every apartment as part of replacing the whole system are part of a building-wide installation. That is one more reason we mark each handset on an invoice as either a single-apartment repair or part of a full replacement.

Is a new system's monthly service fee something DHCR would count?
+

DHCR's fact sheet describes capital improvements as installations essential to the building and says they are not for operational costs. A monthly fee for a phone- or app-based entry service looks like an operating cost, but whether a particular charge counts is DHCR's decision, not ours. For a co-op the question does not arise: the fee is simply part of the building's running costs, paid through maintenance for as long as the service is used.

Our landlord filed an MCI that lists the intercom. Where can tenants see what was claimed?
+

DHCR's Fact Sheet #24 says tenants can review the owner's application by submitting a Records Access request, form REC-1, to DHCR's Gertz Plaza office in Jamaica, Queens, either to view it there or to have a copy mailed, and that an owner may also keep a copy with its supporting documents at the building. Tenants have sixty days from the notice to answer, can ask for a thirty-day extension for good cause, and can keep adding evidence until DHCR issues its order. After an order, an appeal can be filed within thirty-five days.
