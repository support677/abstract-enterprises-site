The Neighborhood

Why Bathgate Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Bathgate has been rebuilt more than once, and that is the first thing a camera plan here has to account for. The neighborhood takes its name from a Scottish immigrant who worked the land of the Morris estate in the early nineteenth century and later bought 140 acres from Gouverneur Morris II; sources disagree on whether his first name was Alexander or Andrew. Lehman College's neighborhood notes say his descendants sold the land to the city in 1884 for a park they expected to carry the family name, and that after a surveying dispute officials named it Crotona Park instead. The Third Avenue elevated line brought European immigrants up from lower Manhattan at the turn of the twentieth century, and the stretch of Bathgate Avenue from 171st to 174th Streets became, in one reader's memory on Shorpy, a continuous market of stores on both sides of the street. In the 1950s the Cross Bronx Expressway cut across the area just south of East Tremont Avenue. By 1980 so many residential buildings stood derelict that the city rezoned a former residential district of vacant land and buildings for what opened in 1982 as Bathgate Industrial Park. What is left today is a patchwork: century-old masonry beside 1990s houses, new elevator buildings and light-industrial plants. A camera system that suits one of those buildings can be wrong for the one next door.

The concern we expect to hear most often is the street. CBS2 reported residents of the Bathgate section pleading for help with car break-ins, with shards of glass from smashed windows lining the streets and residents saying the break-ins had become an everyday occurrence. The report noted that only 21 car break-ins had been reported to the NYPD in the neighborhood, and residents said the real number was probably more than double. One resident, Hernandez, told the station, "We have no security, no police," and added, "The lights -- there's no lighting." The page we read does not show the date of the report, so we treat it as a picture of what residents were facing at that time, not as a current count. But the two complaints in it, cars at the curb and no light to see them by, are exactly the two problems a camera has the hardest time with. A camera aimed at a dark row of parked cars from a third-floor window, with a wide lens and no light of its own, will record a grey blur. Fixing that is a matter of lens choice, mounting height, the camera's own lighting and where the camera points, and it is the reason every Bathgate quote starts with a walk on the sidewalk.

The precinct numbers explain why owners keep asking. Bathgate falls within the NYPD's 48th Precinct, which also covers some or all of Belmont, East Tremont, West Farms and Fordham University's Bronx campus. The precinct's CompStat report for the week of August 31 to September 6, 2026 shows 200 burglaries year to date against 189 for the same period of 2025, an increase of 5.8 percent. Those are precinct-wide figures, not Bathgate alone. In April 2026, Norwood News reported on a case in which 16 people were charged with 252 thefts of cars, tires, rims and catalytic converters, nine of them in the 48th Precinct, and prosecutors said many of the crimes were captured on surveillance video. Earlier in 2026, the NYPD described a citywide robbery pattern that included the 48th Precinct: 26 incidents in which three people on a motorized scooter snatched chains from victims. None of that tells us what will happen on any given block. It does tell us that in this precinct, footage gets used, and that the footage that helps is the kind with enough detail to show a face, a scooter or a car's shape at night.

The buildings themselves are varied. New York City's Department of Finance uses "Bathgate" as a neighborhood name in its property sales records, and those records for 2017 and 2019 list a one-family house at 1948 Bathgate Avenue built in 1901, a walk-up co-op at 2072 Bathgate Avenue built in 1911, two-family houses at 543 and 559 Claremont Parkway built in 1995, a three-family house on Webster Avenue from 1899, a commercial garage at 1732 Webster Avenue from 1930, store buildings on Third Avenue from 1924 to 1931 and office condominiums on Third Avenue from 2016 and 2017. Pat Arnow's photo essay on the area includes a four-story tenement that a man who grew up there said was like the one he was raised in. Newer housing is much larger: Openigloo lists 1880 Bathgate Avenue as a 379-unit, eight-floor building completed in 2018, and New York YIMBY reported a July 2026 housing lottery for 2246 Bathgate Avenue, a 14-unit building with intercoms and an elevator. In a tenement, every camera cable is a negotiation with brick and old plaster. In a 2018 building, the cable paths may already exist. We plan accordingly.

Then there is the industrial side. According to Wikipedia, Bathgate Industrial Park covers seven city blocks over 20 acres, with eight buildings and about 454,000 square feet of space for light industrial, distribution, office and educational uses. It was a joint project of the Port Authority of New York and New Jersey and the New York City Economic Development Corporation, and two schools, the Mott Hall Bronx High School and the Urban Assembly School for Applied Math and Science, sit on the site within the Bathgate Educational Campus. In January 2021 the park hosted a round-the-clock COVID-19 vaccination mega-site. Community Board 3's 197-a plan describes the streets between Webster and Third Avenues as designated local truck routes connecting with the Cross Bronx Expressway, with several sites zoned for manufacturing. For a business, that means camera questions most homeowners never face: trucks backing to docks, gates that open at odd hours, deliveries shared between tenants, and yards where a vehicle's arrival time matters as much as a face.

There is also the matter of the cameras themselves being attacked. CBS News reported a case in Morris Heights, on Davidson Avenue, in which suspects damaged numerous surveillance cameras inside and outside buildings before running off. That was not in Bathgate, and we mention it only because it shows what a camera at a Bronx entrance may have to survive. A camera that can be reached from the sidewalk or a stair rail and knocked off its mount is a camera that records the first second of a problem and nothing after it.

We want to be plain about what our research did not find. We looked for Reddit threads, HPD violations, 311 complaints, Buildings Department permits and Landmarks Preservation Commission rules tied to cameras in Bathgate and found nothing specific, and we found no historic district covering the neighborhood. Boundaries also vary by source: the Department of Finance uses Bathgate for blocks around Bathgate Avenue and Claremont Parkway, Pat Arnow describes a Bathgate section of East Tremont between the Cross Bronx Expressway and Tremont Avenue, and several listing sites label buildings on Bathgate Avenue as East Tremont or Claremont. We go by your street address, not a map line. Where this page describes what we expect inside a 1901 or 1911 wall, it is drawing on the age and type of the buildings in public records and on what we find in buildings of that age, not on a survey of Bathgate's interiors.

The tenements and walk-ups from around 1900

Buildings like the four-story tenement Pat Arnow photographed, the 1911 walk-up co-op at 2072 Bathgate Avenue and the 1901 house at 1948 Bathgate Avenue are built of brick, with plaster inside and floors framed in wood. They were wired for electric light long after they were built, and every round of new wiring since has gone wherever someone could fit it. For cameras, that usually means the basement is the main road: cable runs along the basement ceiling, then rises through a pipe chase, a dumbwaiter shaft if one survives, or along the outside of the building inside a weatherproof raceway. Drilling through a century-old brick front for a camera takes a masonry bit, a slow hand and a plan for sealing the hole against water. We avoid putting a camera's mount in a mortar joint where we can, because mortar of that age often holds less than the brick around it, and we keep holes small and sealed so that winter rain does not follow the cable into the wall.

The store buildings on Third Avenue

The Third Avenue store buildings from 1924 to 1931 put a camera in a different setting. The shop wants to see its door, its register and the sidewalk in front of the window. Above it, there may be apartments or offices whose owner is not the shopkeeper. Inside, the ceiling of an old store is often a dropped ceiling with decades of abandoned cable above it, and the front is usually framed by a roll-down gate whose housing takes the best spot for a camera. We mount store cameras where the gate housing does not block them when it is down, because a camera that sees nothing once the gate closes is a camera that sleeps through the hours that matter. Cable for the store runs inside the store whenever possible, so the upstairs owner's walls are left alone.

The garage and the industrial park buildings

The 1930 commercial garage at 1732 Webster Avenue and the 1982 buildings of Bathgate Industrial Park share one feature that matters for cameras: exposed structure and long distances. In those buildings cable rarely hides inside a wall. It rides on J-hooks across a ceiling, drops down inside conduit, and passes through concrete block walls through holes we core-drill and seal. Distance becomes the main design constraint, because standard network cable for a powered camera has a length limit, and a yard camera at the far end of a lot may need a closer switch or a different path. Where cable is low enough for a forklift, a truck mirror or a pallet to hit, it goes into conduit.

The 1990s two-family houses

The two-family houses on Claremont Parkway, built in 1995 according to Department of Finance records, were part of the rebuilding that followed the area's abandonment. Houses of that period are generally easier to wire than a tenement, with framed walls, an attic or crawl space, and a basement or utility room for the recorder. The complication in a two-family is not the wall; it is the arrangement. Two households share a front walk and often a driveway, and the camera that protects the owner's car also sees the tenant's door. We plan those views with the owner and make sure the tenant knows where the cameras point.

The newer elevator buildings

The 2018 building at 1880 Bathgate Avenue, with 379 units over eight floors, and the 2026 building at 2246 Bathgate Avenue, with 14 units, an elevator and intercoms, both arrived with infrastructure: a telecom room, risers and an entry system. Our job in a building like that is usually to extend or improve, not to start from nothing, and the first question is who holds the passwords to the existing recorder. A managing agent or developer may need to hand those over, and the building may have rules on contractor hours and insurance. We ask for those rules before we book.

Decide what each camera is for

The most useful thing you can do before we arrive is to write one line for each camera you imagine: "see who comes through the front door", "see the cars from the hydrant to the corner", "see the register and the person standing at it", "see trucks arriving at dock 2". A camera with a clear job can be given the right lens and the right height. A camera with a vague job, "watch the front", usually ends up with a wide lens that shows the whole street and no one's face. Bring the list, and we will tell you which jobs one camera can do and which need two.

Think about where the recorder will be safe

The recorder holds everything, and a recorder sitting on a shelf by the front door can be carried off in the same visit as the thing it was meant to record. In a walk-up, a locked basement room or a cabinet in the owner's apartment usually works. In a store, a locked back office or a cabinet bolted out of sight. In an industrial unit, the landlord's network room may be off-limits, so we may need a lockable cabinet of our own. Have a spot in mind, and if nothing comes to mind, that is a question for the walk-through.

On brick fronts. The front of a Bathgate tenement is usually the best place for a curb camera, because it is high and faces the street. It is also the wall that takes the weather. We place the mount on solid brick, use anchors made for old masonry, and drill the cable hole slightly upward from inside to out where the wall allows, so water runs away from the opening rather than into it. Every opening is sealed. If the brick around the chosen spot is crumbling, we tell you before drilling and pick another spot rather than hang a camera on loose masonry. We also look up: cornices and fire escapes can block a view or give a vandal a perch, and both change where the camera goes.

On fire safety in old buildings. In March 2026, PIX11 reported a multi-alarm fire at 505 East 184th Street, between Bassford and Bathgate Avenues, in which one person died. We have no information about how that fire started or spread, and we mention it only as a reminder of how much is at stake in the roofs and walls of older buildings. When we run camera cable through a cockloft, a floor or a wall that is meant to slow a fire, we seal the opening with firestop afterward, and we keep cable well clear of heating pipes, flues and electrical panels. Cable is never left draped loose in a cockloft.

What a dock or yard camera has to do

A camera at a loading dock is doing a different job from one at a front door. It has to show which vehicle arrived and when, what came off it and who signed for it, often across a wide space with headlights pointing straight at the lens. In a park with several tenants, it may share a dock, a gate or a yard with a business that has its own interests. The views we plan for docks usually include one wide camera that shows the whole bay and the vehicle, and a second, tighter camera at the point where goods change hands, so a dispute about a missing pallet can be settled by looking. Where a gate opens to a truck-route street, a camera set low and tight at the gate sees each vehicle at a slow, predictable point, which gives far better detail than a camera trying to catch a truck moving across a lot. For yards, we look at where trucks park overnight, where light falls after dark and where a person on foot could come in, and we plan coverage around those three.

Industrial buildings also bring their own network rules. A landlord may own the telecom room and the building's network, and may not want a tenant's cameras on it. That is fine; we can set up the camera system on its own switch and recorder in the tenant's space, keep it off the landlord's network entirely, and give it its own internet connection for phone viewing. We ask about this in the first conversation so the plan matches what the lease allows.

Rules and practical limits for business cameras

  • Licensing. New York General Business Law §69-m says no one may install, service or maintain security or fire alarm systems as a business without a license. The Department of State's application notes that installing "passive", non-detecting devices does not require licensure, and whether a particular camera counts as detecting is a question for the Department. We do the work under NYS license #12000287431 either way.
  • Common areas and leases. Brick Underground notes that a tenant needs the landlord's permission to install equipment in common areas, including hallways. In a multi-tenant industrial building, that applies to shared corridors, docks and yards; we ask for the landlord's written approval before mounting anything outside the tenant's own space.
  • No camera mandate. There is no blanket rule in the NYC Administrative Code requiring every apartment building to have cameras, according to security-systems.nyc. Insurance carriers, landlords and leases may set their own requirements, so check those documents.
  • Sealed penetrations. Where cable passes through a fire-rated wall or floor, such as a block wall between units or a floor between a store and the apartments above, we seal the opening with firestop.
  • Things that stick out into a walkway. The 2010 ADA Standards limit how far wall-mounted objects can protrude into a walking path at certain heights. Cameras inside a store or office are mounted high or close to the wall so they do not become something to walk into.
  • Privacy inside a business. Cameras cover doors, registers, docks, stockrooms and yards. We do not place cameras in restrooms, changing areas or anywhere people expect privacy, and we will not install hidden cameras for that purpose.

Field Notes: Planning a Curb Camera on a Dark Bathgate Block

We do not publish stories about a particular customer's building unless we can document them, so this is the routine our technicians follow, not a tale about one address. It is how we plan a camera for the problem residents described to CBS2: cars at the curb, broken windows and poor light.

We start on the sidewalk, not on the ladder. We stand where a thief would stand beside a parked car and look back at the building to see which windows and walls can see that spot, and from how high. Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Bathgate: Questions Answered

Cars at the curb and dark streets

Can one camera on the front of my building cover all the cars parked along the curb?

Usually not with useful detail. A single wide camera on a Bathgate front wall can show the whole block, but at that width a person beside a car fifty feet away is a handful of pixels, enough to see that something happened and not enough to tell who did it. The better plan is to split the job: one camera aimed down the curb in one direction, another aimed the other way, each with a narrower lens covering a shorter stretch, plus a wide view if you want the overall picture. On the walk-through we measure the distance from your wall to the far lane and to each end of the stretch you care about, and we tell you how many cameras it takes to see faces over that distance. Sometimes the honest answer is that your building can cover its own frontage well and the rest of the block belongs to the neighbor's building.

A camera with its own spotlight, or plain infrared: which is better when the streetlight is out?

They do different things. Infrared lets a camera see in the dark in black and white without visible light, which is discreet but shows no colors, so a car or a jacket loses the detail that makes it identifiable. Infrared can also bounce off the glass and shiny paint of parked cars and wash out the picture. A camera with a built-in white light or one that works with a separate light shows color at night and makes it plain to anyone nearby that the area is lit and watched. On a dark Bathgate block where residents have complained about missing lighting, we often recommend color low-light cameras with their own light for the curb views, aimed down and along the street so they do not shine into neighbors' windows. We check the light's spread at night before we leave.

Why do fast scooters turn into a blur on my camera at night?

Because at night a camera keeps its shutter open longer to gather enough light, and anything moving fast smears across the frame. The NYPD has described a 2026 pattern in the 48th Precinct of chain snatchings by people on a motorized scooter, which is exactly the kind of fast movement that low-light settings handle badly. The fix is more light at the spot, so the camera can use a faster shutter, and a camera placed where moving things slow down or come toward it rather than across it, such as facing along the sidewalk instead of straight across. We also set the recording frame rate and the camera's night settings deliberately instead of leaving the factory defaults, and we test at night with someone walking quickly through the view.

Someone tore down the camera over our entrance. What survives a determined vandal?

Height and shape matter more than anything. A camera that can be reached from the stoop, a railing or a fire escape will eventually be hit. We mount entrance cameras out of easy reach, choose dome housings with impact-rated covers where a camera must be lower, and run the cable inside the wall or a metal raceway so there is no loose wire to pull. A second camera that watches the first one, often from inside the vestibule, means that if someone does attack the outside camera, the attack itself is recorded. CBS News reported a case in Morris Heights in which suspects damaged numerous cameras inside and outside buildings; the buildings that recover best from that are the ones whose recorder was locked away and whose cameras covered each other.

Old buildings and cable paths

Is it safe to run camera cable through the cockloft or over the roof of an old tenement?

It can be, if it is done carefully. In a Bathgate tenement the cockloft, the space between the top ceiling and the roof, is often the only continuous path between the front and back of the building at the top. We use it when a camera is needed at the roof door or the rear of the building and the basement route is blocked. Cable in a cockloft is fixed in place, kept clear of flues, heating pipes and electrical work, and any opening it makes through a ceiling or a fire-rated wall is sealed with firestop afterward. Over the roof, cable goes in a sunlight-resistant raceway and is fixed so it does not lift in wind or get stepped on. We do not drape cable loose, and we do not cut through roofing to get it in.

Can camera cable share the old pathway the cable TV company ran up the side of our building?

Sometimes it can run alongside it, but we do not use another company's equipment without permission. Many Bathgate buildings have a line of old raceway or clipped cable running up the outside wall from the TV or phone company. That route often shows us where the building lets cable pass, which saves us looking. Where the old raceway is the building owner's and has room, a camera cable can sometimes share it. Where it belongs to a utility, we run our own weatherproof raceway beside it rather than inside it. Either way, the new run is labeled and documented so nobody mistakes your camera cable for a dead TV line and cuts it.

The developer's cameras in our new building: how do we get the recorder and the passwords handed over?

Start by asking the developer or managing agent for three things in writing: the administrator login for the recorder, the list of cameras and where each cable lands, and the name of whoever installed the system. New buildings on Bathgate Avenue, like the 14-unit building at 2246 Bathgate Avenue with its intercoms and elevator, often come with systems whose passwords stay with the installer. Without the administrator login, nobody can add cameras, change recording settings or export footage properly. Once you have the login, we change it to one you control, check every camera, and write down the system's details for your records. If the login has been lost, many recorders can be reset, but some lose their settings and recordings in the process, so we look before we do anything.

Docks, stores and footage

Our unit at Bathgate Industrial Park shares a loading dock. Can our cameras watch our bay without recording the neighbor's?

Yes, within limits. We aim the dock cameras at your bay and the doors and floor space you use, and most cameras let us black out parts of the picture so that the neighboring bay is masked in the recording. Where a shared approach or yard has to be in view to show a vehicle arriving at your door, we keep that view as narrow as the job allows. Before anything is mounted in a shared area, we need your landlord's written approval, and it is worth telling the neighboring tenant what the cameras will and will not see. A clear, agreed set of views is also what makes the footage useful if there is ever a dispute about who received what.

How do I hand footage to the 48th Precinct after an incident without leaving the store?

We set the system up so that you can do it from your phone or the store computer. At the handover we show you how to find the clip by time and camera, export it as a file with the date and time on it, and save it to a USB drive or share it as a download. We also show you how to save the clip on the recorder so it is not overwritten while you wait for detectives. Keep the original recording untouched and give out copies. In the April 2026 car-theft case reported by Norwood News, prosecutors said many crimes were captured on surveillance video, which is a reminder that a clean, well-dated export is worth knowing how to make before you need one.

Headlights on Third Avenue wash out our storefront camera at night. Can that be fixed?

Usually, yes. Headlights, bus lights and shop signs pointing toward a lens can turn everything else in the picture dark. Cameras with good wide dynamic range handle bright and dark areas in the same frame much better than budget models, and a small change in angle so the lens is not looking straight into oncoming traffic helps a lot. We also check whether the camera is looking through the shop window, because glass reflects the store's own lights back into the lens at night; mounting outside under the sign, or tight against the glass with a shroud, solves that. We test at night with traffic passing before we call it done.

Neighbors and the law

My neighbor says my backyard camera looks into his yard. Can he take me to court?

He may be able to. New York Civil Rights Law §52-a, sometimes called the backyard camera law, lets residential owners or tenants sue over a neighbor's video surveillance of their backyard under certain conditions. The simple way to avoid the problem is to aim cameras at your own property and mask any part of the neighbor's yard that falls into the picture, which most cameras allow. On a Bathgate house or two-family with a small rear yard, we plan rear cameras to cover your doors, windows and yard, and we show you the masked view before we leave. If a dispute is already under way, talk to a lawyer; we can adjust the cameras, but we cannot give legal advice.

Does any city law make a Bathgate landlord put cameras in the building?

Not as a general rule. Our research found no blanket law in the NYC Administrative Code requiring every apartment building to have security cameras. Separate rules apply to entry systems in many multiple dwellings, and leases, insurance policies, lenders or regulatory agreements for affordable housing may require cameras in particular buildings. For a tenant, that means asking the landlord is the place to start, and for a landlord it means checking those documents, not the city code, to see if cameras are owed. Where an owner decides to add cameras, we plan them for the common areas, entrances and exterior, not inside apartments.

FAQ

Bathgate Security Camera Installation FAQ

I rent in a Bathgate walk-up. Can I put a doorbell camera on my apartment door?
+

Ask your landlord first. Doorbell cameras on apartment doors are a gray area: many New York landlords tolerate them, but they are subject to your lease, and the hallway outside your door is a common area that belongs to the building. A camera that only sees the space directly in front of your door is less likely to cause a dispute than one that looks down the whole hall. Get the landlord's agreement in writing, and if they say no, a camera inside your apartment pointed at your own door is the usual alternative.

If my car is broken into outside our building, will the landlord hand over the camera footage?
+

Not necessarily. Brick Underground notes that a landlord is not obligated to share building surveillance footage with a tenant. Many will help, especially if the police ask for it, but you cannot count on it. Ask quickly, before the recorder overwrites that day, give the exact time, and file a police report so detectives can make the request. If the curb in front of your own house matters to you, cameras you own are the only footage you control.

Do I have to put up a sign saying the building has cameras?
+

We could not confirm a city law requiring it. One vendor blog cites a NYC local law requiring notice of common-area cameras, but we were unable to verify that law, so we do not state it as a rule. Leases, co-op rules or insurance policies may ask for signs, and posting a simple notice at the entrance costs little, tells residents where they stand and may make someone think twice. We are happy to suggest where signs make sense.

Is it legal for a camera on my building to record the sidewalk and the street?
+

Generally, recording what anyone can see from a public sidewalk or street is allowed, which is why storefront and building cameras commonly cover both. The limits come where a camera looks into places people expect privacy, such as a neighbor's windows or backyard, where New York's backyard camera law can apply. We aim Bathgate curb cameras along the street and mask neighbors' windows and yards out of the picture.

Can a camera inside my front window watch my car on the street?
+

By day, often yes. At night it usually fails, because the camera's infrared light bounces off the window glass and fills the picture with glare, and the room's own lights reflect back into the lens. If an outside mount is not allowed, the camera must sit flat against the glass with its infrared turned off and a shroud around the lens, and it will need streetlight or an outside light to see anything after dark. An outside camera on the wall almost always does a better job.

Why does the installation cost more than the cameras themselves?
+

Because in an older Bathgate building most of the work is the cable, not the camera. Finding a path from a front wall to a safe recorder through brick, basements, risers or a cockloft, drilling and sealing each opening, labeling every run, and testing each camera by day and night takes far longer than screwing a camera to a wall. That work is also what decides whether the system still records next winter. The written quote shows labor and equipment separately.

Should I just put the cameras up myself?
+

For one camera on a small house with good Wi-Fi, many people do fine on their own. It gets harder fast in a brick tenement or a business: drilling old masonry without cracking it, keeping water out of the hole, getting cable through a basement or roof safely, sealing rated walls and aiming a long lens at the right distance. If you start yourself and get stuck, call us; we can take over from where you are and use the equipment you bought if it suits the job.

Our walk-up co-op is adding cameras. Who should be able to see the footage?
+

That is the board's decision, and it is worth writing down before installation. A common arrangement is that one or two board members and the managing agent have full access, the super can view live but not export, and footage is released to shareholders or police through a set request. Building-wide security changes in co-ops often need board approval in the first place. We set up separate user accounts with the permissions the board chooses, so nobody shares a password.
