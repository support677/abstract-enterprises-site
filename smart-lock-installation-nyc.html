The Neighborhood

Why New York City Apartments Need Smart Lock Installation Done Properly

Most smart-lock advice is written for a house with one owner, one front door and nobody else holding a key. New York is the opposite. The typical front door here belongs to an apartment in a building of dozens or hundreds of units. Someone other than the occupant usually owns it. A managing agent, a superintendent or a co-op board has rules about who can change it, and state and city law say what must stay on it. The lobby door downstairs is a separate system run by the building, and the person who wants the smart lock may be a renter whose lease runs out in eleven months.

That is why so many keyless projects in the city go wrong in ways that have nothing to do with the lock itself. A tenant swaps the landlord's deadbolt for a keypad model and gets a letter from management. A landlord installs app-only entry and finds out the city now says tenants cannot be forced to use it to get into their own apartment. A shareholder goes keyless and then learns the proprietary lease requires the board to hold a working duplicate. A shop owner fits a slick touchscreen lock on a door that a fire inspector expects to open from the inside with a single, simple motion. Each of those is a rules problem first and a hardware problem second.

The rules are not obscure once you know where to look. New York's Multiple Dwelling Law, section 51-c, gives a tenant in a multiple dwelling the right to install and maintain a lock of their own on the unit's entrance door, in addition to the owner's lock, so long as it is no bigger than three inches in circumference and the tenant hands the landlord a duplicate key on request; a lease clause charging extra for that right is void. The city's Housing Maintenance Code, section 27-2043, puts the duty to provide the key lock on the owner, and in class A buildings that means a heavy-duty latch set and a heavy-duty deadbolt operable by key from outside and by thumb-turn inside, plus a chain door guard. HPD's fire-safety guidance says plainly that double-cylinder locks — the kind that need a key on the inside — are illegal on apartment doors.

Then there is the newer layer that applies directly to keyless entry. The Tenant Data Privacy Act, Local Law 63 of 2021, covers owners of multiple dwellings that use smart access systems: key fobs, cards, phone apps or biometrics. Among other things it bars an owner from requiring a tenant to use a smart access system to get into their own unit, requires express consent before collecting tenants' data, limits what may be collected, and requires authentication data to be destroyed within 90 days unless it is anonymised. It followed fights that played out in public. In 2019 tenants in a Hell's Kitchen building sued their landlord for a physical key after a Latch system went in; the case settled privately. The same year, tenants at Atlantic Plaza Towers, a 700-unit rent-stabilised complex in Brownsville, objected to state regulators over their owner's plan for facial-recognition entry.

The buildings themselves add the physical complications. A large share of the city's apartments sit in pre-war construction, where entrance doors are often heavy, steel-clad or fire-rated, and fitted with mortise locks set into a pocket inside the door rather than the bored cylindrical deadbolts that most consumer smart locks are designed around. Post-war towers swap that problem for concrete walls and long corridors that swallow Wi-Fi and Bluetooth. New condos often come with their own access platform already chosen by the developer. None of this rules out a smart lock; it decides which kind fits and how it is fitted.

  • 3.7 million: housing units in New York City in 2023, according to HPD's testimony on the New York City Housing and Vacancy Survey — overwhelmingly in multi-family buildings with shared entrances.
  • 1.41 percent: the citywide net rental vacancy rate in the 2023 survey. Renters stay put, so the lock you add may need to come off cleanly years later.
  • About 1 million: apartments covered by rent stabilisation, per the same HPD testimony — tenancies where lock changes, keys and services are closely regulated.

Put together, those facts describe the job. A smart lock in New York has to respect a landlord's right to a key, a tenant's right not to be locked into an app, a fire code that wants one-motion exit, and a door that may be eighty years old. We install to all four, and we tell you up front when the lock you bought cannot meet one of them on your door.

Door and lock specifics we check on every New York job

Mortise or cylindrical? A bored cylindrical deadbolt sits in a round hole through the face of the door with a short latch into the edge. A mortise lock is a rectangular box sunk into a pocket cut into the edge of the door, with the cylinder screwed into it from outside. Most consumer smart deadbolts and most retrofit units are designed for the first. On a mortise door the choices are a smart lock made in mortise format, keeping the mortise body and changing only the cylinder and trim where the products allow it, or adding a separate smart deadbolt where the door has room and the building permits it. A 2014 thread on the SmartThings community that is still being added to opens with a New Yorker's question — “I have metal Brownstone doors with mortise-type locks. Are there any smart locks for this type?” — and more than a decade of replies never settles on a simple drop-in answer. That matches what we see: mortise doors need a decision, not a default.

Steel-clad and kalamein doors. Many older apartment and stair doors are fire-rated slab doors with a gypsum core, sometimes clad in steel — the type the trade calls kalamein. Fire-safety writing notes that older examples sometimes had an asbestos-cement core and that many are still in buildings put up before 1980. We don't core or rout an older rated door blind. Where a lock change needs new holes in a door that might be original, we stop, tell you, and work with the building on whether the door can be prepared or whether a surface-applied or cylinder-swap approach is the safer route.

Door thickness and backset. Heavy pre-war doors are often thicker than the range a consumer lock's tailpiece is built for, and the backset — the distance from the door edge to the centre of the lock — may not match. We measure both before you buy, or before we open the box if you already have.

Self-closing hardware. City law requires doors opening onto interior corridors and stairs in residential buildings to be self-closing, and HPD can write a class C immediately hazardous violation when they aren't maintained. A smart lock that drags, or a motor-driven bolt that can't throw because the door no longer pulls fully shut, turns into a self-closing problem. We adjust the strike and closer so the door latches on its own before we call the job finished.

One-motion exit from inside. Every lock we fit on a dwelling-unit door opens from the inside without a key, a code or a phone. HPD says double-cylinder locks are illegal on apartment doors. Keypad models with a keyed or coded inside are not something we install on a residential entrance.

Most building owners who modernise the unit doors also pair the job with the street door. See our NYC door buzzer installation service if the entrance still runs on an older buzzer and strike.

Compliance callouts for business doors

  • Free egress from inside. Exit doors must open from the egress side without a key or special knowledge. A keypad on the outside is fine; a keypad or key on the inside of a public exit is not. Electrified locks have to release for exit, which is why many of our installs include a push-to-exit button or motion request-to-exit sensor.
  • Fail-safe versus fail-secure. An electric strike or magnetic lock either unlocks or stays locked when power fails. The choice depends on whether the door is part of a required exit route and on what the building and the fire code expect; we specify it per door, not per catalogue.
  • Roll-down gates. A 2009 city law required many storefront gates to be at least 70 percent transparent by July 1, 2026. In July 2026 the City Council unanimously passed Int. 910 repealing that deadline so a compliant gate is needed only when a gate is replaced for other reasons, with Department of Buildings permit rules unchanged. Your door lock and your gate are separate decisions, but we plan them together so the keypad is reachable and protected.
  • Mixed-use buildings. Where a shop shares an entrance with apartments above, the residential side carries the Housing Maintenance Code and, if smart access is used for tenants, the Tenant Data Privacy Act.

For larger commercial sites — multiple doors, schedules, elevator readers — the work moves into full access control. Our commercial security camera installation in NYC is often paired with it so a door event and the camera view sit on the same timeline.

Smart Lock Installation in NYC: Questions Answered

These are the questions New Yorkers ask before a smart lock goes on a door, grouped by who is asking: renters, co-op and condo owners, anyone dealing with fire-safety rules and old doors, building owners managing several units, and shop owners. Cost, scheduling and warranty questions are in the FAQ further down. None of this is legal advice; where a statute or city rule is mentioned, we name it so you can read it yourself or ask your own lawyer.

Renting in New York

Can a New York City renter put a smart lock on the apartment door without asking the landlord?

Partly. Multiple Dwelling Law § 51-c gives a tenant in a multiple dwelling the right to install and maintain their own lock on the apartment's entrance door in addition to the lock the owner provides. The lock can be no more than three inches in circumference, and the tenant must give the landlord or the landlord's agent a duplicate key when asked. Any lease clause that charges extra rent or a fee for exercising that right is void. The section does not cover apartments in a municipal housing authority building, or units used as a hotel, motel or dormitory.

What the law describes is an additional lock, not a replacement. Taking out the owner's deadbolt and putting a keypad model in its place is a different act, and most leases treat it as an alteration that needs consent. The two routes that stay comfortably inside a tenant's rights are a retrofit unit that replaces only the inside thumb-turn of the existing deadbolt — the outside of the door and the landlord's key are unchanged — or a separate, modest secondary lock where the door has room for one. We will tell you which applies to your door before we start, and we keep the job reversible.

Our honest advice is still to send management a short note. It costs nothing, it heads off a letter from the managing agent, and it gives you a paper trail if anyone later says the lock was not allowed.

Does the landlord get a code or a key to a tenant-installed smart lock?

The law speaks of a duplicate key, so the safe assumption is that the owner is entitled to a working mechanical means of opening the lock you add, on request. With a retrofit unit that sits over the existing deadbolt, that is already taken care of: the landlord's key opens the door exactly as before. With a separate keypad deadbolt, choose a model with a keyed cylinder so a physical key can be handed over. A temporary PIN code is a courtesy you can also offer, but it is not a substitute for what the statute describes.

The reason buildings insist is emergency access — a burst pipe, a fire, a medical emergency when you are away. A New York court ordered a Manhattan co-op shareholder in the 1990s to surrender a duplicate key without conditions after she tried to limit when the building could use it, according to the Cooperator's account of 111 Tenants Corp. v. Stromberg. Plan for the key, not around it.

Can my landlord make a phone app the only way into my apartment?

Not under the Tenant Data Privacy Act. Local Law 63 of 2021 applies to owners of multiple dwellings that use smart access systems — key fobs, cards, apps or biometric readers — and HPD's summary says an owner may not require use of a smart access system to gain entry to the tenant's own dwelling unit. The law also requires express consent, in writing or through the app, before the owner collects tenants' reference data, limits what may be collected, bars selling it, and says the data cannot be used to harass or evict.

In practice that means a unit door that a landlord converts to keyless still needs a non-smart way in for a tenant who wants one, usually a key. The 2019 Hell's Kitchen case, where tenants sued for a physical key after app-based locks went in and an elderly resident couldn't operate the new lock, is the situation the rule was written to prevent.

What happens to my smart lock when I move out of a rental?

If you installed it, it is generally yours to take, and the door should go back to the way you found it. That is the strongest argument for a retrofit unit: we remove it, refit the original thumb-turn, and the door looks untouched. A separate secondary lock leaves holes, so either agree with the landlord that it stays, or budget for the door to be patched and the lock removed.

Before you hand back the keys, delete the lock from your account, remove any guest codes and factory-reset it, so the next tenant isn't living behind a lock that still answers to your phone. If the landlord is keeping it, transfer ownership in the app rather than leaving your account attached.

Co-op and condo boards

Does a co-op board or condo board have to approve a smart lock on my unit door?

It depends on the building's rules and on what is being changed. Co-op and condo alteration agreements are written mainly for renovation work — walls, plumbing, electrical, windows — and the Cooperator notes that the line for small cosmetic jobs varies from building to building. The unit entrance door is a particular case, though: in many buildings its exterior face, its fire rating and its closer are treated as building elements, and the hallway side is common space.

A retrofit lock that changes nothing on the corridor side is the least likely to need approval. A full deadbolt swap, a new hole in the door, a change of hardware finish visible from the hall, or anything that touches a fire-rated door is more likely to. Ask the managing agent one specific question — “Does replacing the deadbolt on my unit door need board approval or an alteration agreement?” — and send us the answer. Boards also routinely ask for proof that the contractor is licensed and insured; we send the certificate of insurance to the agent before the visit.

Can a co-op board insist on a working key when the shareholder goes keyless?

Usually yes. Proprietary leases commonly give the corporation the right to enter at reasonable times on notice and at any time in an emergency, and require a shareholder who changes a lock to deliver a duplicate of the new key. That is on top of the Multiple Dwelling Law's duplicate-key provision. Keyless entry does not change the obligation; it changes how you meet it.

The cleanest way is a smart deadbolt with a keyed override and a key lodged with management, or a retrofit unit that leaves the building's key working. A code given to the superintendent can be added as well, but keep in mind that codes can be changed or deleted from a phone without anyone noticing, which is exactly why boards prefer a mechanical key they can rely on.

Fire rules and old doors

Is a keypad deadbolt that needs a key on the inside legal on a New York apartment door?

No. HPD's fire-safety guidance says it is illegal to install double-cylinder locks on apartment doors because they can trap people inside during a fire, and that apartment doors should open from inside without a key. Some keypad and decorative models are sold with a key cylinder on both faces or a code required to exit; neither belongs on a New York dwelling-unit entrance.

The model-code commentary that most American building codes follow allows a dwelling-unit door to have a second releasing motion for a deadbolt, night latch or chain, provided it opens from the inside without a key or tool; NYC's own code and the authority having jurisdiction have the final word on any specific door. Our rule on residential doors is simple: thumb-turn or lever inside, nothing that needs a code, a phone or a key to get out.

Will a smart lock interfere with the self-closing door the city requires?

It can, if it is fitted carelessly. Local Law 111 of 2018 requires doors that open onto interior corridors or stairs in residential occupancies to be self-closing, and HPD treats an unmaintained self-closing door as a class C immediately hazardous violation with 21 days to correct. HPD's fire-safety page puts it as buildings with three or more apartments needing self-closing doors and residents not tampering with them.

A motorised smart deadbolt does not close a door, and it cannot throw its bolt if the door hasn't fully latched. If the strike is slightly out of line, the closer gets blamed for a door that won't lock, and someone props it or loosens the closer. We set the strike so the latch catches on the closer's swing alone, check that the bolt throws without the door being pushed, and leave the closer as we found it unless you ask us to adjust it. On a new install that is part of the job; on an existing problem it is a short service call.

Can a smart lock go on a steel-clad fire door in a pre-war building?

Often, but carefully. Many older apartment and stair doors are fire-rated assemblies — some with a gypsum core under wood or steel skins, the type known as kalamein — and fire-safety writing notes that some older ones had an asbestos-cement core. Cutting a new bore or enlarging an existing one changes the door, and on a rated door that is not a casual decision.

Our approach on these doors is to use what is already there. A retrofit unit on an existing single-cylinder deadbolt needs no new holes. A smart lock that fits the existing mortise pocket or uses the existing bore is next best. Where a lock you want would need new preparation on a rated or possibly original door, we stop, explain the options and, in a co-op or condo, let the building weigh in before anything is drilled.

Can a mortise lock on a co-op unit door go smart when the hallway side has to match the building?

Yes, and in many pre-war co-ops that matching requirement is the real design constraint. Some buildings that have kept uniform corridor hardware — the same lever or knob, escutcheon and finish on every unit door — expect the hallway face to stay that way, while what happens inside the apartment is the shareholder's business. That points toward solutions that leave the outside alone: a retrofit unit on a separate single-cylinder deadbolt where the door has one, or a smart conversion that changes only the inside trim of the lock.

Where a shareholder wants a keypad on the hallway side, we bring the managing agent a photo of the proposed trim in the building's finish and the lock's specification before anything is ordered. Mortise-format smart locks replace the lock body in the existing pocket, which avoids new holes in a heavy door, but they do change what the corridor sees. We measure the pocket, the backset and the door thickness first, and we would rather tell you the board is likely to say no than install something that has to come off.

Can the Wi-Fi bridge or hub go in the public hallway outside my apartment?

Usually not, and it rarely needs to. The corridor, its outlets and its ceiling belong to the building, not to the unit, so plugging your own equipment into a hallway outlet or fixing anything to a corridor wall is the kind of thing house rules and managing agents object to, and a device left in a common area can simply walk away. Bridges and border routers belong inside the apartment, as close to the entrance as an outlet allows.

That is normally close enough. August, for example, recommends keeping its Connect bridge within 10 to 15 feet of the lock with full Wi-Fi bars where the bridge sits, which in most apartments means a foyer, coat-closet or hall outlet on your side of the door. If the only nearby outlet is in the corridor, the answer is a lock with Wi-Fi built in, a mesh node inside the unit, or a Matter-over-Thread lock paired to a border router in the front room — not a box in the hallway.

Building owners and small landlords

Should the lobby door and the apartment doors be on the same keyless system?

Sometimes, but not by default. The building entrance is the owner's responsibility and, in class A buildings of eight or more units, usually tied to an intercom with remote door release under the Multiple Dwelling Law. The unit doors are where tenants' rights and the Tenant Data Privacy Act bite hardest. One platform for both is convenient for a small owner, but it also means every tenant's unit access sits in the same database as their lobby entries, with the consent, data-minimisation and 90-day retention rules attached.

For two- to six-family buildings, a common and sensible split is a keypad or fob reader with an electric strike on the street door, run by the owner, and individual unit locks that remain keyed, with smart features only where the tenant opts in. Larger buildings with a managing agent usually want the lobby, package room and amenity doors on one access-control platform and unit doors left out of it.

As a small landlord, how do I cut off a former tenant's code without breaking the lockout law?

Timing is everything. New York City's unlawful-eviction law, Administrative Code § 26-521, makes it unlawful to remove, plug or disable the lock on the entrance door, or to change it without giving the occupant a key, for anyone who has lawfully occupied the unit for thirty consecutive days or more, unless there is a court warrant or a government vacate order. Section 26-523 makes a violation a class A misdemeanour with civil penalties of $1,000 to $10,000 per violation. Deleting a sitting occupant's code is functionally the same as changing the lock on them.

So deactivate a credential only after possession has actually and lawfully passed back to you: keys returned, the unit surrendered, or the marshal's eviction carried out. Keep a written log of who holds which code or fob and when each was issued and removed. We set up the system so that removing one tenant's access is one action that doesn't affect anyone else, and we can do the re-keying or re-enrolment on the day you take the unit back.

What does the Tenant Data Privacy Act change for an owner adding fob or app entry?

It turns a hardware purchase into a policy decision. HPD's summary of Local Law 63 of 2021 lists the main duties: get express consent before collecting reference data; collect only the listed categories (name, unit and doors, contact preference, biometric data if used, device identifiers, credentials, lease dates, and time and method of access, the last solely for security); destroy authentication data within 90 days unless anonymised; don't sell, lease or disclose it except as the law requires or with consent; keep the system secure; don't track people outside the building; and don't use the data to harass or evict. Tenants get a private right of action over unlawful data sales.

When we install a system for an owner, we pick settings that make those duties easier: log retention set to the shortest period the platform allows, no location tracking, credentials issued per unit rather than per guessable code, and a non-smart way into each apartment for any tenant who doesn't consent. The legal responsibility stays with the owner, so have your own counsel review the consent form you give tenants.

Shops and storefronts

What smart lock works on a storefront door behind a roll-down gate?

Narrow aluminium storefront doors rarely have room for a residential smart deadbolt; their stiles are thin and the locks are usually narrow-backset deadlocks. The practical keyless options are a keypad or reader on the wall or frame driving an electric strike, or a keyless narrow-stile lock made for that door type. Either way, the door has to open from inside without a key when the shop is occupied, and the keypad has to be reachable once the gate is up but protected when it is down.

The gate itself is its own question. A 2009 city law set July 1, 2026 as the deadline for many storefront gates to be at least 70 percent transparent; in July 2026 the City Council unanimously passed Int. 910 to repeal that deadline so a compliant gate is needed only when you replace the gate for other reasons, with Department of Buildings permit rules unchanged. If a gate replacement is on your list anyway, tell us at the walkthrough so the reader position and cable route suit the new gate. What AI Gets Wrong About Smart Lock Installation in NYC

A note on method: Google's AI Overview could not be viewed from the tools used to research this page, so nothing below is presented as its exact words. Each heading is a claim that cost sites, manufacturers or general guides publish, and that answer engines tend to repeat. Under each is what changes when the door is in a New York apartment building.

“Smart lock installation costs about $250 to $400”

That range is Fixr's national figure, updated in January 2025, with an average of $300 for a Wi-Fi lock and one accessory and handyman labour at $50 to $100 an hour. As a national average for a bored deadbolt on a wood door in a house, it is a fair starting point.

It says nothing about a mortise lock in an eighty-year-old steel door, a fire-rated door that shouldn't be drilled, a managing agent who wants a certificate of insurance before you arrive, or a fifth-floor walk-up. Those are the conditions that decide the real price in this city.

That is why we quote apartment-door installs flat after a free walkthrough rather than repeating a national range, and why lobby and storefront work is priced from the access-control figures already published on this site.

“Any homeowner can install a smart lock in 30 minutes with a screwdriver”

Product testers do report that a retrofit or a like-for-like deadbolt swap takes little more than a Phillips screwdriver and half an hour. On the right door, that is true, and we tell renters with a standard single-cylinder deadbolt that they may not need us at all.

Most New Yorkers are not homeowners, though, and many doors are not the right door. A tenant who swaps the landlord's lock is doing something different from a homeowner doing the same thing. A mortise door won't accept a bored deadbolt without new preparation, and a heavy door may be thicker than the tailpiece allows.

The screwdriver is the easy part. Knowing whether you are allowed to make the change, and whether your door can take it without being cut, is the part a quick DIY video doesn't cover.

“Renters can't install smart locks”

General renter guides often say a tenant needs the landlord's permission for any lock change and leave it there. In New York that is incomplete. Multiple Dwelling Law § 51-c gives tenants in multiple dwellings the right to add their own lock, within a three-inch circumference limit, provided they give the landlord a duplicate key on request.

The right is to an additional lock, not to remove the owner's. That distinction is why a retrofit unit over the landlord's existing deadbolt, which leaves the outside and the key unchanged, is such a good fit for renters here.

The honest summary for a New York renter is: you can usually add keyless convenience; you usually shouldn't remove the owner's hardware; and the landlord keeps a way in.

“Smart locks fit any standard door”

Manufacturers' compatibility checkers assume a bored cylindrical deadbolt with a standard backset in a door of standard thickness. August's own page is explicit that its retrofit locks are compatible only with single-cylinder deadbolts and that some models need extra clearance.

A large part of New York's pre-war stock uses mortise locks, and a community thread that began with a New Yorker's question about smart locks for metal brownstone doors with mortise locks has run for more than a decade without a simple drop-in answer.

There are good smart options for mortise doors, but they are chosen per door. Measure first, buy second.

“Going fully keyless is more secure”

Removing the key removes one attack, picking, and adds others: dead batteries, lost phones, deleted admin accounts and cloud outages. That trade can be worth it in a house. In a New York apartment building it runs into rules.

Owners of multiple dwellings may not require tenants to use a smart access system to get into their own units under the Tenant Data Privacy Act, and co-op proprietary leases usually require a duplicate key to be held by the building. A fully keyless unit door can put a landlord or a shareholder on the wrong side of either.

The better design in this city is a smart lock with a mechanical override, a key lodged where the law or the lease expects one, and smart features layered on top.

“Matter makes every smart lock work with everything”

Matter is a real improvement: a lock certified for it can be shared across several ecosystems, and one manufacturer's guide says its lock can join up to five at once. But a Matter-over-Thread lock needs a Thread border router, and Yale's own setup guide says the router must be the same brand as the app you plan to use.

In a concrete building the border router also has to be close enough to the door to hold the Thread connection, which may mean a smart speaker or hub near the entrance rather than in the living room.

So before a Matter lock goes on your door, the question we ask is not which lock is newest but which app runs your home today and whether a compatible hub can live within reach of the entrance.

“A landlord can just change the code when a tenant leaves”

Property-management software makes deleting a credential a one-click job, and guides for landlords treat it as routine turnover. Mechanically it is. Legally, in New York City, the timing matters.

Administrative Code § 26-521 makes it unlawful to change or disable the entrance-door lock of an occupant of thirty days or more without supplying a key, outside a court warrant or vacate order; violations are a class A misdemeanour with civil penalties of $1,000 to $10,000 each. Deleting a code early is the digital version of changing the lock.

We set systems up so access can be removed in one step on the day possession lawfully returns, and we recommend a written log of every credential issued and removed.

Renter's Guide

A Renter's Checklist Before Adding a Smart Lock

Most of our apartment-door calls come from tenants. This is the checklist we walk through on the phone so the lock you add is legal, reversible and doesn't start an argument with management.

  • Look at what's on the door now. Is there a separate deadbolt with a thumb-turn inside, or only a mortise lock with a lever and a cylinder? Is the deadbolt single-cylinder (thumb-turn inside) or keyed on both sides? A photo of the inside and the edge of the door tells us most of what we need.
  • Read the lease clause on locks and alterations. Many New York leases repeat the Multiple Dwelling Law's duplicate-key requirement and add a clause about alterations. Knowing the wording lets you choose a lock that stays within it.
  • Prefer additions to replacements. The statute protects your right to add a lock; it doesn't give you the owner's lock to remove. A retrofit over the existing deadbolt, or a modest second lock, keeps you on firm ground.
  • Plan the duplicate key. With a retrofit, the landlord's key still works. With a separate keypad lock, choose one with a key cylinder so a duplicate can be handed over on request.
  • Keep exit simple. Inside, the lock must open with a thumb-turn or lever, not a key or code. HPD says double-cylinder locks are illegal on apartment doors.
  • Check the closer. If the door already struggles to latch on its own, fix that first or have us do it as part of the install; a smart lock can't throw its bolt into a door that isn't closed.
  • Tell management. A two-line note naming the lock and confirming a key will be provided avoids most disputes.
  • Plan your exit. Keep the original thumb-turn and screws in a labelled bag. When you leave, we refit them and remove the smart unit.

If your building already uses fobs, cards or an app for the lobby or your unit, the Tenant Data Privacy Act gives you rights over that system too: consent before your data is collected, limits on what is kept and for how long, and the right not to be required to use the smart system to get into your own apartment. Ask management for their written policy if you haven't seen one.

Connectivity

Keeping a Smart Lock Online Through Brick and Concrete

An apartment door is usually the worst place in the home for a radio signal. It sits at the end of a hallway, often in a masonry or concrete wall, as far from the router as the apartment allows, and next to a public corridor full of neighbours' networks. That is why so many smart locks in New York work perfectly by Bluetooth when you are standing at the door and fail the moment you try to open it from the office.

How we choose the connection

  • Bluetooth only: fine for a renter who just wants to open the door with a phone or keypad at the door and doesn't need remote access. Nothing to place, nothing to fail.
  • Bluetooth with a Wi-Fi bridge: the bridge plugs into an outlet near the door and relays to your router. August recommends its bridge within 10 to 15 feet of the lock, with full Wi-Fi bars at the bridge. In many apartments the closest outlet on your side of the door is in the foyer or a coat closet, and we check it before you buy.
  • Wi-Fi built into the lock: no bridge, but the lock itself needs a solid signal at the door. In concrete buildings that often means a mesh node closer to the entrance.
  • Matter over Thread: the lock joins a low-power mesh through a Thread border router — examples manufacturers list include a Nest Hub (2nd generation) and an Apple HomePod mini. Yale's guide says the border router must match the brand of the app you plan to use. Once paired, Matter locks can be shared with several smart-home apps.

Whichever we choose, the test is the same: we switch the phone to mobile data, step away from the apartment, lock and unlock remotely, and confirm the notification arrives. If it doesn't work from the street, it isn't finished.

Owners wiring a building for keyless entry often run a cable to the street door rather than relying on Wi-Fi at all. Our structured cabling installation in NYC and AESS network installation team handle that backbone.

Field Notes

Field Notes on New York Apartment Doors

How our installers read an apartment door before recommending a lock — working practice, not a single job

The first thing we do at a New York apartment door is close it and let go. If the closer pulls it shut and the latch catches on its own, the door is ready for a smart lock. If it stops an inch short, or bounces off the strike, the lock will be blamed for a problem that belongs to the closer, the hinges or a frame that has moved over the years. We fix the swing before we fit anything.

The second is the edge of the door. A plain round bore with a short latch means a bored deadbolt and most smart options. A long rectangular faceplate with a lever and a cylinder near it means a mortise lock, and a different conversation. Several filled or abandoned holes tell us the door has been re-drilled before, and that any new hole needs to land in solid material.

The third is the door itself. We tap it, look at the hinge edge and check for a fire-rating label. A heavy, cold, solid-sounding slab in a pre-war building may be steel-clad or have a mineral core, and we treat it as a door that should not be cut casually.

Last, we stand at the door with a phone and check the signal before anyone chooses between Bluetooth, a bridge or Thread.

How much does smart lock installation cost in New York City?
+

A smart lock on an apartment or house door is quoted flat after a free walkthrough, because a retrofit on a standard deadbolt and a mortise-format lock in a pre-war door are very different jobs. Building doors use our published figures: a single-door keypad or fob reader with electric strike is $1,500 to $2,500 installed at the Brooklyn base, a new lobby electric strike is $250 to $450, and a failed strike repair is $295 to $485.

Do you work on apartment doors in NYCHA developments?
+

Only with the housing authority's permission. The Multiple Dwelling Law section that lets tenants add their own lock does not apply to buildings run by a municipal housing authority, so locks and doors in NYCHA developments follow NYCHA's own rules. If you live in public housing, ask your development's management office what is allowed before buying a smart lock, and we can work to whatever they approve in writing.

Which smart lock brands do you install in NYC apartments?

Yes. Does a smart deadbolt still satisfy the city's deadbolt and chain-guard rule for class A apartments?
+

It can. Housing Maintenance Code section 27-2043 requires the owner of a class A multiple dwelling to provide a heavy-duty latch set, a heavy-duty deadbolt operable by key from outside and by thumb-turn inside, and a chain door guard on each unit entrance door. A heavy-duty smart deadbolt that keeps a keyed cylinder and an inside thumb-turn fits that description, and the chain guard and peephole stay where they are.
