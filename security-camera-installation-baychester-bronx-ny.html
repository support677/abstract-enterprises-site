The Neighborhood

Why Baychester Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Baychester was farmland and marsh until the twentieth century, and then it filled with houses very quickly. CityNeighborhoods.nyc says the transformation unfolded between 1920 and 1940, when modest one- and two-family homes, many in Tudor and Colonial Revival styles, began to line newly cut streets between Boston Road and Eastchester Road. The name itself is older than the houses. According to Wikipedia's Co-op City entry, Baychester Avenue was once called South 18th Avenue and Comfort Avenue, and took its present name from the Baychester real estate venture of the 1890s. The neighborhood was also shaped by a road it lost land to: a local history site records that the city took existing land and homes to build and widen the Hutchinson River Parkway between 1924 and 1941, with the stretch affecting Baychester finished around 1937 to 1940. What is left today is a grid of numbered streets and avenues, edged by Boston Road to the northwest, Gun Hill Road to the southwest, the New England Thruway to the east and East 222nd Street to the northeast, as Mapcarta describes the boundaries, with Eastchester Road running through the middle as its main street.

For a camera installer, the most important fact about Baychester is that its homes stand on their own. Wikipedia describes the neighborhood as dominated by one- and two-family homes of various styles. NeighborhoodScout reports that most residential real estate here is owner occupied and that 81.0 percent of the homes were built between 1940 and 1969. Point2 Homes, working from a different data set, puts the median construction year at 1953, with about 28 percent of homes built before the 1940s and another 15.3 percent going up by 1949. Those two sources do not fully agree on how old the stock is, and we would not lean on either one for a single house. What they agree on is the shape of the job. A detached house has a front, a back and two sides, a driveway that may be shared, a gate, a garage or a back stoop, and windows on every face. An apartment tower needs cameras in a lobby, a mailroom and an elevator. A Baychester house needs cameras on its outside walls, under its eaves, looking down a driveway and across a back yard. That is a different kind of install, with different cable routes, different mounting surfaces and very different privacy questions.

The city has decided, twice, that Baychester should stay that way. On February 15, 2006, the City Council adopted the Village of Baychester Rezoning. City Planning's summary noted that 65 percent of homes in the area were one- and two-family detached, and the new zoning permits only detached single- and two-family residences. Then, on October 5, 2011, the Council adopted the Williamsbridge/Baychester Rezoning, a 181-block change covering the area between East 233rd Street and East Gun Hill Road. City Planning's presentation for that rezoning photographed the mix that exists on some blocks: a detached single-family home and a multifamily apartment building on East 220th Street, and attached multifamily homes on De Reimer Avenue. So while most of our Baychester calls are about houses, some are about small attached buildings and walk-ups, where hallway and entry cameras come into it and the owner is a landlord rather than a family watching its own driveway.

Why do people here want cameras at all? The numbers are worth reading carefully rather than repeating as a scare. Wikipedia's Eastchester entry, citing the 47th Precinct, which covers Baychester, lists 461 robberies, 300 burglaries, 758 grand larcenies and 461 grand larcenies of autos in 2022. In February 2024 the Bronx Times police beat reported that grand larceny in the 47th Precinct, which it described as including Woodlawn, Wakefield, Williamsbridge and Baychester, had risen as much as 97.5 percent in the 28 days ending February 4. That is a short window and a whole precinct, not one block, and we do not claim it describes your street. But it explains why so many of the calls we expect from Baychester are about cars and driveways: the car parked at the curb overnight, the car in a shared driveway between two houses, and the package that sat on the front steps for an afternoon. On the commercial side, CBS New York reported a case in which nine people used suitcases to take 56 coats from a store on Baychester Avenue. Retail theft on that scale is a different problem from a car window, and it needs a different camera plan, which we cover in the commercial section below.

Public money is also going into cameras here, and that changes what private cameras are for. News 12 Bronx reported that Northeast Bronx district leaders announced a $1 million investment to expand NYPD ARGUS camera systems across the 47th Precinct, with police saying they would study crime hotspots to decide where the new cameras go. The 47th Precinct itself posted that more than $2 million had been secured for the community since 2023 for public-safety technology. Citizen Portal's account of the announcement adds that questions about privacy safeguards, footage retention, community oversight and the source of the $1 million were not answered at the event. Separately, the Bronx Times reported in December 2025 that State Senator Jamaal Bailey secured $1 million for ARGUS cameras throughout Co-op City, and a Co-op City guide reports an additional $250,000 from Council Member Kevin Riley, who, Wikipedia notes, grew up in and lives in Baychester, toward local camera upgrades. Street cameras on poles watch intersections. None of them is going to show who opened your side gate at three in the morning, or which neighbor's kid kicked a ball through the back window. That gap, between the street and your door, is where a private system earns its place.

Baychester also has a transit quirk worth knowing. Wikipedia lists Baychester Avenue station, opened on May 29, 1912 and served by the 5 train, at Baychester Avenue and Tillotson Avenue, and notes that it is one of only five stations in the whole subway system without at least one booth staffed around the clock. We mention it because it is a real feature of the neighborhood and because some web pages, including an older page on this site, say Baychester has no subway at all. It does. For a homeowner, the practical point is simpler: many people arrive home on foot from the train or the bus after dark, and a camera that shows the front steps and the driveway on a phone before you reach the gate is one of the most common requests we expect to hear.

We want to be plain about the limits of our research. We could not open live Google People Also Ask boxes, related searches, Bing, DuckDuckGo, AnswerThePublic or Yelp pages from our build environment, and we found no Reddit threads about camera installs in Baychester. We also found no Baychester-specific HPD violations, 311 complaints or DOB permits for camera work, and no city landmarks or historic districts in Baychester itself, though we did not confirm that on the Landmarks map. Where this page talks about what we usually find on houses of a certain age, it is our field experience with houses of that era generally, not a survey of Baychester homes. The only way to know your eaves, your walls and your cable route is to look at them, which is what the walk-through is for.

The interwar Tudors and Colonial Revivals

The oldest Baychester houses are the ones that give the neighborhood its character: steep Tudor roofs with stucco and dark timber trim, or symmetrical Colonial Revival fronts with a centered door. For cameras, the Tudor roof line is both a gift and a puzzle. A steep gable gives a high, sheltered spot to mount a camera that looks down the driveway, but the eave depth is often shallow, and the trim that looks like solid timber can be a decorative board over stucco. We never drive anchors into the decorative timber alone. Where we can, we mount into the brick lower course or into the framing behind the stucco, using a junction box that hides the cable connection and keeps water out. Stucco itself cracks if it is drilled carelessly, so we score it, drill slowly with a masonry bit, and seal every penetration. Colonial Revival houses with wood or later siding are kinder: a camera mount on a siding block, fixed into the sheathing and studs behind, gives a flat, sealed base that does not crack the siding when the house moves with the seasons.

The post-war two-families

Most Baychester houses, if the build-year data is right, went up in the years after the Second World War. Those houses tend to have a basement that runs the full footprint, a brick or block face, and an attic that may or may not be reachable. That basement is useful: it often lets us bring a camera cable up the inside of a foundation wall to a back-yard or driveway camera, keeping the run short and hidden. Many of these houses also had aluminum or vinyl siding added in later decades, sometimes right over the original brick. Siding over brick changes the anchor completely, because a screw that bites into siding alone will loosen, and a masonry anchor needs a spacer so it does not crush the siding. We find out which one we have with a small probe hole where the camera base will cover it. The other feature of the post-war two-family is the second household. A camera plan for a two-family has to answer who sees what, and we deal with that directly in the questions section below.

The attached rows and small multifamily buildings

City Planning's photos of attached multifamily homes on De Reimer Avenue and a multifamily building on East 220th Street are a reminder that not every Baychester property is a stand-alone house. In an attached row, the side passage disappears and the shared wall with the neighbor becomes the main limit on where a camera can sit. In a small building with several apartments, the owner is often thinking about the front door, the mailboxes, the stair hall and the back entrance to the yard rather than a driveway. Hallway cameras inside a building raise tenant questions that an owner-occupied house does not, so we plan those with the owner, keep cameras to genuinely shared spaces, and never aim into an apartment door's peephole line or a window.

Renovated houses with surprises behind the siding

Because the rezonings protect the detached form, many Baychester owners have renovated rather than rebuilt, and renovations hide things. New soffits may cover old rafter tails with no blocking behind them to screw into. Fresh siding may cover a doorbell wire, an old alarm cable, or the coaxial line from a camera system two owners ago. We trace any leftover cable we find before deciding whether to reuse it or leave it, and we scan for live electrical wiring near every drilling spot. The renovation itself is not a problem. Assuming the wall behind it is the same as the one next door is.

Decide what each camera has to prove

The most useful thing you can do before we arrive is to think about what you would need to show someone after a bad night. Do you want a clear face at the front steps? The whole length of the driveway so you can see a car pull in? The car at the curb from the front window height? The back gate, so you know whether someone came through it or over the fence? Write those down as plain sentences. Each one becomes a camera, and each camera is aimed and tested against its sentence before we leave. Owners who do this end up with fewer cameras that do more, rather than many cameras that each show a little of everything.

Look at your house from the street at night

Walk out to the sidewalk one evening and look back at the house. Where are the dark patches? Which porch light is on, and which one has a dead bulb? Is the driveway lit by a streetlight, a neighbor's floodlight, or nothing at all? Cameras with infrared night vision see in the dark, but color at night and readable detail depend on some light. A dark side passage is often the first place to add a camera with its own built-in light, and a porch light on a timer can make a front camera's night picture much better. Tell us what you saw.

Security Camera Installation for Baychester's Architecture

Baychester's housing is less varied than a mixed Bronx neighborhood with towers and walk-ups, but it is more varied than it looks from the car. Each type below changes three things about a camera job: what the camera is fixed into, how the cable gets back to the recorder, and who has a say in where the cameras point.

Detached One-Family Houses

The type the 2006 rezoning protects. The owner decides everything, the basement and attic usually give hidden cable routes, and the main care is keeping the lens on your own property.

Two households under one roof. Cameras on shared areas like the driveway, front steps and yard need a clear plan for which unit sees them, and tenants need to know they exist.

Steep gables, stucco and decorative timber. Good high mounting points, shallow eaves, and stucco that must be drilled slowly and sealed well.

Symmetrical fronts with a center door, often with wood or later siding. Siding mounting blocks give a flat, sealed base for cameras at the corners.

Attached Multifamily Rows

Like those City Planning photographed on De Reimer Avenue. Shared walls, no side passage, and cameras focused on the front door, mailboxes and rear exit.

Small Apartment Buildings

Like the multifamily building on East 220th Street in City Planning's photos. Entry, stair hall and back-door cameras for an owner managing tenants.

On stucco and half-timbering. The dark boards on a Tudor front often look structural but are trim fixed over the stucco. A camera screwed into that board alone will work loose as the wood shrinks and swells. We look for the brick that often makes up the lower walls, or for the wall framing behind the stucco, and fix the camera base there. Where the only good spot is stucco, we use a mount that spreads the load over a larger area, drill with a masonry bit at low speed to avoid spalling, and fill the cable hole with an exterior sealant so water does not get behind the finish. Stucco that is already loose or cracked around the spot gets reported to you before we drill, with a different spot suggested.

On soffits. Many post-war houses had vinyl or aluminum soffits added later. They look like an obvious place for a dome camera, but the soffit panel itself is thin and flexes in the wind, which makes a camera shake and blur at night. We fix soffit cameras through the panel into the rafter tail or blocking behind it, adding a wood block above the panel where nothing solid exists. That extra step is the difference between a sharp night picture and one that wobbles every time a truck passes on the Thruway side of the neighborhood.

On two-family houses. In a two-family, the owner usually lives in one unit and a tenant or relative in the other. The driveway, front steps, side passage and back yard are often shared. It is reasonable for an owner to record those shared outdoor areas, but the arrangement works best when everyone knows where the cameras are and who can view them. We can set the recorder so the tenant has an account that sees the front door and the driveway but not the camera over the owner's back door, or so the owner alone has playback rights while the tenant sees live views only. We never install cameras aimed into the other unit's windows or private areas.

What a shop camera has to do that a house camera doesn't

A house camera is mostly watching for something unusual at quiet times. A shop camera is watching a busy room all day, and what matters most is usually what happens in a few seconds at the door or the counter. The suitcase theft on Baychester Avenue that CBS New York reported shows the pattern: several people, moving fast, leaving with goods. The cameras that help in a case like that are not the wide ceiling views that show the whole floor from above. They are the door camera mounted at about head height just inside the entrance, which catches each face as people come in, and the counter camera that shows hands, the register drawer and the card reader. A ceiling view still has a place, for tracking where people went, but on its own it gives the tops of heads. We plan a shop the same way we plan a house: write down what each camera must prove, then place it to prove that.

Staffing changes the setup, too. Many small-shop owners want to look at the floor from home, let a manager see live views and play back the day, and stop part-time staff from deleting or exporting anything. Modern recorders handle that with separate user accounts and permissions, and we set those up at handover rather than leaving everyone on the default owner login. For a shop with a landlord, such as a tenant in a larger center, we also check the lease and the building's rules before mounting anything on common walls or outside the storefront.

Compliance and practical limits for business cameras

  • A licensed installer. New York's General Business Law Article 6-D requires a Department of State license to engage in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems. We hold NYS license #12000287431. One installer's guide also cites a new NYC rule, AC 28-105.4.7, on low-voltage electrical work, effective December 21, 2025; we follow current Department of Buildings guidance on every job.
  • New outlets are an electrician's job. PoE cameras are powered by their own data cable, which is why we use them. Where a shop wants a new receptacle for a recorder or monitor, that circuit is work for a licensed electrician. The Department of Buildings warns that electrical work done without a permit can bring violations, summonses, court appearances and fines for both the owner and whoever did the work.
  • Audio is a separate question. New York requires the consent of at least one party to an audio recording. Most cameras can record sound, and we leave audio off by default in shops and offices unless the owner has taken advice and wants it on.
  • Exits and fire equipment stay clear. No camera, bracket or cable run may block an exit sign, a fire extinguisher, a sprinkler head or a path to an exit. We check sight lines before fixing anything to a ceiling.
  • Things that stick out into walkways. Under the 2010 ADA Standards, wall-mounted objects at certain heights may not protrude too far into a circulation path. A camera on a long arm beside a corridor in an office needs to sit high enough to stay clear.
  • Landlord and center rules. Tenants in larger shopping centers often need the landlord's written approval and an insurance certificate before contractors work. Send us the requirements with the booking and we prepare the paperwork before the visit.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Baychester: Questions Answered

Cars, curbs and driveways

Our car lives on the street because the driveway is shared. Can a camera on the house actually see it well enough to matter?

Usually, yes, if the camera is chosen and placed for that one job. A wide camera on a high corner sees the whole front of the house but shows a car at the curb as a small shape. For the car, we use a camera with a narrower or adjustable lens, mounted lower on the front of the house, often near the first-floor ceiling height, aimed so the car fills a good part of the picture with room for a person standing beside it. At night, the streetlight matters: a car under a lamp is far easier to see clearly than one in the dark gap between lamps. On the walk-through we hold a test camera at the proposed spot and show you the car on a phone screen, day and night settings, so you can judge it yourself before anything is mounted.

Our shared driveway runs between our house and the neighbor's. Do we need their agreement to put a camera over it?

If the camera is on your own wall and looks at the part of the driveway you have a right to use, the decision is yours, but on a shared driveway we strongly suggest telling the neighbor before the install. A short conversation avoids most disputes, and many neighbors are glad of the coverage. If anything would be fixed to their wall, fence or garage, we need their written agreement first. We also set a privacy mask over their windows and any part of their yard that falls in the frame, so the camera records the driveway and not their home. New York's Civil Rights Law section 52-a, which one legal blog describes as letting a homeowner sue a neighbor for damages for filming their backyard, is a good reason to aim carefully on blocks where houses sit close together.

Eastchester Road traffic sets off motion alerts all evening. Can the cameras alert us only when someone actually comes onto our property?

Yes, and setting this up properly is one of the main differences between a useful system and one people learn to ignore. Instead of alerting on any movement in the whole picture, modern cameras let us draw a detection line or an area, such as the line where the sidewalk meets your driveway, and alert only when a person or vehicle crosses into it. Many cameras can also tell a person from a car, so you can have alerts for people in the back yard but none for cars passing on the road. We set the lines with you on the day, then ask you to watch the alerts for a few days and tell us what still comes through, so we can tighten them on a follow-up call.

Neighbors and privacy

Houses on our block are close together. How do you stop a back-yard camera from looking straight into the next house?

Three ways, used together. First, placement: a camera mounted to look along your yard toward your back fence, rather than across it toward the neighbor, sees far less of their property. Second, lens choice: a narrower view keeps the picture on your yard. Third, privacy masking: most recorders and cameras let us black out parts of the picture permanently, so the neighbor's windows and yard are never recorded at all, not just hidden in the app. We show you the masked view on a phone before we leave. If a neighbor ever asks what your camera sees, you can show them that masked view, which settles most worries quickly.

The upstairs apartment in our two-family is rented. Can the tenant see the camera feeds, and should they?

That is your choice as owner, and the system can be set either way. Many owners give the tenant an account that sees live views of the shared front door and driveway, which helps everyone, while keeping playback and any cameras over the owner's private areas to the owner's account only. Others keep the whole system to themselves. Either way, tenants should know where the cameras are and which areas they cover. We will not install a camera aimed into another household's windows or private space, and we suggest putting the camera arrangement in writing with the tenant so there is no confusion later.

Police cameras and police requests

The NYPD is adding ARGUS cameras across the 47th Precinct. Does a homeowner still need private cameras?

For most homes, yes, because the two do different jobs. News 12 Bronx reported that police will study crime hotspots to decide where the $1 million in new ARGUS cameras goes, which means intersections and corridors, not your back door. A pole camera at a corner may show a car driving away, but not who walked up your side passage or took a package from your steps. Citizen Portal also reported that questions about footage retention and access were not answered at the announcement, so a homeowner cannot count on getting that footage. Your own system records your property, keeps the footage in your home, and lets you find a clip in minutes. The two can complement each other: police may ask residents for private footage of a street event, and yours may be the clip that shows the face.

If something happens on our street, how do we get our footage to the detectives quickly and in a form they can use?

The key is to export it before it is overwritten and to keep the original on the recorder. At handover we show you how to find a clip by camera and time, mark the start and end, and save it to a USB drive in the recorder's export format, along with the player software if the recorder requires one. We also suggest noting the recorder's time against your phone's time, because a recorder clock that has drifted a few minutes can confuse an investigation. If you are unsure, do not reset or unplug anything; call us and a technician can help you export the clip. You decide what to share, and with whom.

Recorder, power and placement

Our house sits low and the basement has taken water before. Where should the recorder go?

Not on the basement floor. Locals call the area "The Valley" for its low-lying terrain, and whatever the cause in your house, water and recorders do not mix. If the basement is the natural hub, because that is where the internet line enters, we mount the recorder and switch on a wall shelf well above any water line, with the battery backup up there too. If the basement has flooded badly, we often move the recorder to a closet on the first floor and run one cable to it. Either way it goes somewhere a burglar would not find in the first minute of being inside, which is one more reason not to leave it on the TV stand.

Shops

For a small shop on Boston Road, which camera actually identifies people: the one in the ceiling or one by the door?

The one by the door, mounted at about head height and looking at people as they walk in. Ceiling cameras are useful for following movement around the shop, but from above they mostly show the top of a head and a cap brim. A door camera sees each face straight on for a moment as people enter, which is the shot that helps after an incident. We pair it with a counter camera that shows hands and the register, and, where there is one, a camera on the back exit. Lighting at the door matters as much as the camera: a bright street behind someone can turn their face into a silhouette, so we choose a camera that handles strong backlight and test it at the busiest time of day.

Family and travel

We travel for weeks at a time to see family. Can a relative or neighbor get the alerts while we're away?

Yes. We can add a separate user account for a trusted relative or neighbor on their own phone, with the permissions you choose, such as live view and alerts only, without the ability to delete footage or change settings. When you come back, you can turn that account off or remove it. We also suggest setting the recorder to keep a longer stretch of footage while you are away, and checking from your own phone that the cameras are still online once a day. Ask us to set this up at handover, or call later and we can add the account remotely if you have given us access.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Baychester

We could not capture Google's AI Overview or People Also Ask boxes for this neighborhood from our build environment, so we are not going to pretend to quote them. Below are claims we see repeated in quick online answers about camera installs in general and about this part of the Bronx, set against the public record and our field practice. Marketplaces such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish national cost guides that feed many of those answers. They are fine for a rough idea, but they are not a quote for your house.

"Baychester has no subway station."

This one appears on several web pages, including an older page on this site. It is wrong. Wikipedia lists Baychester Avenue station on the 5 train at Baychester Avenue and Tillotson Avenue, opened on May 29, 1912.

The same entry notes something more unusual: it is one of only five stations in the subway system without at least one booth staffed around the clock, alongside Eastchester–Dyre Avenue, Gun Hill Road, Pelham Parkway and Morris Park.

For a camera plan, the practical point is that many residents walk home from the station after dark. A front camera that shows the steps and the driveway on a phone, before you round the corner onto your block, is one of the most common requests we plan for.

"Baychester and Co-op City are the same place."

Some maps and listings fold Co-op City and Bay Plaza into Baychester. Others treat Baychester as the detached-house blocks of ZIP 10469 in the 47th Precinct, while Co-op City sits in ZIP 10475 and the 45th Precinct. Wikipedia records that Co-op City was built on the site of the Freedomland amusement park, which ran from 1959 to 1964.

The difference matters for cameras. Co-op City's 35 high-rise buildings and seven townhouse clusters, with 15,372 units according to Wikipedia, are run by RiverBay, which has its own camera network feeding a Public Safety Command Center. Work there goes through RiverBay.

The Baychester houses on this page are owned and decided on by individual families. When you book, the street address tells us which situation we are walking into.

"Wireless cameras mean no wires."

A wireless camera sends its picture over Wi-Fi, but it still needs power, either from a battery that has to be recharged or from a power lead that runs to an outlet. On a Baychester house, outdoor outlets at eave height are rare, so the "wireless" camera often ends up with a cord running down the wall anyway.

Wi-Fi also has to reach the camera. Brick, stucco over metal lath, and the length of a deep back yard all weaken the signal, and a camera that drops off Wi-Fi on a cold night records nothing.

A PoE camera solves both with one cable that carries power and picture. That is why our standard Baychester install is wired, and why we save Wi-Fi cameras for the rare spot no cable can reach.

"More megapixels means you'll see the face."

Resolution helps, but distance and angle decide whether a face is usable. A high-resolution camera on a corner thirty feet from the front door, looking down at a steep angle, shows a cap brim and a jacket, however many pixels it has.

The cameras that identify people are placed close to where people must pass, at roughly head height, facing them, with enough light. On a Baychester house, that usually means a doorbell or small camera at the front steps and a second one at the side gate.

We plan each camera around its job first and choose the resolution second. A system of four well-placed cameras often shows more than eight placed for coverage alone.

"Anyone can install cameras in New York; no license is needed."

The sources genuinely disagree on this, which is why quick answers get it wrong in both directions. New York's General Business Law Article 6-D requires a Department of State license to engage in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems.

Some guidance from the Department of State has described "passive" devices differently, and installer blogs read the rules in different ways. On top of that, one installer's guide cites a new NYC rule on low-voltage electrical work, AC 28-105.4.7, effective December 21, 2025.

We do not ask customers to sort that out. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431, and any new electrical circuit on your job is done by a licensed electrician under the Department of Buildings' rules.

"You need a monthly subscription to watch cameras on your phone."

That is true for many self-install products, which store clips in the maker's cloud on a paid plan. It is not true of a system with its own recorder.

A network video recorder in your house stores the footage on its own drive, and the manufacturer's phone app connects to it so you can watch live and play back from anywhere with a data connection.

You own the recorder and the cameras outright. If you ever want cloud backup on top, it can be added, but it is a choice, not a requirement to see your own driveway.

"Mid-century houses are simple to wire because they're newer."

Baychester's houses are mostly from the middle of the last century: NeighborhoodScout puts 81.0 percent between 1940 and 1969. That is newer than a pre-war tenement, but it does not make the wiring simple.

Decades of renovation have added siding over brick, new soffits over old rafters, and leftover cable from earlier camera, alarm and cable TV systems. Attics may be sealed, and basements may be finished with no easy way up.

The walk-through, the tracing of old cable and a probe hole behind each camera base are what turn a "simple" house into a predictable one. Skipping them is how a quote and a final bill drift apart.

Is the first visit to look at my Baychester house free, and what happens during it?

Would a store-bought camera kit be cheaper than having one installed on our house?
+

The kit itself may cost less up front, and for one doorbell on a lit front door it can be fine. On a detached house the costs show up later: Wi-Fi that struggles through brick and stucco, cords running down the wall to outlets, cameras aimed at the neighbor, and cloud plans to keep footage. Is it legal for my front camera to see the public sidewalk and street?
+

Cameras on a house commonly take in some of the sidewalk and street, which are public places, and that is generally how a front camera sees the curb and the car. The care is with private spaces: keep the lens off neighbors' windows and back yards, and leave audio recording off unless you have taken advice. We mask neighbors' windows and yards on every install. This is practical guidance, not legal advice.

A neighbor's camera seems to look into our back yard. What can we do?
+

Start with a friendly conversation, since many people do not realize what their camera sees and most cameras can be re-aimed or masked in minutes. A legal blog notes that New York Civil Rights Law section 52-a lets a homeowner sue a neighbor for damages over filming their backyard, so if talking does not work, a lawyer can advise you. Will a video doorbell work with the old chime wiring in a 1950s Baychester house?
+

Often it can, if the old wire is sound and the transformer supplies enough power for the doorbell you choose. Many older chime transformers are too weak for a video doorbell and need replacing, and some old wire runs are damaged inside the wall. We test the wire and transformer on the walk-through. If either is unsuitable, we run a new low-voltage cable from the recorder's switch, which also makes the doorbell part of the main system.

Why does the night picture sometimes fill with moths and spider webs?
+

Infrared night lights on a camera attract insects, and spiders follow the insects and build webs across the lens, which then glow in the night picture and trigger false alerts. Placement helps: cameras under deep eaves or away from porch lights collect less. Turret cameras suffer less glare than domes. A quick wipe of the lens and housing a few times a season keeps the picture clear.

Can the cameras be painted to match our house trim?
+

Many camera housings and mounting boxes come in white or black, which suits most Baychester trim. Painting a camera can be done on the housing and junction box with the right paint, but never on the lens window, the night lights or any sensor, and painting may affect the maker's warranty on that camera. We usually choose the color that best matches before ordering rather than painting afterward.
