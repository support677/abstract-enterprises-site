Lenses Sized for the Garden · Landmark Rules Checked First · Written Line-by-Line Quotes

Security camera installation in Carroll Gardens has two problems most installers never meet in the rest of Brooklyn. The first is distance. On First through Fourth Place, and on President, Carroll and Second streets between Smith and Hoyt, the houses stand behind front gardens laid out in 1846, many of them 25 to 39 feet deep. A camera at the parlor door is a long way from the gate, and a cheap wide-angle lens turns the person at that gate into a few blurry pixels. The second problem is the line around the Carroll Gardens Historic District. Inside it, the Landmarks Preservation Commission's rules name security cameras by type and steer them off the street-facing front. Outside it, which covers most of the neighborhood's lots, an owner has far more freedom, but the houses look almost the same. So we start every Carroll Gardens job by checking which side of that line the lot sits on and measuring from the stoop to the gate. Then we pick each lens for the distance it actually has to cover, run cable through the cellar instead of across the brick, keep the recorder dry and powered, and write every camera, mount, cable run and setting on a quote before anything is ordered.

The Neighborhood

Why Carroll Gardens Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Carroll Gardens was built for people who lived behind their gardens, and that layout still shapes every camera we put on a house here. Interest in the area began with the Hamilton Avenue Ferry in 1846, the same year surveyor Richard Butts laid out the deep front yards that give the neighborhood its name. A state law of 1846 set courtyards of 33 feet 5¼ inches on the blocks between Henry and Smith streets, and Wikipedia lists the largest gardens on First through Fourth Place between Henry and Smith, and on President, Carroll and Second streets between Smith and Hoyt. Most of the rowhouses on those blocks went up between the late 1860s and the early 1880s, in late Italianate and neo-Grec styles, and builder William Bedell put up the entire odd-numbered side of Smith Street from President to Carroll between 1872 and 1873. The Gowanus Expressway opened in 1941 and cut the area off from Red Hook, and the name Carroll Gardens only came into use in the 1960s. In 1973 the Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Carroll Gardens Historic District on President and Carroll streets between Smith and Hoyt, and in 2008 the City Council approved a zoning text change, 45 to 0 with one abstention according to CityLand, to protect the deep front yards from being built over.

Those gardens are lovely, and they change what a camera has to do. On most Brooklyn blocks a camera over the front door is a few steps from the sidewalk, and a face fills the frame without much effort. Here, the City Planning study describes mapped street widths of 100 to 130 feet that include the front yards, while the sidewalk and roadway themselves are only about 50 feet wide. Put plainly, the house can sit thirty feet or more behind the gate, with trees, shrubs and an iron fence between them. A camera at the parlor door that sees the whole garden sees the gate as a small rectangle at the far end of the picture. A person standing there, or reaching over the railing for a bike, is a figure without a face. That is the single most common mistake we see on Carroll Gardens houses: a perfectly good camera in the right place with the wrong lens. The fix is not more cameras. It is matching each lens to the distance it has to cover, and in many cases adding one view built just for the gate.

The other thing that decides a camera job here is the district line. Our count of the city's PLUTO tax-lot records, inside a study area bounded by Degraw Street, Hoyt Street, Hicks Street and the Gowanus Expressway, finds about 2,156 lots holding roughly 7,641 homes. Only about 140 of those lots fall inside the historic district. The rest look much the same from the sidewalk, with the same brownstone and brick fronts, the same stoops and often the same gardens, but they are not under the Commission's district rules. Inside the district, the Commission's 2018 rule changes made its staff practice official for objects mounted on facades and areaway features, and security cameras are named in that list alongside intercoms, light fixtures and security grilles. The proposed rule would keep many of those objects off the primary, street-facing facade altogether. So two houses a block apart can need completely different camera plans, and you cannot tell which plan you need by looking at the front.

What the cameras are guarding against is mostly ordinary, and it has a long record in the 76th Precinct, which covers Carroll Gardens from 191 Union Street. Court Street Journal reported this fall that Line Bagels, at Smith and 9th Street, was burglarized, and that the same person is thought to have hit two other businesses, including Organic Convenience Corp on Court Street at Huntington. News 12 Brooklyn reported that in February 2026 a man broke into a Carroll Gardens home while the 43-year-old woman who lived there was asleep. In July 2026 NBC New York aired video of an Audi crashing into the Garden Gourmet Deli & Grill on Henry Street near Union Street. Older reports fill in the pattern: Brooklyn Paper counted a burglary spike in 2008 centered in Carroll Gardens and mostly during working hours, with some windows left unlocked; DNAinfo described at least seven burglaries in early August 2015, most starting with a broken door lock while residents were away; and a 76th Precinct report described a package taken from the vestibule of an apartment building between President and Carroll streets. None of this makes Carroll Gardens a dangerous place, and one local broker says package theft is rare here compared with busier areas. It does mean the doors, the ground-floor windows, the garden and the shops are where a camera earns its keep.

Carroll Gardens facts that shape a camera plan

  • Distance from the gate: front yards of 25 to 39 feet on the garden blocks, with the 1846 law setting 33 feet 5¼ inches between Henry and Smith streets, so a door camera often sits well over thirty feet from the sidewalk.
  • A small district inside a big neighborhood: about 140 of the roughly 2,156 lots in our tax-lot count are inside the 1973 historic district, which Wikipedia describes as 134 contributing rowhouses on about 12 acres.
  • Cameras are named in the landmark rules: the Commission lists security cameras among facade equipment its staff regulate, and the 2023 proposed amendment says an installation on a primary facade should not be visible.
  • Small buildings, many owners: three-quarters of the residential lots in our count hold two to four homes, with about 493 three-family and 349 four-family lots, around 105 small condominiums and 66 walk-up co-ops.
  • Old fabric everywhere: about 95 percent of the residential lots in our count were built before 1968, most of them before 1930.
  • Shops in houses: about 243 store-and-apartment lots, many on Court and Smith streets, where a shop sits in the ground floor of a rowhouse and the landlord controls the front.

What Carroll Gardens owners often add next. Once the gate, the stoop and the back of the house are covered, the next request is usually from a small condo or a two-family that wants each owner or household to have its own login, or from an owner who wants a video intercom at the street door someday. The District Line

Inside the 1973 District or Outside It: Why Two Carroll Gardens Houses That Look Alike Get Different Camera Plans

Walk from Carroll Park east along President Street toward Hoyt and you are inside the historic district. Turn south onto Smith, then west onto First Place, and you leave it, although the stoops, the cornices and the gardens look much the same. LivingPlaces describes the district as more than 160 buildings, mostly two- and three-story rowhouses, lining President and Carroll streets between Smith and Hoyt, with shops on the first floors of the houses on Smith Street. Wikipedia counts 134 contributing rowhouses. Our tax-lot count, which works by lot rather than by building, finds about 140 lots and 339 homes. However it is counted, it is a small share of the neighborhood, and it carries its own rules for cameras.

What the Commission's rules actually say about cameras

The Commission does not ban cameras on district houses. Its Permit Guidebook has a section, 11.7, called Installing Security Cameras, and it says staff can approve a camera when the installation meets the criteria. Those criteria come from the rules. The 2018 rule changes listed security cameras among the objects that may be mounted on facades or areaway features, and proposed keeping many such objects off the primary facade and requiring that any installation minimize or avoid damage to significant architectural features. The amendment language the Commission published in 2023 puts it more directly: on a primary facade, the installation will not damage or remove significant architectural features and will not be visible; on a secondary facade, it will not be more than minimally visible. A secondary summary from Ambient.ai also describes a density limit of two cameras per 25 feet of street frontage on small buildings and three per 25 feet on large ones. We treat that figure as a summary to be checked against the current rule text before any application, not as the rule itself.

How we plan around those rules on a district rowhouse

On a house inside the district, the back of the house does most of the work. The rear facade faces other yards, not the street, and that is where we cover the garden-floor doors, the rear windows and the yard with as few mounts as possible. Inside, a small camera in the vestibule, looking out through the outer door, can watch the stoop and the top of the garden path without anything on the brownstone at all. For the gate itself, the question is whether any position on the front meets the "not visible" test while still seeing down the garden, and the honest answer is sometimes no. One vendor, Seneca Security, says rear or interior-courtyard mounting often avoids the Commission's review entirely, and that street-facing cameras in historic districts may need a permit or a Certificate of No Effect. Where a front camera is the only way to get the view an owner wants, we prepare the equipment details and photos for the Commission before ordering, rather than mounting first and asking later.

Outside the district: freedom, with the neighbors in mind

Most Carroll Gardens owners are outside the district line, and for them the front of the house is theirs to plan. That makes the job quicker, but it does not mean anything goes. New York's Civil Rights Law §52-a lets a resident sue over a camera that records them in their backyard where they reasonably expect privacy, and on blocks where rear yards sit side by side behind low fences, a careless rear camera can take in four gardens at once. Every outdoor view we set stops at the owner's own fence line, and any neighboring window or yard that still falls in the frame is masked out in the camera settings. In front, the camera watches the owner's own garden, gate and stoop, and the sidewalk beyond only as far as is needed to see someone arriving.

Shops in the district on Smith Street

The ground floors of the houses on Smith Street inside the district hold shops, and the building fronts above them are part of the protected rows. For a shopkeeper, that usually means the camera goes inside the store, behind the window glass or just inside the door, rather than on the facade, the sign band or the awning. The landlord has to agree to anything on the front in any case, and inside the district the Commission's rules apply to the facade as well. We set the inside camera so the glass does not throw back reflections at night, which is covered in the questions section below.

A Face at the Gate

Thirty Feet of Garden Between the Stoop and the Sidewalk: Choosing Lenses for a Carroll Gardens House

A camera does not see a face; it records a number of pixels across one. A wide-angle camera that shows the whole garden spreads its picture over a broad area, so the farther away a person stands, the fewer pixels land on them. At the stoop, a few feet from the lens, nearly any camera will do. At the gate, thirty feet or more away, the same camera may give a recognizable outline but no usable face, and at night, with only a streetlight and the garden's own lights, even less. On the garden blocks of Carroll Gardens this is the central fact of the job, and it is why we measure from the mounting point to the gate before choosing anything.

The practical answer is usually two views instead of one. A wider camera near the door shows the stoop, the under-stoop door and the near half of the garden, where a visitor stands close enough to fill the frame. A second, narrower view is aimed down the path and zoomed so the gate opening fills most of the picture; anyone who comes through it walks toward that lens for the whole length of the garden. A varifocal lens lets us set that zoom on site instead of guessing from a catalog, and we set a faster shutter for night so that someone moving through the gate does not blur.

Where the gate view comes from

There are three places a gate view can be taken from, and each suits different houses. The first is the house itself, high enough to see over shrubs and low enough to see under a hat brim, which works where nothing in the garden blocks the line of sight and, inside the district, only where the position passes the Commission's rules. The second is the vestibule, looking out through the glass of the outer door, which keeps everything off the facade but brings glare problems at night that have to be tuned out. The third is a camera at the gate end of the garden, on a gatepost or the owner's own fence, wired back to the house through a conduit under the path; that one sees faces close up but means digging, and in the district it raises its own questions.

The garden itself

CityNeighborhoods describes the front gardens as running 33 to 45 feet behind wrought-iron fences, often shaded by linden, maple and plane trees. Leaves move, and so do shadows. A camera that sends an alert on any motion in the garden will ping a phone every time the wind gets up. We draw alert zones tightly: a line just inside the gate, the path, the stoop and the under-stoop door, not the planting beds. Recording runs continuously regardless, so the seconds before and after an alert are always there. Where a tree fills in each summer and cuts across the gate view, we plan the camera position with the canopy in mind rather than the bare branches of a winter walk-through.

Garden-block rowhouses: the cellar is the cable route

The rowhouses on the Places and the district streets almost always sit on a cellar that runs the full depth of the house, and that cellar is the route we rely on. Cable for the front and rear cameras travels along the joists and comes up through small holes at the front and back walls, so nothing is stapled across brownstone or brick. Mounts go into mortar joints rather than into the face of the stone, because mortar is softer, easier to drill cleanly and easy to repoint if a camera is ever moved, while a hole through a brownstone face is permanent. The party walls shared with the houses on either side are never used for a mount. The cornice, the door hood, the window lintels and the stoop ironwork are left alone; they are exactly the significant features the Commission's rules talk about, and they are also the hardest things to repair if a mount cracks them. Where the cellar is damp, as many are in summer, the recorder goes up to a closet on a living floor, on a battery unit, with the internet modem beside it if possible.

Small conversions and condos: one recorder, several owners

Our tax-lot count finds about 105 small condominium lots and 66 walk-up co-ops, many of them rowhouses split into a garden duplex and one or two upper apartments. In those buildings the entrance, the garden, the hall and the cellar are shared, and nobody is the obvious owner of the camera system. We put the shared cameras on one recorder in a locked spot in common space, set up a main account held by the board, and give each owner a login that shows the shared views. Each owner can add cameras inside their own unit on a separate account if they want them. That way no single owner controls the shared footage, and a sale of one unit does not take the system with it.

Converted churches: plan inside, apply for the outside

Wikipedia notes that the South Congregational Church complex at Court and President streets, a city landmark since 1982, was converted to condominium apartments, and that the former Westminster Presbyterian Church, later a Norwegian seamen's church, at 450 Clinton Street at First Place, is also now condominium apartments. Buildings like these mix tall interior spaces, heavy masonry and historic details with the needs of an ordinary condo: a shared entrance, a package area, a stair, a cellar. On an individually designated landmark, exterior work is a matter for the Commission, so most of the camera coverage is planned from inside the doors and in shared halls. Anything that must go on the outside is treated as part of an application, with mounts, cable paths and photographs prepared in advance.

New buildings at the edges: rough-in while the walls are open

The neighborhood's edges are changing. NYC in Focus reported in April 2026 that the City Council was voting on a rezoning around 46 Nelson Street, on the block bounded by Nelson, Henry, Huntington and Hicks streets, which would allow one building of roughly seven stories and another of roughly nine. Just east of Hoyt, 6sqft describes 420 Carroll in Gowanus as a 21-story tower and a 16-story tower joined by an underground tunnel, and Team Dom describes a nine-story building at Bond and Carroll streets near the Carroll Street Bridge. Planning for cameras someone will try to dodge

Field Notes

Field Notes: How We Walk a Carroll Gardens Rowhouse, From the Garden Gate to the Back Fence

This describes how we work on these houses, not one particular job. We begin at the gate, on the sidewalk side, because that is where a visitor or a thief first appears. From there we look back at the house and note what stands in the way: a tree, a hedge, a planter, the curve of the stoop. Then we pace the path to the stoop and write down the distance, since it decides the lens for the gate view. At the stoop we look at the doorbell picture on the owner's phone, if there is one, to see what it already covers. We check the vestibule: whether the outer door is glass, whether the floor is tile, whether there is a light, because all three affect the night picture from any camera inside. We look under the stoop at the garden-floor door and its areaway. Before planning anything on the front, we ask whether the house is inside the historic district, and if the owner is unsure, we check the lot record. Inside, we go down to the cellar and look along the joists for a clear route front to back, note any old wire that needs tracing, find where the internet line enters, and judge whether the cellar is dry enough for equipment. Then we go up through the garden floor to the back, look at the rear windows and doors, and step out into the yard to the back fence, turning to see where a rear camera would cover the house and where it must stop short of the neighbors. By the end we know the camera count, each lens and cable length, where the recorder lives, which views need anyone's approval and who gets which login, and the written quote is built from those notes.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Carroll Gardens: Questions Answered

Gardens and gates

With a garden more than thirty feet deep, what kind of lens does a camera on the house need to recognize someone standing at our gate?

A narrower one than you would expect, usually on a varifocal camera that can be zoomed on site. A wide lens that shows the whole garden spreads its pixels across the planting, the path and the fence, so a person at a gate 30 or more feet away is too small to recognize. We set a second view that is zoomed until the gate opening fills most of the frame, so anyone coming through walks straight toward it for the length of the garden. The wide camera near the door still covers the stoop and the near garden. At night we set a faster shutter on the gate view so movement does not smear, and we check the picture after dark before we leave, because the garden's own lights and the streetlight decide how much detail the camera has to work with.

The City Map counts our front garden as part of the street. Can a camera post be set in the garden itself?

It is worth settling before anyone digs. The Department of City Planning's 2008 study describes mapped street widths on the garden blocks that include the deep front yards, while the sidewalk and roadway are about 50 feet wide, and a Board of Standards and Appeals filing has described some Second Place gardens as land claimed to be city-owned. The sources we have do not give a single answer for every lot. So we mount cameras on the house or on the owner's own gatepost and fence where possible, and we do not set a footing or post in the garden until the owner has confirmed, with their deed or their attorney, that they may. Inside the historic district, a post in the front garden is also a question for the Landmarks Preservation Commission.

The district line

How do we find out for certain whether our house is one of the lots inside the Carroll Gardens Historic District?

Check the lot, not the look of the street. The district covers President and Carroll streets between Smith and Hoyt, plus the Smith Street fronts on those blocks, and our count of the city's tax-lot records finds about 140 lots inside it. Each lot's city record carries a historic-district field, and the Commission's own designation materials show the boundary. Houses on the Places, on Second Street and on many blocks west of Smith look much the same and have the same gardens, but are outside. We look the lot up before quoting, because a front camera that is ordinary work outside the line may need a Commission approval inside it. Individually designated buildings, such as the John Rankin House at 440 Clinton Street or the converted South Congregational Church, are a separate case and are covered by their own designations.

The Landmarks rules say a camera on a primary facade should not be visible. How can a front camera on a district house stay out of sight and still watch the garden?

Sometimes it can, and sometimes the honest answer is to take the view from somewhere else. The positions that come closest are inside the vestibule, looking out through the outer door's glass; tucked into a recess where the brownstone and the door surround already hide the housing from the sidewalk; and on the rear facade for everything behind the house. A small, dark-housed camera inside the vestibule can see the stoop and the top of the path without touching the facade at all. For a true gate view, though, a camera usually has to see down the full length of the garden, which is exactly what makes it visible from the gate. Where no position passes the test, we either plan the gate view from inside or prepare a Commission application with equipment details and photos before ordering anything. We never mount first and apply later.

Our condo is in a converted church that is its own city landmark. Is a camera there handled like one on a district rowhouse?

The same Commission rules on facade equipment apply, but the building is a landmark in its own right, so the whole exterior is designated rather than just a district streetscape. Wikipedia notes that the South Congregational Church complex on Court Street was designated in 1982 and converted to condominium apartments, and that the former Westminster Presbyterian Church at 450 Clinton Street is now condominium apartments too. In practice, the condo board decides what the building wants, and most coverage is planned from inside: the entrance from the lobby side, the package area, the stairs, the cellar. Any camera, mount or cable path on the outside goes through the Commission first. Because these buildings often have historic windows and doors, we also keep cameras clear of any glazing the board or the Commission considers significant.

Houses

Our parlor door has a glass storm door in front of it, the vestibule floor is tile, and at night the camera picture flares white. What causes that, and what fixes it?

The camera's own infrared night light is bouncing back at it. A camera mounted inside the vestibule or behind the storm door fires infrared light at night, and the glass and the glazed tile reflect it straight into the lens, so the picture washes out into a white glare. The fixes, in order: switch off the camera's built-in infrared and rely on a warm, low-glare light in the vestibule or at the stoop; press the lens close to the glass with a soft hood so the reflection cannot reach it; or move the camera so it looks through the glass at an angle rather than straight on. A low-light color camera helps because it does not need infrared at all if there is a little light. We test the picture after dark before handover, since a vestibule that looks fine at noon can be unusable at 11 at night.

In 2015 a burglar on Union Street pulled an air conditioner out of a rear window near a fire escape. Which back windows on a rowhouse most deserve a camera?

The ones a person can reach without a ladder, and the ones beside anything they can climb. DNAinfo reported that in the August 2015 burglaries, a thief on Union Street removed an air conditioner from a rear window near a fire escape to get in. On a typical Carroll Gardens rowhouse, that means the garden-floor windows and doors opening onto the yard, any window over a rear extension roof, and windows next to a fire escape or a rear stair. A rear camera mounted on the back wall, looking down at the yard and back toward those openings, records anyone approaching them, and its frame stops at your own fence. A window that holds an air conditioner should be in view, because the unit itself is the easy way in.

Shops and studios

At the Carroll Gardens yoga studio Patch wrote about, the thief took things from the waiting area while classes ran. Where should a studio's cameras watch people's belongings?

At the cubbies, coat hooks and bench where bags are left, and at the door between that area and the street. A camera placed high in a corner of the waiting area, looking down at the belongings and toward the entrance, shows both who reaches for a bag and where they go next. A second camera just inside the street door records faces on the way in and out. The changing rooms and restrooms are never recorded, and we check that no camera sees into them through a doorway or a mirror. Because the studio is busy during classes, alerts are usually best scheduled for hours when the waiting area should be empty, with recording running all the time.

Permission, Privacy and Licenses

Owner, Board, Landlord, Commission or Neighbor: The Rules Behind a Carroll Gardens Camera Job

Carroll Gardens has a landmark district, several individual landmarks and a great many small buildings with more than one owner, so the rules that shape a camera job here are about landmarks, shared ownership, privacy and who is allowed to install. This is a plain-English summary, not legal advice.

Landmarks. Inside the 1973 district and on individually designated buildings, cameras are among the facade objects the Commission regulates. Its Permit Guidebook says staff can approve a camera that meets the criteria, and the criteria focus on keeping equipment off primary facades, out of sight and clear of significant architectural features. Outside the district, the Commission has no say over a camera on an ordinary rowhouse.

Permits from the Buildings Department. Seneca Security, a vendor, says low-voltage camera installation in a residential brownstone typically does not need a DOB permit, and Impera Security says most residential camera installations need no permit. We found no official source that says otherwise for a simple camera job. A new outlet or circuit for the recorder is electrical work for a licensed electrician.

Who may install. General Business Law §69-m requires a license for anyone in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security systems. The Department of State says passive, non-detecting devices do not need licensure; other sources read the rule more broadly. AESS holds NYS license #12000287431 and sends a copy to anyone who asks.

Condos, co-ops and tenants. In a small condo, the board decides about the facade, garden and shared spaces, and each owner decides inside their own unit. In a co-op, the proprietary lease and house rules decide, and a vendor notes that building-wide camera projects may need board approval. Impera says tenants generally may not install cameras in shared halls and lobbies without the landlord's permission, and that a landlord may never install cameras inside a tenant's apartment. Seneca notes there is no general city law requiring cameras in apartment buildings.

Neighbors. Civil Rights Law §52-a gives a resident a claim against a neighbor who records them in their backyard where they reasonably expect privacy. Behind Carroll Gardens rows, where gardens sit side by side, every rear view we set stops at the owner's fence, with anything beyond masked in the settings.

Public property and the gardens. Cameras go on your building, your gatepost, your fence or a post on ground you are sure is yours, never on a lamppost, street tree or sign pole. On the garden blocks, where the City Map treats the yards as part of the mapped street, we confirm with the owner before digging or setting anything in the garden.

Walls and openings. Every opening through brick, brownstone or mortar is sealed against water, and every opening through a fire-rated wall or floor in a multi-family or mixed-use building is sealed with rated firestop before we leave.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Carroll Gardens

Quick answers from search summaries, AI tools and national cost sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr tend to treat Carroll Gardens as either a generic Brooklyn brownstone block or a quiet neighborhood that needs nothing. These are claims we see repeated, written as summaries rather than quotations, and what the sources and the work show instead.

"Carroll Gardens is a safe brownstone neighborhood, so cameras are mostly for show."

There is truth in the starting point. The neighborhood is residential, settled and, by most accounts, calm, and one local broker says package theft is rare compared with busier areas.

The record still shows burglaries and thefts. Court Street Journal reported this fall that Line Bagels at Smith and 9th was burglarized and that the same person is thought to have hit Organic Convenience on Court Street. News 12 reported a February 2026 break-in while a resident slept, and older reports from Brooklyn Paper and DNAinfo describe burglary spikes in 2008 and 2015.

A camera is not a prediction about crime rates. It is a clear record on the day something happens, and in a quiet neighborhood, a clear record is often what lets a single incident be solved.

"Historic districts don't allow security cameras."

Summaries often say this flatly. It comes from the fact that the Landmarks Preservation Commission regulates what goes on district facades.

The Commission's own Permit Guidebook has a section on installing security cameras and says staff can approve them when the criteria are met. The rules steer cameras away from primary facades, toward positions that are not visible and away from significant features. They regulate cameras; they do not forbid them.

On a district rowhouse, that usually means the rear, the vestibule and the interior do most of the work, with any front camera planned around the rules and, where needed, approved first.

"Every house in Carroll Gardens is landmarked."

The neighborhood's look encourages this. Many blocks have the same stoops, cornices and gardens as the district, and the district is the best-known thing about the area.

Our count of city tax-lot records finds about 140 lots inside the historic district out of roughly 2,156 in the neighborhood. The Places, Second Street and many blocks west of Smith are outside it, along with most of Court Street.

Owners outside the line have far more freedom with front cameras. The only way to know is to check the lot, which we do before quoting.

"A video doorbell covers the front of a brownstone."

On many Brooklyn blocks, a doorbell at the top of the stoop is close enough to the sidewalk to see most of what happens out front.

On the garden blocks, the doorbell may be 30 feet or more from the gate. It sees a visitor who climbs the stoop, but not the person who reaches over the railing for a bike, opens the gate and turns back, or takes a box left inside the fence.

A doorbell plus a zoomed gate view, an under-stoop camera and a rear camera is what usually covers a Carroll Gardens house.

"Any camera installation in New York City needs a Buildings Department permit and an electrician."

It is understandable to assume that drilling into a building needs a permit, and some installers' sites say as much.

Vendors including Seneca Security and Impera Security say a low-voltage camera job on a residential building typically needs no DOB permit. What does change the picture is a new outlet or circuit, which is electrical work for a licensed electrician, and, in the district, the Commission's rules for the facade.

So the right checklist here is district status first, landlord or board approval second, and an electrician only if new power is needed.

"Anyone handy can install cameras for pay; no license is needed."

The Department of State's application does say that passive, non-detecting devices do not require licensure, and that line gets repeated widely.

General Business Law §69-m still requires a license for anyone in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security systems, and other sources read it as covering video surveillance in the city. "Mount cameras as high as possible for the widest view."

Height does keep a camera out of reach, and a high, wide view looks impressive in a demo.

On a house set back behind a garden, a high, wide camera records the tops of heads and turns the gate into a speck. The view that identifies people is lower, around eight to ten feet, and narrower, aimed so people walk toward it.

We are relaying the front garden path next spring. Should a conduit for a gate camera go under it while it is open?
+

Yes, if there is any chance you will want a camera or a doorbell at the gate. A conduit laid under the path while the stones are up costs far less than lifting a finished path later, and it gives a gatepost camera a protected cable route back to the house without anything running across the planting. We suggest a conduit with a pull string and room for a second cable, so a video doorbell or gate station can share it. On the garden blocks, confirm first that you may dig in the front yard, since the City Map treats those yards as part of the mapped street, and inside the historic district check with the Landmarks Preservation Commission about anything at the gate.

Bikes and strollers stay inside our front railing. Which camera view records someone lifting one out over the fence?
+

A view aimed along the inside of the railing where the bikes are kept, from the house end, so the bikes and the fence top are both in frame and anyone reaching over faces the camera. Brooklyn Paper has reported bikes taken from front yards in the 76th Precinct and from in front of a house on Union Street, and DNAinfo reported a bike taken from a Smith Street vestibule by removing part of a banister. We keep that view separate from the gate view, set it to record continuously, and draw an alert zone around the bikes only, so the sidewalk does not trigger it. If the bikes can be moved closer to the house, the same camera gives a much larger face.

Our Court Street shop would rather keep its camera inside, behind the front window, than on the landlord's facade. Will the glass spoil the night picture?
+

It will if the camera uses its own infrared light, because the glass reflects it straight back and the picture turns into a view of the shop interior. The fix is to turn off the camera's built-in night light, set the lens close to the glass with a soft hood around it, and keep a little light outside the window, such as the shop's own sign or a low fixture the landlord approves. A low-light color camera handles that best. Inside the window is often the right choice on Court and Smith streets, where the landlord controls the front and, inside the historic district, the Landmarks rules apply to the facade too.
