The Neighborhood

Why Throggs Neck Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Throggs Neck is a peninsula, and almost everything about a camera job here follows from that. Wikipedia draws the edges plainly: the East River and Long Island Sound to the south and east, Westchester Creek to the west, and Baisley Avenue and the Bruckner Expressway to the north, in Bronx Community District 10 and ZIP code 10465. The NYC Parks historical sign traces the name to John Throckmorton, who settled here with 35 families in 1642 and sold the land ten years later; both the one-g and two-g spellings are still in use. Irish laborers who came between 1833 and 1856 to build Fort Schuyler at the tip stayed on. By the late 1800s the shore was a summer resort, and after the Bronx joined the city in 1898, transit brought Italian farmers and tradesmen. In the 1920s the big estates were broken up into smaller homes and tightly packed bungalow lots, and once the Throgs Neck Bridge opened on January 11, 1961, year-round building picked up again. What came out of all that is a neighborhood that sources describe, again and again, as closer to a small coastal town than to the rest of the borough.

The housing is overwhelmingly low-rise and privately owned. Local guides describe detached and semi-detached single-family houses built mostly between the 1920s and the 1960s, a large share of brick two- and three-family houses, detached Colonial Revival houses in Locust Point, Cape Cod bungalows in the Edgewater Park co-op, mid-century garden apartment complexes near Tremont Park and along East Tremont Avenue, and newer waterfront condominiums such as Harbour Pointe, Shore Haven and buildings on Pennyfield Avenue and Schurz Avenue. In 2004 the City Council approved a rezoning that City Planning built on a lot-by-lot survey of 242 blocks, after residents complained that attached townhouses going up among the older houses were bulkier and taller than their neighbors; most blocks were moved into districts that no longer allow attached rows. The public housing is concentrated at Throggs Neck Houses, built in 1953 on streets such as Dewey and Calhoun Avenues. For a camera installer, the result is clear: on most jobs the person calling owns the wall the camera goes on, and the decisions are theirs to make, or a co-op or condo board's.

The crime picture is mixed, and it is worth reading carefully rather than loudly. The Wikipedia article on the neighborhood records that the 45th Precinct, which covers more than Throggs Neck alone, saw crime fall by about two-thirds between 1990 and 2022, and that the precinct still logged 108 burglaries, 609 grand larcenies and 323 grand larcenies of vehicles in 2022. That last number is the one that matches what people here describe. News 12 reported residents near Swinton and Harding Avenues finding at least six Hondas with broken locks and missing airbags, with a neighbor's Ring camera showing a car pull up and someone move from car to car. Another News 12 report showed thieves on camera taking three tires and rims off a resident's car on Hollywood Avenue at 4:30 a.m. The Bronx Times reported a man breaking into a 78-year-old woman's home near Graff and Schurz Avenues at about 12:50 a.m. on September 2, after which police released surveillance photos of him. A daytime package theft from a Throgs Neck home, with the family out, also made the news. And SpotCrime's log for late October 2026 lists burglaries on the 3000 block of Middletown Road and the 1800 block of Lafayette Avenue and a robbery on the 3800 block of East Tremont Avenue.

Two more things make this neighborhood different from a typical Bronx camera call. The first is the commercial spine. The Throggs Neck Business Improvement District, approved by the City Planning Commission in 2018, covers both sides of East Tremont Avenue from Bruckner Boulevard to Miles Avenue, about 180 properties with stores on the ground floor and offices or apartments above. Assembly Member Michael Benedetto, who calls the strip the Bronx's "Restaurant Row," secured a state grant for NYPD Argus cameras along East Tremont Avenue between Bruckner Boulevard and Harding Avenue, and the 45th Precinct and police headquarters watch them live. That changes what a shop's own cameras need to do. The second is the water. The Bronx Times notes that Edgewater Park floods in ordinary storms, Sandy left some waterfront houses with up to five feet of water, and City Limits found that only one Edgewater Park property had pulled a permit to raise its building in the years afterward. A recorder sitting on a basement floor near the shore is a recorder waiting to be lost. No law requires a camera on a house or a small building here, so every one of these jobs is a choice, and the point of planning is to make that choice produce a clip someone can use.

Throggs Neck facts that shape a camera plan

  • A car-dependent peninsula: the Throggs Neck Houses' own history notes that the nearest subway station is about a mile away at Pelham Bay, so most households have cars, and most of those cars spend the night at the curb or in a short driveway.
  • Vehicle theft is the steady complaint: 323 grand larcenies of vehicles in the 45th Precinct in 2022, alongside news reports of airbag and tire thefts on Swinton, Harding and Hollywood Avenues.
  • Low-rise, owner-held housing: detached and semi-detached houses from the 1920s to the 1960s, brick two- and three-families, garden apartments and waterfront condos, with townhouse rows largely stopped by the 2004 rezoning.
  • Public cameras on the main street: NYPD Argus cameras along East Tremont Avenue from Bruckner Boulevard to Harding Avenue, watched in real time by the 45th Precinct.
  • Doors below grade: the 2020 deli burglary went in through a basement door, and many Throggs Neck houses and shops have side or rear cellar entrances that nobody sees from the street.
  • Flood-prone edges: Edgewater Park, Throggs Neck and Country Club continue to flood, by the local Council Member's account at the 2022 Sandy anniversary event, which decides where recorders and power supplies can safely live.

What Throggs Neck owners usually add next. Once the curb and side-door cameras are recording, homeowners often want the front-door camera to show up on a video intercom or a screen in the kitchen, and shop owners want a fob reader on the back door so the camera and the door log line up. We leave a labeled spare port on the recorder and, where an entry upgrade is likely, a spare cable to the door, so a basement ceiling or a store's dropped ceiling only has to be opened once.

The Car on the Street: Aiming Throggs Neck House Cameras Where the Thefts Happen

Read the Throggs Neck car reports side by side and the setting is the same each time: a parked car, at night, on a residential street of houses. Near Swinton and Harding Avenues, the useful video came from a neighbor's doorbell camera. On Hollywood Avenue, the tire thieves were recorded at 4:30 a.m. Almost no one in these stories was relying on a camera built for the job. Most houses on the peninsula already have something by the front door, and it is usually aimed at the porch, a few feet from the lens, which is exactly where a car thief is not. The camera that helps after a night like that is one planned around the curb: where the car sits, how far it is from the wall, what light falls on it, which way a car pulling up will face, and what a person crouched by a wheel looks like from the house. Look across the car, not down at it

On a brick semi-detached house or a two-family with a stoop, the tempting spot for a camera is high under the roofline, out of reach and out of sight. From up there the camera shows the whole street, which feels reassuring on a phone screen during the day. At night it shows the tops of parked cars and the crown of anyone walking between them. The camera that turns into evidence is lower, usually under the first-floor eave, the porch roof or the soffit over a bay window, and angled so it looks across the parked car toward the street side, where a thief working on a door or a wheel actually stands. We still mount it high enough that a person on the sidewalk cannot simply reach up and turn it, and we bring the cable in from behind the mount so there is nothing exposed to cut.

The street side of the car is the blind side

Someone taking an airbag or a set of tires has every reason to work on the side of the car facing the road, with the car between them and the house. A camera on your own wall sees that side poorly. Two answers work on Throggs Neck blocks. The first is a camera positioned at an angle from the corner of the house, so it looks along the curb and catches the gap between your car and the one parked ahead or behind. The second is cooperative: a camera on the house across the street sees the street side of your car head on, and your camera sees theirs. Neighbors who agree to keep a curb camera running and to save clips for each other cover far more than either house can alone. We never aim a camera into someone else's windows or yard to get that view; the frame stops at the curb and the roadway.

Driveways, garages and the short gap between houses

Many detached houses here have a short driveway along the side leading to a garage at the back, and many semi-detached houses have a narrow drive shared or paired with the neighbor's. A car halfway down that drive is in a dark slot between two walls, lit only by whatever spills from a side window. We usually place one camera at the street end of the drive looking in, which records anyone entering from the sidewalk at head height, and keep the frame on your side of the property line. Where the garage holds tools, bicycles or a second car, a camera inside the garage, aimed at the door, records anyone who gets in and is far harder to reach than one outside.

Light that helps the camera without lighting up the block

A curb camera works best when the car sits in steady light rather than in the sudden glare of a motion floodlight. Most Throggs Neck frontages have a porch light and a streetlight somewhere nearby, and the street lighting is uneven from block to block. Where the streetlight already reaches the car, the camera can stay in color most of the night. Where it does not, a modest porch or eave light left on, shaded so it falls on the walk and the curb rather than into the lens or the windows across the street, does more for the picture than a brighter camera. New outdoor fixtures or outlets are an electrician's job and are noted on the quote when needed.

Patterns over weeks, not just a single night

The Swinton and Harding report described several cars hit on the same stretch, and the tire theft on Hollywood Avenue came at an hour when nobody on the block was awake. One clip from one night tells you what happened. Several weeks of continuous curb footage tells you whether the same car comes back, at what hour, from which direction and whether it slows at the same houses. That is why we size storage for the curb camera generously and set it to record continuously, with alerts layered on top only for the hours when a person standing by your car is out of place. When police or a neighbor ask about a night three weeks ago, the footage is still there.

Public and Private Cameras

Argus Cameras on East Tremont Avenue, and the Gaps a Shop's Own Cameras Have to Fill

Few Bronx shopping streets have what East Tremont Avenue now has: NYPD Argus cameras paid for by a state grant secured by Assembly Member Benedetto, placed along the avenue between Bruckner Boulevard and Harding Avenue, and watched live by the 45th Precinct and police headquarters. The Bronx Times reported the BID announcing them at a May 2 press conference outside the precinct, and News 12 reported the BID's account that the cameras helped catch a thief at Walgreens on their very first day. For a shop owner inside that stretch, the question is no longer whether there is a camera on the block. It is what the public cameras will never show, and who will have that footage when it matters.

What the public layer is good for

A police camera watched in real time is built to see things unfold in public: a fight on the sidewalk, a car fleeing, a person running from a store with something in hand. When an officer is watching, that can mean a faster response than any private alarm. It also means the avenue has a continuous record of who walked past, which can help investigators connect a person seen inside your store with the direction they left in. None of that depends on you. You do not get a login to it, and you should not plan as if you will.

What it cannot see, and why that matters

Argus cameras look at the street. They do not see what happens past your glass, in your aisles, behind your counter or in a cellar reached from the rear. The 2020 burglary that the Bronx Times described at a Throggs Neck corner deli went in through a basement door and ended at two safes, none of it visible from the sidewalk. The BID itself, at its press conference, pointed out that some robbed businesses do not call police at all. A shop that wants to settle a dispute with an employee, a customer or an insurer needs footage it controls. So inside the camera stretch, we spend the shop's budget on the inside of the store, the counter, the cellar and the rear door, and keep the outdoor view to the doorway itself.

Outside the stretch: beyond Harding Avenue and on the side streets

The BID's district runs farther along East Tremont Avenue than the camera grant described, out to Miles Avenue, and plenty of Throggs Neck businesses are on Throgs Neck Boulevard, Pennyfield Avenue, Bruckner Boulevard, Lafayette Avenue or small side streets such as Schley Avenue and Plaza Place. Shops there have no public camera on the block, so their plan needs an outward view as well: a camera mounted inside the front window, looking out at the sidewalk and curb, which records anyone loitering, any car that pulls up and which way people leave. Mounted inside the glass, it keeps recording even if a door is broken. If the building owner agrees, a camera under the sign band or awning can add a wider view of the frontage.

Apartments above the stores

City Planning's description of the BID notes that many of its buildings mix stores at street level with offices or apartments upstairs. That means a store's camera often shares a facade, a cellar or a side door with residents. We keep the store's cameras on the store's own doors and rooms, frame out the residential entrance next door, and get the building owner's written agreement before anything is fixed to the front or the shared cellar. When the owner wants cameras for the residential entrance too, those belong on a separate recorder or a separate account, so tenants' comings and goings are not on the shop's screen.

Detached houses: four sides, one owner, and a garage out back

A detached house on the neck usually has a front walk and porch, a driveway along one side, a garage at the rear and a back yard bordered by fences. That means four sides that can each be approached, and the side away from the street is where a burglar has privacy. We start with the curb and the front door, add the driveway and the garage door, then the back of the house, and only then think about the far side yard. Cable from the front and the drive comes down through the attic and the soffits; cable to the rear and the garage usually travels across the basement ceiling and out through one sealed opening. A detached garage gets its camera fed by a short buried run in conduit or, where the garage already has power and a clear line of sight, a small wireless bridge. On aluminum or vinyl soffits we use a mounting block or backer so the camera sits rigid and the panel is not bent.

Semi-detached houses: half a building, all the decisions on your side

A semi-detached house has one wall shared and the other open to a drive or side yard. The open side is where the side door, the basement entry and the trash cans tend to be, and it is often very narrow. A camera mounted high at the front corner of the open side and aimed down the passage, toward the back, records anyone who walks in from the street. A second camera at the back corner, aimed toward the front, records anyone already inside the yard. On the shared side we mount nothing unless both owners agree in writing. Inside, cable follows the basement ceiling along the shared wall, which is usually the clearest run in the house.

Brick two- and three-families: owner and tenants under one roof

The brick two-family is one of the most common houses here, and it brings two sets of people who use the same doors differently. The owner wants the curb, the yard, the basement and the garage covered; the tenant wants to know who is at their own entrance. We put the recorder in the owner's space, give the tenant an account that shows only their entrance and the street door, and keep the yard and the owner's private areas off the tenant's screen. Exterior cameras on the brick go into the mortar joints with sleeve anchors where we can, because a mortar joint is easier to repair than a brick face. Cable passes through the foundation or the rim joist into the basement at a single planned opening, sealed against water and insects.

Cars, plates and the block

On our block, the only usable clip of the car thief came from a doorbell camera across the street. Why did a camera across the road see more than the one on our own house?

Because of geometry. A camera on your wall looks out at your car from behind it, so it sees the near side and the roof, while anyone working on the street side or crouched at a wheel is hidden by the car itself. The house across the road looks at your car face on, from a low angle, and sees the street side clearly. That is what happened in reports like the one near Swinton and Harding Avenues. You can get some of that view from your own property by placing a camera at the corner of the house so it looks along the curb into the gap between parked cars, and you get the rest by agreeing with the neighbor opposite that each of you keeps a curb camera running. Both frames should stop at the roadway, not reach into each other's windows.

In the Swinton and Harding Avenue break-ins, a car pulled up and someone went from car to car. Can a camera on a house capture the plate of a car that stops in the street after midnight?

Sometimes, if it is a separate camera built for it. The camera that watches your curb is set for people and is usually too wide to resolve a plate at night. A plate camera uses a narrow lens aimed at the exact spot where cars stop or pass, a fast shutter so motion does not smear the characters, and its infrared or exposure tuned so the reflective plate does not turn into a white glare. It works best when cars cross its view at a shallow angle and at low speed, such as at a stop sign, a corner or a driveway apron, and poorly when it has to catch a car passing quickly and square-on. We test it at night before we leave, with a car at the curb, and tell you honestly if the spot cannot deliver readable plates.

Houses, families and older parents

After the home invasion near Graff and Schurz Avenues, we want an alert to reach us, the adult children, if anyone comes to Mom's side or back door after midnight, without alarms going off in her house. Can cameras do that?

Yes. The cameras record all the time, and alerts are a separate setting. We draw a detection zone around each door that matters, usually the side and back doors and any basement entry rather than the front, set it to look for people rather than any motion, and switch it on only for the overnight hours. During those hours, a person at one of those doors sends a short clip to the phones you choose, and nothing has to sound in the house. The Bronx Times reported that police released surveillance photos after that case, which is a reminder to aim each door camera so it records a face at a few feet, not only a shape crossing the yard. We test the alert schedule with you on the day of the install.

Our townhouse is one of the rows built before the 2004 rezoning, with the garage at street level and the front door up a flight of steps. Where do the cameras go when the garage door is most of the front?

Under the step canopy or just above the garage door, aimed out toward the curb at a shallow angle. From there one camera records people walking up the steps at head height and the cars at the curb, and it sits above the reach of someone on the sidewalk. A second camera inside the garage, aimed at the door, records anyone who opens it, and is far harder to tamper with than one outside. The rear is usually a small yard against other yards, so that camera is framed tightly on your fence and door. Cable runs through the garage ceiling, which is normally the easiest path in the unit. Anything shared with the rest of the row, such as a common gate or court, is for the owners or association to decide together.

Residents in our garden apartment complex near Tremont Park say things go missing from the basement laundry room. Can a camera go in there, and where?

It can, because a laundry room is a common area, not a private space, and the owner or co-op board decides on it. The camera goes high in a corner opposite the door, so it records each person's face as they come in, with a second angle across the machines and folding tables if the room is large. It stays well away from anything resembling a changing area. The room should be lit enough to keep the camera in color. Before it goes up, the owner or board posts notice in the room and adopts a short written rule on who may review the footage and when, so it is used to settle missing-laundry and damage disputes rather than to watch neighbors.

Our neighbor's camera sits on a pole and looks over our six-foot fence into the yard. Does the fence change what New York law allows?

A fence is a strong signal that you expect privacy in that space, which matters under New York Civil Rights Law §52-a. That law lets a resident bring a civil suit against a neighbor who installs or keeps a video camera aimed at the resident's backyard without consent, with the intent to harass or annoy, and the yard does not have to be fully hidden for the claim to exist. What usually works first is a calm conversation, since most such cameras were aimed for the neighbor's own driveway or garage and can be re-aimed or partly masked in software. If that fails, a lawyer can tell you whether your situation fits the statute. On our own installs, we frame yard cameras on the owner's property and mask any part of a neighbor's yard that falls in view.

Licenses, Boards and Privacy

The Rules Around a Throggs Neck Camera Job, in Plain Terms

Most Throggs Neck camera jobs involve fewer layers of permission than a Manhattan co-op or a landmarked block, but a handful of rules still apply. They come from four places: the state's installer licensing law, New York's privacy and recording laws, the documents of any co-op, condo or homeowners' association, and the landlord or building owner where you are not the owner.

Who may install for pay. New York's General Business Law Article 6-D requires anyone in the business of installing or servicing security systems to be licensed by the Department of State, and training materials for the license describe it as covering cameras, CCTV and access control as well as alarms, with fines for working unlicensed. Master electricians do not need the education requirements but must still register. A homeowner who puts up a doorbell on their own house is not in that business. A contractor doing it for money should be licensed. AESS holds NYS license #12000287431, and we send a copy to anyone who asks before the visit. New line-voltage power, such as a new exterior outlet, is a licensed electrician's job and is listed on the quote when it is needed.

Where a camera may look. New York does not stop you recording what anyone can see from a public place, such as your curb, your driveway and the street in front of your house. Civil Rights Law §52-a gives a resident a claim against a neighbor's camera aimed at their backyard without consent and with intent to harass or annoy, which matters on blocks where houses sit a few feet apart. Penal Law §250.45 makes it a crime to record people in places such as bathrooms and bedrooms without consent. Penal Law §250.00 makes the state one-party consent for audio, so recording conversations nobody consented to is a real risk. We frame every view on your own property and the public curb, mask anything else, and set cameras to record video without sound.

Co-ops, condos and associations. In Edgewater Park and Silver Beach, shareholders own their homes but lease the land from the co-op, and the co-op's rules can limit what goes on a house or in common areas; legal commentary on co-op and condo rules notes that some boards bar shareholders from placing video doorbells in common areas at all. Waterfront condo boards control exterior walls and common areas and usually require an alteration agreement and proof of insurance. A townhouse row with shared courts or gates may have an association that owns those pieces. We read the rules you are given and work inside them. For co-op bungalow homes, our Edgewater Park security camera installation page covers that community in detail.

Public housing. NYCHA announced funding in 2012 for security cameras and layered access at Throggs Neck Houses and Pelham Parkway Houses, with installation set to begin in 2013. Those systems belong to NYCHA, and residents raise camera problems or requests through NYCHA, not through a private installer.

Renters. If you rent, your landlord controls the exterior and any common areas. Ask in writing before mounting anything outside or in a hallway, and keep the written answer.

Is anyone required to have cameras? No general city law requires cameras on houses or small buildings in Throggs Neck. A store's lease or an insurer may ask for them, and a co-op or condo board may adopt its own policy, but the decision is otherwise yours.
