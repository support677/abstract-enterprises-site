The Neighborhood

Why Stuyvesant Heights Properties Need Alarm Installation

Stuyvesant Heights is the eastern, rowhouse-lined heart of Bedford-Stuyvesant, and most of it is protected twice over. The original Stuyvesant Heights Historic District was designated in 1971, and in April 2013 the Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the much larger Bedford-Stuyvesant/Expanded Stuyvesant Heights Historic District around it: about 825 buildings, bounded roughly by Halsey and Macon Streets on the north, Fulton Street on the south, Malcolm X Boulevard on the east and Tompkins Avenue on the west. Most of the housing went up between 1880 and 1920 as speculative rows in the Queen Anne, neo-Grec, Romanesque Revival and Renaissance Revival styles, with four- and five-story French flats from the 1890s on the corners and avenues and a few wood-frame houses from the middle of the nineteenth century still standing. For an alarm installer, those facts boil down to three things: thick masonry, original woodwork and plaster that owners do not want opened, and a front facade where very little may be added.

The second fact that shapes alarm work here is how the houses are lived in. A brownstone on MacDonough, Decatur or Bainbridge Street may still be one family's house from the cellar to the roof, but just as often it is an owner's duplex on the garden and parlor floors with a rental apartment above, or a three- or four-family house with a household on each floor and one shared stoop. The French flats hold eight or ten apartments behind one street door. Churches use the rowhouses beside them as offices. Each of those arrangements needs a different alarm plan, because the question is never only how a burglar gets in. It is also who arms the system, who gets the alert, and how one household comes and goes without setting off another's siren.

Third, the ways in are rarely the front door. On a row of attached houses, the roofs run level from one end of the block to the other, and each house has a scuttle or bulkhead that a person on any roof can reach. The garden floor has a door under the stoop and usually a back door to the yard. The cellar has its own door, often from an areaway below the sidewalk. A CBS New York report on a 2019 burglary near Kosciuszko Street and Marcus Garvey Boulevard, a few blocks west of the district, described exactly that route: the burglar came into the basement of a multi-unit building through a cellar door around 10 p.m., left, came back about 1 a.m. and forced his way into the apartment next to the basement while the two residents slept, taking about $4,215 in laptops, a tablet, earbuds and speakers. The parlor door, up a stoop under a street light, is usually the best-guarded opening a house has.

Fourth, the numbers. The NYPD's CompStat report for the 81st Precinct, which covers Stuyvesant Heights and the eastern half of Bedford-Stuyvesant from its station house at 30 Ralph Avenue, shows 94 burglaries in 2026 through November 8, against 100 at the same point in 2025, a drop of 6 percent, and 6 in the latest 28 days against 8. Over two years, though, the same report shows burglary up 32.4 percent, and over sixteen years down 54.6 percent; in 1990 the precinct recorded 981. In plain terms, forced entry is far rarer than it was a generation ago, but it has not kept falling every year, and the burglaries that do happen still come through the parts of a house nobody sees from the street. An alarm here does not need to be large. It needs to cover the garden floor, the cellar and the roof, report somewhere other than a siren, and be simple enough that every household in the house actually arms it.

  • About 825 buildings sit in the 2013 district alone. Need Repair in Stuyvesant Heights?

An Inherited Keypad Nobody Can Arm, a Wireless Zone That Drops Out on the Top Floor, or a Siren the Whole Row Can Hear: Alarm Repair in Stuyvesant Heights

Plenty of the alarm calls we get from these blocks are not about new systems. Houses here change hands often and are renovated often, and alarms get left behind in the process. A buyer closes on a brownstone on Hancock Street and finds a keypad in the parlor hall, a siren box in the cellar and no paperwork. An owner who rents the top two floors learns that the old system was only ever armed by the previous tenant, who took the code with her.

Most of these are one-visit fixes. Many older panels in the neighborhood were installed to report over a telephone line or an early cellular radio, and the line or the network has since gone; a panel whose wiring tests sound can usually keep its wires and sensors with a new communicator, or a new panel on the old wiring, which is the panel-replacement line on our price list rather than a whole new system. A wireless sensor that drops out four floors up is usually a placement and range problem, and the fix may be a repeater, a different spot for the panel, or a hardwired run in a closet. Call (347) 934-8335, tell us the brand name on the keypad and what the screen says, and send a photo if you can. If a siren is sounding and will not stop, we talk you through silencing it over the phone first. Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Stuyvesant Heights' Architecture

Almost every building in Stuyvesant Heights is a masonry rowhouse or a small apartment house more than a century old, and the alarm plan depends less on its square footage than on two questions: how many households live behind its front door, and how many floors of brick and plaster stand between the place the panel will go and the openings it has to watch. These six building types cover nearly every request we get from the neighborhood.

The one-family brownstone

Four floors over a cellar, a parlor door up the stoop, a garden-floor door under it, a back door to the yard, tall parlor windows, a rear extension on many houses and a roof scuttle at the top of the stair, all used by one household.

The owner's duplex with a rental floor

The owner lives on the garden and parlor floors and rents one or two floors above, or the reverse. One street door and vestibule, one cellar, one roof, two households on different schedules.

The three- and four-family rowhouse

A household on each floor, a shared stoop and stair hall, a cellar used for meters and storage, and an owner who may live in the building or several miles away.

The 1890s French flat

Four- and five-story apartment houses with eight or more flats around a central stair, often on a corner or an avenue, with fire escapes at the back and a roof bulkhead at the top of the stair.

The wood-frame survivor

Mid-nineteenth-century frame houses that predate the brick rows around them, with wood walls, porches or side entries, and windows closer to the ground than on a brownstone.

The church office and community rowhouse

Rowhouses used by congregations and community groups as offices, classrooms or storage, busy a few days a week, empty the rest, and often opened to the public for events.

The one-family brownstone: four floors, one panel, and the stairs as the spine

In a one-family brownstone, the alarm's job is to cover the bottom and the top of the house and leave the middle free. The garden floor and the cellar are where most of the openings are: the door under the stoop, the back door, the garden windows, the cellar door and often a rear window over the extension. The roof scuttle is the other end. Between them, on the parlor floor and the bedroom floors, the household needs to walk around at night without setting anything off. So we put contacts on every door and on every window someone could reach from the yard, the areaway, the extension roof or the stoop, a contact on the scuttle, and one or two motion detectors where a person moving between floors has to pass: the foot of the stair on the garden floor and the stair landing on the parlor floor. At night the system arms in stay mode, which keeps every door and window live and lets the bedrooms and the upper stair move freely. When the house is empty, away mode adds the motion detectors.

The owner's duplex with a rental floor: two households, one stoop

The owner's duplex is the most common house we are called to in the district, and the most common mistake in it is a single-area alarm. If the whole house arms as one area, either the tenant can disarm the owner's floors or the owner has to disarm the tenant's, and within a month somebody stops arming it. Our panels are divided instead. The owner's floors are one area with the owner's codes; the rental is another with the tenant's codes, the tenant's own app and the tenant's own history; and the vestibule, cellar and roof scuttle are a shared area that is armed by whoever goes out last or, more often, kept armed overnight by a schedule. A tenant who forgets a code cannot open the owner's floors, and the owner cannot see when the tenant comes and goes.

The rental floor also brings detector rules. HPD requires carbon monoxide detectors in rented one- and two-family homes that have fuel-burning appliances, within fifteen feet of the entrance to each sleeping room, and smoke detectors in each unit of a multiple dwelling. Monitored detectors on the alarm panel meet those requirements and also tell the owner when one goes off, which a battery detector on the tenant's ceiling never will.

The three- and four-family rowhouse: one area per household, and a call list that knows who is who

When every floor is a separate household, the alarm becomes a small building system. Each apartment gets its own area, with contacts on its own door and windows and a keypad or fob of its own. The common parts of the house, the stoop door, the stair hall, the cellar and the scuttle, are a shared area whose alerts go to every household and to the owner. The important detail is the call list. An alert from the third-floor apartment should go to the third-floor household first, not to the owner who lives in New Jersey and not to the family on the parlor floor. We write each area's notification list separately and name each device in plain words, so an alert reads "garden-floor rear door" rather than "zone 7."

The 1890s French flat: protecting one apartment, or the building's shared openings

In a French flat, a resident's alarm protects one apartment: the apartment door, any window on the fire escape or over a lower roof, and a motion detector in the hall of the flat. The building's halls, doors and roof belong to the owner or the co-op, so a renter's system is wireless, mounted inside the apartment and removable without leaving holes that matter. Owners of these buildings ask a different question: how to watch the openings everyone shares. For them the answer is a building-level area with contacts on the cellar door, the roof bulkhead and the rear door, reporting to the owner or managing agent, and kept apart from any alarm a tenant chooses to install in a flat.

The wood-frame survivor: easier signal, more low openings

The handful of mid-nineteenth-century wood-frame houses that survive among the brick rows are easier in one way and harder in another. Wireless signal passes through wood walls much more readily than through a foot of brick, so a single panel usually reaches every sensor. But frame houses tend to have more openings close to the ground, sometimes a side entry or a porch, and windows that someone can reach from a fence or a porch roof, so the device count can be higher than for a brownstone of the same size.

Brick, Plaster and Signal

Four Floors of Masonry Between the Panel and the Roof: Choosing Wired, Wireless or Both in a Stuyvesant Heights House

The question owners in this neighborhood ask first is wired or wireless, and the honest answer is that most Stuyvesant Heights houses end up with some of each. The reason is the house itself. A brownstone's party walls are solid brick, its interior walls are often plaster on wood or metal lath, and the panel usually wants to live in the cellar or the garden floor, where the power, the internet and the meters are. The farthest sensor, on the roof scuttle, is four floors and sixty feet of masonry away. Wireless alarm sensors are small radios, and every floor and wall between them and the panel takes something out of the signal.

Brooklyn owners have been arguing about this on Brownstoner's forums for years, and their experience lines up with ours. In a 2011 thread, an owner of a four-floor brownstone asked whether wireless would work through plaster walls; every reply favored wired, one owner noting there were "too many batteries to keep track of with wireless" and a poster who described himself as an alarm professional advising "Avoid wireless if at all possible." In an older thread, another owner warned that a receiver in the basement might not reach a roof-hatch sensor across four levels, and a fire-alarm professional said dense brownstone walls can be hard on wireless signal. Those are opinions from people who were not looking at your house, but the physics behind them is real.

Our approach is to test, not to guess. Before quoting, the technician puts the panel's receiver where it will actually live, then trips a test sensor at the scuttle, at the top-floor windows and at the rear of every floor and reads the signal strength the panel reports. Where every sensor comes in strong, the house can be wireless, and it saves the owner hundreds of dollars and any disturbance to plaster and moldings. Where the top floor is weak, there are three fixes, and we choose by what the house allows: move the panel up to the parlor floor, which halves the distance; add a wireless repeater on a middle floor, powered from an outlet; or run one wire up an existing chase, a closet, the dumbwaiter shaft some houses still have, or alongside an old riser, and put a hardwired zone expander at the top of the house so the upper sensors talk to a box a few feet away.

Wired systems still make the most sense in two situations. The first is a gut renovation. When walls and ceilings are open, running a cable to every opening costs little more than the cable, and the finished house has no transmitters, no batteries to change and nothing visible on the woodwork. That is the "custom hardwired" line on our price list, $2,000 to $6,000 depending on the house, and it is the one we recommend to anyone who calls us while the plaster is still down. The second is a house that already has sound wiring from an old system. Many brownstones here had hardwired alarms installed decades ago, and if those wires test good, a new panel on them, at $800 to $1,500, keeps every sensor on copper without opening anything.

The panel's own connection to the outside world is a separate question, and here we do not compromise. Every panel we install reports over cellular first, so the alarm still reaches the owner's phone and the monitoring station if the house's internet goes down or someone pulls the router. Vincent Ingrisano, whose company installs alarms in New York townhouses, put it plainly to Brick Underground: "you don't want your wifi to go down and your alarm not to work." Cellular signal in a cellar with brick walls and a sidewalk overhead can be weak, so we measure it at the panel location too, and move the panel or the antenna if we have to.

Empty Floors

The Floor Nobody Is On: Alarming a Stuyvesant Heights House Through a Renovation, a Vacancy or a Sale

The most useful local story we know about alarms in this part of Brooklyn is not about a break-in at all. In a series for Brownstoner, two owners renovating a Bed-Stuy house described living in the garden-level rental while the duplex upstairs was gutted. One night one of them heard someone walking around in the empty floors above. When he went to look, the intruder fled; police found the front door ajar, nothing had been taken or damaged, and they never worked out how the person had got in. "It's pretty scary being woken up in the middle of the night hearing footsteps above your head," they wrote. Their response was a list most owners in Stuyvesant Heights would recognize: a deadbolt on the front door and a lock on the vestibule door, double latches on the roof hatch, a check that every window and skylight locked, and an alarm with door and window sensors, motion detectors, a keypad, cameras and cellular and battery backup.

The lesson for anyone in a house like theirs is that an empty floor is the weakest part of a lived-in house. A floor under renovation has workers coming and going with their own keys, doors propped open for deliveries, windows left unlatched for ventilation, a dumpster on the street that tells everyone the house is being worked on, and expensive tools and fixtures inside. A rental floor between tenants is quieter but just as empty. And a house between owners, sold and waiting for the buyer's contractor, may have nobody in it at all for weeks.

An alarm for that situation is planned differently from one for a finished house. The empty floor becomes its own area, separate from the floor where the family lives, with contacts on its doors and windows and at least one motion detector on the stair or landing that anyone moving through it must cross. That area stays armed around the clock, except when someone with a code is working there. The contractor and each trade get their own codes that work only on the renovation area, only on working days and only in working hours, and that can be deleted the day their part of the job ends. The app shows the owner every disarm and every arm, so a code used at eleven at night stands out. And because the household downstairs is the one most likely to hear something, its own area stays independent, armed in stay mode at night whatever is happening upstairs.

The equipment for a renovation can be temporary or permanent. Wireless sensors on an empty floor can be moved as walls come down and go up, and then either stay for the finished house or come out. If the walls are open, it is usually better to run the permanent wiring now, put a few wireless sensors on it as placeholders, and replace them with hardwired devices when the plaster goes back. Either way, nothing protecting the occupied floor depends on the renovation crew remembering to arm anything.

Commercial Alarms

Business Alarm Installation in Stuyvesant Heights

Commercial Stuyvesant Heights is a ring around a residential core. Fulton Street runs along the southern edge of the historic districts, Malcolm X Boulevard along the eastern edge and Tompkins Avenue along the western, and Lewis Avenue and Stuyvesant Avenue cut through the middle with corner stores and ground-floor shops in buildings that have apartments above. Almost every business here occupies the ground floor or the garden floor of an older building, so a commercial alarm has the same two neighbors as a residential one: the households upstairs and the buildings on either side. These six settings cover most of the businesses we hear from.

Fulton Street

The neighborhood's main shopping street, running above the A and C trains between the Utica Avenue and Ralph Avenue stations, with stores, restaurants, salons and services on the ground floors of two-, three- and four-story buildings, many with apartments overhead and roll-down gates at night.

Malcolm X Boulevard

The eastern boundary of the 2013 district, a wide avenue with corner groceries, food businesses, churches and small offices, where a store's back door often opens onto the yards of the rowhouses on the side street.

Lewis Avenue

A quieter commercial avenue through the center of the district, with cafés, restaurants and small shops on the corners, and the Macon Library at Macon Street, the Carnegie branch that houses the African American Heritage Center.

Tompkins Avenue

The western edge of the expanded district, with ground-floor businesses in older apartment buildings and rowhouses and a mix of long-standing and newer shops.

Stuyvesant Avenue

The avenue that gives the neighborhood its name, mostly residential, with corner stores and professional offices in converted rowhouses and garden floors.

Churches and community buildings

Congregations such as Bethany Baptist Church, inside the 2013 district, and the community groups, schools and nonprofits that use rowhouses and church buildings across the neighborhood for offices, classrooms and storage.

Stores with apartments above: two alarms in one building that must not trip each other

The typical shop on Fulton Street or a corner of Lewis Avenue is the bottom of a building with one, two or three apartments above it, reached by a separate street door next to the shop window. The store has its own hours, its own staff and often its own landlord, and the families upstairs have nothing to do with it. One alarm for the whole building does not work. What does work is one panel with separate areas, or two separate panels where the owner and the store tenant prefer, so that the store is armed at closing by its staff, each apartment is armed by its household, and the shared cellar and roof are on an area that the owner controls.

The cellar is where these buildings need the most thought. In many of them, the store uses the cellar for stock and the apartments use it for their meters, laundry or storage, and there is a door from the cellar into the back of the store. That door belongs on the store's alarm. The cellar itself may be on the shared area, armed overnight, with codes for the residents who need to reach their meters. And if the cellar has a separate street door or a sidewalk hatch, it gets its own contact, because that is the route the CBS-reported burglary near Kosciuszko Street took into a building's basement.

Food businesses and corner groceries: the back door and the cash

Corner groceries, delis, restaurants and bakeries open early, close late and handle cash, and their weak points are behind the counter, not at the gate. The back door to the yard or the alley, the cellar where stock and the safe are often kept, and the office or cash room are where we spend the survey. The cellar and the safe go on an area that stays armed during business hours, so a manager disarms it with a code to go down and the panel reports if it opens otherwise. The safe or cash box gets a shock sensor. The counter gets a hold-up button that sends a silent signal rather than a siren. And because a restaurant kitchen makes smoke and heat all day, any detector near it is a heat detector rather than a smoke detector, so the monitored alarm does not go off every time the grill is busy.

Churches and community groups: open to the public, armed in the back

A church or community building is the opposite of a store. It is closed most of the week and then, for a service, a program or an event, filled with people who do not all know each other. The alarm plan for these buildings divides the public rooms from the private ones. The sanctuary or the hall is one area, disarmed for services and events. The office, the room where offerings are counted, the pastor's study and any storage with sound equipment or musical instruments are separate areas that stay armed whenever their keyholders are not in them, even while the hall is full. The Antioch Baptist Church episode the Attorney General described in February 2026, when a man walked through the fellowship hall and sanctuary during a celebration, is the kind of moment when it matters that the back rooms are still protected.

Compliance notes for Stuyvesant Heights homes, shops and churches

  • The siren must stop on its own. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building's audible burglar alarm to shut off automatically within fifteen minutes. On a row of attached houses where a siren is heard through both party walls, we set the cutoff well inside that limit.
  • Landmarks rules apply to the outside, not the inside. Sensors, keypads and panels inside a house are not exterior work. On the facade, the LPC Permit Guidebook allows staff-level approval of no more than two security cameras per 25 feet of street frontage on a small building, asks that conduit be concealed through plain masonry or kept to about ten inches, painted to match and set into mortar joints, and rules out fastening anything through cast iron or onto decorative surfaces. Ordinary maintenance and repair of existing safety equipment needs no permit, and in-kind replacements of cameras and intercoms need less paperwork than new ones. We keep sirens, strobes and keypads inside or at the rear wherever we can.
  • The installer must be licensed. New York's Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers under Article 6-D of the General Business Law. Our license number is #12000287431, and any installer's license can be checked with the Department of State.
  • Rented houses need the right detectors. HPD requires smoke detectors in each unit of a multiple dwelling and carbon monoxide detectors in rented one- and two-family homes with gas or fuel-burning appliances, within fifteen feet of each sleeping room's entrance. Monitored detectors on an alarm panel can meet that requirement and report to the owner at the same time.
  • Exits stay exits. Nothing we install locks, slows or blocks a required exit, a fire escape window or a roof door used as a way out. Contacts report whether something is open or closed; they do not lock it.
  • A burglar alarm is not a building fire alarm. A burglar alarm panel can carry monitored smoke, heat and carbon monoxide detectors for a house or a shop, but it does not replace a fire alarm system that a church, school or larger building may be required to have, and it is never tied into one without that system's contractor. AESS does not service kitchen hood fire suppression systems.
Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Stuyvesant Heights: Questions Answered

These are questions owners, tenants, congregations and shopkeepers in the historic districts bring to us, grouped by the kind of building they come from. Questions about price, timing, monitoring and paperwork are in the FAQ further down.

Brownstones, roofs and stairs

The roofs on our row run level across eight houses, and the roofer who checks the flashing twice a year comes up through our scuttle. Can the scuttle be on the alarm without the roofer ever knowing our master code?

Yes. The scuttle gets a contact on its own zone, and the roofer gets a temporary user code that can disarm only that zone, only on the day you choose and only between hours you set; once the day is over, the code stops working on its own. If you prefer not to issue a code at all, you can disarm the scuttle from your phone when the roofer arrives and re-arm it when he leaves, and the app keeps a record of both. Because a level roof connects every house on the block, the scuttle should stay armed every night, including when you are home, and on most of the houses we alarm it is one of the first zones we install.

Houses shared with tenants or buyers

Our top-floor tenant works overnight shifts and comes home through the stoop door around four in the morning. How does our garden-floor area stay armed at that hour without her code disarming it?

Her code is assigned only to her own area and to the shared front door, never to yours. When she comes in, the vestibule contact starts an entry delay on the shared area, her code disarms that and her own floor, and your garden floor and parlor floor stay armed in stay mode the whole time. The motion detector at the foot of your stair is on your area, not on the path she takes to the upper stair, so her walk up the stairs does not touch your zones. If the stair hall is shared all the way up, we place the motion detectors inside each household's own floors rather than in the hall, which keeps both of you protected without either of you tripping the other.

We are buying a house on Bainbridge Street, and the seller's alarm company says its monitoring agreement transfers to the buyer. Do we have to take it over?

That depends on the agreement and on your contract of sale, so read both and ask your lawyer before closing; the agreement is usually between the seller and the company, not the house. What we can tell you is what happens to the equipment. A panel the seller owns outright normally stays with the house, and we can take it over, change every code and connect it to monitoring of your choosing, or none, once the old account is closed. If the seller's company owns or leases the equipment, it may want it back or want you to sign up. Either way, ask the seller for the installer code before closing, because without it some panels have to be reset or replaced.

Renovations and the house next door

Our general contractor and four trades will need to come and go for about six weeks. When the job is finished, how do we know nobody still has a working code?

Give each of them their own code, not a shared one, and set each code to expire on the last scheduled day of the work. On the panels we install, a user code can be limited to certain areas, certain days and certain hours, and the app shows every code in use and every time it was used. At the end of the job you open the user list, confirm every temporary code is gone or expired, and change any code that was ever written down for the crew. If a lockbox key or garage-style remote was handed out as well, that is a separate item to collect; the alarm record shows you who disarmed and when, but it cannot take back a physical key.

The rowhouse next to ours has been empty and boarded up for years and shares our party wall and our roof line. What should our alarm watch because of it?

The roof and the top floor first, and the cellar second. An empty house next door is a quiet place to get onto the roof of the row, and from there your scuttle or a top-floor rear window is a short step. So the scuttle gets a contact, the top-floor rear windows get contacts, and a motion detector on the top landing covers anyone who comes down through a skylight. In the cellar, look for any old opening in the party wall, a bricked-up door or a gap around a pipe; where one exists, a shock sensor or a motion detector facing it gives early warning.

French flats and walk-ups

Several tenants in our ten-unit French flat on Halsey Street want alarms in their own apartments. If we also alarm the cellar and the roof bulkhead, can the two kinds of system live side by side?

They can, and they should be kept separate. The building's system belongs to you and covers what everyone shares: the street door if you want it, the cellar door, the rear door and the roof bulkhead, reporting to you or the managing agent. Each tenant's system covers only that tenant's apartment, is mounted inside the apartment, and reports only to that household. Different panels on different radio channels do not interfere with each other in our experience, and we label every device in the building so nobody mistakes one system's sensor for the other's. What we will not do is give a tenant's panel control over the building's doors, or put a building sensor on a tenant's panel.

Churches and community rowhouses

Our congregation runs a food pantry on Saturday mornings from the rowhouse beside the sanctuary. Can the volunteers open the pantry without being able to disarm the church office upstairs?

Yes. The pantry rooms and the door the volunteers use become one area, and the office, the counting room and any storage upstairs become another. Volunteer coordinators get codes that work only on the pantry area and only on Saturday mornings, and the office keeps its own codes held by the pastor, the administrator and whoever counts the offering. The stair between the two floors gets a contact or a motion detector on the office's area, so if anyone goes up while the pantry is open, the office's keyholders are told at once, without a siren in a room full of neighbors collecting groceries.

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Stuyvesant Heights

Ask a chatbot or a search summary about an alarm for a Brooklyn brownstone and the answer is usually written for a detached house with drywall, with prices averaged from national lead sites such as HomeAdvisor, Angi and Fixr. Here are seven claims we hear repeated, put the way they usually appear, and what we actually find in the houses and shops between Halsey Street and Fulton Street.

"Wireless alarms work anywhere in the house."

In a one-story house with drywall partitions, they usually do. In a four-story brownstone with brick party walls, plaster on lath and a panel in the cellar, the farthest sensor may be sixty feet and four floors away through masonry, and the signal can arrive weak or not at all. Brooklyn owners on Brownstoner have warned for years that a basement receiver may not reach a roof-hatch sensor four levels up.

When that happens, the top-floor sensors report trouble on quiet nights, and owners tired of the messages often stop arming the system at all.

The fix is to test before installing. We trip a sensor at every floor and read the signal at the panel location, then decide whether the house is wireless, wireless with a repeater, or partly wired. That test takes a few minutes and decides more about how well the system works than any brand choice.

"You need Landmarks approval to put an alarm in a historic district."

Not for the alarm itself. Door and window contacts, motion detectors, keypads and panels inside a house are interior work, and the Landmarks Preservation Commission regulates the exterior. What draws the Commission's attention is anything added to the facade: cameras, conduit, exterior sirens, strobes and lights.

For those, the LPC Permit Guidebook sets clear limits. Staff can approve no more than two cameras per 25 feet of street frontage on a small building, conduit should run through plain masonry or show no more than about ten inches, and nothing may be fixed through cast iron or onto decorative surfaces. Equipment should not go on the primary facade at all unless there is no other option, and then in discreet places such as beneath a band course or under the stoop.

We plan around those limits from the start. The siren goes inside, the keypad goes inside the vestibule or the garden door, any exterior camera goes at the rear or in a permitted spot, and if a front camera is wanted, we tell you before the quote what the Commission is likely to require.

"Most burglars come in through the front door."

On many streets that is true. On a Stuyvesant Heights block, the parlor door sits at the top of a stoop under a street light, in full view of the houses opposite, and it is usually the best-protected opening the house has. The doors under the stoop, the back doors to the gardens, the cellar doors in the areaways and the roof scuttles on the level roofs are where nobody is looking.

The burglary CBS New York reported near Kosciuszko Street and Marcus Garvey Boulevard began with a cellar door, and the intruder in the Brownstoner owners' renovation was never traced to the front door at all, although police found it ajar afterwards.

Every system we install covers the front door. The correction is about the rest of the house, and on a rowhouse that is where most of the sensors go.

"Burglary in Bed-Stuy is going up, so get the biggest package."

The 81st Precinct's own report is more mixed than that. Through November 8, 2026 it shows 94 burglaries for the year against 100 in 2025, a 6 percent drop, while the two-year comparison is up 32.4 percent and the sixteen-year comparison is down 54.6 percent. Depending on which line you read, burglary is falling, rising or far below where it was.

None of those numbers tells you how many sensors your house needs. That depends on the house: how many openings it has off the street, how many households share it, and whether any floor stands empty.

We would rather install a modest system that every household arms every night than a large one that is switched off after a month of nuisance alarms. If a request includes devices the house does not need, we say so on the quote.

"An alarm on the house protects everyone in it."

Technically, one panel can cover every floor. In practice, a single-area alarm in an owner's duplex with a rental above, or a four-family with a household on each floor, is the alarm most likely to be left off. Someone always has to arm or disarm it for everyone else, and within weeks nobody does.

The panels we install are divided into areas, one for each household and one for the shared vestibule, cellar and roof. Each household arms its own floors, gets its own alerts and sees only its own history.

Averages like that come from national cost guides that blend apartments, suburban houses and stores.
