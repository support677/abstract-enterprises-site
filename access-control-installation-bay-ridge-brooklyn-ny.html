The Neighborhood

Why Bay Ridge Properties Need Access Control

Brooklyn Community Board 10, which covers Bay Ridge together with Dyker Heights and Fort Hamilton, describes its own housing in a single sentence that explains most of what we see on the job: one- and two-family homes make up the major portion of the district’s housing, while aging multiple dwellings, together with some new structures, line Shore Road, Ridge Boulevard and Fourth Avenue, and smaller clusters of four- to six-story buildings are scattered through the rest of the district. The same profile lists the commercial corridors that carry the neighborhood’s shops and jobs — Third Avenue, Fourth Avenue, Fifth Avenue, Fort Hamilton Parkway, 11th Avenue, 13th Avenue and what it calls the bustling 86th Street strip. Those three building types, the apartment house, the small home and the storefront, each have a different weak door, and access control has to be designed around each one rather than sold as a single product.

The apartment houses are the clearest case. Listing and brokerage records for the buildings along the waterfront show how many of them run without a doorman. 9701 Shore Road, known as Lebern Towers, is a six-story Art Deco co-op built in 1935 across from Shore Road Park, with a live-in superintendent, laundry, bike storage and an elevator. Crestwood Bayview at 130 Bay Ridge Parkway is a six-story prewar elevator co-op of 79 apartments, listed as having a live-in superintendent and no doorman. 6665 Colonial Road, overlooking Owl’s Head Park, is a six-story building of 49 units from 1954, again with a live-in super. 7259 Shore Road is listed as 90 percent owner-occupied with an elevator, a live-in super and a storage and bike room, and 9201 Shore Road is a 1960 building of six stories and 254 units. We name them only to describe the building stock, not as clients. The pattern is the point: dozens of lobbies with mailboxes, a package shelf and an elevator, and nobody at a desk.

New York’s Multiple Dwelling Law already assumes that the door, not a person, does the guarding in buildings like these. Section 50-a requires the entrances of class A multiple dwellings covered by the law — from the street, a passageway, court, yard or cellar — to have automatic self-closing and self-locking doors that stay locked at all times except when an attendant is actually on duty, and requires an intercom at the main lobby door of covered buildings with eight or more apartments. A mechanical lock with a key cut decades ago meets the letter of that rule. It does not tell the board which of the hundreds of copies in circulation opened the door at 3 a.m., and it cannot be switched off when a shareholder sells, a renter moves out or a contractor walks off with a key. That is the gap a credential system closes.

Package traffic has made the gap wider. When the Brooklyn Eagle’s police beat covered the 68th Precinct in December 2020, it reported two people stealing packages from the lobby of an apartment building at Ridge Boulevard and 67th Street in the middle of the afternoon. Bay Ridge residents posting in r/Brooklyn have described packages taken from their own building lobbies and buildings putting up signs telling tenants not to buzz in anyone they do not know or are not expecting. A sign is a rule people break the moment a courier says the magic word. A door that only opens for a valid fob, a time-limited code or a delivery credential limited to the package room enforces the rule for them, and leaves a record when it is broken.

The commercial strips carry their own lesson. In the early hours of Friday, August 28, 2026, thieves hit three restaurants on Third Avenue within about an hour. According to the Brooklyn Eagle, one was entered through the basement and lost $3,000 from a safe, a second had a door broken and lost $10,000 from safes and registers, and a third was entered through a side window. ABC7 reported that staff at one restaurant arrived to find the office door kicked in and the safe broken open, and that the NYPD warned other restaurants on the avenue to activate alarms, inspect entry points and consider additional lighting. Access control is not a cure for a forced window, and we say so on every walkthrough; what it does is make sure the back door, the basement door and the office door are actually locked when the last person leaves, show who locked them, and pair with alarms and cameras for the rest.

Same-Day Repair

Lobby Reader Dead or a Gate That Will Not Latch in Bay Ridge?

A failed access system rarely fails safely from the residents’ point of view. Either the door will not open for anyone, and the super ends up propping it with a wedge, or it will not lock, and the building is wide open until someone notices. Both get a same-day slot where we can. The faults we meet most often on Bay Ridge doors are unglamorous: an electric strike that has worked loose from a soft wooden frame, a lobby door closer so weak that the door drifts back without latching, a power supply in a damp basement that has corroded at the terminals, a reader whose cable was nicked during a hallway paint job, a controller that lost its date and time after a long outage, or a management login nobody has used since the person who set it up left.

Service and repair visits are billed at our standard rate of $195 an hour with a 3-hour minimum ($585). That covers diagnosis, testing the lock, the power supply and every cable between the reader and the controller, adjusting or replacing the door closer where it is the real cause, re-enrolling credentials, and recovering administrator access once you can show the system belongs to the building. Replacement readers, strikes, magnetic locks and controllers are quoted once we know what failed. If another company installed the system, send a photo of the controller’s label and of the reader on the door, so the right parts are on the van and the super does not lose a second morning.

If the door is stuck locked in an emergency, the rule is simple: never cut power to a lock on a door people need to leave through without knowing what happens next. A properly wired magnetic lock releases when power is lost and when the request-to-exit button or sensor is used; an electric strike set to fail-secure stays locked from outside but still lets people out with the lever. When we arrive at a door we did not install, the first thing we check is which kind it is and whether the exit side really works, because that is a life-safety question before it is a security one.

What We Install

Access Control Services in Bay Ridge

Every Bay Ridge job starts with the same short list: which doors lead from the outside world to something worth protecting, who needs to pass through each of them, and at what hours. The answers decide the hardware.

Lobby fob and card entry for co-ops

An encrypted reader at the main entrance of a Shore Road, Colonial Road or Ridge Boulevard elevator building, wired to a controller in the basement or a locked closet, with each apartment’s fobs enrolled to that apartment. Lost fobs are switched off in minutes instead of re-keying the whole building.

Package room and mailroom readers

A separate reader on the package room or a lockable alcove off the lobby, so carriers and residents can reach parcels while the inner lobby door and the elevator stay locked. Delivery credentials can be limited to that one door and to daytime hours.

Side gates, yards and trash-room doors

The secondary entrances the Multiple Dwelling Law also expects to be self-locking: the alley gate beside the building, the door from the yard, the cellar entrance used for refuse and deliveries. A keypad or reader on each, on the same system as the lobby, so the back of the building is not the easy way in.

Keypad and code entry for small buildings

For three- to eight-family walk-ups and rowhouses where a full controller is more than the owner wants: a weather-rated keypad or keypad-and-fob reader, an electric strike, and user codes that can be changed when a tenant moves out without calling a locksmith.

Storefront back doors and basements

Rear service doors, basement doors and office doors in restaurants and shops on Third Avenue, Fifth Avenue and 86th Street, with staff codes or fobs, a schedule that keeps the door locked outside working hours, and a log of who opened it last.

Intercom panels with built-in readers

Replacing a worn lobby buzzer with a single door station that handles both visitor calls and resident fobs, so the board pays for one faceplate, one hole in the frame and one cable run instead of two systems side by side.

Phone and mobile credentials

Optional phone-based entry for buildings and offices that want it, alongside fobs for the residents who prefer something on a key ring. Most Bay Ridge co-ops end up with both, because not every shareholder wants an app on their phone.

Takeovers and upgrades of old systems

Buildings with a fob system from an installer who is no longer around, a controller nobody can log into, or old unencrypted fobs that can be copied in seconds. We document what is on the doors, keep sound wiring and hardware, and replace what cannot be secured.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Bay Ridge’s Architecture

The same reader behaves differently on a 1930s steel-framed lobby door, a 1950s aluminum storefront and a hundred-year-old wooden rowhouse door. These are the Bay Ridge building types we plan for, and what each one usually needs.

Prewar elevator co-ops on Shore Road and Bay Ridge Parkway

Six-story buildings such as the 1935 co-op at 9701 Shore Road and the prewar building at 130 Bay Ridge Parkway, typically with a live-in super rather than a doorman, and often with an outer door, a vestibule and a mailbox wall. The outer door often stays on its original hardware; the inner vestibule door is where the reader and lock usually go, so mail carriers can reach the boxes without entering the lobby proper.

Postwar co-ops on Colonial Road and upper Shore Road

Larger 1950s and 1960s buildings, some with hundreds of units, garages and several side entrances. More doors, more credentials and more turnover, which makes a networked controller and clean apartment-by-apartment enrollment worth the extra cost.

Walk-ups and four- to six-story clusters

The smaller multiple dwellings scattered between the avenues: a single entrance, a buzzer panel from another era, a cellar door to the yard and no elevator. A keypad-and-fob reader on the front door and a keypad on the cellar door usually covers them on one small controller.

One- and two-family rowhouses

The housing type CB10 says makes up most of the district. Owners usually want a keypad on the front door or the side gate, a separate code for a tenant floor or a dog walker, and nothing that changes how the house looks from the street.

Limestone rowhouses of Doctors’ Row

The landmarked block of Bay Ridge Parkway between Fourth and Fifth Avenues, where many fronts still hold medical offices. Anything visible on a protected facade is a Landmarks question first; we plan hardware for the inside face of the door or the areaway where possible.

Storefronts with apartments above

Third and Fifth Avenue buildings with a shop at street level and two or three floors of apartments upstairs reached through a narrow side door. Two sets of users, two schedules, often one cable path through the cellar; we keep the residential door and the shop door on separate rules even when they share a controller.

How the hardware changes from one Bay Ridge door to the next

Steel lobby doors and frames. Many prewar co-op entrances have hollow steel frames with an existing strike pocket. An electric strike sized to the original latch is usually the neatest fit, because it keeps the mechanical lock and the key override that boards want for blackouts. Cable is fished through the frame and down to the controller, rather than run in surface raceway across decorative plaster.

Aluminum storefront doors. Narrow-stile aluminum doors on Third and Fifth Avenue shops and on postwar lobbies rarely have room for a standard strike behind the latch. A surface or concealed magnetic lock is often the practical choice, which brings the release rules of the Building Code into play: a motion sensor or exit button on the inside, release on power loss, and release on fire alarm where the building has one.

Wooden rowhouse and walk-up doors. Older wooden doors and frames can be soft, out of square or patched from earlier locks. We check the frame before choosing a strike, sometimes add a reinforcing plate, and often recommend a closer adjustment first, because a reader on a door that does not latch reliably only creates a record of the door failing.

Gates and areaways. Steel gates beside rowhouses and walk-ups take weather-rated keypads and gate-rated locks. The cable from the house to the gate is the hard part; where trenching is not welcome we look for an existing conduit, a fence line or a wall to follow.

Where the cable can go in an occupied building

Most of our Bay Ridge work happens in buildings full of people, which makes the cable route the single biggest cost driver. In elevator co-ops the old intercom cable usually runs up a riser from the basement to every floor; the new reader often needs only one run from the lobby door down to the basement, where the controller and its battery-backed power supply can sit on a dry wall away from the boiler room. In walk-ups we follow the existing buzzer path where it tests clean. We avoid fishing through lath-and-plaster walls we cannot repair to match, and we tell the board up front if a run has to be surface-mounted.

  • 86th Street. The 86th Street Bay Ridge BID, based at 9201 4th Avenue, promotes the strip as the main street of Bay Ridge with national brands alongside local stores.   • Fourth Avenue and Ridge Boulevard.   • Bay Ridge Parkway and the Doctors’ Row block. Medical and dental offices in rowhouses and apartment buildings. Waiting-room doors, records rooms and medication cabinets each have different users, and visible hardware on landmarked fronts is limited.

In our general experience the small businesses on streets like these tend to have two openings that matter, a roll-down gate and a personnel door, and only the personnel door needs a reader or keypad.

Compliance points every Bay Ridge door has to meet

Egress first. The NYC Building Code allows electrically locked doors in the means of egress of residential, business and mercantile buildings, but on its terms. For sensor-released doors, section 1010.1.9.8 requires a sensor on the egress side that unlocks the door when someone approaches, automatic unlocking on loss of power, a manual “PUSH TO EXIT” device 40 to 48 inches above the floor within five feet of the door that cuts power to the lock directly and keeps it unlocked for at least 30 seconds, release when the fire alarm or sprinkler system activates where the building has one, and locking hardware listed to UL 294. Section 1010.1.9.9 allows magnetic locks released by hardware on the door itself, operable with one hand, that cuts power to the lock immediately.

Self-locking entrances. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a expects the street, yard, court and cellar entrances of the class A multiple dwellings it covers to be self-closing and self-locking, kept locked except when an attendant is on duty, with an intercom at the main lobby door of covered buildings with eight or more apartments. Roof doors are the exception: they must be self-closing but not self-locking, and openable from inside without a key. We never put a reader-controlled lock on a roof bulkhead door.

Tenant data. Under Local Law 63 of 2021, owners of multiple dwellings that use keyless entry — fobs, cards, phone apps or biometrics — must give tenants a data retention and privacy policy, may collect only a short list of information, must destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, and may not use the system to harass a tenant or track tenants outside the building, according to HPD’s summary of the Tenant Data Privacy Law. We set retention on the controller to match the building’s policy and give the managing agent the settings in writing.

Landmarks. Work visible on the facade of a building in the Bay Ridge Parkway–Doctors’ Row Historic District is subject to the Landmarks Preservation Commission. We plan readers and keypads for the inside of vestibules, the side of an areaway or existing openings, and we leave the permit question to the owner and the Commission rather than guess at it.

Licensing. Installing access control and security systems in New York requires a license under General Business Law §69-m. Ours is #12000287431, held by Anwar Timothy; ask any installer for theirs and check it before work starts. This page is general information, not legal advice; your board’s counsel or managing agent has the final word on how these rules apply to your building.

Restaurants and shops that tighten their back doors usually ask about alarms in the same conversation. A door contact on each controlled door can report to an alarm system in Bay Ridge, so a door forced open after hours raises an alert rather than just an entry in a log.

Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Bay Ridge: Questions Answered

These are the questions Bay Ridge boards, supers, owners and shopkeepers actually put to us, grouped by the decision they belong to. Each answer is written for this neighborhood’s buildings rather than for buildings in general.

Co-op lobbies without a doorman

Our Shore Road co-op has an elevator and a live-in super but nobody at a desk. What should the front door actually do at night?

It should behave like a careful doorman who never leaves: open for residents with a valid credential, open for visitors only when a resident answers the intercom and presses release, and otherwise stay locked and say so. In practice that means a reader and intercom at the outer or vestibule door, an electric strike or magnetic lock that relocks the moment the door closes, a door-position contact that knows whether the door actually latched, and a controller that records each event. The last two items are the ones boards skip and later wish they had. If the door has been propped or left ajar for more than a set time, the controller can sound a local chime or send the super a phone alert. That one feature does more to stop the “someone propped the door for the movers and forgot” problem than any amount of signage in the elevator.

We also set the controller so the super and the board president can see the door’s status from their phones, and we give the super a physical override. The Multiple Dwelling Law expects the entrance to stay locked when no attendant is on duty; a credential system lets the building meet that rule around the clock without asking residents to carry a key that can be copied at any hardware store.

How do we get a fob proposal through a co-op board meeting without it stalling for months?

Bring the board one document that answers the questions shareholders will ask, before they ask them. Ours covers six things on a few pages: the exact doors being changed and what each will look like; what happens in a blackout and in a fire alarm; how many fobs each apartment gets, what a replacement costs and who issues it; who can see the entry log and for what reasons; how long the log is kept, matched to the building’s data policy under the Tenant Data Privacy Law; and the price, broken down by door from our published Brooklyn ranges. Co-op management writing over the years has made the same point about privacy: residents worry that the system tracks them, and boards should adopt clear rules on when anyone may look at the data.

The step that most often slows things down is distribution, not installation. We suggest the board pick a two- or three-evening window in the lobby when the super or a board member hands out fobs against a signed sheet, and that the old key keep working for a short overlap period. When shareholders see that nobody will be locked out on day one, the vote tends to be easier.

Who in a co-op should be allowed to look up which fob opened the door at 3 a.m.?

Fewer people than you might think, and only for a reason the board has written down. The controller can show every entry by apartment and time, which is exactly why access to it needs rules. A sensible arrangement is two administrator accounts, usually the managing agent and the super, plus a read-only report the board can request for a specific incident such as a burglary or damage in the lobby. Everyone else gets nothing. HPD’s summary of Local Law 63 of 2021 says owners using smart access systems may use the data only for granting access and monitoring entrances, exits and common areas, must destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, and may not use it to harass or evict a tenant or to track the relationship status of tenants and their guests.

We set the retention period on the controller to match whatever the board’s counsel decides, turn off any location features the hardware offers, and leave a one-page record of the settings with the managing agent. If a shareholder asks what the system knows about them, the board should be able to answer in one sentence.

Package rooms, gates and the doors behind the building

Can the package room off our lobby open for couriers without letting them upstairs?

Yes, and in buildings without a doorman it is often the most useful door on the whole system. The arrangement is a reader or keypad on the package room door, a separate credential or code for carriers that works only on that door and only during the hours you choose, and the inner lobby door and elevator left on residents’ credentials alone. Couriers get in to leave parcels; they never get a credential that opens the stairs, the elevator or the basement. Residents use their own fobs on the same door to collect.

Two practical notes. First, the package room needs a door that closes and latches on its own, and a camera inside it if the board wants to settle disputes about who took what. Second, carrier codes should be changed on a schedule and whenever the building suspects one has leaked, which takes a minute on a networked controller. Some carriers and buildings use dedicated delivery-access products; we can integrate those where the board prefers, but a separate code on a separate door does most of the job.

If the board can afford only one more door after the lobby, which door at the back of the building should it be?

The one with the most keys nobody can account for. In most Bay Ridge apartment houses that is the refuse or cellar entrance the super and porters use, or the steel gate in the alley beside the building; occasionally it is the yard door residents use for the garden or the bike room. The Multiple Dwelling Law’s entrance rule reaches beyond the front door: for the buildings it covers, entrances from a passageway, court, yard or cellar are also expected to be self-closing and self-locking. A door that every former porter and contractor can still open meets that rule on paper and fails it in practice.

On the walkthrough we list every secondary door with the super, note what opens it now and who is known to hold that key, and rank them. The top one goes into the first phase even when the budget is tight, usually as a staff keypad, which costs less than a full reader. The rest are priced separately so the board can add them later on the same controller without reopening the lobby.

Our Colonial Road building has a garage under it. Can the garage entrance use the same fobs as the lobby?

Yes, and it belongs in the plan from the start, because a garage is a second front door that most residents never think of as one. Our published Brooklyn full-building range explicitly covers parking alongside the lobby, the service entrance and elevator restriction, and the larger postwar buildings along Colonial Road and upper Shore Road are where we see it most. The usual arrangement is a reader or keypad at the driveway for the vehicle door, a reader on the pedestrian door from the garage into the building, and a separate group of credentials so only residents with a space, and the garage attendant if there is one, can open them.

We connect to the overhead door’s operator through its own control input and leave its safety-reversing devices exactly as the door company installed them. The door from the garage into the building gets the same treatment as the lobby: it relocks on closing, reports if it is held open, and releases on the exit side.

Shops, restaurants and offices on the avenues

After the August break-ins on Third Avenue, what can access control actually do for a restaurant’s basement and back doors, and what can’t it?

It can make sure those doors are locked when they should be, show who opened them and when, and raise an alert when one is opened outside business hours or forced. In the August 28 burglaries reported by the Brooklyn Eagle, one restaurant was entered through its basement, one through a broken door and one through a side window, and safes were emptied. A reader with a schedule would have relocked a basement or rear door that a closing employee forgot, and a door contact tied to an alarm would have reported a door opening at 3:20 in the morning. Neither would stop someone who breaks a window, and we say that plainly: windows, roll-down gates and safes belong to alarms, glass protection, cameras and how much cash is left on site overnight.

What we usually recommend for a Third Avenue restaurant is modest: a keypad or reader on the rear service door, a reader or keypad on the office where the safe is, an automatic relock schedule, a door contact on the basement or cellar door, and a link to the alarm panel. Staff codes are personal, so a former employee’s code is deleted on their last day rather than discovered months later.

The chain store next door on 86th Street has corporate access control. Can an independent shop get the same protection without an IT department?

Yes. The 86th Street Bay Ridge BID promotes the strip as a main street where national brands sit beside local stores, and the chains usually arrive with access standards set by a head office. An independent shop does not need that machinery to get the same essentials: a reader or keypad on the back door, a code or fob for each employee, schedules that match opening and closing times, automatic relock, and a log the owner can read on a phone. Staff who leave lose access the day they leave, and nobody wonders how many copies of the back-door key exist.

For two or three staff, a keypad with an electric strike on the existing door is usually enough. For a shop with a stockroom, an office and a back door, a small controller managing all three costs more but gives one screen for everything. We price both in the same quote, and we set the system up so the owner, not us, holds the administrator login.

How should a building with a shop downstairs and apartments upstairs split the two doors on one system?

Give each door its own users and its own schedule, even if the hardware is shared. On the mixed-use buildings along Third and Fifth Avenues, the shop’s door follows business hours and the shop’s staff list, while the side door to the apartments follows residents’ fobs around the clock. One controller in the cellar can run both, but the owner of the building and the tenant running the shop should each be able to manage only their own users. We set up two user groups with separate administrator logins so the landlord does not end up issuing codes to the shop’s employees, or the shop to the tenants upstairs.

The awkward spot is usually the cellar, which both sides use for storage or meters. We put the cellar door on its own rules, normally the landlord’s staff and the shop manager only, and note it clearly in the handover so nobody is surprised.

Our board approves sublets and pied-à-terre use for fixed periods. Can those apartments get fobs that expire when the approval does?

Yes. Board-approved occupancy is common in Bay Ridge co-ops — the listing for 9701 Shore Road, for example, notes that pied-à-terres are allowed with board approval — and a credential system can follow those decisions automatically. Each subtenant or occasional occupant gets fobs enrolled to the apartment with an end date that matches the approval letter. On that date the fobs stop working without anyone having to remember to collect them, and if the board renews the approval the managing agent extends the date in a minute.

We suggest the managing agent keep subtenants’ fobs in their own group, so a report of who currently holds credentials for each apartment is easy to produce at renewal time. The shareholder’s own fobs stay active throughout. It is a small setting, but it removes one of the most common sources of keys that outlive their welcome.

Small buildings, rowhouses and everyday use

How do we give a dog walker, home health aide or contractor access only on the days they come?

With a credential that has a schedule and an end date. On a networked controller or a smart keypad, each person gets their own code or fob limited to certain days and hours, and it stops working on the date you set. A dog walker might have weekdays from 11 to 2, an aide every morning from 7, a contractor Monday to Friday for the three weeks of a kitchen job. You see each entry in the log, and nobody has to collect a key at the end.

In co-ops, temporary credentials for a shareholder’s helpers should follow the building’s rules: usually the managing agent or super issues them on the shareholder’s request, and they are limited to the doors that person needs. In a rowhouse the owner simply does it from the keypad’s app. Either way, the credential belongs to the person, not the apartment, so one helper leaving never means changing everyone else’s code.

We live on Doctors’ Row. Can a keypad go on a landmarked limestone rowhouse?

Possibly, but the facade is the Landmarks Preservation Commission’s business before it is ours. The Bay Ridge Parkway–Doctors’ Row Historic District, designated in 2019, protects a largely intact block of early twentieth-century limestone-fronted rowhouses between Fourth and Fifth Avenues, and visible changes to those fronts generally need the Commission’s approval. We do not file permits on owners’ behalf or tell you what the Commission will decide.

What we can do is plan hardware that keeps the question small. A keypad or reader inside the vestibule, between the outer and inner doors, often avoids touching the facade at all. Where the outer door has to carry the lock, an electric strike inside the existing frame and a slim reader on the side wall of the areaway may be enough. We give you drawings and photos of the proposal so you can take them to the Commission, and we install only once you have what you need.

What should the lobby door do during a blackout or when the fire alarm sounds?

Let people out every time, and decide in advance what happens to people trying to get in. The Building Code’s rules for electrically locked egress doors require them to unlock on loss of power, and doors released by a sensor must also unlock when the fire alarm or sprinkler system activates where the building has one. A magnetic lock on a lobby door therefore releases in a blackout, which keeps people safe but leaves the door open until power returns. An electric strike set to stay locked on power loss keeps the outside secure while the lever still lets people out from inside.

Co-op management writing has noted that fob systems will not work in a blackout and that some buildings keep a key cylinder available for emergencies. We prefer to keep a working key override on the main entrance, a battery backup sized to carry the reader, controller and lock through a typical outage, and a clear note to the super about what each door does when the lights go out. We test the backup on handover day by switching off the power.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Bay Ridge

AI answers and national cost guides, including the ones published by sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, are written to be true on average across the country. Bay Ridge buildings are not average. These are seven claims we hear repeated, and how they hold up on Shore Road, Third Avenue and the side streets in between.

“Any fob system is more secure than keys.”

Only if the fobs themselves are secure. Many older building systems use 125 kHz proximity fobs that broadcast a fixed number with no encryption, and installers on r/accesscontrol and hobbyists elsewhere say openly that those credentials can be cloned with cheap readers. A building that swaps brass keys for copyable fobs gains an audit log but not much protection against copying.

What matters is the credential technology, the reader and how the system is administered. Encrypted credentials, readers that support them, and a habit of deleting fobs the day an apartment changes hands make the difference. When we take over an older Bay Ridge system, the first thing we test is which kind of fob the building has been handing out.

If the answer is the old kind, we price a migration that lets residents keep using their current fobs for a short overlap while new ones are issued, so the upgrade does not become a lockout.

“Access control is a front-door product.”

In Bay Ridge apartment houses the front door is often the best-protected opening in the building. The weak ones are at the back: the alley gate, the yard door to the cellar, the refuse entrance, the package room off the lobby. The Multiple Dwelling Law treats entrances from yards, courts and cellars the same way it treats the street entrance in the buildings it covers.

A plan that covers only the lobby leaves the building’s real master key in circulation — the old key to the side door that every porter, contractor and former tenant has. We walk the whole perimeter before we price anything.

Boards do not have to do every door at once. But they should see every door on the plan, priced separately, so the decision to leave one on a key is a decision rather than an oversight.

“Use magnetic locks; they are cheaper and easier.”

Magnetic locks are often simpler to fit on narrow aluminum doors, but they are tightly regulated on exit doors. The NYC Building Code requires sensor-released electric locks to unlock when someone approaches from the inside, on power loss, from a push-to-exit device within five feet of the door, and on fire alarm or sprinkler activation where those systems exist, with listed hardware. Magnetic locks released by door hardware have their own conditions.

That adds parts and labor, and it changes how the door behaves in a blackout: a magnetic lock releases, so the door is unlocked until power returns. On many steel-framed Bay Ridge lobby doors, an electric strike that keeps the mechanical lock and key override is the better fit.

We choose per door. Sometimes the answer is a magnetic lock; often it is not. The cheaper part is not always the cheaper door.

“Cloud access control is always better than a local system.”

Cloud management is convenient for a managing agent who looks after many buildings, and for owners who want to issue codes from anywhere. It also adds a recurring cost and a dependence on the building’s internet connection for administration.

For a small walk-up or a single shop, a local controller with an app on the owner’s phone may do everything needed with no monthly fee. For a 200-unit co-op with a management company, cloud administration usually earns its keep. Either way the doors should keep working on credentials already stored at the door if the internet goes down.

We price both where both make sense and explain what the building gives up with each, rather than steering every job toward a subscription.

“Installation takes a few hours.”

One reader on one door with an easy cable path can, but most Bay Ridge apartment buildings are not that. The cable has to get from the lobby door to a basement or closet in a building full of residents, often around decorative plaster and through a frame painted many times over. Boards also need time to approve, notify shareholders and distribute fobs.

A realistic lobby job is one working day at the door, with the lock disabled or on its key for only part of it, followed by enrollment and fob handout on a day the board chooses. A whole building with several doors takes longer.

We give the board a schedule in writing so the super can post it in the elevator and nobody is surprised by a technician in the vestibule.

“The national average price tells you what you will pay.”

National averages mix single-family smart locks with office towers. Our published Brooklyn ranges, which apply in Bay Ridge, start at $1,500 to $2,500 for a single door with a keypad or fob reader and an electric strike, and run to $4,000 to $15,000 for a lobby plus service entrance and basement, and $15,000 to $40,000 or more for a full building with elevator restriction and parking.

Where a Bay Ridge job lands in those ranges depends on the doors and the building: the condition of the frame, the cable path, the number of apartments to enroll, whether the old intercom is replaced in the same visit. Those are things a national figure cannot know.

Our quote lists each door separately, so the board can see exactly what it is paying for and what it could defer.

“Face recognition is the modern way to run a building door.”

The hardware exists and some panels offer it. In New York City residential buildings, biometric entry also brings obligations: the Tenant Data Privacy Law lists biometric identifier information among the data a smart access system may collect and sets rules for how such data is kept, destroyed and shared, as HPD’s summary explains.

Most Bay Ridge boards decide that fobs and codes do the job without the extra policy work, and many shareholders are uncomfortable with face scanning at home.

We install biometric features only where a board or owner has chosen them in writing and has a policy that covers them. Otherwise we leave them switched off, even on panels that support them.

After handover: a five-minute monthly check for the super

Access systems fail quietly, so we leave every Bay Ridge building with a short routine. Field Notes

Field Notes: Count the Keys Before You Count the Doors

This is a note on method rather than a story about a particular building. The first question we ask a Bay Ridge board is not how many doors it has but how many keys it thinks are out there, and the honest answer is almost always “nobody knows.” Keys to a lobby door in a building that has turned over apartments for decades have gone to former shareholders, renters, dog walkers, contractors, cleaners and relatives. The lobby lock may have been changed; the side door or the cellar door often has not.

So before we price anything we make a simple list with the super: every exterior door and gate, what currently opens it, and who is known to have that key. The list usually shows that one secondary door, often the one to the yard or the refuse area, is effectively open to anyone who has ever held a building key. That door goes on the system in the first phase, even if the board had planned only the lobby. At handover we leave the same list updated with what each door now does and who administers it, so the next board inherits a record instead of a mystery.

None of this costs extra hardware. It costs an hour on the walkthrough, and it is the difference between a lobby upgrade that looks good in the minutes of the board meeting and a building that is actually harder to walk into.

FAQ

Bay Ridge Access Control FAQ

How long is a Bay Ridge lobby door out of action while the fob reader goes in?
+

Usually part of one working day. We fit the lock and pull the cable with the door still operating on its key, then switch over once the reader and controller test correctly. For most of the visit residents use the door as normal, with a short window when the lock is changed. We agree the date with the super and the board so a notice can go up in the lobby beforehand.

Can a Bay Ridge building’s fob system share a network with its lobby cameras?
+

Yes, and it usually should. The access controller, the intercom door station and the lobby and package-room cameras can share one network switch and one battery-backed power supply in the basement, which saves cable and keeps everything running together through a short outage. We still separate them logically, so a camera problem never takes the doors offline, and the board can view a door event and the matching camera clip side by side.

Can a reader be mounted on a Bay Ridge building’s brick or stone entrance without damaging it?
+

Usually, yes. Where possible we mount readers on the door frame or a steel mullion rather than the masonry. When the wall is the only option, we drill into mortar joints rather than brick faces or carved stone, use surface-mounted units so nothing has to be cut into the wall, and seal around the fixings. On a landmarked facade, such as those on Doctors’ Row, we plan the reader inside the vestibule instead.

If a Bay Ridge resident loses a fob, how quickly can it stop working?
+

As soon as the super or managing agent deletes it, which takes about a minute from the management screen or app. The lost fob is dead from then on, and a replacement can be enrolled to the same apartment straight away. What the building charges residents for a replacement is a board decision. If the building does not keep its own stock of blank fobs, we quote replacements in writing before ordering.

Can fobs in a larger Bay Ridge co-op limit the elevator to each resident’s own floor?
+

Yes, in buildings where the elevator controls allow it.
