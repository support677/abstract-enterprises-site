The Neighborhood

Why Queens Properties Need Video Wall Installation

Most video-wall advice is written for one kind of room: a corporate lobby with a single brand, a single language and a single source. Queens businesses ask for something harder. The Census Bureau's QuickFacts table for Queens County puts the share of residents aged five and over who speak a language other than English at home at 55.4 percent, and the foreign-born share of the population at 47.6 percent, in the 2020–2024 American Community Survey. The Endangered Language Alliance, which has spent years mapping where languages are spoken across the New York area, estimates that as many as 800 languages are spoken in New York City and that no place on its map has more than Queens. A sign or a screen in this borough is read by people who arrived from dozens of countries, and a good share of them read a script other than the Latin alphabet first.

That changes what a video wall is for. On a corridor such as Main Street in Flushing or Roosevelt Avenue in Jackson Heights, the screen behind a counter is often doing the job a printed bilingual menu used to do, but with more items, more photos and prices that change with the supplier's invoice. Scripts such as Chinese, Devanagari or Bengali generally need more height on screen than a line of English to stay comfortable to read at the same distance, so the size of the wall is set by the hardest script it has to show, not by the width of the counter. We size and lay out a Queens menu wall by counting the longest line in every language the owner wants, not by picking a television size first.

Live sport pulls the other way: more feeds, not more text. In July 2026 the real-estate site 6sqft listed Queens places to watch the World Cup final — the Queens Night Market in Flushing Meadows-Corona Park with a 30-foot LED screen, the Queens Botanical Garden with three jumbotrons on its lawn, Queens Borough Hall with a 16-by-9-foot LED screen, and Pig Beach in Astoria with a 28-foot jumbotron outdoors and dozens of televisions inside. A neighborhood bar does not need a jumbotron, but it does get asked to put a match from one continent beside a game from another, sometimes with different audio for different rooms. That is a switching and audio problem before it is a display problem, and we treat it that way.

Then there are the rules. Chain restaurants in New York City with 15 or more locations must show calories on their menu boards, and the Health Department's rules add a salt-shaker icon for items with at least 2,300 mg of sodium and, since October 2025, an added-sugar icon for certain high-sugar items. Screens in storefront windows can become signs in the eyes of the Department of Buildings. The power behind the wall is licensed electrical work. None of that is unusual on its own; what is unusual about Queens is how often all of it lands on one small business with one wall and one weekend to get it done.

Language on storefronts has been a public argument here before. In 2011 a Flushing council member proposed requiring store signs in the city to be at least 60 percent English, a story the New York Times covered under the headline “Queens Councilman Wants English to Dominate Store Signs.” Whatever one thinks of that debate, it shows how visible the language mix of a Queens shopfront is to the people walking past it. A screen in the window is part of that mix, and owners usually want both their regular customers and newcomers to feel addressed. We leave the choice of languages and their order entirely to the owner and make sure the hardware and layout can carry whatever they decide.

Two-by-two or larger LCD walls that can show one picture across all panels or a different match on each, switched from a tablet behind the bar. Audio follows the feed you choose, room by room, instead of every screen fighting for the same speakers.

Storefront window displays

High-brightness panels that stay legible facing a sunny avenue, mounted on floor stands or ceiling drops behind the glass so nothing is fixed to the facade. We size and program them with the Department of Buildings sign guide in mind, and say plainly when a design needs a sign permit.

Narrow-bezel LCD or fine-pitch direct-view LED walls for office lobbies, reception areas and conference rooms, with a content system that lets a building manager post notices without calling us. Common around Court Square, Queens Plaza and the office floors of Flushing's newer mixed-use buildings.

Studio and production-office monitor walls

Monitor walls for production offices, edit suites and green rooms in western Queens, where several camera or playback feeds need to be watched at once. Fed by a matrix switcher or video-over-IP so a coordinator can move any source to any screen.

Walls for classrooms, student lounges, community halls and worship spaces that must carry captions or translation alongside the main picture. Built so a volunteer or a staff member can change the layout from one control panel.

Building Specialists

Video Wall Installation for Queens's Architecture

Queens commercial space comes in a handful of recognizable shapes, and each one decides where a wall can hang, how cable reaches it and who has to approve the work.

Mall food halls and food courts

Flushing's Tangram mall runs a second-floor food court that Grub Street reported in April 2026 as booming while other New York food halls closed, and New World Mall in downtown Flushing has its own food court. Stalls here sit under a shared ceiling, on the landlord's power and often the landlord's network, with mall rules on working hours and on what may hang over the common aisle.

Mixed-use storefronts with apartments above

The typical Roosevelt Avenue, 37th Avenue, Steinway Street or Jamaica Avenue shop: a ground-floor store under one or more floors of apartments. Walls are frequently masonry party walls, ceilings may be pressed tin or plaster above a dropped grid, and the cellar is the only practical route for cable.

Long Island City office towers and converted lofts

Glass towers such as One Court Square, the 50-story office tower in Long Island City, sit a few blocks from former factories converted to offices and studios. Towers bring raised standards for contractor paperwork and freight elevators; conversions bring concrete ceilings, steel columns and long exposed runs.

Studio campuses in Astoria and Long Island City

Kaufman Astoria Studios on 36th Street and Silvercup Studios on 22nd Street in Long Island City host film and television production. Production offices and support spaces in and around studios like these are where monitor walls and playback screens get moved and re-patched from one show to the next.

Campus and institutional buildings

Queens College on Kissena Boulevard in Flushing and St. John's University's Queens campus off Utopia Parkway are the largest campuses in the borough's center. Work in buildings like these goes through facilities departments with their own IT, network and scheduling rules.

Free-standing restaurants and halls

One- and two-story commercial buildings along the boulevards — Northern, Queens, Hillside — with their own roofs and service, used for restaurants, banquet rooms and community halls. These give the most freedom on cable routes and the most responsibility on power, because nobody else's riser is there to share.

How each building type changes the method

Concrete slabs and columns. In Long Island City conversions and newer towers we fix wall mounts to concrete with anchors rated for the load and the substrate, and where the wall is drywall over steel studs we add a backing plate or a floor-supported frame rather than trust thin-gauge studs with a tiled wall. Cable rides in surface raceway or in the ceiling plenum, and in a plenum space it must be cable rated for that space.

Masonry party walls. In older Queens storefronts the wall the owner wants is often brick shared with the next building. We drill only into the owner's side, keep anchors well clear of the neighbor's face, and bring cable down from the ceiling or up from the cellar through a single sleeve rather than chasing a channel through old brick.

Shared ceilings in food halls. Above a food-hall stall the space is usually a common dropped ceiling carrying sprinkler pipes, ducts and other tenants' cable. We confirm with mall operations which tiles we may lift, hang cable on its own supports instead of tying it to pipes or ceiling wires, and keep heavy screens on wall or floor structure rather than on the grid.

Studio campuses. Western Queens has some of the largest production space in the city. CoStar reports that Kaufman Astoria Studios spans more than 500,000 square feet of production space with 11 soundstages totaling more than 147,000 square feet and New York's only studio backlot, and that it is billed as the first motion picture studio in the United States listed on the National Register of Historic Places; it also reports that Silvercup Studios runs 23 soundstages and about 558,000 square feet across its three lots, 13 of them at the Long Island City flagship on 22nd Street. In space like this, a monitor wall is usually temporary or semi-permanent: it belongs to a production office or a support room rather than to the building. We build these walls on rolling or floor-supported frames where the studio's rules call for it, keep every cable labeled at both ends, and leave a patch sheet so the next crew can re-route sources without tracing wires.

Retail strips. The Census Bureau's Economic Census puts total retail sales in Queens County at about $23.0 billion in 2022, roughly $10,100 per resident. Much of that passes through ordinary storefronts on the corridors described below, which is why window displays and promotional walls come up as often as menus. In these narrow shops the wall the owner wants is usually the one facing the door, the deepest point of the room; we plan cable from the cellar or the ceiling to that wall first, because it decides everything else about the layout.

Kitchens behind the counter. A menu wall over a wok line or a fryer lives in heat and grease. We mount screens out of the direct plume, choose displays rated for the temperature, leave room behind them for air to move and put the media player where a cook will not hang a towel over its vents.

Rules that reach a Queens display wall

Menu-board rules (NYC Health Department). Restaurants with 15 or more locations under the same name nationally must post calories on menus and menu boards, as noticeably as the item's name or price (Health Code §81.50 and the city's Calorie Posting guidance). The same chains must put a salt-shaker warning icon beside items with 2,300 mg or more of sodium, and since October 4, 2025 an added-sugar icon beside qualifying high-sugar items, with summonses carrying a $200 fine from January 2026. A digital menu board is a menu board; the icons belong on screen.

Sign rules (Department of Buildings). The DOB's business-sign guide sets size and illumination limits by zoning district. In its chart, up to three illuminated, non-flashing window signs totaling no more than eight square feet are exempt from the surface-area count; C3 districts permit no illuminated signs at all, and C1 and C2 districts cap illuminated signs at 50 square feet. A screen facing the street through a window can fall inside those rules, so we check the zoning district on the city's ZoLa map before designing a window display.

Electrical permits and low-voltage work. Under NYC Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, wiring that runs at under 50 volts for signaling, communication and data does not need an electrical permit when done by a licensed master electrician, special electrician or qualified person, but any point that interfaces with a control circuit switching light, heat or power circuits must be done by a licensed master or special electrician. New outlets and circuits behind a wall are general electrical work that the DOB says needs a NYC licensed master electrician and a permit.

Accessibility (ADA). In a corridor or other circulation path, a wall-mounted object whose leading edge sits between 27 and 80 inches above the floor may protrude no more than 4 inches (2010 ADA Standards §307.2). A screen on a standard tilting mount in a narrow restaurant passage can exceed that; we recess it, mount it higher or move it out of the path.

Your Questions Answered

Video Wall Installation in Queens: Questions Answered

The questions below are grouped in the order the decisions usually have to be made on a Queens job: language first, rules second, cable and power third, then feeds, content and leases. Your own wall will raise a few more, which is what the walk-through at the counter or the bar is for.

Menus in More Than One Language

How do we know a Chinese or Bengali line will still be readable from the back of the queue?

We test it before anything is ordered. At the site visit we take the longest dish name in each language the owner wants, set it at the size the proposed screen would show it, and hold a printed or tablet mock-up at the counter while someone reads it from where the line actually forms. If the hardest script fails, the answer is a bigger screen, fewer items per slide or a second screen — not smaller text. A layout that only works in English is the mistake this test exists to catch.

Should each language get its own screen, or should every screen carry both?

It depends on how customers order. Where most of the queue reads one language and a minority another, a single bilingual layout per screen keeps everyone on the same item number and avoids confusion at the register. Where two groups of customers are roughly equal, separate screens per language let each layout breathe and keep the font large. A third option many counters like is a fixed column of item numbers and prices with the descriptions alternating language every few seconds; it works if the price never moves and the cycle is slow enough to read once.

Who types the menu in each script, and who checks the characters come out right?

The owner supplies or approves every word; we never machine-translate a menu and publish it. Our part is technical: making sure the media player has fonts that contain every character in the menu, so a dish name does not turn into empty boxes, and that the content software handles right-to-left text if Arabic or Urdu is involved. Before handover we put every slide on the wall and ask a staff member who reads that language to check it line by line, then save that version as the master copy.

Do our dish photos need a professional photographer, or will phone pictures do?

Recent phone cameras are good enough for a menu wall if the pictures are taken with care: daylight or a bright, even light, a plain background, the dish shot from the same angle each time, and the photo cropped to the shape the template uses. What ruins menu walls is not the camera but inconsistency — twenty photos taken under twenty different lights. We give owners a one-page shooting guide with the exact frame size the template expects, and we drop any photo that is too small to fill its space sharply rather than stretch it.

Calories, Salt and Sugar on Digital Menu Boards

Our franchise has more than fifteen locations nationally — do calories have to appear on the screens too?

Yes. The city's rule covers menus and menu boards for restaurants with 15 or more locations under the same name or ownership, and its guidance says calorie information must be as noticeable as the item's name or price. A digital board is still a menu board. We build the calorie figure into the same template line as the name and price, in the same or larger type, so it cannot be dropped when someone edits a price on a phone at closing time.

Can the salt-shaker or added-sugar icon sit on a slide that rotates away?

We do not design it that way, and we advise against it. The Health Department's sodium rule requires the warning icon next to each item with 2,300 mg or more of sodium, and the added-sugar rule that took effect on October 4, 2025 requires an icon next to qualifying items plus a written warning; summonses carry a $200 fine from January 2026. The safe layout keeps the icon beside the item on whatever slide shows that item and its price, and keeps the warning statement on screen whenever those items are. If a chain's brand team supplies the templates, we check them against the rule before they go live and point out gaps; the final compliance call belongs to the operator and the Health Department.

We are a single independent restaurant in Queens — do we have to show calories at all?

Under the city's calorie posting rule, restaurants with fewer than 15 locations do not have to post calories; the sodium and added-sugar icons are tied to the same chain threshold. Many independents still show item numbers, photos, spice levels or allergens on their walls because customers ask for them, and those are worth building into the template once rather than adding by hand later. If you franchise or expand past the threshold, the template already has a place for the numbers.

Window Screens and the Sign Rules

How large can a window screen get before the Department of Buildings wants a permit?

The DOB's business-sign guide says a permit is not required for certain small signs, including wall signs of no more than six square feet, and that anything larger needs one; even signs that need no permit must still comply with zoning. Its zoning chart also exempts up to three illuminated, non-flashing window signs totaling no more than eight square feet from the surface-area count. A typical 55-inch display is well over six square feet of screen, so a street-facing window display of any real size deserves a check against your district's rules on the city's ZoLa map before it is ordered. We do that check and tell you in writing when a sign permit, a licensed sign hanger or a registered design professional is needed.

Can a window display play full-motion video, or will it be treated as a flashing sign?

The DOB guide treats flashing signs separately from illuminated ones, does not permit illuminated or flashing signs at all in C3 districts, and says illuminated signs that change periodically to show the time, temperature or similar information are not considered flashing. It does not give a simple yes or no for full-motion video in a window, and we do not pretend it does. Our default for a street-facing screen is content that changes on a slow cycle rather than constant motion and strobing, and for anything more ambitious we recommend a permit expediter or registered design professional confirm the plan with the DOB first.

How bright does a window display have to be on a sunny Queens avenue?

Brighter than any television. Ordinary indoor displays wash out in direct afternoon sun on a south- or west-facing window, which is why manufacturers sell separate high-brightness window models with anti-glare glass and thermal designs meant for sun on the face of the panel. We check which way the window faces, whether an awning or the elevated tracks shade it, and the hours the store is open, then choose the panel and set a brightness schedule so it is readable at noon without glaring into apartments across the street at night.

Power, Cable and Who Does What

Can one keypad behind the bar switch the lights and the screens together?

Yes, but part of that job belongs to an electrician. The keypad, its low-voltage wiring and the commands that turn screens and switchers on and off are low-voltage work, and NYC Administrative Code §28-105.4.7 exempts wiring under 50 volts for signaling, communication and data from an electrical permit when a licensed master electrician, special electrician or qualified person does it. The same section makes an exception for any point that connects to a control circuit switching light, heat or power circuits: that connection may only be made by a licensed master or special electrician. So we wire and program the keypad and the AV side, and your electrician makes the connection to the lighting controls, with both trades working from one drawing.

Mall operations will not give us their Wi-Fi password — how will the menu update?

Several ways, and we pick the one the landlord will accept. If the stall has its own internet service, the player sits on the stall's own router on a wired port. If it has none, a small cellular router dedicated to the screens is often simplest. If updates are rare, the player can run entirely from local storage and be updated by USB or by a laptop on site. What we avoid is a player that depends on a guest network whose password changes without notice, because a changed password silently stops every update until someone notices.

Can the cable come up from the cellar in a building with apartments upstairs?

Usually, and it is often the best route. In a mixed-use storefront the cellar ceiling is open framing or exposed joists, so cable can run from wherever the cable box, network and player live to a point directly below the wall and come up through one sleeve. We keep low-voltage runs off the building's electrical and gas lines, seal every floor penetration with a listed firestop product, and stay out of the residential stairs and halls unless the landlord asks for a route through them and approves it in writing.

Live Games and Several Feeds on One Wall

Can each panel show a different match, then all panels join for the big one?

Yes, that is what a video wall processor or a matrix switcher with wall modes is for. Each panel takes its own source most of the night, and a preset joins two, four or all panels into one picture when the match everyone came for starts. We program the presets the bar actually uses — “four games”, “two plus one big”, “one game across the wall” — onto buttons on a tablet or wall keypad behind the bar, so staff never have to open a panel menu with a remote.

How do customers hear one game while the other screens stay muted?

The audio is switched separately from the video. Each source's sound goes into a small digital audio matrix, and each speaker zone — the bar, the back room, the patio — is set to follow one source. On a busy night the back room can listen to the cricket while the front bar listens to the football, and the rest of the screens stay silent with captions on. That is also the cleanest way to keep the sound level under control for neighbors upstairs.

Why does the same goal show up seconds apart on two screens?

Because the feeds take different paths. A cable or satellite box, a streaming app and an over-the-air antenna each add their own delay, and two streaming apps carrying the same match rarely agree. A video wall cannot remove that delay. What we can do is make sure every screen showing the same game is fed from the same source through the switcher, rather than from separate boxes, so the whole room cheers at the same moment and nobody's phone spoils the goal for the rest.

Can the layout change itself when an early match ends and an evening game begins?

Yes. Presets can be scheduled as well as pressed. A wall can open in the morning with a European match across all panels, switch at a set time to a split layout for afternoon games, and change again for an evening broadcast, with the audio zones following each change. Staff can still override any preset from the tablet if a match runs into extra time. For venues in Queens that draw fans of sports played in many time zones, a weekly schedule set once saves someone re-patching screens all day.

How many cable or streaming boxes does a multi-game wall need?

One for every different game you want on screen at the same time, plus one spare if you never want a dead panel on a big night. A four-panel wall that shows four separate matches needs four sources; a wall that rarely shows more than two games needs two, and the rest of the panels can carry menus, promotions or the same game. The subscription side — which services, which commercial licenses for public viewing — is between the venue and its providers; we build the wall around whatever boxes and apps the venue is entitled to use.

Types of Video Wall We Install

Narrow-bezel LCD video wall panels

Commercial LCD tiles built to sit edge to edge, with thin bezels and calibration for color matching across the wall. The workhorse for two-by-two and three-by-three walls in restaurants, bars and lobbies where viewers stand a few meters away.

Wall processors and matrix switchers

The hardware that splits, joins and routes sources across panels: wall controllers for one-picture-across-many, HDMI matrices for bars with several boxes, and video-over-IP encoders and decoders for larger or changing layouts.

Signal extension and cabling

Pop-out or push-to-release mounts that let any single panel be serviced without taking down its neighbors, and floor-supported frames where a wall cannot carry the load or a landlord will not allow fixings.

Zoned audio and control

Small digital audio matrices, amplifiers and wall keypads or tablets so staff can pick which game is heard in which part of the room, and one-button presets for opening, closing and match-day layouts.

Choosing between LCD and LED in Queens

For most Queens restaurants, bars and small lobbies, tiled LCD is the better value: it is sharper up close, cheaper per screen, lighter, and easy to replace one panel at a time. Its weakness is the thin seam between panels, which matters for a single big picture and hardly at all for menus, where each panel usually carries its own section anyway.

Direct-view LED earns its higher price where the wall must be seamless, very large or unusually bright: a lobby feature, an event room, a showroom. Pixel pitch is chosen from the closest point anyone will stand, and the cabinets need a sturdier structure, more power and more ventilation than LCD. We will tell you plainly when LED would be money spent on something your customers will not notice.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Video Wall Installation in Queens

Google's AI Overview could not be captured by our research tools for this edition, so this section tests the claims that feed those summaries instead: published 2026 cost guides from LED vendors, and owner and installer comments in Reddit's commercial-AV and digital-signage communities. Each claim is quoted or summarized from its source, then checked against how a Queens job actually runs.

“A complete LED video wall starts around $9,500”

A 2026 cost guide from an LED supplier puts a complete package at anywhere from $9,500 for an entry-level indoor wall to more than $200,000 for premium MicroLED. The low end of that range is real for what it describes, which is hardware for a small indoor wall.

What it does not describe is a finished wall in a Queens storefront or lobby: a mounting structure suited to the substrate, a new circuit from a licensed electrician, low-voltage cabling, a controller, content set-up, calibration and the paperwork a landlord or mall asks for before anyone drills. On our published Queens list, a 2.5 mm direct-view LED wall is $36,750 to $57,750 installed.

The useful way to read an entry-level number is as a floor for hardware, not a budget for the project. If a quote is near that figure, ask what is missing from it before comparing it to anything else.

“You can buy a P2.9 LED wall kit online for $11,000 to $15,000”

One digital-signage vendor's 2026 guide lists exactly that: a 10-by-5-foot P2.9 indoor kit with processing at roughly $11,000 to $15,000. To its credit, the same table lists what is not in the box — wall structure, power circuits, installation and calibration.

Those missing items are where Queens costs come from. A food-hall stall may need the landlord's approval and an evening work window; a mixed-use storefront may need the cable brought up from the cellar; a converted loft in Long Island City may need a frame bolted to concrete because the drywall cannot carry the cabinets.

Kits are not a bad idea for every buyer. We install customer-supplied equipment at labor-only rates, and we will tell you before the visit whether a kit you are looking at suits the wall you have.

“Any 4K TV will do for a menu board”

This is the most repeated shortcut in forum threads, and the replies in r/digitalsignage and r/CommercialAV push back hard: consumer televisions are not built to run all day with a static image, and commercial displays rated for 16 or 24 hours a day are the usual recommendation. One commenter adds that some manufacturers shorten consumer-TV warranties when the set is used commercially.

We cannot verify that warranty claim across every brand, so treat it as a question to put to the seller, not a fact. What we do see is that menus are mostly still images, shown for long hours, often in a warm room near a kitchen — the conditions commercial displays are designed for.

For a single bar television watched a few nights a week, a good consumer set can be fine. For a wall that is the menu, the cost of a dark screen at lunch is higher than the price difference.

“Price it by the square foot”

Another 2026 guide prices LED walls installed at roughly $1,400 to $2,400 per square foot for fine pitch (P1.5 to P1.9) and $700 to $1,300 for P2.5 to P3. Square-foot figures are a reasonable way to compare large walls with each other.

They break down on small walls, which is most of Queens. A counter menu or a two-by-two bar wall carries nearly the same fixed costs — site visit, electrician coordination, controller or player, programming, travel and handover — as a much bigger wall, spread over far fewer square feet.

That is why our Queens pricing is set out by configuration, not by area, and why the cost per square foot of a small wall will always look high next to a lobby-sized one.

“Mounting is a minor line item”

In a Reddit thread on video wall pricing, an installer estimated that the mounting structure alone for a large wall could reach $20,000. That is for big, heavy walls, but the direction is right: structure is not an afterthought.

Our own published range for mounting structure and custom fabrication is $1,500 to $9,000, and where a Queens job lands inside it depends on the wall behind the screen — concrete, old brick, steel studs, or a landlord who will only allow a floor-supported frame.

Pop-out mounts cost more than fixed ones and are worth it on any wall of four or more panels, because they let one panel be serviced without taking down the others.

“Translation is just a font setting”

Generic signage advice often suggests that a second language is a matter of turning on auto-translate in the content software. In a borough where more than half of residents speak another language at home, customers notice a bad translation immediately.

The technical part is real — the player needs fonts with every character, and right-to-left scripts need software that handles them — but it is the smaller part. The bigger part is that the owner has to approve every line, and text in some scripts needs more height to stay legible at the same distance.

We build multilingual templates and test them on the wall with a reader of each language before handover. We do not write or translate menus.

“Any AV installer or electrician can handle all of it”

The work splits along a line that New York City draws in law. New outlets and circuits need a NYC licensed master electrician and a permit; low-voltage signaling and data wiring under 50 volts is exempt from the permit when done by a licensed master electrician, special electrician or qualified person, under Administrative Code §28-105.4.7.

A video wall needs both sides done well, and each trade is better at its own side. An electrician who does not plan for recessed boxes and cable paths leaves outlets in the wrong place; an AV installer who extends power with cords breaks the electrical rules.

Our approach is to run the low-voltage side ourselves under our New York State license and coordinate the electrical side with a licensed master electrician, with the outlet locations drawn on the same plan.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Readability Test Comes Before the Purchase Order

The step that saves Queens owners the most money takes ten minutes and costs nothing. Before we price a menu wall, we ask the owner for the longest dish name on the menu in every language they want to show, and the price and any icons that go with it. We set that line at the size the proposed screen would display it, put the mock-up on the counter, and walk to where the queue actually forms at the busiest hour — often out past the door of a narrow shop.

If someone at the back can read it comfortably in every script, the wall size is right. If not, we change the plan on paper: a bigger panel, fewer items per screen, or one screen per language. It is much cheaper to have that conversation with a printed sheet in hand than after four screens are on the wall and the owner discovers that the second language only works up close.

The same habit applies to sports walls, with a different test: we stand at the far end of the bar and ask which screen each table can see, and which game the owner expects each part of the room to want on a busy night. The answers decide the panel count, the number of boxes and the audio zones. Hardware is the last decision, not the first.

There is one more test we run in every Queens space with apartments above or beside it: the sound check with the doors closed. We play a match at the level the owner expects on a busy night, then step outside and, where the landlord or a neighbor allows, into the hallway upstairs. If the commentary carries, we lower the speaker level, turn speakers away from the shared wall or add a zone so only the room that wants the game hears it. A wall that keeps the neighbors happy keeps the landlord happy too, and that matters the next time the owner asks for permission to change something.

None of these tests needs special equipment — a printed sheet, a phone and twenty minutes of the owner's time. They are on every Queens site visit because they answer the questions that decide whether the finished wall gets used.

FAQ

Queens Video Wall Installation FAQ

Should menu screens hang above the counter or on the side wall facing the queue?
+

It depends on where people decide what to order. If customers make up their minds while standing in line, the screens belong where the line faces, which in many narrow Queens shops is a side wall rather than the wall behind the register. If they decide at the counter, above and behind the cashier works, as long as the bottom edge clears staff heads and equipment and the top is not so high that people strain to read it. We stand in the queue at a busy hour before choosing, and sometimes split the menu between both positions.

Can the wall show a QR code customers can scan for online ordering or delivery?
+

Yes. A QR code in a fixed corner of the menu lets customers in a long queue order ahead or save the menu to their phone. It has to be large enough to scan from where people actually stand, on a plain high-contrast background, and it should stay on screen rather than rotate away. We test it from the back of the line with a couple of different phones before handover, and we point it at a page the owner controls so it can be changed without editing the wall.

Can the menu show halal, kosher, vegan or vegetarian marks?
+

Yes. Dietary and religious marks are built into the template as icons beside each item, the same way required calorie figures or warning icons are, so they stay attached to the right dish when prices change. Which marks appear, and whether the business is entitled to use a certification mark, is the owner’s decision and responsibility; we only make sure they display clearly and consistently in every language version of the menu.

Can we change a price from a phone when a supplier raises costs?
+

Yes, if the wall is built on content software with a phone or web editor, and that is how we set up most Queens menu walls. Prices sit in fields the owner can edit without touching the layout, so the font, the icons and the second language stay in place. For walls that run offline, price changes are made on a laptop or USB stick instead. Either way, the owner holds the login, not the installer.

Is it safe to mount screens above a wok station or fryer?
+

Not directly above it. Heat and grease rise from the cooking line, so we place menu screens out of the direct plume, behind the counter rather than over the range, and choose commercial displays whose rated operating temperature suits the room. We leave space behind each panel for air to move and keep the player’s vents clear. If the only possible position is close to the cooking line, we discuss an enclosure or a different layout before installing anything.

Can we add another language to the menu later without new screens?
+

Usually, if the wall was sized with room to spare. Adding a language means new text in every item, which takes space; on a wall that was already full, the choice is between smaller text, more slides or another screen. When a Queens owner tells us at the start that a third language may come later, we plan the template and the screen count for it, so adding it later is a content job rather than a hardware one.

Can one corner of the menu wall carry a live news channel or a game?
+

Yes. The wall processor or player can place a live television picture in a window on one screen, or give one whole screen to a live feed while the others carry the menu. Staff can switch it off for a quiet lunch and on for a match. We keep the live picture away from prices and required icons, so a busy broadcast never covers information the customer needs to order.

Do portrait menu screens need different mounts from landscape ones?
+

Yes. A display turned to portrait needs a mount and adapter rated for portrait use, and the display itself has to be approved by its manufacturer for portrait orientation, because cooling and the internal layout are designed around one direction. We confirm both before ordering, line the screens up at the same height so the menu reads as one row, and keep the connectors reachable through a recessed box or a pop-out mount.

Can the screens carry paid ads for other businesses to earn extra income?
+

Technically yes, and some owners do it through advertising networks that supply players and pay per display. Two checks come first. Your lease may restrict advertising for other businesses, and the Department of Buildings treats advertising signs differently from business signs, permitting them only in certain zoning districts, which matters if any screen can be seen from the street. Inside the shop and facing customers, the main question is whether the ads crowd out your own menu.
