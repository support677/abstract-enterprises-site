The Neighborhood

Why Long Island Rental Buildings Need a Whole-Property Security Plan

Long Island is mostly houses, and that shapes how its apartment buildings get secured. The Regional Plan Association counts rentals as 21 percent of all homes on Long Island, and the Census Bureau puts Nassau County's owner-occupied rate at 81.9 percent for 2020 to 2024. Rental buildings are scattered among single-family streets rather than lined up block after block, and the security companies that dominate the Island sell mostly to homeowners: a keypad by the garage door, a doorbell camera, a monthly monitoring plan. A multifamily apartment building is a different job. It has a front door that dozens of people open every day, mailboxes and packages for every apartment, a laundry room that tenants share, a basement with the boiler and the meters, and often a parking lot or a row of carports. A homeowner package does not cover any of that, and the owner who tries to stretch one across a building usually finds out the night a tenant calls about a stranger in the hall.

The buildings themselves vary more than people expect. Western Nassau has village downtowns with apartments over stores and prewar elevator buildings around train stations, such as Great Neck Plaza, which the village itself describes as a third of a square mile holding 90 multiple-family apartment buildings. Hempstead, Freeport, Long Beach and Glen Cove have mid-century walk-ups and garden complexes. Suffolk has long, low garden communities spread across acres, houses converted into two and three apartments under town rental permits, and a wave of new buildings beside Long Island Rail Road stations. Each type fails in its own way, and the plan for a twelve-building garden complex in Suffolk looks nothing like the plan for six apartments above a pharmacy on a Nassau main street.

The new building is changing the picture fastest. Newsday reported in September 2026 that Mineola alone has opened more than 1,100 rental units since 2010, with roughly 1,000 more planned, and its mayor, Paul Pereira, gave those figures while a developer predicted that “the model of Mineola will eventually grow in other villages and towns.” Station Yards in Ronkonkoma broke ground in October 2026 on a phase of 285 apartments in three five-story buildings across from the train station. Hicksville, Westbury, Farmingdale, Patchogue and Wyandanch have similar projects built or under way. These buildings open with video entry panels, fob readers, package rooms and garage gates already installed, and their owners inherit a stack of logins, software contracts and equipment closets that somebody now has to run.

Two more things make Long Island different from the city, and both catch owners out. The first is the law: the New York City rules that most online articles quote do not apply in Nassau or Suffolk, and the rules that do apply come from the state, the county police, the town and sometimes the village, each with its own paperwork. The second is the weather and the distance. A garden complex can spread its buildings over several acres, which is farther than ordinary network cable can carry a signal, and a South Shore building can lose power for a day after a summer storm or take water in its basement in a hurricane. A security plan for a Long Island rental has to answer all of that before the first panel goes on the wall. This page is written for the owner who has to make those calls. Managing agents running whole portfolios will find their version on our property management security page for Long Island, and owners who only want cameras can go straight to camera installation for Long Island apartment buildings.

Long Island facts a rental building owner should know

  • Rentals are scarce, and tenants notice what works. The Regional Plan Association puts Long Island's rental vacancy rate at 4.3 percent, fewer available rentals than any other suburban part of the New York region, and says 56 percent of renters pay more than 30 percent of their income for housing.
  • The city's apartment law does not reach the Island. The Multiple Dwelling Law, home of the New York City intercom rule in §50-a, applies to cities of 325,000 or more. The Multiple Residence Law applies to every smaller city and to all towns and villages, which covers all of Nassau and Suffolk.
  • The state maintenance code covers locks. Section 304.18 of the Property Maintenance Code of New York State requires security devices on doors, windows and hatchways of dwelling units, deadbolts that open from inside without a key and throw at least one inch, and sash locks on rented ground-level windows.
  • Local governments enforce the code. The state Department of State notes that the Uniform Code “is automatically in effect in each city, town, and village in the State (except New York City),” and each one enforces it, so the inspector at a Long Island rental works for the town, the village or the city.
  • Some Nassau buildings are rent-stabilized. According to New York State Homes and Community Renewal, ETPA rent stabilization applies in sixteen Nassau localities, from the Village of Baxter Estates to the Village of Thomaston, to buildings of six or more units built before January 1, 1974, and some localities limit it to larger buildings. No Suffolk locality appears on the list.
  • Good Cause Eviction has not reached the Island. The Attorney General's list of opt-in localities as of April 2025 includes no Long Island city, town or village, and Newsday reported that the Village of Hempstead board voted 3–2 against the law in November 2025.
  • Alarms need police permits. Nassau County Police list alarm permits at $100 for a residence and $200 for a business, valid two years, and say they are asked to respond to more than 125,000 false alarms a year. Suffolk County Police register alarms at $50 and $100.
  • Storms test the backup. PSEG Long Island reported after a July 2026 storm with gusts up to 84 miles an hour that it had restored more than 34,800 customers, with 4,170 of its 1.2 million customers still out the next morning.

What goes wrong on a property secured one piece at a time

The Long Island owners who call us after years of piecemeal purchases describe the same property. The front building has a newer video panel, but the rear buildings still have the original buzzers, and half the apartment handsets were unplugged long ago. Someone added a keypad lock to the laundry building, and the code has not changed since the tenant who installed it moved out. A camera company put four Wi-Fi cameras on the lot that work when the router in the office is on and stop when it is not. A home alarm protects the boiler room, monitored on a contract in a former manager's name, and the police bill for false alarms arrives at the wrong address. None of these pieces is bad on its own. Together, they leave the owner without a list of what is installed, who can get in, or what happens when the power goes out. A whole-property plan starts by writing that list.

Entrances: making every door close and latch

Every security system on a rental property starts at the door, because a panel or a camera cannot help an entrance that does not latch. On Long Island we meet three kinds of entrance doors over and over. Aluminum and glass storefront-style doors on mid-century walk-ups and garden buildings, often with closers that have been leaking oil for years. Steel doors in steel frames on side and rear entrances, where the strike plate has been bent by people pulling on a locked door. And on converted houses, the original wood front door, which now serves two or three households and was never built for that much traffic. We set the closer and the strike together, so the door swings shut, catches the latch and holds. When a door needs an electric strike for the entry panel, the strike is fitted to the existing frame where it can be; when the frame cannot take one, a magnetic lock goes on the header instead, wired so it releases on power loss and on the fire alarm signal and lets anyone leave from inside without a key.

On doors that must stay shut, such as a basement door that opens to the outside or a rear stair door that tenants keep propping for a breeze, we add a door position switch. It tells the panel or the alarm when the door has been left open, which is the single cheapest fix for a building where the doors are fine and the habits are not. Our published Long Island price for an electric strike replaced with the door aligned is $450 to $950, and a new door release relay or button is quoted from the same door buzzer repair page for Long Island.

Entry panels: one per entrance or one for the complex

The entry panel is what tenants and visitors see, and on Long Island the right answer depends on how the property is laid out. A single building with one front door needs one panel and a way for each apartment to answer. A garden complex with eight or twelve buildings either gets a panel at each building's entrance or, where the complex is fenced, one panel at a pedestrian gate with simple fob readers at the building doors. Converted houses with two or three apartments often need nothing more than a small call station with a button per apartment, wired to stations or to tenants' phones. We install audio panels and video intercom systems, keypad and directory panels, and panels that call tenants' cell phones instead of using apartment wiring; our intercom installation page for Long Island covers the models and the multi-unit range we publish, $1,200 to $4,500 and up for a building-wide system.

Owners with an older system that still mostly works should know that we repair before we replace. An entry panel that rings but will not release the door, a few dead apartment stations, a transformer running hot in the basement: those are service calls, not new systems, and they are handled on our intercom repair service on Long Island. Where a building only needs a simple buzzer and door release, for instance a three-apartment house or a single side door, the smaller job is laid out on our door buzzer installation page for Long Island. A whole-property plan tells the owner which of those three paths each building should take.

Fobs and keypads for side doors, outbuildings and gates

Keys are the weak point of most Long Island rentals, and key fob entry systems are the usual cure. A garden complex can have dozens of exterior doors on one key, plus a laundry building, a storage building and a boiler room, and every key that walked away with a former tenant still works. Fob readers fix that: each tenant gets a credential that opens only the doors they should use, and the owner cancels it on move-out instead of changing locks. On properties where the owner does not want to manage fobs at all, a keypad with a code per tenant does much of the same job at a lower cost. Our published price for a single-door reader with an electric strike on Long Island is $1,500 to $2,500 installed, and a gate with a vehicle reader and barrier arm runs $3,500 to $12,000, both on our access control installation page for Long Island.

Smart locks are the other tool. On an office, a storage room or a vacant apartment between tenants, a keypad or app lock can be the right answer, and we install them through our smart lock installation service on Long Island. On an apartment's own entrance door, any lock has to meet the state maintenance code's deadbolt rule: it opens from inside without a key or special knowledge, and it throws at least an inch. We will not put a lock on a tenant's door that traps them inside.

Mail and packages where there is no lobby

Many Long Island rentals have no lobby at all. Garden buildings open straight into a stairhall, converted houses have one front step, and newer buildings near the train stations have a lobby but no staff. Deliveries end up on steps, in vestibules and against apartment doors, package theft follows, and the commuter who leaves on the 6:40 is not home to bring them in. The fix is to give packages a place that is locked and recorded: a small package room off the vestibule with its own reader, or a bank of parcel lockers in a covered spot. Couriers get a code that opens only that door or the lockers, and the camera at that door records faces, not the backs of heads. We install the reader, the door hardware, the camera and the cabling; the locker cabinets themselves come from the locker maker.

Door alarms, burglar alarms and monitoring

Boiler rooms with copper, storage buildings, roof hatches on mid-rise buildings, and apartments sitting empty between tenants are the places a building should hear about when they are opened. Contacts on those doors and a motion sensor or two, wired to an alarm panel, will sound locally, text the owner and, with monitoring, call the central station. On Long Island that alarm also needs a police permit: in the Nassau County Police district, and in the Suffolk County Police district except for the towns and villages the Suffolk program does not cover. Our central-station monitoring is $30 a month keypad-only or $45 a month with the phone app, as published on our alarm installation page for Long Island, and we help the owner put the permit in the right name.

Cameras, in short

Cameras belong in the plan, but they come after the doors, because a camera records a problem while a working door prevents one. On a rental property we aim them at the entrances, the package area, the lot and the outside of the boiler room, record on a recorder in a locked closet, and let the owner view them from a phone. Audio stays off in common areas, since recording conversations a person is not part of raises eavesdropping questions under the state's Penal Law. Camera counts, recorders and retention are covered on our apartment camera page for the Island; our published four-camera price is $999 in Nassau and $1,099 in Suffolk on our Long Island security camera pricing page.

Getting far buildings back to one closet

This is where Long Island properties differ most from city buildings. Ordinary Cat6 network cable carries a signal about 100 meters, or 328 feet, between active devices, and a garden complex can easily put its farthest building beyond that from the office. There are three ways across. Where there is existing conduit between buildings, we pull new cable or fiber through it. Where the buildings share a basement or an attic run, cable goes inside. And where neither exists, a pair of point-to-point wireless radios mounted building to building carries the signal without trenching the lawn or the lot. Each building then gets a small network switch with battery backup, and the readers, panels and cameras on that building connect locally. Our structured cabling service on Long Island publishes $150 to $300 per network connection point for the cable side of that work.

Power, batteries and storm season

An entry system that dies with the power leaves tenants locked out or the door left open, depending on how the lock was wired. We size battery backup for the door controllers and strikes, the entry panel, the network switches and the recorder, so they keep running through a normal outage, and the equipment list we hand over shows what each battery carries. Magnetic locks on exit doors release when power fails, as the building code requires, so a long outage is also a reason for a door contact and an alarm on battery to keep reporting. Batteries wear out with age, so the list also shows when each one was installed. We do not install generators or new electrical circuits; any new high-voltage power is the building's electrician's work, and we coordinate with them.

Elevators and garages on newer buildings

Station-area buildings in Mineola, Hicksville, Westbury and Ronkonkoma often have an elevator and a parking garage, and both are openings. An elevator reader limits floors to the people who live on them, and a reader or keypad at the garage door keeps the garage from becoming the building's unguarded back entrance. We install the reader in the car and the cable to the equipment room, and the building's licensed elevator company makes the connection inside the elevator controller. Our published Long Island price is from $1,725 per car in Nassau and $1,800 in Suffolk, on our elevator access control page for Long Island.

Fire alarm coordination

Any door with a magnetic lock or an electrified exit device has to release when the building's fire alarm goes off, so security and fire alarm work meet at the door. In Suffolk County we take on commercial fire alarm work for buildings that require it, designed to engineer's drawings and approved by the town or village fire marshal, as described on our fire alarm installation page for Long Island. In Nassau County, fire alarm permits require a company number from the County Fire Marshal that we do not hold, so we do not take Nassau fire alarm jobs; we wire the door release to the existing fire alarm alongside the fire alarm company of record.

What we do not sell

We do not run a virtual doorman, a remote concierge or a managed security service, and we do not sell monthly security subscriptions of our own. We install and maintain the entry panels, readers, cameras and alarms that a building's staff or an outside video service would use, and we can connect them to a provider the owner has already chosen. We do not do electrical work beyond low voltage, we do not install generators, and we do not give legal advice; where a rule matters, we name the source so the owner's attorney can check it.

Garden apartment complexes in Nassau and Suffolk

A garden complex is the classic Long Island rental: two- or three-story brick or frame buildings around lawns, each with one or two stairhall entrances, a basement laundry or a separate laundry building, and a parking lot or carports. The security problem is spread, not height. Each stairhall door is an entrance, and on many complexes those doors were never on an intercom at all; tenants simply carry a key. We plan these properties building by building. Each entrance gets a closer and strike set properly and either a small entry panel or a fob reader, depending on whether visitors arrive at the building door or at a central gate. The laundry and storage buildings get readers so only tenants use them. The lot gets cameras on the drive entrances and the dumpster area. Signals from the far buildings come back over conduit, shared basements or wireless links, and each building has its own small battery-backed switch so one building's outage does not take down the rest.

The method matters as much as the equipment. Garden complexes are occupied every day, so we work one building at a time, switch each entrance over in a short window, and leave the old keys working until the owner tells tenants the date they stop. Exterior work is planned around weather and, in winter, around snow: readers and posts at drive entrances go where plows will not clip them.

Houses converted to two and three apartments

A large share of Long Island's rental housing sits in houses: a two-family in Hempstead, a house divided into three apartments in Bay Shore, a legal accessory unit in Huntington. Newsday's database compiled with the Long Island Index found 112 towns, cities and villages on Long Island with their own rules on accessory apartments. These houses usually carry a town rental permit, and in towns such as Islip and Brookhaven the permit comes with an inspection that checks the smoke and carbon monoxide alarms. For security, the shared front door is the key opening: it needs a proper closer and latch, a call button per apartment, and a door release from each unit or from each tenant's phone. Each apartment's own entrance needs a deadbolt that meets the state code. The rest is modest: a camera on the front step and the driveway, a contact on the basement door. A converted house rarely needs a full building system, and we say so.

Apartments over stores on village main streets

Main streets from Lynbrook and Rockville Centre to Patchogue and Huntington have two- and three-story buildings with shops below and apartments above, reached through a narrow street door beside the shopfront. That street door is the whole residential security system in many of these buildings, and it takes a beating: deliveries for the shop, tenants coming and going, and passers-by trying it at night. We give it a heavy closer, an electric strike or a magnetic lock with a release button inside, and a small entry panel with a button for each apartment. The stair and the rear yard get a camera each. The shop keeps its own alarm and its own cameras on its own account, and where the apartments and the shop share a rear door or a basement, that door gets a reader so each side's access stays separate.

Downtown walk-ups and elevator buildings

Western Nassau has older apartment buildings closer in style to Queens than to suburbia: Great Neck Plaza's 90 multiple-family buildings within a third of a square mile, walk-ups and small elevator buildings in Hempstead, Freeport, Lynbrook, Rockville Centre, Mineola and Glen Cove. Many are the six-or-more-unit, pre-1974 buildings that ETPA covers where a locality has adopted it. These buildings have a lobby, a riser of intercom wire from the basement to every apartment, and the problems that come with age: shorted pairs, a transformer installed in the 1980s, half the apartment stations replaced with mismatched models. We test the riser before recommending anything. A riser that tests clean can carry a new panel and new stations; one that does not may be a reason to move apartments to phone answering and retire the riser altogether.

New buildings by the train station

The newest rentals on the Island are four- and five-story buildings beside Long Island Rail Road stations: the Station Yards phases in Ronkonkoma, the buildings around the Mineola station, Hicksville's 189-unit building next to its station that the Governor's office announced in December 2023, and projects in Westbury, Farmingdale, Patchogue and Wyandanch. They open with a video entry panel, fob readers on every common door, a package room, garage access and often an elevator reader, all installed by the developer's contractors. The owner's job is to take it over: get the administrator logins, confirm which platform fees run each month and who pays them, check the battery backup, and make sure the equipment closet is documented. We do that takeover for owners and then maintain the systems, whichever brand the developer chose.

Oceanfront and South Shore buildings

Long Beach is the only place on Long Island with oceanfront high-rises, as a 2021 news report posted by the area's state senator put it, and the buildings there and along the South Shore deal with salt air every day. Salt eats exposed metal: entry panels, strikes, camera housings and the screws holding them. We specify stainless or sealed housings on the ocean side, mount panels under cover where the layout allows, and check gaskets at each service visit. Hurricane Sandy put basements and lobbies under water on this shore in 2012, so we keep recorders, switches and power supplies off basement floors and, where we can, out of the basement altogether.

Accessory apartments in owners' houses

An accessory apartment is the smallest rental there is: one unit, often with its own side or rear door, in the house its owner lives in. The rules vary by town; the Town of Hempstead, for instance, limits them to family members and to owners or spouses aged 62 and over, according to Newsday's Long Island Index database. Security here is the tenant's door and the owner's peace of mind: a deadbolt that meets the state code, a video doorbell or small call station at the tenant's door, a camera on the path to it, and, when the owner wants it, a contact on the door between the two units. It is a short job, often finished in one visit.

Owning a Long Island Rental: Rules, Permits and Paperwork

The owner of a Long Island rental answers to more offices than a city owner does: the state, the county police, the town or city, and often the village. None of them is in charge of security systems as such, but each touches a piece of one. This section sets out the rules we work to and where they come from. We are not lawyers, this is not legal advice, and an owner's attorney should confirm how any rule applies to a particular building.

Which state law applies: Multiple Residence, not Multiple Dwelling

Much of what is written online about apartment building security in New York is about New York City. Its intercom rule, which requires self-closing, self-locking entrance doors and a two-way intercom with a door release in each apartment in larger buildings, is §50-a of the Multiple Dwelling Law, and §3 of that law limits it to cities of 325,000 people or more. On Long Island the Multiple Residence Law applies instead: its §3 covers “all cities of less than three hundred twenty-five thousand population” and “all towns and villages.” We have not found an intercom requirement like §50-a in the Multiple Residence Law, so we do not tell Long Island owners they are legally required to have one. What we do tell them is that the state maintenance code still requires locks, that tenants still have a warranty of habitability, and that an entry system which exists has to work.

The Property Maintenance Code of New York State

Outside New York City, the state's Uniform Code includes the Property Maintenance Code of New York State, and its §304.18 is the closest thing to a security rule for rentals. Doors, windows and hatchways of dwelling units must have devices designed to secure the occupants and the property. A deadbolt on a rented unit's entrance door must open from the inside without a key, special knowledge or effort, must throw at least one inch, and must be installed to the manufacturer's instructions and kept in working order; a sliding bolt does not count. Operable windows in a rented unit within six feet of the ground or a walking surface need a sash lock. Every lock and every apartment door we touch is set to those terms.

Town and village enforcement and rental permits

The state Department of State's memorandum for local officials says the Uniform Code is automatically in effect in every city, town and village except New York City, and the local government enforces it. That is why the inspector at a Long Island rental comes from the town, the village or the city, and why the rental permit rules differ from one place to the next. The Town of Islip's Code requires a rental occupancy permit with an on-site inspection, valid for two years. The Town of Brookhaven requires rental registration with an inspection by the town or a licensed professional. The Town of Babylon requires a rental permit to legally rent a unit or a whole home. Those inspections focus on life safety, such as smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, exits and occupancy, rather than intercoms or cameras, but a broken entrance lock or a missing deadbolt is the kind of item an inspector can write up.

Rent stabilization under ETPA in Nassau

Rent stabilization is not only a city matter. Under the Emergency Tenant Protection Act, a locality that declares a housing emergency can bring buildings of six or more units built before January 1, 1974 under stabilization. New York State Homes and Community Renewal's Fact Sheet #8 lists sixteen Nassau localities that have done so: the Villages of Baxter Estates, Cedarhurst, Floral Park, Flower Hill, Freeport, Great Neck, Great Neck Plaza, Hempstead, Lynbrook, Mineola, Rockville Centre, Russell Gardens and Thomaston, the City of Glen Cove, the City of Long Beach and the Town of North Hempstead. Some localities limit it to larger buildings. The same fact sheet says stabilized tenants are entitled to receive required services, which is why we schedule entry changeovers in these buildings so the door and the intercom never stop working, and why the owner should check with HCR before treating an upgrade's cost as something that can be added to rents.

Good Cause Eviction and the warranty of habitability

New York's Good Cause Eviction Law covers New York City and any city, town or village outside it that opts in. As of the Attorney General's April 2025 list, no Long Island locality had, and in November 2025 the Village of Hempstead's board voted 3–2 against it, as Newsday reported, before tenants rallied at Village Hall that December. What does apply everywhere is Real Property Law §235-b, the warranty of habitability, which the Attorney General's tenants' rights guide summarizes as the landlord's duty to keep the apartment fit to live in. A front door that will not lock is the kind of problem tenants raise under it.

County alarm permits and false alarms

A burglar alarm on a Long Island building is registered with the police. The Nassau County Police alarm permit section lists permits at $100 for a residence and $200 for a business, valid for two years. Suffolk County's Alarm Management Program registers alarms at $50 for residences and $100 for commercial premises, gives a registered location two false alarms before service fees, and charges unregistered locations from the first; its code states that no alarm owner shall operate an alarm in the Suffolk County Police District without a permit. The towns and villages outside that district, such as Riverhead, Southampton, East Hampton, Southold and Shelter Island, are not covered by the county program. A building's alarm permit should be in the owner's name, or the owner's company, not the name of a contractor or a former manager.

Tenant privacy outside the city

New York City's Tenant Data Privacy Act, which governs fob and app systems in city buildings, does not apply in Nassau or Suffolk. That does not make tenant data unimportant. Fob and app systems record who opened which door and when, video panels capture faces, and a careless owner can end up with years of tenants' movements on a laptop. We set systems up so the owner decides how long logs are kept and who can see them, we keep audio off in common areas because of the state's eavesdropping law, and we suggest that the owner's attorney write a short notice for tenants describing what is recorded.

Compliance callouts for Long Island owners

  • Multiple Residence Law §3 sets which state housing law applies on Long Island; the city's §50-a intercom rule does not.
  • Property Maintenance Code of New York State §304.18: security devices on dwelling-unit doors, windows and hatchways; deadbolts openable from inside, one-inch throw; sash locks on rented ground-level windows.
  • Code inspections at each rental are carried out locally, by whichever town, village or city the property sits in (Department of State memorandum LG03).
  • Town rental permits where the town requires them (Islip, Brookhaven and Babylon among them).
  • ETPA in sixteen Nassau localities for pre-1974 buildings of six or more units (HCR Fact Sheet #8).
  • Real Property Law §235-b: every lease carries an implied promise that the apartment is fit to live in.
  • Police alarm permits in Nassau and in the Suffolk County Police District.
  • Magnetic locks and electrified exit hardware release on power loss and fire alarm signal, with free egress from inside.
  • Fire alarm approvals sit with the Nassau County Fire Marshal or, in Suffolk, the town or village fire marshal; our own fire alarm work is limited to Suffolk.
Your Questions Answered

Apartment Building Security Systems on Long Island: Questions Answered

These are the questions Long Island owners ask us most, grouped by the part of the property they come up in. Each answer is short and specific; the sections above have the background.

Permits, rules and shortcuts through the property

We own buildings in both Nassau and Suffolk. Does each one need its own police alarm permit?

Yes, and in each county the permit goes to a different police department. Nassau County Police issue alarm permits through their alarm permit section, at $100 for a residence and $200 for a business, valid for two years. Suffolk County Police register alarms in their district through the Alarm Management Program, at $50 or $100, and buildings in towns outside that district, such as Riverhead or Southampton, are not covered by it. We list every building’s alarm and the office it registers with on the equipment list, so the owner can file each permit in the owner’s own name.

What does the state property maintenance code ask for on each apartment door and the ground-floor windows?

Section 304.18 asks for devices that secure the occupants on dwelling-unit doors, windows and hatchways. A deadbolt on a rented unit's door must open from inside without a key or special effort and throw at least one inch, and a sliding bolt does not qualify. Operable windows in a rented unit within six feet of the ground or a walkway need a sash lock. A keyed-both-sides deadbolt on an apartment door fails the first test, and it is one of the things we replace.

The garage at our new building opens for tenants’ cars. How do we stop people walking in behind them?

Treat the garage as an entrance, not a parking feature. The overhead door gets a reader or a long-range tag at the drive lane, a closing timer that brings it down as soon as the car clears, and a camera aimed at the opening so anyone ducking in on foot is recorded. The door from the garage into the building gets its own reader and closer, so a person who slips into the garage still cannot reach the halls. On station-area buildings, where the garage is often the busiest entrance at night, that inner door matters more than the overhead one.

Our village is on the ETPA list. Can the cost of a new entry system be passed on to stabilized tenants?

Only through HCR, not by the owner's own decision. HCR's fact sheet says owners of stabilized buildings may pass on certain improvement costs only with the agency's approval, under its rules for apartment and building-wide improvements. We give owners an itemized invoice with equipment and labor separated, which is the paperwork such an application starts from, but whether a particular entry system qualifies is a question for HCR and the owner's attorney.

People cut through our garden complex from the next street to reach the train. Should we fence, gate or just watch the lot?

Watching alone records the problem without stopping it. If the shortcut runs past building doors and parked cars, the owner usually starts with a fence and gate from a fencing contractor, and we add a reader or keypad to the pedestrian gate so tenants pass and others do not, plus a camera on the gate itself. Where a fence is not possible, cameras on the path and lighting from the owner’s electrician at least make the shortcut less inviting.

Garden complexes and spread-out properties

Our garden complex has twelve separate buildings. Does every entrance need its own intercom panel?

Not always. If visitors can reach every stairhall door from the parking lot, each building needs its own panel, or a simple call station, so a visitor can reach the right apartment. If the complex is fenced with one or two pedestrian gates, a single panel at each gate and fob readers on the building doors usually costs less and gives the owner one place to manage. We draw both layouts for the same property so the owner can compare them before choosing.

How do door and camera signals from the far buildings get back to one equipment room without trenching the lawn?

Through whatever path already exists, or through the air. Old conduit between buildings can usually take new cable or fiber, and a shared basement or attic run can carry cable inside. Where neither exists and the distance is beyond Cat6's 100-meter limit, a pair of point-to-point wireless radios mounted with a clear line of sight carries the network from building to building. Trenching is the last resort, not the first.

Packages are left at each building's outside door. Is a parcel locker or a package room better for a complex?

On a complex where every building has its own entrance and no lobby, one covered bank of parcel lockers near the office or the mailboxes is usually easier than a package room in each building, because couriers make one stop. A package room works better in a single larger building with an unused room off the vestibule. Either way, couriers get a code that opens only the lockers or that room, and a camera records the faces of the people who use it.

Our door alarm calls the police when tenants forget their codes. What stops the false alarms?

Usually the alarm is protecting doors that tenants are meant to use. The fix is to take those doors off the burglar alarm and put them on fob readers with a door-held warning, leaving the alarm on the doors nobody should open, such as the boiler room or the roof hatch. Entry delays get set to how long it really takes to reach a keypad. That cuts the false alarms that bring fees from the county police.

Weather, power and smaller rentals

After a storm, our entry panel came back on but the front door would not release. What happened?

Usually one of three things. The battery in the door power supply ran flat during the outage and has not recharged, a fuse or the transformer feeding the strike was damaged by the surge when power returned, or the controller came back up with its door schedule reset. A $250 service call finds which one it is. To keep it from happening again, the door power gets its own battery and a surge protector, and the equipment list shows when each battery was installed.

Our equipment sits in a basement that took water during Sandy. Where should it go now?

Up. We move recorders, switches, power supplies and batteries to a ground-floor or upper-floor closet where the layout allows, or at least onto a wall-mounted rack well above the basement floor. Cable that has to stay in the basement is run high and sealed where it enters boxes. The goal is that a flooded basement means a wet floor, not a dead entry system.

The accessory apartment in our house has its own outside door. Is a video doorbell enough security for it?

For one unit it often is, if the door itself is right. The tenant's door needs a deadbolt that meets the state code, a solid strike and a closer if it is used by more than the tenant. A wired video doorbell lets the tenant see and talk to visitors; a camera on the path to that door covers the approach. If the owner wants to know when the inside door between the two units is opened, a contact on it does that job.

Our garden buildings are on separate electric meters. Where should the shared security equipment get its power?

From one place the owner pays for and controls, normally the house or common meter that already feeds the hall lights and the laundry. The main equipment closet sits on that circuit, and each building’s small switch and door power supply is fed from a common-area outlet in that building, never from a tenant’s apartment. If a building has no common circuit, the owner’s electrician adds one; we then install the battery backup and the low-voltage side.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Apartment Building Security Systems on Long Island

Search summaries and chatbot answers about apartment security in New York are built mostly from New York City sources and national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Applied to a Long Island rental, they get several things wrong. These are the claims we hear most from owners, and what we find on the ground in Nassau and Suffolk.

1. “Every New York apartment building must have an intercom with a buzzer in each unit”

This one comes straight from New York City. The requirement for self-closing, self-locking entrance doors and a two-way intercom with a door release in each apartment is §50-a of the Multiple Dwelling Law, and the Attorney General's tenants' rights guide describes it in those terms.

But the Multiple Dwelling Law covers cities of 325,000 people or more. Nassau and Suffolk fall under the Multiple Residence Law, which applies to all smaller cities and to every town and village, and we have not found a matching intercom mandate there.

That does not make the intercom optional in practice. Tenants expect one, the warranty of habitability covers what a building provides, and a door that cannot be answered or released is a daily complaint. The point is to plan it on its merits, not because of a city rule that does not apply.

2. “A suburban landlord can protect a rental with the same kit a homeowner buys”

Rentals are 21 percent of all homes on Long Island by the Regional Plan Association's count, and many of them are not single houses. Garden complexes, apartments over stores, downtown walk-ups and new four- and five-story buildings beside the train stations all have shared entrances, shared laundry and shared mail.

A home kit assumes one household with one code, one app account and one front door. In a building, every tenant needs their own credential, the owner needs to remove a tenant without changing every lock, and the doors that tenants share need hardware that survives hundreds of openings a week.

A home kit is a fine answer for an owner-occupied house with one accessory apartment. For anything with a shared entrance, the plan has to start from the openings, not the kit.

3. “Rent stabilization is a New York City thing”

Not in Nassau. HCR's fact sheet on the Emergency Tenant Protection Act lists sixteen Nassau localities, including the Cities of Glen Cove and Long Beach, the Town of North Hempstead and Villages such as Freeport, Hempstead, Mineola and Rockville Centre, where pre-1974 buildings of six or more units can be stabilized.

Stabilized tenants are entitled to required services. An entry system or a front door lock that stops working during a botched changeover is a service problem, not just an inconvenience, and owners can only pass improvement costs on through the agency's own process.

So in those localities we schedule changeovers so nothing is ever off, keep the paperwork itemized, and suggest the owner confirm the building's status before the project starts. Suffolk has no locality on the list.

4. “Good Cause Eviction now applies to all rentals in New York”

Summaries of the 2024 law often skip the part that matters outside the city: it applies in New York City and only in other localities that opt in. The Attorney General's list as of April 2025 and a 2025 State Senate list of seventeen opt-in municipalities include none on Long Island.

The question has come up locally. Newsday reported that the Village of Hempstead's board voted 3–2 against the law in November 2025, and News 12 covered tenants rallying at Village Hall in December urging the board to adopt it.

For security work, the practical point is that an owner's obligations on doors and locks come from the maintenance code and the warranty of habitability, which apply either way. Status can change, so we cite the date of every list we rely on.

5. “Wireless is simpler for a spread-out complex”

Wi-Fi cameras and battery devices look easy on a property with buildings far apart, and national guides recommend them for exactly that reason. One r/longisland thread about home cameras includes a resident's comment that thieves in the area used jammers and the cameras did not record anything.

Consumer Wi-Fi depends on one router, its range and its signal, and a garden complex has brick walls, distance and dozens of tenants' own networks competing on the same channels. Battery devices need someone to charge them.

Our approach is wired inside each building, with a dedicated point-to-point link between buildings where cable cannot reach: a narrow, aimed radio link that carries the network, not dozens of separate Wi-Fi devices. It keeps working when a tenant's router is off and records locally if the internet drops.

6. “An alarm is just a device you plug in and register online”

On Long Island an alarm that calls the police is a permitted system. Nassau County Police issue alarm permits for residences and businesses, valid for two years, and say they are asked to respond to more than 125,000 false alarms a year. Suffolk's Alarm Management Program registers alarms and charges service fees after a set number of false alarms.

For a rental building the trouble is usually not the permit itself but whose name is on it and which doors are armed. Alarms placed on doors tenants use every day trigger false alarms, and the bills go to whoever registered the system.

We put the alarm on the doors nobody should open, put tenant doors on readers instead, and help the owner register the permit in the owner's own name.

7. “New buildings near the station come with security that runs itself”

The new station-area buildings in Mineola, Hicksville, Westbury and Ronkonkoma do open with modern video entry, fob readers and package rooms. Online descriptions of them make the systems sound finished.

What they rarely mention is everything an owner has to take over: administrator logins held by the developer's installer, platform contracts with a fee per door or per apartment, battery backup nobody has tested, and an equipment closet with no labels. PSEG Long Island's July 2026 storm update, with thousands of customers still without power the next morning, is a reminder of why the batteries matter.

We do the takeover for owners of new buildings: logins moved into the owner's name, fees listed, batteries tested and the closet documented. After that, the system runs as well as it is maintained, like any other.

Field notes: walking a garden complex for the first time

On a first visit to a garden complex we do not start in the office. We start at the far end of the lot and walk every building's entrance in turn, closing each door from a foot open to see whether it latches, and counting how many doors open with the same key. We note which buildings have a call button at the door and which have nothing, where the mailboxes are and where packages end up, and whether the laundry building is locked or propped. In each basement we look for the old intercom power supply, the alarm panel if there is one and the high-water mark if the basement has flooded. Back at the office we look for the router, the recorder if one exists and any conduit leaving the building. By the end, the owner has a sketch of the property with every opening marked, and the plan is written from that sketch.

Field notes: switching a building's entry without a lockout

On Long Island the hardest changeovers are on buildings with no super, because there is nobody on site to let a tenant in if something goes wrong. So we mount and power the new panel or reader beside the old one ahead of time, load every tenant from the owner's rent roll, and test the new door release from the panel and from two or three apartments before touching the old one. The switch itself is done in one short morning window, with the owner's contact reachable. Old keys keep working at the building door until the date the owner gives tenants, then the cylinder is changed or the door goes fob-only. Nobody is left outside on changeover day.

How long the work takes

A repair is usually done on the first visit, or on a second when a part has to be ordered. A new panel on one building with good wiring takes about a day on site once the equipment has arrived. A garden complex is done building by building over several weeks of on-site days, and links between buildings add time if conduit has to be cleared. Special-order equipment can add time we do not control, and we give a date only once it ships.

What moves the cost on a Long Island property

Seven things move the number on the Island. The number of buildings and entrances, because each entrance is its own set of hardware. The distance between buildings, which decides whether a link is cable in existing conduit, a pair of wireless radios or a trench. The condition of the old wiring in older buildings, tested before any quote is final. The doors and frames, since a strike cannot hold in a frame that has been pried. Exposure to weather and salt, which calls for stainless or sealed hardware on the shore. Platform fees, which can be nothing or a monthly charge per door or apartment for years. And travel within the Island: our published camera prices are higher in Suffolk than in Nassau, and that pattern carries through other work.

Comparing two quotes for the same property

Two Long Island quotes can differ by thousands of dollars and still both be honest, because they are often pricing different things. Check that both count the same entrances, readers and cameras, building by building. Check how each gets signals from the far buildings back to the office: a quote that assumes Wi-Fi from one router is not the same job as one with dedicated links. Check whether batteries and UPS units are included, whether the old equipment comes off the wall, whether monthly platform fees are listed, and whose name the logins end up in. A low total that leaves out the links or the batteries is not a low total.

Spreading the cost over several years

Most Long Island owners phase their apartment building security upgrades: the failing entrances and anything a town inspector or tenants have flagged first, then readers on side doors and outbuildings, then cameras and links, building by building. The written plan from the walk-through is ordered that way so each year's work fits into the next. Financing is available through Wisetack, subject to approval.

FAQ

Long Island Apartment Building Security Systems FAQ

Do you cover rental buildings in both Nassau and Suffolk, including the East End?
+

Yes. We work across Nassau County and Suffolk County, from the city line to the North and South Forks. Our published prices for some services are higher in Suffolk than in Nassau, and the walk-through for an East End property is scheduled with that travel in mind.

If we add fobs at a Nassau or Suffolk building, what tenant data should we be careful with?
+

The door logs and any photos the system stores. New York City's tenant data law does not apply on Long Island, but fob logs still show when each tenant comes and goes. We set the system so the owner chooses how long logs are kept and who can see them, and we suggest the owner's attorney write a short notice for tenants.

Can tenants who do not own smartphones still let visitors in?
+

Yes. An apartment station on the wall, or an entry panel that calls any phone number including a landline, lets those tenants see or hear a visitor and release the door. We often mix the two in one building: app answering for tenants who want it and wired stations or phone calls for those who do not.

Can the pool gate at a garden complex open only for tenants during pool season?
+

Yes. A reader or keypad on the pool gate, tied to the same tenant list as the building doors, unlocks it only for current tenants and only during the hours the owner sets for the season. The gate keeps its own self-closing hinges and latch; the reader only decides when it unlocks.

Do you install keypad locks on individual apartment doors?
+

We can, as long as the lock meets the state maintenance code: it must open from inside without a key or special knowledge, and a deadbolt must throw at least one inch. Many owners use them on vacant units between tenants, so showings and contractors do not need keys.

A tenant installed their own video doorbell at their apartment door. Can it stay?
+

That is the owner's decision under the lease, not ours. What we can tell the owner is whether the doorbell views common areas or other tenants' doors, whether it is wired to anything of the building's, and whether the building's own entry system would make it unnecessary.

Do you run power to light poles in the lot for cameras?
+

No. New high-voltage power is the building's electrician's work. When a pole or a wall in the lot already has power, or the electrician adds it, we mount the camera and run the low-voltage and network side, often powering the camera over its network cable instead.

Can the entry panel at a complex gate ring the leasing office instead of the apartments?
+

Yes, many panels can. A common setup has the panel at the main gate list the leasing office first during office hours and the tenant directory after hours, so prospective tenants and contractors reach staff while visitors still reach the apartment they came to see.

What does monitoring cost for a building's door alarms?
+

Our central-station monitoring is $30 a month for keypad-only systems and $45 a month with the phone app, as published on our Long Island alarm page. The police alarm permit is separate and is paid to the county police by the permit holder.

Who wires the fire alarm release on a Nassau building's magnetic lock?
+

We wire the lock side and the release input, and the building's fire alarm company of record makes the connection at the fire alarm panel, because Nassau fire alarm permits need a County Fire Marshal company number that we do not hold. In Suffolk we can take on the fire alarm work ourselves where it is in scope.
