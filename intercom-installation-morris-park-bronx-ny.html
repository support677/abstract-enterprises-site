The Neighborhood

Why Morris Park Properties Need Intercom Installation

Morris Park sits in the east Bronx, between Pelham Parkway and Neill Avenue to the north, Eastchester Road to the east, the Amtrak Northeast Corridor tracks and Sackett Avenue to the south and east, and Bronxdale Avenue or White Plains Road to the west, depending on whose map you use. Williamsbridge Road and Morris Park Avenue are the two streets everyone knows. The land was a racecourse built by John Albert Morris in 1889, and after the track burned it was cut into building lots. The city laid out streets, a streetcar began running along Morris Park Avenue in 1910, and house building carried on into the 1940s and after, with many of the early buyers Italian immigrant families. What went up was mainly houses. Wikipedia describes the housing as mostly one- and two-family homes with several apartment buildings, and the Department of City Planning's rezoning summary puts the small and medium-sized apartment buildings along Morris Park Avenue and Williamsbridge Road. That tells you the scale of an intercom here before anything else. Most jobs in Morris Park are not a hundred-button lobby console. They are a station for a house with two or three households, or a panel for a walk-up with a stair hall and a few dozen apartments.

The two halves of the neighborhood ask for different things. A local broker quoted on Homes.com puts it plainly: the north part, called Indian Village, has classic Tudors on more land, while the south side is more congested and has more two- and three-family homes. Listings across Morris Park describe legal two-family houses with additional finished living space in the basement and basements with their own separate entrance. Add the fact that roughly 65.8 percent of the occupied homes in Morris Park are rented, and the typical south-side picture is clear: one street door, a second door under the stoop or down the side, two or three households who are not related to each other, and an owner who may live downstairs or may live somewhere else entirely. A single bell and a chime in the front hall were never meant for that. They tell somebody that the door was pressed. They do not tell the right household, they do not let anyone upstairs see who is there, and they do not let a tenant open the street door without walking down to it.

The line between a house and a regulated building runs straight through the south side. New York's Multiple Dwelling Law treats a building occupied by not more than two families as a private dwelling and a building with three or more families living independently as a multiple dwelling. Summaries of the city's Housing Maintenance Code published by security firms describe the owner of a multiple dwelling as responsible for keeping a working door intercom or buzzer that lets tenants identify and admit visitors, and they describe HPD treating a non-working intercom as a Class B hazardous violation, enforced through inspections that often start with a tenant's 311 complaint. They also note that a system where some apartments work and others do not still counts as a violation. A two-family next door to a three-family can look almost identical from the sidewalk, but the owner of the three-family has an obligation the owner of the two-family does not. For the older walk-ups, a pre-1968 building of eight or more apartments falls under the intercom requirement once tenants in a majority of the apartments ask for one in writing, according to the same summaries. We explain the rules more carefully further down; the point here is that in Morris Park, the number of households behind a door decides as much about the job as the brand on the panel.

  • Houses first, apartment buildings second. Morris Park is mostly one- and two-family homes, with small and medium apartment buildings concentrated on Morris Park Avenue and Williamsbridge Road, according to Wikipedia and the Department of City Planning.
  • Two kinds of blocks. Detached Tudor-style houses with more land in Indian Village to the north; tighter blocks with more two- and three-family houses to the south, as a broker describes it on Homes.com.
  • Mostly renters. About 65.8 percent of occupied homes are rented, so many doors serve an owner and one or more tenant households, or only tenants.

Same-Day Repair in Morris Park

Need Repair in Morris Park? A Release That Hums but Won't Open, a Basement Unit Nobody Can Ring, or Half the Building Gone Quiet

Most repair calls from Morris Park fall into four groups. The first is the release that sounds but does not open. A tenant presses the button, the strike hums at the street door, and the visitor still cannot push the door open. In a walk-up that door has usually been slammed thousands of times, the frame has moved, the closer has weakened, and the latch now sits hard against the strike instead of inside it. The second is the household that was never connected. A basement apartment was finished years after the house was built, it has its own door under the stoop, and nobody ever ran a button or a station to it, so visitors for the basement ring upstairs or knock on the window. The third is the building where some apartments ring and others do not. In an older walk-up, one bad joint on the riser or one failed handset can silence every apartment above it, and in a building of three or more homes that partial failure is exactly the kind of thing that leads to an HPD violation. The fourth is the house that has outgrown a battery video doorbell. It works, but it rings only the owner's phone, and the tenant upstairs has no way to see or answer anyone at the shared door.

If the street door of a building is not latching, keep it locked by hand and tell the tenants until it is fixed, rather than leaving it on the latch. If an apartment handset is buzzing or crackling constantly, unplugging or disconnecting it can sometimes bring the rest of the building back, and that is worth knowing before a technician arrives. Then call (646) 490-0629. Many single faults, a worn strike, a drifted latch, a dead transformer, one shorted handset or one broken joint, are found and fixed on the same visit, and we tell you on the phone what the photos let us promise and what they do not. Service calls and extended tracing are billed at our $195-an-hour service rate with a three-hour minimum, and we give an honest estimate of the tracing time before starting. When an old system would cost a large share of a new one to keep alive, or its parts are no longer made, we put both choices in writing so you can decide without paying twice.

Intercom Installation for Morris Park's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for almost every intercom we plan in Morris Park. They differ in how many households sit behind the street door, how the cable can travel from the door to each of them, what the walls are made of, and which rules the building falls under. The cards give the short version; the sections after them explain what actually changes in the work.

The Indian Village Tudor

A detached brick house on the north side, many built around 1940, with an arched entry, a deep lot and one family or one family plus a relative.

The semi-detached brick two-family

Two apartments stacked over each other, sharing a stoop and street door, and sharing a party wall with the house next door.

The two-family with a basement door

A house with a finished basement that has its own entrance, so two doors and sometimes three households behind them.

The three-family

Three apartments on a south-side lot, which under state law is a multiple dwelling, with door and intercom duties that come with that.

The 1930s walk-up

A small brick apartment building near Williamsbridge Road or White Plains Road, with a stair hall, a vestibule and a riser that has seen several systems.

The store with apartments above

A building on Morris Park Avenue or Williamsbridge Road with a shop at street level and a residential door beside it.

The Indian Village Tudor: a short walk to the door, a long walk to the kitchen

An intercom fixes that by moving the answer point instead of the door. The outdoor station goes where the old button was, so visitors find it in the same place, and the indoor station goes in the room that is actually used. Because the walls are brick and the inside walls are often plaster, we look hard for the existing bell wire first: if it tests well, a two-wire video system can run over it to the first indoor station, and a short new run continues to the kitchen through the cellar ceiling and up through the floor. The arched entries need a little care. Many of them put the door in a shallow recess, and a camera mounted flush on the face of the brick can end up looking past the visitor rather than at them. We hold the station up in the spot before drilling, check the picture with someone standing where a visitor stands, and use a mounting plate or a slight angle where the recess calls for it. In a one-family house there is often no need for a release at all, because whoever answers will walk to the door anyway; the point is to know who is there first.

The semi-detached two-family: one stoop, two households, a wall shared with the neighbor

Listings across the south side describe semi-attached brick two-families, and they are the backbone of the neighborhood's rental housing. Two apartments sit one above the other, the owner often lives in one and rents the other, and both share a stoop and a street door. The intercom job is straightforward to describe and easy to get wrong. Each apartment needs its own button, labeled by apartment and not by family name, and its own indoor station. Each apartment should be able to see and talk to the person at the shared door and release it. And neither apartment's station should ever be able to open the other apartment's own door, which keeps its key lock and is never touched by the intercom.

Cable is the part that takes judgment. The upper apartment's station needs a run from the street door up one floor, and the obvious path, straight up through the hall ceiling, often means opening plaster. We usually look for a better route first: up through the cellar and an interior wall, alongside an existing pipe chase, or behind the stair stringer. The shared party wall with the house next door is not a path we use. It belongs to two owners, and anything fixed into it needs the neighbor's agreement. Where the old two-button bell still works and its wire tests well, a two-wire system can sometimes reuse both pairs, which keeps the job to a couple of short new runs.

The two-family with a basement door: the household that gets forgotten

The Morris Park listings that mention additional finished space in the basement, or a basement with a separate entrance, describe a house that has quietly grown another household or another room that visitors come to. Sometimes it is a grown child or an aging parent. Sometimes it is a tenant. Whatever the arrangement, the basement door usually sits under the stoop or down a side path, the original bell plan never reached it, and the people living there hear about their visitors when someone upstairs comes down to tell them.

We plan that door as its own entrance. It gets an outdoor station with a clear label, its own indoor station in the basement, and a release only if it needs one. If the basement is lived in by family, the stations can be set so either level can answer the other's door and call between floors. If it is rented, the basement station rings only for the basement door and opens only that door. Two practical things matter more than the model. The station at a door under a stoop sits in shade most of the day, so the camera needs to handle low light, and the door itself often swells in damp weather, so we check that it closes and latches reliably before anyone fits a strike to it. Whether a basement apartment is legal is a question for the owner and the city, not for us; we install for the doors and households that are there and say plainly what we can see.

The three-family: where the house becomes a regulated building

A Morris Park three-family looks like its two-family neighbors from the street, but under the Multiple Dwelling Law three households living independently make it a multiple dwelling. That changes what the owner is expected to keep working. HPD's ABCs of Housing says that in every type of multiple dwelling the building entrance door and the apartment entrance doors must be self-closing, and the summaries of the Housing Maintenance Code we cite above describe the owner's duty to maintain an intercom or buzzer that lets tenants identify and admit visitors. In practice that means every apartment needs a working button, a station that can talk to the door, and a release that opens the street door and nothing else, and it means the street door has to close and lock by itself after every visitor.

On a three-family we plan a station with one button per apartment, an indoor station in each apartment, and a strike on the shared door fitted so the door still catches every time the release stops. If the owner lives on the premises, we often add a feature that matters to owners more than tenants: a simple way to see whether the system is working without knocking on doors, such as a test call from each apartment during the handover and a written list of which button goes where. A three-family with a basement door as well has two entrances to cover, and both need the same care.

The 1930s walk-up: one riser, several generations of equipment

For a walk-up the first decision is whether the riser can carry a new system. We trace and test every pair, find the joints in the cellar and on each floor, and decide run by run what stays and what is replaced. If the old cable is sound, a two-wire system can use it and the stair hall walls stay closed. If it is not, new cable usually goes up the stair hall in a slim raceway painted to match, or inside an existing chase if the building has one. The second decision is which door gets the strike. In a building with an outer street door and an inner hall door, the one that locks is the one that releases, and we confirm which that is before ordering hardware. The third is the handsets: replacing the apartment stations means getting into every apartment, so we plan the visits floor by floor with the owner and the tenants.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Count the Mailboxes Before You Count the Wires

Start at the mailboxes and the meters. On a south-side house the technician counts the mailboxes by the door, the electric meters in the cellar and the buttons on the old plate, and compares all three with what the owner says. When they do not agree, there is usually a household nobody mentioned on the phone, often in the basement.

Walk up the way a visitor would. Stand on the sidewalk, read the labels, press a button and see who hears it. Then walk to the basement door and do the same. Most of the plan comes from that short walk.

Find where the old power lives. The transformer is usually in the cellar near the meters, sometimes on a joist, sometimes behind a panel under the stair. Every splice between it and the door gets noted. A wire that ends cut off cleanly is often where a renovation stopped.

Let the door go from wide open. Every door that will get a strike is opened fully, released and watched. If it stops short, bounces or needs a push, the closer and latch get fixed first.

Ring every apartment from the street. Before anything comes down, each existing button is pressed while someone listens in the apartment. What works, what does not and what rings the wrong place all go on paper.

Brick, Plaster and Old Pipe

Brick Fronts, Plaster Walls and Risers Older Than the Tenants: How Cable Reaches Each Apartment in Morris Park

Most of what decides whether a Morris Park intercom looks tidy and keeps working happens out of sight, in the cellar, inside the walls and in the stair hall. Houses built from the 1920s through the 1940s were built in brick and plaster, and the walk-ups have had their wiring changed more times than anyone can document. This section explains what the survey looks at and what we do about each finding, so the quote makes sense when you read it.

Getting through the brick. The station at the street door has to be fed through the front wall, and in Morris Park that wall is usually brick, sometimes with limestone trim around the door. Where an old button already sits in a hole, the new cable uses that hole and the station covers it. Where a new hole is needed, we drill into a mortar joint rather than the face of a brick, because mortar can be repointed and a cracked brick cannot be uncracked. Where cable has to pass through a foundation or cellar wall, it goes through one clean, sleeved hole that is sealed afterward against water and pests. The outdoor station is fixed with stainless hardware into solid masonry, on a mounting plate where the surface is rough, and the cable entry behind it is sealed so rain driven against the front of the house does not run into the wall.

Working with plaster. Plaster on wood lath does not like being cut, and patching it well takes more time than most people expect. So we plan routes that avoid it. In a two-family, cable for the upper apartment often goes up from the cellar inside an interior wall cavity, alongside a pipe, or behind the stair. In a detached house, the cellar ceiling usually gives an open path from the front of the house to the back, and the cable comes up into the kitchen through the floor or a baseboard. Where a cut in plaster cannot be avoided, it is small, planned, and the quote says who patches it.

Testing what is already there. Old bell wire and old intercom cable can often carry a modern two-wire system, but only if the copper is continuous, the insulation is not leaking between conductors, and the run is not so long and thin that the signal and power fade at the far end. In a walk-up, where several systems may have been spliced together on one riser, the joints are usually the weak point. We test every pair we might reuse, open the junction boxes we can find, and write the result on the quote. Runs that pass can stay. Runs that fail, or that have been patched so many times nobody trusts them, are replaced.

Old metal pipe in the walk-ups. Some older buildings carried their bell and intercom wiring in metal pipe. If that pipe is continuous, dry and not shared with power wiring, it can sometimes be cleaned out and used to pull new cable to each floor, which saves opening the stair hall. If it is crushed, rusted shut, or shared with electrical conductors, we leave it alone and find another path. Low-voltage intercom cable does not belong in a pipe that also carries line-voltage power.

Where the power goes. The intercom's power supply should sit on its own labeled circuit, in a dry place that the owner can reach without moving the tenants' storage, usually near the meters or the main panel in the cellar. It should be sized for the stations and the strike together, because a supply that is fine until someone presses release is a common cause of a door that hums but will not open. Any new circuit for it is work for a licensed electrician, which we coordinate. If the system uses networked stations powered over their cable, the switch that powers them goes in the same spot, with a small battery backup if the owner wants the door to keep working through a short outage.

Leaving a record. When we finish, the owner gets a written record of which button goes to which apartment, where every junction box is, where the power supply is and which breaker feeds it, and which runs are new and which were kept. In a neighborhood where houses are sold, inherited and rented again, that sheet is often the most useful thing we leave behind.

The Rules at the Street Door

Two Families or Three: Where New York's Intercom Rules Start for a Morris Park Building

Because Morris Park has so many two- and three-family houses side by side, owners here are more likely than most to be unsure whether the intercom rules reach their building. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing on this page is legal advice. What follows is what the public sources say, so you can ask the right questions of your attorney, your accountant or HPD.

One- and two-family houses. The Multiple Dwelling Law treats a building occupied by not more than two families as a private dwelling. The best-known intercom duties are written for multiple dwellings, and we have found nothing that extends them to a one- or two-family house. In Indian Village and on most two-family blocks, then, an intercom is the owner's choice. That said, if a tenant in a two-family depends on the door system to let visitors in, keeping it working is part of keeping the apartment in good order, and it is the kind of thing worth writing into the lease.

Three or more families. Three households living independently make a building a multiple dwelling under the same law. Summaries of the Housing Maintenance Code published by security firms describe the owner's duty to keep a door intercom or buzzer working so tenants can identify and admit visitors, and they describe HPD classing a non-working intercom as a Class B hazardous violation, with inspections often triggered by tenant complaints through 311. They also say a system where some apartments work and others do not is still a violation. HPD's own ABCs of Housing tells building managers, superintendents and owners to repair broken locks or intercoms immediately.

Older walk-ups. Habitat magazine describes the state's rule that buildings built or converted after 1968 with eight or more apartments must have a two-way voice intercom from the front door to each apartment. Parker Custom Security's summary adds that a building put up before 1968 falls under the same requirement once tenants occupying a majority of the apartments ask for it in writing, and that New York courts have treated a working intercom as an essential service under the warranty of habitability in Real Property Law §235-b. Many of the walk-ups near Williamsbridge Road date from before 1968, so the tenants' written request is often the point where the requirement begins.

Self-closing doors. HPD's ABCs of Housing says building entrance doors and apartment entrance doors in every type of multiple dwelling must be self-closing. When a strike goes on the street door of a three-family or a walk-up, the door has to close and lock on its own every time the release ends. We fit the strike to where the latch lands and adjust the closer so the door does not stop short or bounce.

Getting out. Free egress, the ability to leave a building from the inside without a key, a code or a credential, is described in the same summaries as a life-safety requirement that no access arrangement may override. Every system we install controls the way in only. A door with an electric strike still opens from the inside by its normal handle.

Mounting heights. The Department of Buildings rule 1 RCNY 42-01 says no push button may be more than six feet from the floor and the speaking and listening device must be between four and five feet from the floor. Many Morris Park street doors sit at the top of a stoop and many basement doors sit below the sidewalk, so we measure from where the visitor actually stands. For offices and practices open to the public, ADA reach rules for operable parts apply as well.

Compliance notes for business and community doors in Morris Park

  • Free exit, always. Any door with an electric strike opens from the inside without a key, code or button. The intercom controls entry only.
  • Reachable controls. 1 RCNY 42-01 caps push buttons at six feet and places the speaking device between four and five feet. Offices and practices open to the public also follow ADA reach rules, which usually puts buttons lower.
  • Self-closing shared doors. Where a business shares a street door with apartments in a multiple dwelling, the door must close and lock by itself, and a strike has to preserve that.
  • Separate power for separate tenants. The apartments' intercom power is kept off the store's circuits, so a shop's rewiring or a change of tenant does not knock out the residents.
  • Life-safety devices clear. Stations and cable never block an exit sign, a fire alarm device or the swing of an exit door, and low-voltage cable never shares a pathway with power wiring.
  • Owner approval first. In a rented office or storefront, the building owner's written approval comes before any new hole in the facade or frame.
Your Questions Answered

Intercom Installation in Morris Park: Questions Answered

These are the situations Morris Park owners, landlords and tenants describe when they call: houses on blocks that were rezoned, three-families changing hands, first-time landlords, walk-ups with wire in old pipe, buildings next to construction, break-ins that started past the front door, and offices and stores that share their entrance with apartments. Each is answered once here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Houses on the north and south blocks

Our house is on one of the blocks rezoned to R4A in 2005. Does that zoning say anything about where a door station or camera can go on the front of the house?

Not in any way we have found. The R4A district the city mapped on the core Morris Park blocks controls what kind of building can be built and how big it can be: detached one- and two-family homes, with a perimeter wall no higher than 21 feet. A door station, a camera beside the door and an electric strike do not change the size or use of the house, so the zoning is not what decides their placement. What does decide it is the city's rule on intercom heights, which keeps buttons no higher than six feet and the speaking device between four and five feet above where the visitor stands, the shape of the entry, and where the camera gets a clear view of the steps. We also found no historic district over Morris Park, so the Landmarks Commission's rules on facades do not usually come into it. If your house is being enlarged or rebuilt under the zoning, that is a good time to pull cable for the station while the walls are open, but the zoning itself does not limit the intercom.

We are about to close on a three-family on one of the south-side blocks. What should we look at on the intercom before the inspection is over?

Ring every apartment from the street, with someone in each apartment, and have each apartment release the street door while you watch it open and then close and lock by itself. That one test tells you most of what matters, because in a building of three homes the owner is expected to keep the intercom working for every apartment, and a system where one apartment is silent is still treated as a violation in the published summaries of the Housing Maintenance Code. Then look at the details. Count the buttons and compare them with the apartments; an extra button often means a basement household or an old unit that was combined. Find the transformer or power supply and see whether it has its own circuit. Check that the street door is self-closing, because HPD's guidance requires that in multiple dwellings. Ask the seller for any manual, installer name or programming code. If you send us photos before closing, we can tell you whether what you are buying looks like a repair, a partial replacement or a new system, which can matter when you negotiate.

We are renting out the upstairs of our Morris Park two-family for the first time. What should the lease and the move-in day say about the intercom?

A two-family is a private dwelling under the Multiple Dwelling Law, so the intercom duties written for multiple dwellings do not apply in the same way. That makes it more important, not less, to agree on it in writing. We suggest the lease say plainly that the upstairs apartment has its own button and station, that the tenant can see and talk to the street door and release it, and who to tell if it stops working. On move-in day, ring the tenant's station from the street with the tenant inside, show them the release, and show them how to set the ring volume. If the system uses an app, the tenant gets their own account on it, separate from the owner's, so the owner is not handing over the administrator login or the house Wi-Fi password. Keep the wiring record we leave somewhere the tenant can find a phone number if you are away. And if you ever turn the basement into a third household, talk to us and to your attorney first, because three households is where the rules for the building change.

We live on the first floor of our three-family and handle the building ourselves. Should every apartment be able to release the street door, or only ours?

Every apartment. In a building of three homes, the summaries of the Housing Maintenance Code we cite on this page describe the owner's duty as keeping a system that lets tenants identify and admit their own visitors, and a release that works only from the owner's apartment does not do that. Owners sometimes want a single release for good reasons, such as worrying about tenants buzzing in strangers, but the better answer is a video station in each apartment, so each tenant can see who they are letting in. What the owner can keep is control of the system: the administrator login, the ability to add or remove an app account when a tenant moves, and the only key to the power supply in the cellar. Each tenant's release should open the street door and nothing else. It should never open another apartment's door or the cellar. And the street door should close and lock behind every visitor on its own, so a tenant's decision to let someone in does not leave the door open for whoever comes next.

Walk-ups near Williamsbridge Road and White Plains Road

The buzzer wire in our 1930s walk-up runs up the stair hall inside old metal pipe. Can the new cable be pulled through that pipe instead of opening the walls?

Sometimes, and it is worth checking before anyone decides to cut plaster. If the pipe runs continuously from the cellar to each floor, is dry inside, is not crushed at a bend, and carries only the old low-voltage wires, we can often pull the old wire out and use it to draw the new cable through, floor by floor. That keeps the stair hall closed and the job quicker. Three things stop us. If the pipe also carries electrical power wiring, the intercom cable does not go in it, because low-voltage and line-voltage conductors should not share a pathway. If the pipe is rusted shut or the old wire will not move, forcing it can tear the wall behind it. And if the pipe only reaches some floors, we would need a separate route for the rest anyway. The survey answers this by tracing each run from the cellar and testing whether the old wire pulls freely. If the pipe will not work, a slim painted raceway up the stair hall or an existing chase is usually the next choice, and the quote says which.

After the burglaries inside the building on Tomlinson Avenue, our tenants asked whether a better intercom would have helped. What can a door system do once someone is already past the lobby?

Less than people hope, and it is better to say so. News 12's May 2026 report described two apartments burglarized inside a Tomlinson Avenue building, with a suspect believed to have picked the apartment locks, and a neighbor's doorbell camera catching him in the hall. An intercom's job ends at the street door. It lets a tenant see and talk to a visitor before opening that door, and it keeps the door locked otherwise. It does not protect an apartment door once someone is inside the building; that is the job of the apartment's own lock, and in some buildings of cameras in the halls. What a better system can add is a clearer picture of who was let in and when. Some video stations keep a short clip of each call, and if the street door also has access control, there is a record of which code or fob opened it. That will not stop a determined burglar, but it can tell the owner and the police how someone got in, and it discourages tenants from releasing the door for people they cannot see.

A sidewalk shed is going up in front of our building for work next door. Will our video station still show visitors' faces under it?

Often not as well as before, and it is easy to fix. A shed turns the area in front of the door into a dim tunnel, and a camera that was set for open daylight can end up showing a bright strip of sidewalk and a dark shape where the visitor's face should be. Most current video stations have settings for backlight and low light that help, and many have a small light of their own near the lens. If those are not enough, a light fixed under the shed near the door, which the contractor building the shed may be required to provide anyway, makes the biggest difference. The other thing to watch is dust and vibration. Demolition and excavation next door put a film of dust on the station's lens and can shake a strike or closer out of adjustment, so wipe the lens gently with a soft cloth and test the door release every week while the work goes on. With construction in the rezoned blocks and along Sackett Avenue expected for some time, these are worth knowing even if your building is a street away.

Getting in safely and doing business

News 12 reported a gunman following a Morris Park couple up their front steps and into their home. In a two-family where we come and go with keys, what can an intercom change about that moment?

It cannot stop someone who walks in behind you, and nobody should sell it as if it could. What it can change is how much you know before you put your key in the door, and how quickly the door is shut behind you. With a video station that has phone or app viewing, you can look at the steps and the sidewalk from the car or the corner before walking up, which is most useful late at night. A camera aimed at the stoop rather than across the street gives a clear picture of anyone who approaches. A strong closer on the street door, adjusted so the door shuts and latches quickly rather than drifting, closes the gap someone could use. Good lighting at the door helps both the camera and the person coming home. And in a two-family, each household having its own station means a tenant can check the door before going down to open it, rather than opening it to see who knocked. Those are modest improvements, but they are the ones a door system honestly offers.

Our practice is on Williamsbridge Road and we want a video station at the door. Does recording patients as they arrive raise any privacy concern?

It is a fair question to ask before buying, and the answer depends more on settings than on hardware. A video door station does not have to record anything. Many models show the picture only while someone is ringing or being answered and store nothing. Others keep a short clip of each call on the station, on a recorder in the office, or on the maker's servers. For a medical or other professional office, we suggest starting with the least that works: a live picture for the front desk to see who is at the door, recording turned off or kept short, any stored clips held locally rather than in the cloud, and the camera aimed at the doorstep rather than at the waiting room or the sidewalk. We are not your compliance adviser, and if your practice has one, they should approve the settings. What we can do is show you every option the station has, set it the way you choose, and write down what is and is not stored, so your policy and the equipment match.

Our building on Morris Park Avenue has a store downstairs, and the store's alarm company wants to put its keypad right beside the residential door station. Is that a problem?

It can be, for practical reasons more than legal ones. Two panels side by side confuse people. Residents press the alarm keypad by mistake, visitors try the keypad looking for an apartment, and anyone watching the door learns which one controls the store. If the keypad has to be at that entrance, we suggest it sit inside the store's own door rather than at the shared street door, and that the residential station have a clear label so nobody mistakes it for the store. The wiring matters too. The apartments' intercom should run on its own power supply and its own cable route, not tied into the store's alarm panel or its circuits, so that an alarm service visit, a power cut on the store's panel or a change of tenant downstairs does not take the residents' door system with it. If the store wants a bell or a call for its own deliveries, it can have one, but on its own button, ringing the store, and never releasing the residents' door.

FAQ

Morris Park Intercom Installation FAQ

Why doesn't this page give one installed price for a Morris Park intercom?
+

Because the buildings here vary too much for one figure to be honest. A detached house with one family and good bell wire is a small job. A three-family with a basement door, plaster walls and a riser that needs rebuilding is a much larger one. We quote after a survey, list every station, cable run and piece of door hardware separately, and show how the total is built so it can be compared line by line.

In a walk-up, which part of an intercom job usually takes the biggest share of the cost?
+

Usually the apartment stations and the labor of getting cable to them, rather than the street panel. Every apartment needs a station and a visit, and if the old riser fails testing, new cable has to reach every floor. A building whose existing pairs pass testing can often keep that part of the cost down. The survey tells us which situation your building is in before we quote.

Can work on one apartment's station stop the other apartments from ringing?
+

On an old shared riser it can, if the work is careless, because one disturbed joint can affect every apartment beyond it. That is why we test every apartment before we touch anything and again before we leave, and why we note the result for each one. If an apartment that worked when we arrived does not work when we finish, fixing it is our job, not a new charge.

Does the Metro-North station-area rezoning change what an existing Morris Park house needs at its door?
+

Not directly. The rezoning changes what can be built on certain blocks near the planned stations, and the new buildings there will need their own entry systems. An existing house keeps whatever rules applied to it before, based on how many households live in it. What you may notice is construction nearby, which is a reason to check your station's picture and your door's latch more often while the work goes on.

Will the street door have to stand open for a long time on a cold day while the new station goes in?
+

No. Most of the outdoor work, drilling the cable path, fixing the mounting plate and connecting the station, happens with the door closed. The door only has to be held open for short periods while a strike is fitted into the frame, and we keep those as brief as we can. In a building with tenants, the door is never left propped and unattended, and it is locked by hand whenever the strike is out.

Can an indoor station go on a plaster-and-lath wall in a 1920s or 1930s house without cracking it?
+

Yes, with the right method. We drill slowly with sharp bits, use anchors meant for plaster or find the lath and studs behind it, and keep the holes small. Where the cable has to enter the wall, we prefer to come up from the cellar or along a baseboard rather than cutting a long channel. If any patching is needed, the quote says so and says who does it.

Can our installation be scheduled around the Columbus Day Parade, when Williamsbridge Road and Morris Park Avenue fill up?
+

Yes. We avoid booking work on or near the parade route on parade day, because closed streets and crowds make it hard to bring in ladders and materials and hard on tenants trying to get in and out. If your building is on the route, we will suggest a day the week before or after. The same applies to other busy days the owner tells us about.

Can we have video at the street door without anything being stored on a company's servers?
+

Yes. Many systems work entirely inside the building, with the picture shown only on the indoor stations and nothing sent to the internet. If you want phone answering, some makers route it through their servers and some can be set to store nothing there. Tell us how much you want kept and where, and we will recommend models that match and set them that way.

Older houses can have lead in their old paint layers. How do you keep dust down when drilling in a Morris Park house?
+

We keep holes few and small, drill with a vacuum attachment or a catch cup held under the bit, lay drop cloths below every place we work, and clean up with a vacuum rather than sweeping. If you have a lead test for the house or there are young children living there, tell us before the visit so we can plan the work around the areas that matter most.

Will the new intercom use the old doorbell transformer in our cellar?
+

Usually not. Old bell transformers were sized for a chime, and most current intercoms need their own power supply sized for the stations and the strike. The old transformer is disconnected and removed or properly abandoned, so it is not left live behind a wall. The new supply goes on a labeled circuit, and any new circuit it needs is done by a licensed electrician.

Crime-map websites rate Morris Park quite differently. Should those ratings decide whether we choose video?
+

We would not let them decide it. One aggregator calls Morris Park about as safe as the average Bronx neighborhood and another gives it an A-, and neither says anything about your door. Video earns its place when tenants need to see who they are letting in, when nobody can see the steps from inside, or when packages go missing. Those are questions about your building, and the survey answers them.
