The Neighborhood

Why Ditmas Park Properties Need Access Control

Ditmas Park is one of the few parts of Brooklyn where the typical front door is not on the sidewalk. The houses that made the neighborhood famous were built as a suburb inside the city: freestanding Queen Anne, Colonial Revival and Shingle Style houses set back from the street, each with a porch between the steps and the door. The Landmarks Preservation Commission protected the core of it as the Ditmas Park Historic District in 1981. In November 2025 it voted unanimously to add two more districts next door, according to Brownstoner: Beverley Square West, 118 houses on three blocks between Beverley Road and Cortelyou Road built from 1894 to 1910, and Ditmas Park West, 127 houses just west of the original district, mostly built between 1902 and 1910, nearly half of them designed by the architect Arlington D. Isham. For an access control job that changes two things at once. The door is set back behind a porch, so the person at the door is out of sight of the street, and the wall around it is often protected, so where a keypad or station goes is a permit question, not only a wiring question.

The second thing to know is that many of those big houses no longer hold one family. Brownstoner’s 2019 listing for 551 Rugby Road described it as “one of three units in what was originally a two-family Edwardian home,” fronted by “two tiers of columned porches.” Houses divided into condos, owner-plus-tenant arrangements and informal splits all share the same problem: one front door, several households, and a key that has been copied for each of them and for everyone they ever trusted. Community Board 14’s statement of needs for fiscal year 2027 says that more than 48 percent of the district’s area is one- and two-family homes and asks for “more DOB action on illegal conversions in what would be single family homes.” We do not install anything that turns a house into more apartments than it is allowed to have, but we are asked again and again to give the households already living in a legal two- or three-unit house their own codes on a shared door.

East of the houses the neighborhood changes character entirely. Ocean Avenue and the streets near it are lined with six-story apartment buildings from the 1920s to the 1950s. Openigloo’s records give 770 Ocean Avenue 41 units on six floors, built in 1923 and rent-stabilized; 1250 Ocean Avenue 106 units on six floors, built in 1953; and 570 Westminster Road 185 units on six floors, built in 1935, where one tenant review lists “Buzzers and intercoms don’t work” and another praises a “24 hour door person.” Smaller walk-ups sit on the side streets: 377 Argyle Road has 16 units on four floors from 1925, and 799 Ocean Avenue has eight units on five floors from 1899. These buildings live and die by their lobby door, and the people who complain most loudly about it are the residents waiting for a package.

Package theft is the issue that has followed Ditmas Park lobbies for years. Bklyner reported in November 2018 that a building near Cortelyou Road and Stratford Road had logged more than 100 missing packages in six months, kept in a resident’s spreadsheet, with parcels “left downstairs by the mailboxes or in the lobby”; dummy packages put out as a test disappeared the next day, and management added a camera to the mail area only after residents pushed. A Kensington resident quoted in the same story said that “about 40% of the time the lock on the front door is broken.” The issue turned tragic in November 2025, when Patch reported that a 41-year-old building superintendent on Ocean Avenue in Ditmas Park died after a dispute with a man accused of stealing the building’s packages. A camera records a theft. A street door that relocks every time, a vestibule that a courier can enter without reaching the mailboxes, and a package room that opens only for the people it belongs to are what keep the parcels where they were left.

The houses have their own history with break-ins, and it is worth reading closely because it shows which doors to think about. In June 2012, residents at the 70th Precinct Community Council described a run of daytime burglaries: four on the block of Westminster Road between Beverley and Cortelyou, one mid-morning on DeKoven near Rugby Road, and attempted break-ins nearby including one on Argyle Road, Bklyner reported; in South Midwood every one of four burglaries that month came through an unlocked first-floor window. A year later Patch carried the precinct’s advice that “the front or back door is the most common point of entry for a burglar.” Those are old reports and we do not quote them as today’s crime rate. What they show is the pattern a big freestanding house invites: a side door by the driveway, a back door off the kitchen and a porch hidden from the street, all used by different people at different hours, and the one that matters is the one nobody remembered to lock.

Last, the building stock is getting older and the paperwork is getting heavier. Community Board 14 counts 879 housing code violations per 1,000 units in 2024, up from 667 in 2019 and 506 in 2010, and writes that “only 20% of renter occupied homes are adequately maintained by landlords.” At the same time, the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law now governs how fob, app and other smart-access systems may collect and keep tenants’ data in multiple dwellings. A Ditmas Park owner who replaces keys with fobs today is taking on a small legal process as well as a door upgrade, and we plan the job so the paperwork is ready on the day the fobs go out.

Freestanding Victorian houses

Queen Anne, Colonial Revival and Shingle Style houses on wide lots, with a wood porch, a front door with glass panels, a side door by the driveway and often a detached garage at the back. Usually one controlled front door plus a keypad at the side door, with the controller in the cellar.

Houses in the landmark districts

Houses inside the 1981 Ditmas Park district and the two districts added in 2025. Same hardware as any house, but the station’s size, finish and location are chosen to fit the Landmarks guidebook, and nothing is fixed through cast iron or decorative trim.

Houses divided into two or three units

Big houses that now hold separate owners or tenants, like the Rugby Road two-family turned into three condos. A shared front door with one call button and separate codes per household, and sometimes a second controlled door for an upstairs unit reached by its own stair.

Six-story elevator buildings

Pre-war and post-war buildings on Ocean Avenue and nearby streets, from about 40 to nearly 200 apartments. Fob and phone entry on the street and vestibule doors, a package room, cellar doors on schedules, and a video intercom that calls tenants.

Small walk-ups

Four- and five-story walk-ups of 8 to 20 units on the side streets, often with an aging buzzer panel. A reader and strike on the street door wired alongside the intercom, so residents keep buzzing guests in while keys are retired.

Mixed-use buildings on the corridors

Two- and three-story buildings on Cortelyou Road, Newkirk Avenue and Coney Island Avenue with a shop at street level and apartments above. The shop door and the residential street door run on separate schedules and separate administrators on one controller.

What the materials mean for the install

Wood is the rule here, and it behaves differently from the brick fronts of rowhouse Brooklyn. A Victorian front door and its jamb were made from solid lumber that has been painted many times and has moved with a century of seasons. Before a strike goes in we check that the jamb is sound where the strike body will sit, that the door has not sagged so far that the latch rides low, and that the porch floor has not shifted the frame. Where the jamb has been bored out by earlier locks we fill and reinforce it first, because a strike in soft or split wood will fail long before the electronics do. On glass-paneled doors we make sure any exit device or lever stays usable from inside and that the cable path does not cross the glass.

The porch is both a help and a hazard. It keeps rain off the station, which is good for keypads and cameras, but it also hides the door from the street and gathers moisture at the joint between the porch ceiling and the house wall. We mount stations on a flat, solid section of wall or trim close to the door opening, seal every penetration, and bring cable up from the cellar inside the wall wherever the framing allows, rather than stapling it along the porch. On apartment buildings the problem is the opposite: steel or aluminum frames that are hard to cut and doors that slam hundreds of times a day. There we fit heavy-duty strikes rated for the door, replace tired closers before anything else, and add a door contact so the super knows when the street door is propped.

Behind the Porch: Planning Doors on a Freestanding Victorian House

A freestanding Ditmas Park house has more ways in than most Brooklyn homes, and that is where planning starts. There is the front door behind the porch, which guests and deliveries use. There is usually a side door near the driveway, which the family uses most days because it is closer to the car and the kitchen. There may be a rear door to the yard, a cellar door under the back steps, and a garage at the end of the drive. Each has a different set of people who need it, and each is locked, or not locked, by a different habit. An access system is mostly a way of replacing those habits with doors that lock themselves.

We usually recommend controlling two doors first, not one. The front door gets a small video station on the porch so a visitor can be seen and answered from a phone, with fobs or a code for the household. The side door gets a keypad, because it is the door the dog walker, the home aide, the cleaner and the teenager actually use. Both run from one controller with a battery on a cellar wall, so the system keeps working through an outage, and each person gets their own code that can be switched off without changing anyone else’s. The rear and cellar doors can be added later on the same controller, often with only a door contact at first, so you know when they open.

The cellar makes the wiring easier than people expect. Most of these houses have a full cellar under the front hall, so cable can run along the joists to a point directly below the front door and up inside the wall to the jamb, with nothing exposed on the porch. Where the porch floor or a finished front room is in the way, we look for an existing chase, such as the path of an old doorbell wire, before we suggest anything visible. And if a garage at the back needs a keypad too, we decide on the visit whether a buried cable, a run along the fence or a wireless reader is the sensible choice for that distance.

The landmark question, answered before the drill comes out

If the house is inside one of the historic districts, a station on the front of the house is work the Landmarks Preservation Commission regulates. Its Permit Guidebook says intercom panels and doorbells “must be installed within or adjacent to a door opening,” that a panel may go on a fence or wall in front of the entranceway, that “a maximum of two light fixtures and one intercom panel can be installed per door opening,” and that the design, size and finish must be “compatible with the architecture of the building.” It also says commission staff can issue these permits when the criteria are met, asks for the manufacturer’s cut sheets, keeps visible conduit to about ten inches painted to match, and says equipment “must not be attached through cast iron or on any decorative surfaces.” We write the station choice, its finish and its mounting location into the door plan so the owner has what the application needs, and we do not start mounting on the facade until the owner tells us the permit is in hand.

The good news is that most houses need very little on the outside. A strike is hidden inside the jamb, the controller lives in the cellar, and the only visible device is one small station or keypad on a plain section of wall or trim beside the door. A side door at the driveway is often less visible from the street than the front door, and many owners choose to put the keypad there and keep the porch looking exactly as it did.

One Front Door, Three Households: Houses That Became Condos and Rentals

The Rugby Road listing Brownstoner described, a two-family Edwardian house now holding three condo units, is a familiar arrangement on these blocks. So are houses where the owner lives on the parlor floor and rents the top floor, and houses where siblings inherited a home and each took a floor. What they have in common is a front door that belongs to everyone and is managed by no one. The key gets copied for each household, then for each household’s cleaner, sitter and contractor, and after ten years nobody can say how many are out there.

The fix is a shared door with separate accounts. One station on the porch carries a call button for each household. Each household gets its own codes and fobs and can add or remove the people it trusts without involving the neighbors. Nobody can see another household’s entries. And one person, chosen by the owners, holds the master administrator account for the door itself, so that if a lock needs service or a code is compromised, someone can act. We write that arrangement down in the handover, because in a condo it is a decision for the owners to make together, not for the installer.

Who pays for what matters just as much in a divided house. The strike, the station and the controller are common equipment on a common door, and in a condo they normally belong to the association. In an owner-plus-tenant house they are the owner’s. Either way the controller and its power supply should be on a circuit the whole house shares, not on one unit’s meter, so the door does not go dead when one tenant moves out and their electric account closes. That sounds minor until it happens in January.

Privacy is part of the design, not an afterthought. Where a house has rental apartments, the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law can apply to a smart-access system on the door, with rules on consent, the data collected and how long entry records are kept. Even where a two-family house falls outside the law’s exact reach, the fair arrangement is the same: a tenant’s comings and goings are the tenant’s business. We set entry logs to the shortest retention the owner is comfortable with and show the owner how to give each household its own view.

Ocean Avenue Lobbies: Keys to Fobs Without a Fight

The six-story buildings along Ocean Avenue and the streets around it are the part of Ditmas Park where access control is most often a building decision rather than a household one. A board, a managing agent or an owner decides to replace the keys to the street door, the vestibule, the cellar and the laundry with fobs. Done well, it ends key copying, lets the super cancel a lost credential in a minute and turns the package problem into a solvable one. Done badly, it becomes a tenant dispute, and in a rent-stabilized building it can become a case at the state housing agency.

The rules are clearer than most owners realize. Brick Underground’s summary of state housing agency rulings says that when a rent-stabilized building moves to fobs, the landlord must give new fobs free to every lawful occupant; that extra cards for a tenant’s employees or guests, up to four, are free; that replacing a lost card can cost up to $25; and that the switch cannot be used to collect new personal details such as a driver’s license or Social Security number. The same summary says a new key card system on its own does not count as a major capital improvement that raises rents, unless it comes with other work such as a new lobby door or has the consent of more than three-quarters of tenants. On top of that, the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law requires the owner of a multiple dwelling using smart access to get express consent, give tenants a data retention and privacy policy, collect only a minimum list of data, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, never sell it, never track tenants outside the building, and never “require use of a smart access system to gain entry to the tenant’s dwelling unit.”

Brooklyn has already seen what happens when an owner gets this wrong. In 2019 more than 130 rent-stabilized tenants at Atlantic Plaza Towers in Brownsville opposed their landlord’s application to install a facial recognition entry system, with their attorney, Samar Katnani of Legal Services NYC, arguing that “the ability to enter your home should not be conditioned on the surrender of your biometric data.” The case pushed a City Council member to propose the KEYS Act, which would have required physical keys; it is not law, but the privacy act that followed sets the rules above. We do not install face recognition on residential street doors, and we recommend that every Ditmas Park building keep a key override that the super controls.

How we run a fob rollout in an occupied building

First, we install the readers and strikes alongside the existing key locks, so for a week or two both work. Second, the managing agent sends the consent form and privacy policy with the date fobs will be handed out, and residents collect them in the lobby with nothing more than their apartment number. Third, the super keeps a short list of residents who are away, elderly or need help, and we program their fobs in advance. Only when every apartment has its fobs do the key cylinders on the common doors get changed or retired. That order keeps the lobby working, keeps the building on the right side of the rules, and gives nobody a reason to call 311.

The package room is where the new system earns its keep. A courier code that opens only the package room door, and only during delivery hours, means drivers stop leaving boxes by the mailboxes. Residents open the same room with their own fob, and the door contact records when it was opened. Combined with a camera covering the door, it gives a building the record that the residents near Cortelyou and Stratford had to build by hand in a spreadsheet.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control in Ditmas Park

The city’s 2018 commercial needs assessment for Flatbush–Ditmas studied five shopping streets in and around the neighborhood: Cortelyou Road, Newkirk Avenue, Foster Avenue, Coney Island Avenue and Ocean Avenue. It counted 348 storefronts with a 7.2 percent vacancy rate and rated 39 percent of them in poor condition. That mix of busy, independent shops in older buildings is exactly where a back door is most often the weak point. These are the corridors we work on and what the doors there usually need.

Cortelyou Road

The neighborhood’s main street of cafes, restaurants, bars and small shops, mostly with apartments above. In July 2012 Bklyner reported that a cafe on the strip was burglarized at about 2 a.m., with cash taken from two registers in an office. Rear and office doors with staff codes that end at closing, and an after-hours alert, are the common job.

Newkirk Plaza

The pedestrian plaza beside the B and Q station, with about 10,000 weekday riders, that Bklyner calls the oldest outdoor shopping plaza in America. In 2019 its merchants sought private security after robberies at a smoke shop, a pharmacy and a phone store. Shops here need back-room and stockroom control and doors that keep staff safe at closing.

Newkirk Avenue

Storefronts running east and west from the plaza, many in small mixed-use buildings. The usual task is separating the shop door from the apartments’ street door so each has its own schedule and its own administrator.

Coney Island Avenue

The long avenue at the western edge of the study area, with storefronts of every size. Where a shop has a roll-down gate in front, the job is usually the steel personnel door at the back or side, an office door, or a community room that should open only for scheduled events.

Foster Avenue

The southern corridor in the city’s study, with shops and ground-floor offices. Medical and professional offices on ground floors use card or phone entry with visitors buzzed in by the front desk.

Ocean Avenue

Mostly apartment buildings, with ground-floor offices and community facilities in some of them. A professional office that opens into a residential lobby needs its own door control so clients never reach the building’s halls.

What a back door can and cannot do for a Cortelyou Road shop

The 2012 cafe burglary was a reminder that the cash office is the target, not the dining room. A reader on the office door, set so only the owner and managers can open it and only during business hours, does three useful things: it keeps casual staff and visitors out of the room with the registers and the safe, it stops a former employee’s key from working, and it records every opening with a name and a time. A door contact on the rear door adds an alert to the owner’s phone if it opens after the last shift has clocked out. What it does not do is stop someone who comes through a window, a roof or a basement hatch. That is why we walk the whole building on the visit and say plainly where a camera or an alarm sensor would do more good than another reader.

Newkirk Plaza’s merchants made a related point in 2019. Javier Saez, president of the Newkirk Plaza Merchant Association and owner of Almac Hardware, told Bklyner that occasional patrols were not enough: “Once in a blue moon doesn’t cut it. We need consistency.” A locked door is consistent in a way that patrols are not. For a shop on the plaza the job is usually modest: a keypad on the stockroom, a strike on the back door, and a staff list that ends when someone stops working there.

Rules that apply to Ditmas Park business doors

  • Exits come first. Under the NYC Building Code, an electrically locked door on a path of exit must unlock when power fails and when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate, and a sensor-released door needs a push-to-exit button within five feet that keeps the door unlocked for at least 30 seconds. A magnetic lock must release from hardware on the door itself with one motion.
  • Listed equipment. Controllers and locks on exit doors should be listed to UL 294 for access control. We fit listed equipment and keep the listing sheets in the job file for the owner.
  • Apartments upstairs. Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a requires self-closing, self-locking street entrances in class A buildings put up or converted after 1968, and an intercom with voice and remote release where there are eight or more apartments. A shop door must never be the only way into the residential stair.
  • Tenant data. Where the building has apartments using fobs or apps, the Tenant Data Privacy Law’s rules on consent, minimum data and 90-day destruction apply to the residential doors, even when the shop runs its own system.
  • Permits and inspections. A trade article in Doors & Hardware warns that the code’s permit exemptions “do not include access control systems or the installation of electrified hardware,” and that an inspector can order unapproved work changed. We check what the job needs before work starts, not after.
  • Licensing. New York State requires a license to install or service security systems for others. AESS works under license #12000287431.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Ditmas Park: Questions Answered

Porch houses and the landmark districts

Our front door still has its original twist doorbell, and we love it. Can a video station go in without retiring it?

Yes. A mechanical twist bell rings by hand and has nothing electrical in it, so it never interferes with a video station, a keypad or a strike. Most owners who want to keep one put the station on the wall or trim beside the door and leave the bell exactly where it is, so friends who know the house can still ring it the old way and couriers use the station’s button. On a landmark-district house that also keeps the door itself untouched, which is the simplest thing to explain on a permit application.

The one thing we check is that the bell’s mechanism does not share the bore where the strike or a new lock would go. On some old doors the bell sits low in the center rail and the lockset sits at the edge, so there is no conflict at all. If an earlier owner moved the lock and the bores are close together, we plan the strike in the jamb instead of an electrified lockset in the door, which leaves the door’s wood alone.

The 2012 break-ins on our block happened in daytime while families were out. What does a door system do when nobody is home all day?

Three useful things. The doors relock themselves every time they close, so a side door left on the latch by the last person out at 8 a.m. is locked by 8:01. A door contact on each controlled door sends an alert to your phone if a door opens while the house is empty, or if one is left standing open. And every code or fob that opened a door is recorded with a time, so a cleaner, a contractor or an aide who comes during the day leaves a clear record without anyone needing to be there to let them in. The burglaries residents described at the 70th Precinct Community Council in June 2012 were all daytime, which is exactly when those three features matter.

What a door system cannot do is protect a window. The same Bklyner report said four South Midwood burglaries that month all came through unlocked first-floor windows. On a big freestanding house with porch roofs and ground-floor windows on four sides, we will tell you on the visit which windows an alarm contact or a camera should cover, because no reader on the front door helps with those.

Our block of Stratford Road was left out when Landmarks drew the 2025 districts. Does a porch station there still need the Commission’s approval?

Landmarks permits apply to designated properties: individual landmarks and buildings inside a designated historic district. Brownstoner reported that residents asked for Westminster and Stratford Roads and parts of Beverley Road to be added to the new districts and that the Commission did not extend the boundaries. If your lot is outside every district line, including the original 1981 Ditmas Park district, and the house is not an individual landmark, a door station is not Landmarks work. Check your exact address on the Commission’s own district map before relying on that, because the lines follow lots, not whole streets.

Even outside a district we still pick a station that suits the house. A neighboring block may be designated, the house may be added in some later round, and a small device near the door opening with cable hidden in the wall looks right on a Victorian whether or not anyone is reviewing it. It also makes resale easier, because the next owner never has to wonder whether an earlier change was approved.

Our cellar is unfinished and gets damp after heavy rain. Is it still the right place for the controller and its battery?

Usually yes, mounted correctly. The controller and its battery go high on the wall, well above any water line you have seen, in a closed enclosure, close to the electrical panel and away from the boiler or water heater. Cable runs along the joists overhead rather than along the floor. In most Ditmas Park cellars that keeps the electronics dry even when the floor is wet, and it keeps them out of reach of anyone who wanders down there.

If water has ever come up past the panel, or the cellar walls are weeping, we look for a better spot: a closet under the main stair, a dry corner of a pantry, or the back of a hall closet near the front door. A slightly longer cable run costs far less than a controller lost to one storm.

Divided houses and small condos

Our house was split into three condos, and each unit has its own electric meter. Which meter should power the front-door system?

The best answer is none of the units’ meters. If the house has a common or “house” meter for the hall lights and the cellar, the door system goes on that circuit, and its cost is shared like the other common electricity. A front door that runs from one owner’s apartment goes dead the day that owner’s account lapses or a breaker trips in their kitchen, and the other two households are locked into using keys until someone notices.

If there is no common meter, the owners need to agree in writing whose circuit carries the door, and the association should reimburse that owner. The draw is small, about the same as a few light bulbs, but the agreement matters more than the cost. We bring a simple note setting out the circuit, the breaker number and the battery’s backup time to the handover so it can go in the condo’s records.

The owner of the top-floor condo in our house is selling. What has to happen with the door system at the closing?

The seller’s household should be removed from the system and the buyer’s added, and that should happen on closing day, not weeks later. In practice the association’s administrator deletes the seller’s codes and fobs, collects any fobs that are handed back, and issues new credentials to the buyer. If the top-floor unit has its own call button, its phone routing is switched to the buyer’s numbers. Nothing in the door, the strike or the controller changes.

Two things are worth checking before the sale. First, that the seller is not the only person holding the master administrator account; if they are, it should be passed to another owner first. Second, that the condo’s documents say the door equipment is a common element, so the buyer is not surprised to find they now own a share of it. Both take minutes to sort out in advance and can take months to fix afterwards.

Ocean Avenue and the side-street apartment buildings

Our building has a door attendant around the clock. Does the street door still need to lock itself, and what does a reader add?

Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a lets the entrance of a newer class A building stay unlocked only while an attendant is on duty, so a door with an attendant at the desk 24 hours a day can be run that way. In practice attendants take breaks, sign for deliveries, help someone in the elevator and step outside, and those are the minutes a door should not be standing open. Most attended buildings keep the street door self-locking and let the attendant release it from the desk.

A reader adds three things on top of the attendant. Residents can let themselves in with a fob while the attendant is busy, so the desk is not a bottleneck at 6 p.m. A lost or stolen fob can be cancelled at the desk in seconds instead of re-keying the lobby. And a door held open too long sends an alert to the desk and the super, which an attendant who has stepped away cannot otherwise see.

After the Ocean Avenue superintendent’s death in 2025, our board told staff never to confront package thieves. How can the doors take that job off the super?

By making the theft hard and the evidence automatic, so nobody has to stand between a thief and a box. Deliveries go into a package room or a locked mail alcove with its own door, opened by a courier code during delivery hours and by residents’ own fobs. The street and vestibule doors relock behind every person. A camera covers the package room door and the vestibule, and the door system records which code or fob opened the room and when. If something goes missing, the super pulls the record and the footage and hands them to the managing agent or the police.

We would also suggest the board write the policy down and post it in the staff room: staff do not chase, follow or block anyone, they call 911 if they see a crime in progress, and they report afterwards. Equipment cannot replace that policy, but it can make it easy to follow, because the building no longer depends on someone noticing a stranger in the lobby at the right moment.

Our building has dozens of open HPD violations. Should the owner fix those before spending on fobs?

The door-related ones, yes, and often first. HPD gives owners 24 hours to correct its most serious Class C violations, with 14 days for self-closing doors, 30 days for Class B and 90 days for Class A, according to the agency’s own guidance on clearing violations. A broken self-closing street door or a failed intercom is a legal problem before it is a security problem, and putting a reader on a door that does not close by itself does not clear the violation. We often start a job in an older building by getting the door to close and latch reliably and the intercom working, which is the work that clears those items.

Violations that have nothing to do with doors, such as leaks or pests, do not need to wait for the fob project or hold it up. But a building that is also asking tenants to consent to a smart-access system will find that easier with the obvious repairs done. Tenants who have watched the front door break for months are not inclined to trust a new one.

Our building has 185 apartments. How long does it take to get a fob into every household’s hand?

The hardware is the quick part; the handout takes planning. We install the readers alongside the existing key locks, so the lobby keeps working on keys while fobs go out. The managing agent sends consent forms and the privacy policy ahead of time with the pickup dates. We then run pickup sessions in the lobby at times that suit working households, usually a few weekday evenings and a weekend morning, with the super holding a list of who still needs theirs.

For a building this size we plan about two to three weeks between the first pickup session and the day keys stop working on the common doors, to reach residents who travel, work nights or need help. Changing the cylinders before every household has its fobs is the most common way a rollout turns into a complaint, so we keep both systems working until the super’s list is empty.

Cortelyou Road, Newkirk Plaza and the office strips

Our cafe on Cortelyou Road opens at 7, and the baker comes in at 4 a.m. Can her code open the back door and the kitchen but not the office?

Yes. Each person gets their own code or fob, and each code has its own list of doors and hours. The baker’s code can open the back door and the kitchen from 3:30 to 7 a.m. and nothing else, while the office with the registers and the safe opens only for the owner and the managers. If she changes shifts, the schedule changes in the app; if she leaves, her code is deleted and nothing needs re-keying. The record shows each door she opened and when, which settles most questions about early mornings without anyone having to ask.

Bklyner reported in 2012 that cash was taken from two registers in a Cortelyou Road cafe’s office during a 2 a.m. burglary. A schedule-limited office door means a lost staff key or an old code never opens that room, and an alert can reach the owner’s phone if the office or the back door opens at an hour when nobody should be there.

Our shop on Newkirk Plaza closes after the plaza has emptied out. How can the last employee lock up and leave by the back door quickly?

We set the system up so closing is one or two actions, not a walk around the shop checking locks. The front door is already locked once the gate is down; the stockroom and office relock themselves when they close; and the back door has a lock that opens freely from inside and relocks behind the employee. If you also have an alarm, the back-door keypad can arm it as the employee leaves. A door contact confirms on the owner’s phone that the back door actually closed.

The plaza merchants’ push for steady patrols in 2019 came after a pharmacy employee was held at gunpoint and another worker was assaulted. A quick, practiced exit matters for that reason. We put the back-door keypad where the employee can use it without standing in the open for long, light it so the code can be entered without fumbling, and make sure the inside of that door always opens with one push.

Our counseling office on Foster Avenue sees clients by appointment. Can the street door open only for the booked hour?

It can, in two ways. The simplest is a video station at the street door that rings the office, so staff see and admit each client. The other is a one-time code or phone link sent with the appointment confirmation, valid for a window around the booked time and useless afterwards. Many offices combine them: regular clients get a time-limited code, and anyone without one rings the station.

Either way, the waiting area should be on the controlled side of the door and the treatment rooms on a second door if the office is shared. Keep the entry log on the office’s own account rather than the building’s, and keep it short; a list of who visited a counselor and when is sensitive information and should be treated that way.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Ditmas Park

Quick online answers, consumer articles and national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr tend to repeat the same handful of generalizations about access control. Below, each is set against what Ditmas Park doors actually need. We are describing claims we hear often, not quoting any one of those sources.

“A smart lock from the store does the same job.”

For a single household on an interior door, a battery smart lock can be enough, and consumer guides recommend them because they need no wiring.

A Ditmas Park front door is rarely that simple. It is usually an old wood door on a porch that sees wet springs and cold winters, it may serve two or three households in a divided house, and the side door by the driveway is used more than the front. One brand’s app on one battery lock does not give each household its own account, a schedule for the aide or the baker, or an alert when the side door is left open.

Where cable can reach the door, and with a full cellar below the front hall it nearly always can, a wired strike and a battery-backed controller do the job without anyone changing batteries on the porch in January.

“Access control costs a few hundred dollars a door.”

National cost guides quote very wide ranges. ButterflyMX’s New York City guide gives roughly $500 to $5,000 per door for a fob or card system, plus $1 to $5 per fob, while Swiftlane puts a standard door at about $1,500 to $5,000 installed, with hardware alone at $450 to $1,700.

The low end of those ranges usually describes a part, not a working door. A working controlled door is the reader or keypad, the strike, the cable from the cellar, a programmed controller with a battery, and the credentials. On our Brooklyn list that is $1,500 to $2,500, with ten fobs included.

Ditmas Park pays that same Brooklyn base, with nothing added for a landmark district, an old house or the trip from our office on Troy Avenue.

“You can’t put modern entry hardware on a landmarked house.”

This one stops owners from asking at all. The Landmarks Preservation Commission’s Permit Guidebook does regulate entry panels in historic districts, but it plainly allows them.

It says intercom panels must go within or next to the door opening, allows one panel per door opening, asks that the design, size and finish be compatible with the building, keeps visible conduit to about ten inches painted to match, bars attaching equipment through cast iron or decorative surfaces, and says staff can issue the permit when those criteria are met.

On most Victorian houses that means one small station beside the door, a strike hidden in the jamb and cable inside the wall: modern hardware, with the facade looking as it did.

“A fob system will stop package theft.”

Fobs control who opens a door. They do nothing for a box left by the mailboxes in a lobby that anyone can reach once the street door opens behind a resident.

The building near Cortelyou and Stratford Roads that Bklyner wrote about in 2018 lost more than 100 packages in six months to exactly that pattern, and the residents ended up keeping their own spreadsheet. A street door that relocks, a vestibule that stops couriers short of the mailboxes, and a package room opened only by courier codes and residents’ fobs are what change it.

Add a camera on the package room door and the building has both a barrier and a record, without asking its staff to confront anyone.

“A landlord can track tenants with the entry logs.”

Generic answers present entry records as a management perk. In New York City, the Tenant Data Privacy Law sets the limits for smart access in multiple dwellings.

Owners must get consent, give tenants a written retention and privacy policy, collect only a minimum list of data, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, never sell it, and never use it to harass or evict a tenant. In rent-stabilized buildings, Brick Underground’s summary of state housing agency rulings adds free fobs for lawful occupants and a cap on replacement charges.

On an Ocean Avenue building we set short log retention by default and give the managing agent a privacy policy to adapt before the first fob goes out.

“Face recognition is the most secure option for apartment buildings.”

Some answers present biometric entry as the top tier of security. Brooklyn tenants have already tested that idea in practice.

At Atlantic Plaza Towers in Brownsville, more than 130 rent-stabilized tenants opposed their landlord’s 2019 application to install a facial recognition entry system, and their attorney argued that “the ability to enter your home should not be conditioned on the surrender of your biometric data.” The city’s privacy law now lists biometric data among the information that may be collected only with consent and kept only under its rules.

For residential street doors in Ditmas Park we install encrypted fobs, phone keys and video stations, and leave biometric features switched off.

“Access control is for offices, not houses.”

Most guides are written for businesses. Community Board 14 says more than 48 percent of its district’s area is one- and two-family homes, and Ditmas Park’s streets of freestanding houses are a big part of that.

A large house with a front door, a side door, a rear door and a garage has the same problems a small office does: keys handed out for years, people who need access at certain hours, and a door that someone forgets to lock.

A one-door house system on the Brooklyn list starts at the same $1,500 as one door in a large building, and grows one door at a time on the same controller.

Our Installation Process

Eight steps, whether the job is one porch door or a 185-unit lobby. The step that is particular to Ditmas Park is the fourth: on a house inside a historic district, the station and its location are settled on paper before anything is mounted on the facade.

1. Photos and a first call

You send a photo of each door from the street side and the inside, say who uses it, and tell us whether the property is a house, a divided house, an apartment building or a shop.

2. Walk the property

A technician checks every door, frame and closer, the porch or vestibule wall, the cellar or utility room for the controller, the power source and the cable path, and whether the lot is in a historic district.

3. Door-by-door plan and written price

A written plan for each door: the hardware, who holds which credential, what the door does in a power cut and a fire alarm, and the price from the Brooklyn list.

4. Landmarks paperwork where it applies

For a designated house or building, the plan includes the station’s cut sheet, finish and mounting location, so the owner can apply for the Landmarks permit before work starts.

5. Make every door close and latch

Before any electronics, each door is adjusted to close and latch on its own: hinges, closer, strike position and any repair to an old wood jamb.

6. Cable, controller and power

Controller and battery high on a cellar or utility wall, cable inside the wall or along the cellar joists to each door, power from the owner’s or the common circuit.

7. Credentials, schedules and the power-off test

Codes, fobs and phone keys issued to named people, schedules where needed, then every door tested on battery and with all power removed to prove it always opens from inside.

8. Handover and follow-up

Administrator access and a one-page guide for the owner or manager, help with the tenant privacy policy in apartment buildings, and a follow-up a few weeks later to adjust closers, schedules and alerts.

Before the first frost, and again in spring

Wood doors in old houses move with the weather, so a door that latched perfectly in October can start to bind in a wet April. Twice a year, open each controlled door and let it go: it should swing shut and latch without a push. Check the keypad battery level in the app before the first cold snap, wipe the porch station’s camera lens, and read through the user list, removing last summer’s painter, a tenant who moved and an aide who no longer comes. In an apartment building, the super should also prop the street door for a minute and confirm the held-open alert arrives. If anything drags, sticks or fails to alert, a service call now costs less than a lockout later.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Find the Door the House Really Uses

This is how we approach a big freestanding house, not the story of one customer. On a first visit to a Victorian house we ask the family to walk us in the way they actually come home, not the way a guest would. Very often that is not the porch at all. It is the side door halfway down the driveway, or the kitchen door at the back, or the door from the garage path. The front door with the beautiful glass is the one the courier uses; the side door is the one with the worn threshold, the hook for the dog leash and the key under the mat. That is the door that needs the keypad first, and the porch gets the video station.

Then we go to the cellar and look up. We want to see the joists running toward the front and side doors, the electrical panel, a dry wall well away from the boiler, and any old doorbell or alarm wire that already found a path up through the floor. Last, we test each door by letting it close on its own. A century-old door that has to be pulled the last inch will never relock reliably, however good the strike. So the first hour of most Ditmas Park jobs goes into hinges, closers and jambs, and the electronics come after.

FAQ

Ditmas Park Access Control FAQ

Is a fob system ever the wrong choice for a small Ditmas Park building?
+

Sometimes. In a two-family house where both households trust each other and only a handful of people ever need a key, a good keypad or a single video station may do everything a full fob system would, for less. A fob system pays off when keys keep getting copied, when people come and go on schedules, when tenants turn over, or when a lost key would mean re-keying a door several households use. We will tell you on the visit if the simpler option is the better one.

Our walk-up on Ocean Avenue went up in 1899. Is a building that old too old for fobs?
+

No. The age of the building matters much less than the condition of the street door and the path for the cable. An old building usually needs its door, frame and closer brought back into shape first, and the cable run takes some care through old plaster or along the cellar ceiling. Once the door closes and latches on its own, a reader, strike and controller work the same on an 1899 building as on a new one.

Can the fobs on our lobby be swapped for phone keys later without new readers?
+

Often, yes, if the readers were chosen with that in mind. Many current readers accept both encrypted fobs and phone credentials, so adding phone keys later is a software change rather than new hardware. Older readers that only read 125 kHz fobs cannot. If phone entry is likely in the next few years, we specify dual-technology readers on day one so the building can switch, or run both, without opening the walls again.

Do phone keys cost more than fobs for a Ditmas Park building?
+

Up front they can cost less, because there are no fobs to buy or hand out, but phone credentials usually come through a cloud service with a monthly or yearly fee. Fobs cost a few dollars each and need no subscription on a locally managed system. Most buildings end up with both: phone keys for residents who want them and fobs for everyone else, including older residents and anyone whose phone is not always charged.

If Con Edison cuts power to our block, does the porch door stay locked, and can we still get in?
+

On a house we normally fit a fail-secure strike, which stays locked without power, together with a battery-backed controller that keeps the keypad and fobs working for hours. The inside knob always opens the door, so nobody is ever trapped, and the original key cylinder stays working for a long outage. On apartment building exits the rules are different: a door on an exit path must unlock when power is lost, so we plan those doors with the fire and building codes in mind.

Can the porch station take a video message when nobody answers?
+

Most of the stations we install can record a short clip whenever the call button is pressed, whether or not anyone answers, and send it to the household’s phones. That covers the courier who rings while you are on the subway and the neighbor who stops by. We set recording to start at the button press rather than run all the time, and aim the camera at the porch and steps rather than the street or the house next door.

Our porch keypad was put in by a handyman and nobody has the administrator code. Can you take it over?
+

Usually. Many keypads and controllers can be reset to factory settings and reprogrammed, which wipes the old codes and lets you set new ones. Some need the original installer account or a reset procedure from the manufacturer. On the visit we identify the model, check the wiring and the strike, and tell you whether it is worth keeping. Takeovers outside warranty are billed at the $195 an hour service rate with a three-hour minimum.

Equipment we supply comes with the warranty set out in our service agreement and on our warranty page, which covers parts on AESS-supplied products. Customer-supplied hardware stays under its seller’s warranty. Damage from power surges, physical damage and changes to the customer’s own wiring or network are excluded. The written quote spells out exactly what applies to your job before you sign.

Our board wants fobs on the lobby before the holiday delivery rush. How early should we call?
+

Earlier than most boards think. The installation itself is quick, but the board needs to approve the plan, the managing agent needs time to send consent forms and the privacy policy, and residents need pickup sessions before keys stop working. Hardware for larger systems can also take time to arrive. Starting the conversation in late summer or early fall gives a building a realistic chance of having fobs and a working package room before November.

Can the keypad at our side door also tell us when the cellar door under the back steps is opened?
+

Yes. A door contact on the cellar door can report to the same controller as the side-door keypad, even if the cellar door itself keeps its ordinary lock. You get an alert on your phone whenever it opens, or only when it opens at night or while the house is empty. If you later want a keypad on the cellar door too, it connects to the same controller without new wiring to the front of the house.
