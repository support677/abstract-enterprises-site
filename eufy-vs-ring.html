Eufy vs Ring:
Which Security Camera System Is Better?

Storage, Monthly Fees, Doorbells, Floodlights, Privacy and Smart-Home Fit · Also Searched as Ring vs Eufy · Updated November 2026

Eufy vs Ring is really a choice about where your video lives: eufy is the better system if you want recordings kept on the doorbell, a HomeBase or a recorder inside your home with no monthly fee, and Ring is the better system if you run Alexa, want months of cloud history you never have to manage, or want professional monitoring on the same app.

Quick answer: Choose eufy for local storage with no subscription, wired PoE recorder kits and a doorbell that keeps its own clips; choose Ring for Alexa, up to 180 days of cloud video on one plan for the whole address, and optional 24/7 professional monitoring. Installed by our NYC crews, four Ring cameras start from $1,350 and a four-camera eufy system with its recorder and 4TB of storage starts from $1,919 on our current price lists, mounting, wiring up to 100 ft per camera and app setup included. The two-minute version. eufy is the security line of Anker Innovations, and its whole pitch is local storage: its Local Security page promises "no monthly fee," says recordings stay inside your home, and says storage can grow to 16 TB when you add a drive to a HomeBase. Ring is the camera and doorbell company owned by Amazon, and its pitch is the opposite shape: cameras that send clips to Ring's cloud, where a Ring Protect plan keeps up to 180 days of history and unlocks the smarter alerts. Both sell video doorbells, outdoor cameras and floodlight cameras. Both offer an optional end-to-end encryption mode. Both will alert your phone and let you talk to whoever is at the door. Where they part ways is everything that happens after the motion is detected.

With eufy, the clip is written to storage you own: built-in memory on the doorbell, a microSD card in the camera, a HomeBase S380 on a shelf, or a PoE recorder in a closet. Nothing is owed each month unless you choose eufy's optional Cloud Backup. With Ring, the clip goes to the cloud. Live view and alerts work without a plan, but Ring's own product page is plain that without a Ring Protect subscription you "won't be able to review any videos or images you missed." Ring's local option, Ring Edge, needs a Ring Alarm Pro base station and a plan. So the honest rule we give customers is short. If paying every month bothers you, or you want the footage physically on the property, start with eufy. If your home already talks to Alexa, if you want history measured in months without thinking about drives, or if you want a monitoring center to call the police when an alarm trips, start with Ring. Everything else on this page explains the edges of that rule.

One more thing belongs at the top rather than buried at the bottom. In January 2025 the New York Attorney General announced a $450,000 settlement with three companies that distribute eufy cameras, after an investigation found that some eufy video streams had not been fully encrypted and could be opened by anyone with the right link. We explain what was found, what eufy changed and what we turn on for customers in the privacy section below. It is a real part of the eufy vs Ring decision for New Yorkers, and we would rather you read it here than discover it later.

The Verdict by Buyer

Who Should Pick eufy and Who Should Pick Ring

eufy is the better fit if

  • You want no recurring bill at all. Doorbell, HomeBase and recorder storage are included in the hardware, and eufy's Local Security page says every advanced feature is available "with no monthly fee."
  • You want the footage to stay on the property, on a doorbell, card, HomeBase or hard drive you can point to, rather than on a company's servers.
  • You have a run of wall or ceiling where we can pull network cable. eufy's PoE recorder kits put a 4K camera on each cable with power and video together, record around the clock, and come with a 36-month eufy warranty on the recorder and the S4 and E41 cameras.
  • You want to look back further than a month without paying for it. A HomeBase S380 with a 16TB drive is the setup eufy describes as holding up to a year of local recordings.
  • Your doorway is a delivery drop. The S330 and E340 doorbells carry a second, downward-looking camera that eufy sells specifically for watching packages on the step.

Ring is the better fit if

  • Your home runs on Alexa. Ring cameras show on Echo Show screens and Fire TV, and Ring's plan page lists Alexa+ Greetings that can answer the door for you on select doorbells.
  • You want history without hardware. Ring Multi keeps up to 180 days of events for every Ring device at your address for one monthly price, and there is no drive to fill, replace or lose to a burglar.
  • You want somebody to respond when an alarm goes off. Ring Pro includes 24/7 professional monitoring for Ring Alarm, and Ring sells a Virtual Security Guard tier where trained agents watch live video.
  • You want the widest doorbell choice. Ring sells battery, wired, plug-in and Power over Ethernet doorbells, including a 4K doorbell that runs on a network cable.
  • You want written summaries of what happened. Ring Pro's Video Descriptions put a line of text in each alert, and its Video Search looks through every recorded event on the plan from one search box.

When we would steer you to neither

If you are covering a shop floor, a parking lot, a building lobby or more than about eight cameras, a consumer brand of either kind is usually the wrong tool. eufy's recorder kits stretch to 16 channels, which is respectable, but a commercial recorder with professional cameras will give you better low-light detail, proper user permissions for staff, and parts that a service company can source for years. Our quotes default to our own non-branded recorder system for that reason. We are glad to install eufy or Ring where they fit, and we will tell you when they do not.

Storage

Where the Video Lives: eufy's HomeBase and Recorders vs Ring's Cloud

Storage is the heart of the eufy vs Ring argument, so it gets the longest section. Every other difference on this page is smaller than this one.

eufy, step one: storage on the device itself

The simplest eufy setup needs no hub at all. The Video Doorbell (Wired) S330 has 8GB of memory built in, and eufy's own FAQ for the product works it out at about 90 days of events if the door sees 25 motion clips a day at 15 seconds each; when the memory fills, the oldest clips are overwritten. The Video Doorbell C31 and the Floodlight Camera E340 take a microSD card of up to 128GB instead, and both can record around the clock to that card. For a single door or a single driveway this is the cheapest way to keep video with nothing to pay afterwards, and it is the setup we install most often for eufy customers who own one-family houses.

There is one catch eufy states openly on the S330 page: "a stolen or damaged doorbell takes its recordings with it." A burglar who rips the doorbell off the frame walks away with the evidence. That is the case for adding a HomeBase, which keeps a second copy indoors.

eufy, step two: the HomeBase S380

eufy describes the HomeBase S380, also called HomeBase 3, as a local storage and processing hub. It does not record video on its own; compatible eufy cameras and doorbells send their clips to it. It ships with 16GB of non-removable storage, which eufy's support article says is enough for about two months of video from one camera, or one month from two cameras, if each sees 30 motion events a day at 60 seconds each. A 2.5-inch SATA hard drive or SSD of up to 16TB slides in for more, and eufy's support page says such a drive can hold "years of videos" for a one-camera system. eufy rates the S380 for up to 8 wired devices (four of them recording 24/7), up to 16 battery devices and up to 34 sensors, and it has a built-in 100 dB siren and eufy's BionicMind face recognition, which runs on the hub rather than in a cloud.

Two practical notes from installing them. First, eufy warns that it does not support hard drives that need a 12V supply, so a drive has to be a bus-powered 2.5-inch model; we bring the right one rather than trusting whatever is in a drawer. Second, a HomeBase only protects the footage if it is somewhere a burglar will not find it in thirty seconds. On the shelf next to the router in the front hall is the first place a thief looks. A closet, a basement utility room or a high cabinet is better, as long as the Wi-Fi reaches.

eufy, step three: a wired PoE recorder

At the top of the eufy range are the PoE NVR kits, which work like a traditional camera system. The PoE NVR Security System E41 that we price on our list pairs four 4K turret cameras with an 8-channel recorder that eufy says expands to 16 channels, a pre-installed 2TB hard drive upgradeable to 16TB, and 24/7 recording. Each camera runs on one network cable that carries both power and video, eufy rates the kit's cameras IP67, and eufy's US warranty table gives the Network Video Recorder S4 and the S4 and E41 PoE cameras 36 months. Nothing in that system depends on Wi-Fi, on batteries or on a plan. If a customer asks us which eufy setup we would put on our own houses, this is it.

Ring: cloud history on a plan

Ring stores recorded clips in its cloud. Ring's plans page says motion event videos are stored "for up to 180 days from recording" on its Solo, Multi and Pro plans, and that changing the storage length only affects recordings made after the change. For round-the-clock video Ring sells a 24/7 Continuous Recording add-on per eligible camera; Ring's footnote says those recordings are "temporarily stored in the cloud, up to 14 days." Without any plan, a Ring camera still streams live view, sends alerts and lets you talk through it, but Ring's Outdoor Cam 2K page says you "won't be able to review any videos or images you missed."

The strength of the cloud model is that nobody can steal it. A burglar who pulls a Ring camera off the wall has not touched the clip of their own face, which was already uploaded. You also never think about drive space, failed hard drives or where the hub is hidden. The weakness is the dependency: the plan has to stay paid, the internet has to be up when the event happens, and the history is only as long as the plan says.

Ring Edge: Ring's local option

Ring does offer local storage, but it is a narrower path than eufy's. Ring Edge records to a microSD card inside a Ring Alarm Pro base station, which is the base station of Ring's alarm system. On the Ring pages we read for our Ring vs Arlo comparison, Ring Edge required a compatible subscription, linked Ring and Amazon accounts, and cameras on the same eero network, and the current plans page shows Local Video Storage as a feature of the Pro and Virtual Security Guard tiers. So it is a real option for a Ring household that wants an on-premises copy, but it is not a way to avoid a subscription.

When the internet drops

This is where the two designs feel most different in New York, where cable outages during storms and building-wide router swaps are common. A eufy doorbell writing to its own memory, or a PoE camera writing to a recorder in the basement, keeps recording through an outage; you simply cannot view it remotely until the connection returns. A Ring camera with no Ring Edge depends on the upload to save the clip at all. If your block loses service often, that matters more than any resolution figure.

What a burglar takes

Put the two weaknesses side by side and the answer for most homes is a mix. eufy's weakness is physical: a doorbell with on-board storage leaves with the thief, and a HomeBase in plain sight can too. Ring's weakness is financial and network: no plan or no internet, no clip. On eufy jobs we therefore hide the HomeBase or recorder and suggest the optional Cloud Backup for the one or two cameras that matter most. On Ring jobs we make sure the plan is set before the 30-day trial ends and that the Wi-Fi at every camera is strong enough to upload a clip in the seconds that count.

Monthly Cost

Ring Protect vs eufy Cloud Backup: The Running Cost

Ring Protect plans

Ring's plans page, read on November 8, 2026, lists four tiers. Ring Solo is $4.99 a month or $49.99 a year and covers one doorbell or camera. Ring Multi is $9.99 a month or $99.99 a year and covers every Ring device at one location, and it adds an extended warranty that in the U.S. is a service contract administered by Allstate Protection Plans. Ring Pro is $30 a month (keypad) or $45 a month (with the app) or $199.99 a year and adds Video Descriptions, Familiar Faces, Active Warnings, Video Search, Unusual Event Alert and 24/7 Professional Monitoring for Ring Alarm. Virtual Security Guard is $99 a month and adds live video monitoring by trained agents. On top of those, Pro Intelligence adds the AI features to one camera for $5 a month on Solo or Multi, and 24/7 Continuous Recording is $3 a month per camera on any plan. Every plan auto-renews, a new device gets a free 30-day trial, and a second address needs its own plan.

Two state-specific notes from Ring's footnotes matter less in New York but are worth knowing if you own property elsewhere: Ring says its AI features are not available in Illinois, and Familiar Faces is not available in Illinois, Texas or Portland, Oregon.

eufy Cloud Backup

eufy's cloud is optional and narrower. Its support article on Cloud Backup lists a Basic plan priced by device count, $3.99 a month or $39.99 a year for one device, $7.99 or $79.99 for two and $11.99 or $119.99 for three, and a Plus plan for unlimited devices at $13.99 a month or $139.99 a year. Every plan keeps a rolling 30 days, after which clips are overwritten. eufy says videos are stored on Amazon Web Services servers, encrypted when uploaded, and playable only through its app. Two pieces of small print stood out to us: an account can hold only one cloud package at a time, and eufy says users cannot cancel Cloud Backup directly, but must contact eufy support with the camera's serial number. Separately, eufy sells a eufy Plus membership at $15 a month that includes 30-day Cloud Backup history for all devices along with shopping perks; annual members also get an extra six months of warranty on products bought while the membership is active.

The two side by side

The comparison that matters is not plan against plan, because eufy's plan is optional and Ring's is close to essential. For a typical New York house with a doorbell and three cameras, Ring's sensible choice is Ring Multi. That is $99.99 a year if paid annually, which over three years comes to about $300 in plan fees, or about $360 if paid monthly. A eufy system with local storage costs nothing in those same three years. If the eufy owner adds Cloud Backup Plus for an off-site copy of every camera, the total over those same three years is about $420 at the annual rate, which is more than Ring Multi and buys only 30 days of history rather than 180. So the cheapest long-run setup is eufy with local storage only, and the best cloud value is Ring Multi.

What each does with no plan at all

With no subscription, a eufy system does everything its hardware allows: it records locally, sends alerts, shows live view, runs its on-device person and package detection, and lets you scroll back as far as the storage reaches. A Ring system with no subscription is a live camera and a doorbell intercom. It alerts, it streams, it talks, but it saves nothing you can go back to. Ring is upfront about this, and it is fine for some people. A renter who only wants to see who is at the door before buzzing them in may never need a saved clip. Anyone who wants evidence after a package theft or a hit-and-run in the driveway does.

Doorbells

eufy Doorbells vs Ring Doorbells at a New York Front Door

Most eufy vs Ring decisions start at the front door, and the doorbell is where the two brands differ in the most practical ways: how they take power from the old bell wire, where they keep the clip, and whether they can see the package on the step.

The eufy line we install

eufy's support page that compares every eufy doorbell is unusually complete, and it is where most of these figures come from. The Video Doorbell (Wired) S330 is the one we fit most often on houses with existing bell wire. It has two cameras: a 2K main camera at 2560 × 1920 with a 160° view, and a second 1080p camera with a 120° view that looks down at the step. It has no battery, records motion events rather than 24/7, keeps them on 8GB of built-in memory and comes with an indoor chime. eufy's table lists Alexa for live view and doorbell-press announcements on this model. The Video Doorbell C31 is a 2K doorbell at 2048 × 1536 that can run on its 6500mAh battery or on bell wire, stores to a microSD card of up to 128GB that is not included, and is the eufy model eufy recommends for 24/7 recording with a 5-second pre-roll; it lists both Alexa and Google Assistant. The battery-only C30 uses a 5000mAh battery that eufy rates at 120 days. eufy's newest wired model, the S4, is described in eufy's own FAQ as a wired 3K panoramic doorbell with 24/7 recording and color night vision that works with a HomeBase and with Apple HomeKit. Every doorbell in eufy's table carries a 12-month warranty, an IP65 rating and a −4 °F to 122 °F operating range, and every one connects on 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi only.

The Ring line we install

Ring's doorbell range is wider. Our price list carries the Wired Doorbell 2K, Battery Doorbell 2K, Wired and Battery Doorbell 2K Plus, Wired and Battery Doorbell 4K Pro and the Wired Doorbell 4K Elite (2nd Gen). On the Ring pages we read for our earlier comparisons, the Battery Doorbell 4K Pro recorded in what Ring calls "Retinal 4K" with a square head-to-toe view of 140° wide by 140° tall and 10x Enhanced Zoom, used dual-band Wi-Fi 6, was rated from −4 °F to 122 °F, accepted bell wire from an 8–24 VAC transformer of up to 40 VA, and carried a one-year limited warranty. Ring's line comparison also showed a native Power over Ethernet model, the 4K Elite, and noted that some cheaper Ring doorbells connect on 2.4 GHz only. Every Ring doorbell keeps its clips in Ring's cloud, so none of them needs memory on the device.

Transformers: the detail that decides installs

The single most common reason a wired doorbell misbehaves in New York is the transformer feeding it. Many houses built before the 1980s still have a small 10 VA chime transformer in the basement or behind the chime. eufy's S330 page is direct about it: the S330 "requires a 16–24VAC power supply with a transformer rated at 30VA or higher," older 8V or 16V 10VA transformers "may not provide enough power," and "a 24V 40VA replacement is the common fix." eufy's battery and dual-power doorbells, like the C31 and E340, are rated to accept 8–24 V at 10 VA or more because the battery covers the gap. Ring's battery 4K Pro accepts 8–24 VAC up to 40 VA when wired in. In practice that means a eufy S330 on an old house often needs a new transformer, which we carry, and a Ring battery doorbell on the same wire will usually run but may still draw down its battery on a busy day. We meter the transformer before we mount anything.

Packages on the stoop

If package theft is the reason you are buying a doorbell, eufy has the hardware answer. The S330 and the E340 both carry the second, downward camera, and eufy's S330 FAQ suggests mounting about 4 ft above the ground with a clear line to where couriers leave boxes. Ring's answer is software: Package Alerts are part of its Smart Alerts on select models with a plan, and the 4K Pro's 140° by 140° view is what Ring calls a head-to-toe view of the visitor. On a deep brownstone stoop where the box lands below the doorbell's line of sight, the eufy two-camera design is simply better. On a flat entry where the courier is fully in frame, either works.

Apartment doors and buzzer panels

Neither brand makes a doorbell meant to replace an apartment building's intercom or buzzer panel, and neither should be wired into one. In a co-op or rental, a battery doorbell on your own unit door is usually allowed where a hardwired one is not, and both brands sell battery models. If the real goal is seeing who is at the street door of the building, that is an intercom job, not a doorbell job, and our intercom installation team in NYC handles it separately.

Outdoor Cameras

Floodlights and Outdoor Cameras: E340 and PoE Kits vs Ring's Outdoor Line

eufy Floodlight Camera E340

The E340 is the eufy floodlight we install most, and it is a more ambitious device than most floodlight cameras. eufy's support spec sheet lists two lenses on a motorized head, a 3K wide lens at 2880 × 1620 and a 2K telephoto at 2304 × 1296, with 3x optical and 8x digital zoom; the head pans a full 360° horizontally so it can follow a person across a yard. It has two dimmable light panels totalling 2,000 lumens at 4,000 K, infrared night vision eufy rates at 50 ft for a human body, two-way audio, and a siren eufy's spec sheet puts at up to 100 dB (its product page summary says 95 dB, so we quote the range rather than pick one). That upper limit is the one number we would flag: a south-facing wall in a Bronx July can get close to it.

Ring Floodlight Cam Plus

Ring's Floodlight Cam Plus, as listed on the Ring page we read for our Ring vs Arlo comparison, is a simpler design: a fixed 1080p camera with color night vision and a 140° by 80° view, two 2,000-lumen lamps at 3,000 K, a 105 dB siren, hardwired at 110–240 V or available as a plug-in model, 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi, and a −5 °F to 122 °F rating. Ring also sells 2K and 4K floodlights; our price list carries the Floodlight Cam 2K, Floodlight Cam 4K Plus and Floodlight Cam 4K Pro alongside the Plus.

Which floodlight we would mount

For a long driveway or a wide back yard where one camera has to cover a lot, the E340's pan-and-zoom head and its 3K lens see more than a fixed 1080p floodlight, and its clips stay on the card. For a short side path or a single garage door where the job is light, siren and a clear face at ten feet, the Ring Floodlight Cam Plus is a solid, simpler device and slightly less on our price list. In both cases a hardwired floodlight needs a 120V junction box; if one is not there already, your electrician provides it and we mount, connect and set up the camera.

Ring Outdoor Cam 2K and 4K Pro

Ring's four-camera package on our price list is built on the Outdoor Cam 2K. Ring's product page gives it a 2560 × 1440 picture, a 140° horizontal by 85° vertical view, up to 6x Enhanced Zoom, color night vision that falls back to black and white in complete darkness, a remote-activated siren, power from a removable Quick Release battery or a 12.5 W USB-C plug-in adapter, single-band 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi, a −4 °F to 122 °F range and a one-year limited warranty. A step up, the Outdoor Cam 4K Pro, as listed in Ring's support specs, records in 4K with the same 140° by 85° view, has an 85 dB siren measured at 1 m, connects on 2.4 and 5 GHz, needs about 15 Mbps of upload for 4K, is rated −5 °F to 120 °F at IP65, and comes hardwired, plug-in or on PoE+.

eufy's wired PoE cameras

eufy's closest answer to a Ring outdoor camera on a cable is not a Wi-Fi camera at all but the PoE line: the E40 bullet and E41 turret cameras and the S4 and E50 pan-tilt models, each sold alone or in kits with a recorder. Because power and video share one cable, the camera never drops off Wi-Fi and never needs charging. That is a genuinely different category from anything in Ring's lineup apart from the 4K Pro on PoE+, and it is why a eufy PoE kit is often our recommendation for a two-family house that wants six or eight cameras without a monthly bill.

4K, 3K, 2K: does resolution decide it?

Less than the boxes suggest. A 4K Ring camera that has to squeeze its stream through a weak 2.4 GHz link in a brick rowhouse can deliver a softer picture than a 2K camera with a strong signal, and Ring itself recommends around 15 Mbps of upload for its 4K cameras. A eufy camera recording to local storage is not limited by your upload speed at all for what it saves, only for what you watch remotely. In practice, the mounting height, the angle and the light at night matter more to whether you can read a face or a plate than the resolution on the spec sheet. We mount for those first.

Privacy

Privacy and Encryption: eufy's Record and Ring's Options

What happened with eufy

The facts here come from the New York Attorney General's own announcement, dated January 28, 2025. In November 2022 a security researcher publicly disclosed tests suggesting that eufy's marketing claims about security and "end-to-end encryption" might not be accurate. The Attorney General's office investigated a line of eufy cameras, doorbells and video locks distributed by Fantasia Trading LLC, Power Mobile Life LLC and Smart Innovation LLC. It found that, in certain situations, video sent over the internet from these products was not protected by end-to-end encryption, that at least part of the connection used no encryption at all, and that an active video stream could be opened by anyone with the relevant URL without logging in, a URL that it "may have been possible to deduce." The three companies agreed to pay $450,000 in penalties and costs and to regularly show that eufy's developer maintains an information security program, uses secure development and third-party vulnerability testing, runs penetration testing, and encrypts video in storage and in transit.

We think customers deserve to know that, especially New York customers, since it was New York's Attorney General who acted. We also think it should be read accurately. The finding was about video streams over the internet in certain situations, the investigation followed a public disclosure in 2022, and the settlement requires ongoing security work rather than describing a current, open flaw. Video written to a eufy device's local storage was not what the announcement described.

What eufy says it does now

eufy's privacy commitment page says it encrypts video, event lists and face data in transit by default, that local video is secured with AES encryption on the device, that cloud video is protected with TLS and SRTP in transit and encrypted again at rest on AWS, and that an optional Advanced Encryption setting uses end-to-end encryption with keys kept in a vault that only the user's password opens. It also says alerts are text-only by default; if you choose alerts with picture previews, small thumbnails are briefly stored on an AWS server with server-side encryption and then deleted automatically. The page lists ISO/IEC 27001 and 27701, ETSI EN 303 645, NISTIR 8425 and ioXt among its certifications, two-factor sign-in, alerts for new device logins, privacy zones, and a bug bounty run with HackerOne. We have not audited any of that; we are reporting what eufy publishes.

Ring's end-to-end encryption, and what it switches off

Ring also offers optional video end-to-end encryption. Ring's help article says that when it is on, recordings are encrypted with keys only you control, only your enrolled phones can view them, and "no one else, including Ring services," can open them. The trade-off is long. With end-to-end encryption on, Ring lists these as unavailable: Active Warnings, Alexa and Fire TV viewing, Alexa+ Greetings, Bird's Eye View, Familiar Faces, Motion Tracking, viewing on Ring.com, Shared Users, Single Event Alert, Smart Alerts, third-party integrations, Unusual Event Alert, Video Descriptions, video links, Video Search and Virtual Security Guard. It needs iOS 17 or later or Android 9 or later, and Ring says most cameras support it apart from a short list of older models. In other words, Ring's strongest privacy setting turns Ring into a much simpler product.

Where that leaves the choice

If privacy means "no company holds my video," eufy's local storage gets closer to that by design, and its Advanced Encryption covers the parts that do travel. Its 2022 history is the reason to switch that setting on and keep firmware current, not a reason to rule the brand out. If privacy means "my video is encrypted end to end and I accept fewer features," Ring can do that, at the cost of most of what Ring's plans charge for. If privacy means "a large company with a long compliance record," some customers will prefer Ring for that reason alone, and that is a reasonable choice too.

Settings we turn on for every customer, either brand

  • Two-step sign-in on the owner's account, and a separate shared-user login for each household member instead of one shared password.
  • Privacy zones over any neighbor's window, door or yard that falls inside a camera's view.
  • eufy: Advanced Encryption on, text-only alerts unless the customer wants thumbnails, and automatic firmware updates.
  • Ring: end-to-end encryption explained, with its trade-offs, so the customer chooses it knowingly; Smart Alerts and Shared Users set up if they leave it off.
  • Motion zones that stop at the property line, so the system records your stoop and not the whole sidewalk.

Power

Battery, Plug-In, Bell Wire or Network Cable: Powering eufy and Ring

How a camera gets power decides how often you will touch it after we leave. Both brands sell every type, but they lean in different directions.

Batteries

eufy rates its larger doorbell battery, 6500mAh, at about 180 days of use and its 5000mAh battery at about 120 days, while Ring's Outdoor Cam 2K uses a removable Quick Release battery pack that you swap rather than unscrew the camera to charge. Battery life on either brand depends on how busy the spot is: a doorbell facing a quiet garden path lasts far longer than one facing a sidewalk with a bus stop. Cold also shortens it. In New York we fit battery devices where they make sense, on rentals and on spots with no power nearby, and we warn customers that a January stoop is the hardest test a battery faces.

Bell wire and mains

Wired doorbells on both brands take power from the low-voltage bell circuit, which is why the transformer section above matters. Floodlights on both take mains power from a junction box. That part is electrician work in New York; we mount, connect and set up the floodlight once the box and circuit are in place.

Power over Ethernet

This is the wired option we recommend most, and it is where eufy's PoE kits are strongest. One network cable carries power and video from a recorder or PoE switch to each camera, up to typical runs of 100 m under the Ethernet standard, so there is no battery, no outlet and no Wi-Fi to fail. Ring offers PoE on its Wired Doorbell 4K Elite and on PoE+ versions of the Outdoor Cam 4K Pro and some Spotlight and indoor models on our price list, which is useful for a Ring household that wants one or two wired spots. eufy offers PoE across a full recorder system. On a house where we can fish cable through a basement ceiling and up an exterior wall, that difference often settles the brand.

The Installer's Call

Our Honest Call: What Is Fine to Do Yourself and What Holds Up Better Wired

Both brands are sold as do-it-yourself products, and plenty of owners put them up successfully. Some jobs are worth a Saturday afternoon. Others are where we see the callbacks from people who tried first.

Fine as a DIY job, on either brand

  • An indoor camera on a shelf or a plug-in camera beside an outlet you can already reach.
  • A battery doorbell on a wood or vinyl door frame at a one-family house, using the included mounting plate.
  • A Ring Outdoor Cam 2K on a battery, or a eufy battery camera, under a porch roof you can reach from a step ladder.
  • Swapping a doorbell of the same brand for a newer model on bell wire that already powers the old one well.
  • Adding a eufy doorbell or floodlight to a HomeBase that is already installed and working.

Holds up better installed and wired

  • Any wired doorbell on an older house, because the transformer usually needs metering and often replacing. The eufy S330 asks for 30 VA or more; many original chime transformers are 10 VA.
  • Floodlight cameras, which need a junction box on a 120V circuit and a solid mount for a device that weighs around three pounds. eufy lists the E340 at 1,380 g.
  • Anything on brick, brownstone, stucco or stone, where the wrong anchor cracks the face or leaves a camera that drifts out of aim after one winter.
  • Cameras above a first-floor ceiling height. If you need an extension ladder to reach it, you do not want to be reaching it every few months to charge a battery.
  • Every eufy PoE recorder kit. The cameras are plug and play, but the cable is not: getting it from a basement or closet to each corner of a house cleanly, with drip loops and sealed entry holes, is the job.
  • Ring PoE devices, for the same reason: the Wired Doorbell 4K Elite and the PoE+ Outdoor Cam 4K Pro need a cable route and a PoE source.

Our honest summary is this. A single eufy or Ring doorbell and a camera or two are fine for a handy owner. A system meant to last, especially one that should keep recording without charging, Wi-Fi drops or a subscription, holds up better wired. On the eufy side that means a PoE recorder kit. On the Ring side it means hardwired and PoE models with a strong signal at each spot. Either way, it means cable in the wall rather than batteries on the facade.

Landlords and Boards

Eufy vs Ring for Landlords, Co-op Boards and Small Buildings

Whose account holds the footage

The first question in any building is who controls the video. With Ring, the account owner controls the plan and therefore every saved clip, and if a superintendent sets the system up on a personal email, the building has a problem when that person leaves. With eufy, the video is on a HomeBase or recorder the building owns, though the app account still matters for remote viewing. On either brand, we set the account up in the owner's or managing agent's name and add staff as shared users.

How long the evidence lasts

When a tenant reports a package theft from the lobby two weeks later, the system needs to still have it. Ring's plan keeps up to 180 days of events, which is generous. A eufy recorder keeps as much as its drive holds; we size the drive for at least two weeks of continuous recording on every recorder we install, and more if the board asks. The difference is that eufy's 24/7 footage is all there, while Ring's 180 days are motion events unless you buy the per-camera 24/7 add-on, which keeps 14 days.

What tenants are allowed to see

In a rental building, decide in writing who can view live video and who can only request clips. Both apps support shared users; neither replaces a building policy. Never point a camera into an apartment's door viewer or window. Privacy zones on both brands let us black out any part of the view that should not be recorded.

Our usual mix

For a small walk-up, our usual answer is a eufy PoE recorder kit or our own recorder system for the lobby, halls, mailboxes and rear yard, with individual tenants free to put a battery doorbell of either brand on their own doors. That keeps the building's evidence on the building's hardware and leaves personal choices to the people who live there.

Second Look

Eufy vs Ring Claims That Need a Second Look

"eufy has no subscription, so it costs nothing to run"

Mostly true. Local storage is free, but a 24/7 recorder's hard drive is a wear part, an optional cloud copy costs from $3.99 a month, and an S330 on an old house may need a new transformer. None of that comes close to three years of a Ring plan, but it is not literally nothing.

"Ring is useless without a plan"

Too strong. Without a plan Ring still gives you live view, motion alerts and two-way talk, which is all some renters want. What you lose is any recording to look back at, and Ring says so on its own pages.

"eufy video never leaves your home"

Not quite. eufy's own Local Security page carries an asterisk: if you choose Cloud Backup or alerts with thumbnail previews, videos or thumbnails are sent to the cloud to provide those features. Live viewing from outside the house also travels over the internet. The local design keeps far less in the cloud than Ring, but it is not air-gapped.

"More K means a better picture"

Only with the bandwidth to carry it and a mount that suits it. A well-placed 2K camera with a strong signal often beats a 4K camera fighting a weak link through brick. eufy's local recording sidesteps the upload limit for what it saves, which is a quiet advantage on slow connections.

"After 2022, eufy cannot be trusted with privacy"

That is a judgment each customer gets to make. What we can say is what the record shows: the New York Attorney General found real encryption failures, eufy's distributors paid and agreed to ongoing security requirements, and eufy now publishes an optional end-to-end encryption mode and a list of certifications. We turn that encryption on for every eufy customer.

What did the New York Attorney General find about eufy cameras?

Will my old doorbell transformer power a wired eufy or Ring doorbell?
