The Neighborhood

Why Longwood's Converted Buildings and New Filings Need Fire Alarms Designed for Their Current Use

Longwood sits in the southeast Bronx, in Community District 2. Wikipedia draws it from East 167th Street on the north to the Bronx River and the Bruckner Expressway on the east, East 149th Street on the south and Saint Anns Avenue on the west, with Southern Boulevard as its main street. CityNeighborhoods.nyc draws a tighter box: East 163rd Street, Prospect Avenue, East 149th Street and the Bruckner. Both agree on what fills it. Wikipedia describes a neighborhood of dense prewar five- and six-story apartment buildings, filled in since the 1990s with newer apartment buildings and rowhouses on lots that once stood empty, and a population of 26,196 at the 2010 census on 246.55 acres, most of them renters.

The history is why the fire alarm work here looks the way it does. By the late 1970s arson had burned out much of the neighborhood, and CityNeighborhoods.nyc puts the loss at nearly 60 percent of the housing stock destroyed or abandoned by 1977. It came back building by building. On Kelly Street, residents took three buildings the city meant to demolish and rehabilitated them with their own labor; they incorporated as the Banana Kelly Community Improvement Association in February 1978 under the slogan "Don't Move, Improve" (Banana Kelly). Many buildings here have had two lives: built for one purpose, gutted, and rebuilt for another.

Those second lives are what a commercial fire alarm has to match. The Longwood Historic District, bounded by Beck Street and Longwood, Leggett and Prospect Avenues, holds 61 contributing buildings, mostly semi-detached rowhouses by the architect Warren C. Dickerson, and Wikipedia notes that most of them have been converted to single room occupancy housing. A house built for one family and now rented by the room is a different occupancy under the code. On Kelly Street, the seven-story Prospect Hospital opened in 1963 with 175 beds, closed in 1985, and became the Prospect Family Inn (Wikipedia, Prospect Hospital). A synagogue completed in 1906 on Hewitt Place, with its onion domes, is now United Church (Forgotten New York). Along Southern Boulevard, former movie houses became a church and stores (Forgotten New York).

The newest buildings carry a different question: height. In July 2026, permits were filed for a seven-story building at the corner of Westchester Avenue and Reverend James A. Polite Avenue, 895 Reverend James A. Polite Avenue: 74 feet tall, 80 apartments over 7,709 square feet of commercial space and a 535-square-foot community facility, with a cellar, replacing a one-story building on the site (New York YIMBY). A year earlier, an eight-story building at 979 Intervale Avenue was filed at 75 feet with 41 apartments and ground-floor commercial space (Marketproof, July 18, 2025). New York City treats a building as high-rise when an occupied floor sits more than 75 feet above the lowest level of fire department vehicle access, so these filings land right at the point where voice evacuation and a fire command station either come into the design or don't. Construction carries its own risk: in late April 2026 an explosion and fire hit an empty six-story building under construction at 1035 Reverend James A. Polite Avenue. "The way you felt the whole building shake," resident Humphrey Ejyir told News 12.

Then there is the store row. Just before 10:30 p.m. on December 18, 2025, fire broke out in a row of one-story stores at 855 Prospect Avenue (News 12 Bronx; amNY). The building was a single-story retail structure about 75 by 60 feet, with a large abandoned theatre behind it (1st Responder News). "We found fire at a commercial establishment and it was extending to the adjacent stores," FDNY Assistant Chief Kevin Brennan said (amNY). It went to five alarms, drew more than 200 firefighters and EMS personnel, destroyed a 99-cent store, a bodega and a fruit shop, and reached the roof and second floor of the six-story apartment building attached to the row, which was evacuated (ABC7 New York; News 12 Bronx). "I came outside, and the sky was orange. This is not normal," resident Grace Rivera told amNY. The cause was under investigation. A store row that shares walls with a six-story multiple dwelling is exactly the kind of building where the question "whose fire alarm is it?" has to be answered on the drawings.

This page covers commercial and code-required fire alarm systems only. Smoke alarms inside individual one- and two-family homes follow separate rules and are not part of this service.

Building Types

Commercial Fire Alarm Systems for Longwood's Building Types, Old Uses and New

Commercial fire alarm systems are required by use and size, not by neighborhood. These are the building types that bring Longwood owners to a fire alarm design, with the detail that matters most in each.

Dickerson rowhouses rented by the room

The semi-detached houses of the Longwood Historic District were built around 1897 to 1901 for one landowner, George Johnson, on Beck, Kelly and Dawson Streets and Hewitt Place (Forgotten New York). Rented by the room, a house like this is generally treated as a rooming house rather than a family home. According to a summary of the NYC Building Code by NY Engineers, Group R-1 occupancies, the transient category, need a fire alarm unless they meet a set of protection and exit measures, while Group R-2 buildings need one when apartments sit three or more stories above the lowest exit or the building has 16 or more units. Which category an SRO falls into is decided by the design professional from the Certificate of Occupancy and how the rooms are actually rented. Installation means fishing cable through plaster and narrow stairs without opening walls.

A seven-story hospital that became a family shelter

Prospect Hospital at 730 Kelly Street was built as a hospital: corridors, patient rooms and systems meant for people who could not always move themselves (Wikipedia). As the Prospect Family Inn, with a nursery where children can stay for up to 72 hours, the building serves families who sleep there. When a building's use changes like that, the fire alarm design is checked against the new use: where sleeping rooms are, which floors have staff, and whether smoke doors and elevator recall from the hospital years still serve the way the floors are laid out today.

Theatres, synagogues and churches

A movie house is designed for hundreds of seated people facing one way in the dark, often with a balcony. Used as a church, it can carry a similar load. NY Engineers summarizes the NYC trigger for Group A assembly spaces as an occupant load of 300 or more. A synagogue turned church, like United Church on Hewitt Place, may be well under that, or not, depending on seating, fellowship halls and lower-level rooms. The occupant load is calculated by the design professional from the floor area and the use of each room. School fire alarm installation works the other way: the code requires a fire alarm in every school, whatever its size, which is why buildings such as P.S. 39 on Longwood Avenue are designed around one.

One-story store rows beside taller buildings

The Prospect Avenue row that burned in December 2025 was a single story, about 75 by 60 feet, attached to a six-story apartment building and backed by an abandoned theatre (1st Responder News; News 12 Bronx). Mercantile spaces in NYC need a fire alarm at certain sizes and heights, per the NY Engineers summary, and many small stores fall below them. The apartment building next door almost always does need one. Where the two are attached, the design decides whether the stores get their own system, devices on the building's system, or nothing beyond what each store's use requires.

Prewar walk-ups rebuilt in the 1980s and 1990s

Five- and six-story buildings from the 1910s and 1920s, gutted during the abandonment years and rebuilt by community groups and the city, often got the fire alarm that the code of the rehab year required. Replacing those aging panels is usually an upgrade filed with the FDNY, not a like-for-like swap.

New seven- and eight-story mixed-use buildings

Concrete buildings with stores on the ground floor, apartments above, a cellar and sometimes a small community facility, like the filings at 895 Reverend James A. Polite Avenue and 979 Intervale Avenue. Their fire alarm is designed from the start, with the height question settled early.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Read the Certificate of Occupancy Before the Ceiling

Our technicians have a habit in neighborhoods like Longwood: before anyone looks at a ceiling, someone reads the Certificate of Occupancy. In a building with two lives, paperwork and building often disagree. A rowhouse listed as a two-family dwelling may be renting nine rooms. A store listed as retail may have become a storefront church with sixty folding chairs on Sunday. A rehabbed building's panel may have been approved for a layout that later, unfiled renovations changed. The fire alarm design has to follow the use that is real, and the filing has to follow the use that is legal, so the gap between them is the first thing we bring to the owner and the design professional.

The second habit is to ask who sleeps in the building. In a store, a fire alarm protects people who are awake and can walk out. In an SRO, a shelter or an apartment over a store row, it protects people who are asleep at 10:30 at night, which is roughly when the Prospect Avenue fire started in December 2025. That changes how long we let a system stay down during a replacement and how we schedule around residents.

The third is to treat every old panel as a record: photograph its programming and label every circuit before replacing it. These are working habits, written in general terms; we don't describe specific clients' buildings on this page.

Your Questions Answered

Longwood Fire Alarm Questions From Store Owners, SRO Operators, Congregations and Developers

After the Prospect Avenue fire

The Prospect Avenue stores that burned in December were one story with a six-story apartment building attached. When they are rebuilt, does the store row get its own fire alarm or tie into the apartment building's?

Either can work, and the choice is made on the drawings, not on site. If the stores and the apartment building are separate buildings under the code, separated by a rated wall, each is designed on its own, and the stores may not need a building system at all. If they are one building under the code, the stores' devices usually report to the same system as the apartments. Ownership matters too: when the row and the apartment building have different owners, separate systems avoid a store's trouble signal sitting unanswered on a panel in someone else's lobby. The design professional reads the Certificate of Occupancy for both and decides.

An abandoned theatre sat behind the Prospect Avenue stores that burned. Does an empty building next door change what a rebuilt store's fire alarm has to do?

It doesn't change the code requirement for the store, which depends on the store's own use and size. It does change the risk, because an empty building can hide a fire for a long time, and the first sign a store gets may be smoke in its own back room or ceiling. Some owners choose detection along the shared rear wall or in the ceiling space even where the code does not require it. Decide while the ceilings are open.

Landmarked rowhouses, SROs and shelters

Our Dickerson rowhouse in the Longwood Historic District is an SRO. Does a building with rented rooms need a fire alarm system?

Often it does. A house rented by the room is generally treated as a rooming house or another sleeping occupancy, not as a one- or two-family home, and the NYC Building Code requires fire alarms in those occupancies unless specific protection measures are met (NY Engineers, summary of Building Code section 907.2). The Certificate of Occupancy, the number of rooms and how long people stay all matter. Start by finding out what the Certificate of Occupancy says the building is; if it doesn't match how the rooms are rented, that has to be resolved with a design professional before any fire alarm is filed.

Do we need Landmarks Preservation Commission approval to add fire alarm devices to a landmarked Longwood rowhouse?

Interior devices generally do not change anything the Commission regulates, but anything on the outside does: conduit on the façade, a bell or strobe on the front, a communicator antenna on the roof visible from the street. The FDNY's own fire alarm application lists landmark compliance documents among the papers to provide where they apply (NYC Business). Plan exterior work so it either stays out of sight or is approved by the Commission first; it is far easier to route cable through the cellar and rear yard than to explain a new pipe up a 1900 brownstone front.

A family shelter in a former hospital building, like the Prospect Family Inn on Kelly Street: whose fire alarm rules apply when a building's use changes?

The rules for the new use. When a hospital becomes housing, the fire alarm is judged against how the building is used now, through a new or amended Certificate of Occupancy and a fire alarm filing to match. Parts of the old system can sometimes stay, such as the cable in good condition or the elevator recall, but every device has to fit the new layout and the new occupants. In a shelter, that means smoke detection that wakes sleeping families, notification that reaches every room, and a panel staff can read from the front desk.

Churches and old theatres

Our congregation worships in a former Southern Boulevard movie theatre. Is a church in an old theatre treated like an assembly space for the fire alarm?

Yes, a house of worship is an assembly use, and in New York City a fire alarm is generally required once an assembly space reaches an occupant load of 300 (NY Engineers summary). The 550- to 600-seat Art Theatre at 1077 Southern Boulevard, which Forgotten New York found serving as a Pentecostal church, could pass that with its balcony in use. If the balcony is closed and the seating reduced, the load may drop.

Building new at the 75-foot line

The new building filed at 895 Reverend James A. Polite Avenue is 74 feet tall. Is it a high-rise for fire alarm purposes?

Not by the filed height alone, and probably not. New York City's high-rise test looks at the highest occupied floor, measured from the lowest level where fire trucks can reach the building, against 75 feet. A building whose roof is at 74 feet has its top floor well below that. It will still need a full fire alarm as a multifamily building with stores, but not the voice evacuation and fire command station that a high-rise requires.

979 Intervale Avenue was filed at exactly 75 feet. What changes for a fire alarm when a building lands right on the high-rise line?

The design has to be settled early and in writing, because the difference is large. If the highest occupied floor ends up more than 75 feet above fire department access, the job becomes a high rise fire alarm installation with features such as voice evacuation, floor-by-floor notification and a fire command station. If it stays under, it doesn't. A bulkhead, a roof deck or a change in grade at the entrance can move a building across that line, so the measurement is checked again whenever the drawings change.

After the April explosion at the building under construction on Reverend James A. Polite Avenue, what fire protection should a site have before its permanent fire alarm is accepted?

During construction, protection comes from the site safety plan, fire extinguishers, standpipes as the building rises, and a fire watch where required, not from the permanent fire alarm, which isn't accepted until the end. The permanent system should be installed as early as the construction sequence allows and kept free of dust, but it doesn't protect anyone until the FDNY accepts it. The cause of the April 2026 fire at 1035 Reverend James A. Polite Avenue was under investigation when News 12 reported it.

Rehab-era buildings

Our walk-up on Fox Street was rebuilt by a community group in the 1980s. Does the fire alarm from that rehab still meet code?

It may still be legal, because an existing approved system can generally stay in service as long as it is maintained and tested. That's different from being sound. Panels from that era are often out of production, and replacement parts get scarce, so a single failed board can take the system down for weeks. If the building is being renovated, the new work may have to meet current rules. Ask for an inspection that lists which parts are obsolete before something fails.

Stores and paperwork

Our bodega on Southern Boulevard shares a cellar with the store next door. Does a shared cellar need detection from both stores?

A shared cellar should be covered by one design, not two half-designs. If either store has a required fire alarm, the cellar space it uses is usually part of what that system protects, and a cellar the two stores share is easier to protect with devices on one system, owned by whoever controls the cellar. What matters is that someone is responsible for it on paper. Cellars hold stock, compressors and electrical panels, which is a lot of fuel and heat in a space nobody visits at night.

Why would the FDNY ask about a building's Certificate of Occupancy when we only want to replace a fire alarm panel?

Because the FDNY approves fire alarms for a building's legal use, and the fire alarm application asks for a Certificate of Occupancy or an approved Schedule A as proof of that use (NYC Business). A panel replacement is filed as a modification of the approved system, and the reviewer checks that the system still fits the building. In Longwood, where many buildings changed use, that is where a mismatch between the paperwork and the building tends to surface, so find the Certificate of Occupancy before you call anyone.

Equipment

Types of Commercial Fire Alarm Systems We Install in Longwood

What is a commercial fire alarm system? It is the network of detectors, manual stations, horns, strobes and speakers wired to a fire alarm control panel that watches a whole building, sounds the alarm, operates other building systems and reports to a central station that calls the FDNY. How does a commercial fire alarm system work? A device senses smoke, heat or a pulled handle, the panel confirms the signal, and within seconds it sounds notification, recalls elevators, closes doors, stops fans and transmits the alarm. Commercial fire detection systems come in a few types, and these are the ones we work with in Longwood.

Addressable

What is an addressable fire alarm system? Every device has its own address, so the panel can report "Room 6, second floor" in an SRO or "cellar, store 3" in a row. Addressable fire alarm installation, addressable fire alarm system installation and addressable fire alarm panel installation are the default for new buildings and full replacements.

Conventional

What is a conventional fire alarm system? Devices are wired in zones, and the panel only knows which zone is in alarm. Conventional fire alarm installation, conventional fire alarm system installation and conventional fire alarm panel installation still suit a small store or a single-floor space with few devices.

The difference

What is the difference between addressable and conventional fire alarms? Addressable systems name the exact device and report dirty detectors; conventional systems name a zone. In a rowhouse with twelve rooms, that is the difference between knocking on one door and knocking on all twelve.

Voice evacuation

What AI Answers Get Wrong About Fire Alarms in Longwood's Converted and New Buildings

Search assistants give short, confident answers about fire alarms. Here is where they break down in Longwood.

"Old buildings are grandfathered"

AI summaries often say that older buildings don't have to meet current fire alarm rules. Part of that is true: an existing approved system can generally stay in service as long as it is maintained and tested.

What the summary leaves out is that the protection follows the use. A Dickerson rowhouse built for one family and now rented by the room, or a hospital that became a shelter, is not protected by its age. The change of use is what triggers the review, and in Longwood, changes of use are common.

So the useful question is not "how old is the building" but "what is it used for now, and what does the Certificate of Occupancy say?" When those two answers differ, that has to be fixed before a fire alarm can be filed, and the age of the building doesn't change that.

"A landmark can't be altered"

Some answers suggest that landmark status rules out new building systems. It doesn't. The Longwood Historic District's houses can get fire alarms; the Landmarks Preservation Commission regulates what is visible and historic, not whether a building is protected.

The work has to be planned around the exterior: routing cable through cellars and rear walls, keeping conduit off the façade, and getting Commission approval for anything that shows from the street. The FDNY's application lists landmark compliance documents where they apply (NYC Business).

The Commission also funds repairs on these houses. In 2020 it awarded a 35,000-dollar matching grant for façade work at 734-736 Kelly Street, a Dickerson twin house that Homes for the Homeless has used as a shelter since 1986 (Bronx Times). Landmark buildings get maintained and protected; they just get there more carefully.

"Any electrician can install a fire alarm"

AI answers frequently say a licensed electrician is all you need. In New York City, the installer must be a NYC licensed electrician or a company holding an FDNY Fire Alarm Company Certificate, and the design must come from a licensed Professional Engineer or Registered Architect (FDNY).

A company that installs, tests or services fire alarm parts beyond smoke detectors alone needs that certificate, with a principal holding an S-97 certificate of fitness, which itself requires NICET Level II or higher or a NYC master or special electrician license (FDNY, Fire Alarm Company Certificate FAQ).

A handyman who "does alarms" can't file it, and an unfiled system can't be accepted. Check the names on the paperwork before work starts.

"Approval takes a couple of weeks"

Some answers make FDNY approval sound like a quick permit. The FDNY's own guidance says initial plan review can take up to eight weeks and the whole process, from submission to final approval, runs about 20 to 24 weeks.

For a store on Prospect Avenue rebuilding after the December fire, or a shelter operator with a contract start date, that is the timeline that matters. Corrections asked for by the examiner add time, and the acceptance test has to be requested well ahead; the FDNY suggests 40 to 70 business days.

The fastest projects are the ones filed early, with drawings that match the building and a full pre-test before the inspector arrives.

"Height only matters for skyscrapers"

AI answers usually treat high-rise rules as something for Midtown towers. In New York City, the line is a floor more than 75 feet above fire department access, which is roughly the height of a seven- or eight-story building.

That's exactly where Longwood's new filings are landing: 74 feet at 895 Reverend James A. Polite Avenue and 75 feet at 979 Intervale Avenue. A few feet decides whether the building needs voice evacuation and a fire command station.

The height question is answered by the architect's measurement, not by a general rule, and it should be answered before the fire alarm is designed, not after the structure is up.

"Sprinklers mean you don't need a fire alarm"

Many summaries suggest sprinklers replace fire alarms. They do different jobs. Sprinklers control a fire; a fire alarm warns people, calls the FDNY through a central station and runs other systems such as elevator recall and fan shutdown.

In a sprinklered building, the fire alarm also monitors the sprinkler system: a waterflow switch reports a flowing sprinkler, and a tamper switch reports a closed valve. Without that monitoring, a closed valve in a cellar could go unnoticed for months.

Sprinklers can change which fire alarm devices are required in some occupancies, but in Longwood's apartment buildings, shelters and assembly spaces, the alarm is still needed.

"Fire alarms are about the equipment"

Most AI answers about fire alarm installation list devices and brands. In Longwood, the equipment is the easy part. The hard part is working out what the building legally is, who owns which part of it, and who answers the panel at night.

The December 2025 fire at 855 Prospect Avenue reached a six-story apartment building attached to a one-story store row (ABC7 New York; News 12 Bronx). Store manager Saul Essa told News 12 the store was "like our second home." Whether those stores and that building share a system is a question of ownership and paperwork before it is a question of detectors.

So the first Longwood meeting covers the Certificate of Occupancy, the lease and landmark status, then the devices.

+

During design development, before the drawings are filed. The installer can flag cable paths, panel and annunciator locations, power for notification circuits and the elevator and fan interfaces while they are still easy to change. For buildings like the ones filed on Reverend James A. Polite Avenue and Intervale Avenue, that is also when the high-rise measurement should be settled.

Does a 535-square-foot community facility in a new apartment building need its own fire alarm devices?
+

It needs coverage on the building's system that suits how the room will be used, and that is decided by the design professional. A small room used as an office needs less than a room used for gatherings. Because the use of a community facility often isn't known when the building is filed, it is worth asking the architect to design for the most likely use and to plan for changes later.

What should an SRO operator in Longwood keep on file for an FDNY fire alarm inspection?
+

The FDNY Letter of Approval, the approved drawings and as-builts, the Certificate of Occupancy, the central station contract and account number, and test and inspection reports for at least the last three years. Keep a room-by-room device list as well. In a building with many small rooms, inspectors and technicians both work faster when they know which room each detector is in.

Can a store owner on Southern Boulevard reset the fire alarm panel after a false alarm?
+

Only after the cause is known and the FDNY, if it responded, says it is safe. Resetting clears the alarm but not the reason it happened, and a detector that went off once will usually go off again. Write down what the display said before resetting, tell the central station, and have a technician look at the device that started it, especially if it is in a kitchen or a dusty stockroom.

Does a house of worship with fewer than 300 people still need fire alarm devices in Longwood?
+

It may not need a full building system for the assembly space alone, but other parts of the building can still require devices. A church with a day care, a shelter program, apartments above or a large lower-level hall may cross other thresholds. The design professional looks at every use in the building, not only the sanctuary, before deciding.

How are fire alarm devices kept out of sight on a historic rowhouse façade or stoop?
+

Mostly by keeping them inside. Cable is routed through the cellar, rear walls and interior chases, the communicator antenna goes where it can't be seen from the street, and any exterior device is placed and finished so it doesn't change the historic façade. Where something has to show, the Landmarks Preservation Commission approves it first.

Will the FDNY accept a fire alarm on a new building before the ground-floor stores have tenants?
+

Yes, the base building system can be tested and accepted with the stores left as empty shells, covered by the devices the approved drawings show for unoccupied space. When each tenant moves in and fits out the store, the new devices are filed and tested as additions. Planning that sequence on the original drawings keeps a vacant storefront from holding up the building's approval.
