The Neighborhood

Why Vinegar Hill Properties Need Security Camera Repair

Vinegar Hill is one of the smallest neighborhoods in Brooklyn: roughly six blocks between DUMBO and the Brooklyn Navy Yard, running from the waterfront to Front Street and from the Yard to Bridge Street, with Farragut Houses to the south toward the expressway. Its core is the Vinegar Hill Historic District, designated by the Landmarks Preservation Commission in January 1997, which takes in small groups of brick Greek Revival row houses on Front Street, Gold Street and Hudson Avenue, a mid-1850s firehouse and part of a 1908 factory. Hudson Avenue, Plymouth, Water and Front Streets are still paved in Belgian block. That is the background. What matters for a camera repair is narrower: what keeps knocking these particular systems out of service, and how to fix them so they stay fixed.

The first answer is power, and specifically power that sags rather than stops. On June 23, 2025, Con Edison asked about 68,000 customers in parts or all of Dumbo, Vinegar Hill, Brooklyn Heights, Downtown Brooklyn and neighboring areas to conserve energy, and said it had "reduced voltage by 8 percent in the area to protect equipment and maintain service as crews make repairs." Lights dim, refrigerators keep running, and most people forget it by the next morning. Camera equipment does not always forget. A recorder's power supply that is already old can drop out and restart when the voltage sags, sometimes several times in an evening; a battery backup with a tired battery can click over, fail to carry the load and let everything behind it reboot; and a hard drive that is restarted mid-write enough times will eventually mark sectors bad. The camera system looks fine the next day. The gap shows up weeks later in playback.

The second answer is the neighborhood's large neighbors. New York YIMBY reported in January 2024 that demolition was underway at the Hudson Avenue Generating Station for Con Edison's Brooklyn Clean Energy Hub, a transmission substation on the site bounded by Marshall Street, Hudson Avenue, Gold Street and Con Edison's Farragut Station, with barges used to limit truck traffic and completion aimed for 2029. Years of demolition and building next to a residential block mean fine dust settling on everything outdoors, including the clear domes and lens covers of street-facing cameras. Dust film does not kill a camera; it softens the picture a little more each week, scatters infrared light at night into a grey haze, and makes the camera's motion detection less reliable. Owners usually notice when a clip they need turns out to be unreadable. The plant has a history beyond dust, too: DNAinfo reported a small fire there in May 2013 and, in April 2012, a loud noise and a huge cloud of black smoke, after which Brook Stanton of the Vinegar Hill Neighborhood Association said, "There was a lot of smoke damage to nearby homes."

The third answer is the houses themselves. The designation report describes houses built from the early 1800s through the 1870s, many with stores on the ground floor, and notes that newcomers in the 1970s "converted most of the storefronts to residential use, especially along Hudson Avenue." Brownstoner described 50 Hudson Avenue as typical of the older buildings: three stories of brick, two floors of apartments over a storefront. Those houses are still being worked on. City permit records show 69 Hudson Avenue receiving an interior renovation with new ceiling finishes, kitchen and bathroom (a permit issued in December 2024 with an estimated cost of $268,000), floor joists sistered and a rear yard deck in January 2025, and a heat pump split air conditioning system in March 2025; 59 Hudson Avenue took a permit for a new bathroom on its second, third and fourth floors. Every one of those jobs opens walls and ceilings that camera cables pass through, moves outlets the recorder may have depended on, and puts a new electrical load on circuits the cameras share. The calls that follow are among the most common repairs we see in older Brooklyn houses: "two cameras died during the renovation, and nobody touched them."

There is a fourth thread that is easy to miss: water below ground. First Street's flood profile for Vinegar Hill rates the neighborhood's 30-year flood risk as moderate and counts 25 properties at risk, 28.1 percent of the total. In houses whose recorders live on cellar shelves, and in the handful with a sub-cellar below that, a recorder or power box that sits low can be lost to a single wet night, and damp alone shortens the life of drives and connectors. Many Vinegar Hill repairs end with the recorder moved higher or upstairs, not because it failed, but so that the next failure is not caused by where it sat.

Why bother, on streets this quiet? Wikipedia calls Vinegar Hill a "very quiet neighborhood where its residents know one another," and the Belgian-block side streets see few passers-by after dark. That quiet is the reason a working camera counts for more here than on a busy avenue: when something happens on Plymouth Street at two in the morning, there may be no one to see it but the cameras. The 84th Precinct, which covers Brooklyn Heights, Boerum Hill, part of Downtown Brooklyn, DUMBO and Vinegar Hill, reported 143 burglaries in 2026 through November 1, down from 195 at the same point in 2025. The trend is good. The point is that a camera that stopped recording three weeks ago helps no one when the precinct asks. Many owners pair a camera repair with a check of the door buzzer or video intercom at the same door, since on these houses both systems share the entrance.

Building Specialists

Security Camera Repair for Vinegar Hill's Architecture

The same dead camera is a different repair on each kind of Vinegar Hill building, because the mounting surface, the cable path, the recorder's hiding place and the person who signs off all change from one to the next. These are the buildings we plan for.

Front and Gold Street brick rows

Greek Revival row houses of the 1830s to 1850s inside the historic district, some with iron grilles on the basement and first-floor windows and cast-iron fences. Cameras go over the door or under the cornice, and cable drops to a cellar recorder.

Hudson Avenue shop houses

Brick and frame houses whose ground floors were stores until the 1970s, with old shopfront frames, bulkheads and display openings still in place. Cameras are often fixed to that old woodwork.

Clapboard and frame houses

Wood-frame houses such as 51, 53, 55 and 70 Hudson Avenue, some with brick fronts and clapboard sides. Cables go through wood, and water and movement come with it.

Lots with a rear house

A front house on the street and a second, smaller house behind it, like the 1906 rear house at 51 Hudson that the designation report says is "not visible from the street." The rear camera's power and signal travel the length of the lot.

Condos and small rentals

Buildings from the early 2000s on, such as Commodore's Court at 85 Hudson Avenue and The Vista at 206 Front Street, with garages, intercoms and a board or managing agent who owns the system.

Lofts, the firehouse and the factory

The converted 1850s firehouse at 225–227 Front Street, the six-story 1908 factory at 231–233 Front Street and loft buildings toward Bridge Street, with thick masonry, deep floors and long cable runs.

Front and Gold Street rows: a mortar joint, a cornice and a cellar

On the Greek Revival rows the front camera usually sits just above the door, under the cornice, or on the brick beside the doorway, with its cable dropping straight through the front wall to a recorder on a cellar shelf. When that camera dies, the repair is mostly about putting the new one back exactly where the old one was. The LPC's permit guidebook treats ordinary maintenance and repair of safety equipment as work that needs no permit, and its rules for new or moved equipment ask that cameras be fixed at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, never drilled through cast iron or decorative surfaces, with only about ten inches of visible conduit. We reuse the existing anchors where they are sound, and if one has pulled loose from old soft mortar, we reset it in the same joint rather than drilling fresh brick.

The iron on these houses matters at night. The designation report notes security grilles on the basement and first-story windows of 237 and 239 Front Street, original cast-iron fences and gates, and a wrought-iron gate under the stoop. A camera whose infrared lights up bars a foot from its lens will wash out its whole night picture. Turning down the camera's own infrared, moving it a few inches, or adding a separate small illuminator aimed past the iron usually fixes it without a new camera.

Hudson Avenue shop houses: the old woodwork carries the camera

Brownstoner noted that Vinegar Hill's storefronts survive "in varying degrees of original authenticity." On the houses that kept their old shop fronts, the obvious place for a door camera was the wooden frame or fascia over the former shop window, and many installers took it. Wood that has been painted, rained on and dried for well over a century can be soft in exactly the spot where the screws went in, so a camera mounted there slowly tilts, picks up vibration from passing trucks on the Belgian block, and eventually hangs by its cable. When we repair one, we check the wood first. If it is sound, the new camera goes back on it with longer screws into solid wood behind; if it is not, we talk through the two honest options: a small repair to the woodwork by your carpenter before the camera goes back, or a move to the adjacent brick, which on a landmarked house is a change of position and not simply a repair.

The former shop floor also changes where the recorder sits. Where the old store's back room became a kitchen, the recorder often lives in a cupboard beside the refrigerator or on top of a cabinet, sharing an outlet with appliances. That is a common reason for a recorder that restarts whenever the compressor kicks on, and moving it to its own outlet is part of the repair.

Clapboard and frame houses: wood moves, water follows the cable

The designation report describes 51 Hudson Avenue as a wood-frame clapboard house of about 1840–47 by a carpenter and shipbuilder, 53 Hudson as wood-frame behind a brick front, 55 Hudson as wood-frame with a brick facade and an exposed clapboard side wall, and 70 Hudson as clapboard above a stucco base. On houses like these, a camera on the side or rear is screwed through clapboard into wood, and its cable goes in through a hole beside it. If that hole was not sealed and the cable was not given a drip loop, rain follows the cable into the wall and into the connector, and the camera fails months later with green corrosion inside. Clapboard also moves with the seasons, enough to loosen a mount over a few winters. We seal what we open, add a mounting block where the boards are thin, and replace taped connectors with weatherproof ones.

Lots with a rear house: the camera at the far end of a long link

Several Vinegar Hill lots hold two buildings: the house on the street and a second one behind it. The designation report describes a rear house of about 1860–67 behind 204 Front Street and a two-story brick rear house built at 51 Hudson in 1906. Cameras on a rear house, or on the yard wall between the two, reach the recorder in the front house one of three ways: a cable buried or strung across the yard, a wireless link between the buildings, or a camera powered from the rear house's own circuits and sending video over the network. Each has its own way of failing. A cable across a yard gets cut by a gardener's spade or chewed; a wireless link fades when leaves fill in or a new fence goes up; and a camera powered from the rear house goes dark every time that house's power is switched off for work or a breaker trips. We test the link end to end and fix the weak part, which is often cheaper than replacing the camera that gets the blame.

Condos and small rentals: the system belongs to the building

The newer buildings are small by city standards. CityRealty describes The Vista on Vinegar Hill at 206 Front Street as a seven-story, 35-unit condominium completed in 2005, with a part-time doorman, video intercom, bike room, planted courtyard and a private garage on the lower level. Compass lists Commodore's Court at 85 Hudson Avenue as a five-story building of about ten units, built in 2002, with an elevator and voice intercom, and a rental listing at 68 Gold Street mentions an elevator, video intercom and private parking. Their camera systems tend to cover the lobby, the mailboxes, the garage ramp and the bike room, recorded on one box in a locked room. The hardware is often fine; what fails is the chain of people. The developer's installer set it up, the original board member who knew the password moved away, and the managing agent has never logged in. Many repairs in these buildings begin with getting the board back into its own system.

Footage in a building raises questions a house does not. FirstService Residential's guidance for New York co-ops and condos says footage is generally kept 30 to 90 days depending on the building, that access is usually limited to the property manager or a security provider, and that residents should not get copies without board and counsel approval. When we repair a building system, we set users and permissions to match what the board has decided, and we leave the decision itself to the board.

Lofts, the firehouse and the factory: thick walls and long runs

The designation report records the former Constitution Engine Co. No. 7 at 225–227 Front Street, decommissioned and converted to residential use in 1976, with a replacement wood garage door and a roof stairway bulkhead, and the six-story Benjamin Moore factory building of 1908 at 231–233 Front Street, designed by William B. Tubby. Converted industrial buildings like these have masonry walls far thicker than a row house, deep floor plates and cable runs that wander through old shafts. Wi-Fi rarely gets through those walls, so most working systems are wired, and the usual repair is finding where along a long run the signal or power drops. A cable tester and tone probe find it faster than opening anything, and the repair is a splice or a short new section along the existing path.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Three Questions We Ask Before Booking a Vinegar Hill Repair

This is the working method our technicians use on the most common Vinegar Hill call, written as practice rather than a story about any one house. The owner says, "The cameras keep going down." Before we book, we ask three things. First, when did it start, and did anything happen around then: an evening when the lights dimmed, a power cut, work on the house, a contractor in the cellar? Second, where does the recorder sit, and what else is plugged in near it? Third, is the picture bad, or is it missing? A picture that is soft or grey points to the lens, the dome or the light; a picture that is missing points to power, cable or the recorder.

Those three answers sort most calls before anyone climbs a ladder. A system that started dropping after a dim evening and whose recorder shares a power strip with a refrigerator is a power repair, and we bring a supply, a tested backup and a spare drive. A camera that went missing during a kitchen renovation is a cable trace, and we bring the tone probe and the splice kit. A street-facing camera whose picture has slowly gone grey on a block near the Con Edison site is a cleaning and sealing job, and sometimes a hood. We also ask for a photo of the recorder's event log, if you can reach it, because the log often says in plain words "power failure," "video loss" or "disk error" at the time the trouble began. None of this costs anything, and it means the technician arrives with the right part, or tells you before the visit that no part is needed.

Fault by Fault

Sagging Power, Construction Grit, Renovation Damage, Damp Cellars and Long Links: How Vinegar Hill Camera Systems Fail

Sagging power: when the lights dim and the recorder does not cope

A clean outage is simple: everything goes off, and when power returns, everything starts again. A voltage reduction is messier. With supply lowered by several percent, a healthy recorder power supply usually rides through, but an old one running near its limit may drop out and come back, over and over, as the voltage wanders. Multi-camera power boxes for older coax systems behave the same way: their outputs fall, the cameras at the far end of long cables lose power first, and their pictures roll or vanish. PoE switches can shed the cameras that draw the most, typically the ones with infrared or heaters, while keeping the rest. And a battery backup with an old battery may switch over at the first sag, find it cannot carry the load, and let everything behind it restart anyway, which is worse than no backup at all.

The marks this leaves are specific. The recorder's log shows a run of restarts on one evening. The hard drive, interrupted mid-write several times, starts to report errors weeks later. One or two cameras on the longest runs come back unstable. After an event like Con Edison's June 2025 voltage reduction, we check each of those: the recorder's supply under load, the drive's health, the camera power box's outputs at the far end of the run, and the backup's battery with a real load test rather than its own green light. We then set the backup to tell the recorder to shut down cleanly on a long event, so the drive is never cut off mid-write again.

Construction grit: the soft picture that creeps up on you

Dust from demolition and building settles on everything near the site, and a clear camera dome is a perfect collector. The change is gradual enough that nobody notices from day to day: the picture gets a little softer, edges lose detail, and at night the camera's own infrared bounces off the film on its cover and lights up a grey haze in front of the lens. Rain can wash some of it away, so the picture improves for a day and then slides again. Owners often read that as a camera that comes and goes. The fix is a proper cleaning of the dome or cover with the right materials, a check that dust has not got inside the housing through a failed seal, and on the most exposed cameras a hood or a slightly different angle so the cover catches less. Where a dome has been scratched by being wiped dry with grit on it, the cover itself is replaced.

Renovation damage: the cameras nobody touched

When a house is renovated, camera cables meet every trade. Ceilings come down and go back up; the cable that ran across the old ceiling gets a drywall screw through it, or the junction box where two cables were joined is plastered over. An electrician moves the outlet the recorder plugged into. A plumber shuts off a circuit for a day, and the recorder comes back with the wrong date. A carpenter sisters the floor joists, as the permit for 69 Hudson Avenue describes, and the cable stapled to the old joist is pinched by the new one. None of the trades did anything wrong by their own lights; they simply did not know the cable was there. We trace a dead run with a tone probe from the recorder end, find the point where signal stops, and open only that spot. If the old path is now buried for good, we run a new one along a route that will stay accessible.

A renovation also adds new electrical loads. A heat pump system, like the one permitted at 69 Hudson Avenue in March 2025, has a compressor and fans that start and stop through the day. If the cameras or recorder share a circuit with any of that, each start can cause a brief dip that shows up as cameras blinking off. Putting the camera system on its own outlet, behind a properly sized backup, solves it.

Damp cellars and sub-cellars: where the recorder sits decides how long it lives

Recorders are usually put wherever the cables met, which in a row house is the cellar. In older Vinegar Hill houses that can mean a stone or brick cellar that stays damp all summer, and in a few cases a sub-cellar below it; the designation report records a sub-cellar at 67 Hudson Avenue with a doorway to a tunnel under the street. Damp corrodes connectors and drive contacts, and a recorder on the floor is one bad storm from a total loss. A good repair moves the recorder and its power box onto a high shelf or upstairs, extends the cables to reach, and runs a network cable to the router so the phone app connects reliably rather than over weak Wi-Fi from deep underground.

Long links: rear houses, yard walls and the far end of a loft

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Repair in Vinegar Hill: Questions Answered

Old shop fronts, rear houses and deep cellars

The dead camera on our Hudson Avenue house was screwed into the wooden frame of the old storefront, and the wood around the screws has gone soft. Can the replacement go back on that frame, or does it have to move to the brick?

It can go back on the frame if the wood a little deeper in is still sound, and we check that with a probe before anything is mounted. Longer screws into solid wood, or a small mounting plate that spreads the load over a wider area, usually hold well. If the frame is rotten through, the honest choice is a woodwork repair by your carpenter first, then the camera, or a move onto the brick beside it. On a house inside the historic district, putting the camera back where it was is a repair; moving it to new brick is a change of position, and the LPC guidebook's rules on attachment and spacing apply, so we would discuss that with you before drilling.

The small house at the back of our lot has its own electric meter, and its two cameras are powered there while the recorder sits in the front house. Every time the rear house's power is off, the recorder logs hours of video loss. Is something broken?

Not necessarily; the recorder is reporting exactly what happened. The cameras lost power when the rear house did, so the recorder saw no video from them. The real question is whether you want those cameras to keep working when the rear house is shut off for work or a breaker trips. If you do, the cameras can be powered from the front house instead, over the same network cable using PoE from a switch next to the recorder, which also puts both houses' cameras behind one battery backup. If the cable between the houses cannot carry power, a small backup in the rear house does the same job. We test which arrangement your existing cable allows before suggesting either.

Our recorder is in the sub-cellar, a level below the cellar, and the phone app takes ages to load or shows the cameras as offline. Is the recorder too far down to work?

The recorder works fine two levels down; it is its connection to the internet that suffers. If it reaches the router over Wi-Fi, two floors of brick, stone and earth leave very little signal, and the app times out. A network cable from the recorder up to the router, or to a small switch near it, fixes the connection completely. While we are down there, we look at the conditions: a sub-cellar that stays damp is hard on drives and connectors, and moving the recorder up a level, with longer camera cables, can be the better repair.

Renovations on the landmarked blocks

Two cameras died during our renovation, and the contractor says nobody touched their cables. The new ceilings went up right where those two cables were joined. Do the ceilings have to come down?

Usually not. A splice or junction box buried behind new drywall or plaster is still there, and the fault is often a screw through the cable near it or a connector crushed when the board went up. We put a tone on each dead cable at the recorder and follow it with a probe to the point where the signal stops, then open only that spot, typically a hole a few inches across that your contractor can patch. If the buried joint turns out to be the problem, we move it into a small access panel or run a new length of cable so there is no hidden joint in the future.

Our renovation added a heat pump, and its outdoor unit now sits a few feet below our yard camera. Since then the camera sends motion alerts all day and the night picture shimmers. Is the camera faulty?

Probably not. The unit's fan spinning and its warm exhaust rising past the lens both look like motion to a camera, and the heat haze makes the picture ripple, especially at night under infrared. We can mask the unit out of the camera's detection area, switch to person detection if the camera supports it, and if the shimmer is in the part of the view you care about, raise or shift the camera a little so it looks past the exhaust rather than through it. If the camera shares a circuit with the unit and blinks off when the compressor starts, we move it onto its own outlet as well.

Living beside the Con Edison site

Our street camera faces the Con Edison site on Hudson Avenue. Since the demolition began, its picture gets a little softer every week, then sharpens for a day after rain. What is going on, and what fixes it?

That pattern is dust film on the camera's cover. Fine dust settles on the dome or front glass, softening the picture gradually, and rain rinses some of it off, so it improves briefly and then slides back. At night the camera's infrared bounces off the film and adds a glowing haze. The fix is a proper cleaning with materials that do not scratch the cover, a check that the housing seal has not let dust inside, and for a camera that faces the site directly, a hood or a hooded bullet camera whose flat glass is easier to keep clear. Because the construction is expected to continue until about 2029, a cleaning every few months is cheaper than waiting until the picture is unusable.

Our recorder sits on a battery backup that clicks and beeps whenever the lights dim. Is that the backup doing its job, or is it failing?

A click when the lights dim is normal: the backup is switching to battery or correcting the voltage. Constant beeping, a beep that will not stop, or a recorder that restarts anyway when the lights dim are signs that the battery is worn or the backup is too small for what is plugged into it. Most backup batteries need replacing every few years. We load-test the backup with the recorder, switch and power box connected, replace the battery or the unit if it cannot carry them, and connect the backup to the recorder so a long event ends in a clean shutdown instead of a hard cut.

Condos, lofts and part-time staff

Our Front Street condo has a doorman for only part of the day. When the desk is empty, who finds out if the garage camera goes dark, and can the alerts follow the desk's hours?

Yes. Most recorders and their apps can send camera-loss, drive and recording alerts to more than one person, and many can do it on a schedule. A common setup is alerts to the desk screen while it is staffed and to two or three board members or the managing agent outside those hours, with the managing agent getting everything by email as a record. We set it up the way the board decides, test each alert by unplugging a camera, and show the desk and the board what the alert looks like so nobody dismisses it as a glitch.

Our small condo has no managing agent, and the owner who looked after the cameras sold and moved away without leaving the recorder password. Who can authorize a repair and new logins?

The board, or whoever your bylaws name to act for the condominium, authorizes it; the system belongs to the building, not to the owner who used to run it. We ask for that authorization in writing, then reset the recorder's admin access using the manufacturer's recovery method, which for many models means a code request tied to the recorder's serial number. Where that is not possible, a factory reset clears the password at the cost of the settings, which we then rebuild. We set at least two board members as administrators so the system never again depends on one person.

Our loft building's corridors are long, and the cameras at each end show faces at the far end too small to make out. Is that a repair or a new system?

Usually a repair. A wide camera at the end of a long corridor in a converted industrial building spreads its picture over the whole hall, so a face forty feet away is a few pixels. Replacing it with a varifocal camera and zooming in on the stretch that matters, usually the elevator and stair doors, or adding one camera at the midpoint, solves it without touching the recorder. We check the recorder's free channels first so you know whether an extra camera fits the current system.

Quiet streets and the 84th Precinct

After a car was broken into on our block, the precinct asked the few houses with street cameras for video. Ours had nothing, because the recorder was set to record only motion inside our own doorway. Can it keep the street too without filling the drive?

Yes. The motion zone that was drawn tightly around your door is a setting, not a limit of the camera. The simplest fix is continuous recording on the street camera at a sensible quality, with motion events marked on the timeline so they are easy to find. A typical drive holds well over a week of continuous recording from a handful of cameras; we size the drive and the stream together so you keep at least that. If you prefer motion recording, we redraw the zones to include the sidewalk and the street and tune the sensitivity so a person or car crossing triggers it but blowing leaves do not.

The Belgian block on our street makes delivery trucks rattle past, and our facade camera's picture jumps whenever one goes by. Is the mount loose, and can it be made steady?

A picture that jumps with traffic almost always means the mount or the wall behind it is passing on the vibration, and a little play in a bracket becomes a lot of movement at the lens. We tighten or replace the bracket, check that its anchors are set firmly in a mortar joint rather than crumbling brick, and if the camera sits on a long arm, shorten the arm or brace it. Turning on the camera's digital stabilization, where it has one, smooths what is left. A shaking picture also fires false motion alerts, so a steadier mount usually quiets the phone too.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Vinegar Hill

Search summaries and home-service sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr give general advice about fixing cameras. Some of it holds everywhere. Some of it misses how systems fail on these particular blocks. Here are the claims we hear most, and what we find on Vinegar Hill repairs.

"A power outage is the main power risk to a camera system"

Outages matter, but in this part of Brooklyn the quieter risk is a voltage reduction. Con Edison's June 2025 notice named Vinegar Hill among neighborhoods where it lowered voltage by 8 percent while crews made repairs. Nothing goes dark, so nobody checks the cameras afterward.

Old recorder supplies and multi-camera power boxes can drop out and restart repeatedly through a sag, and each hard restart is another chance to damage the drive. The result is often a recorder that looks fine but has gaps in its recordings from that evening on.

After any dim evening, a two-minute check of yesterday's playback tells you more than the live view. On a repair we read the recorder's log for restarts and load-test the supply and backup.

"Clean the lens with a soft cloth and the picture will be clear"

For a camera indoors, often true. For a dome facing a demolition and construction site, wiping it dry can drag grit across the plastic and leave fine scratches that scatter light permanently, especially at night under infrared.

The right sequence is to rinse loose grit off first, clean with a lens-safe solution, and check the housing seal so dust has not got inside, where no amount of wiping helps.

If a dome is already scratched, the cover is replaced, at $30 to $80 for the part plus labor on our Brooklyn list, or the camera is swapped for a hooded bullet that is easier to keep clean.

"If a camera shows no video, the camera is dead"

On a Vinegar Hill lot the camera that shows no video is often at the far end of a long link: across a yard to a rear house, through a renovation's new ceiling, or on a rear house's own electricity. The camera itself is frequently fine.

Replacing it without testing the power, the cable and the connection at each end means paying for a new camera that shows exactly the same "no video" screen.

We test in order from the outlet to the phone, so the part that failed is the part that gets replaced, and nothing else.

"Just restart the recorder"

A restart clears some software faults. On a recorder whose drive has been weakened by repeated brownout restarts, it can make things worse: some recorders come back asking to format a drive they can no longer read, and the log that would show when recording stopped can be lost.

If you need footage, leave the recorder running and call. If you do not, one restart is fine to try, but photograph the screen first.

If the fault returns after a restart, that is the recorder pointing at its drive or its power, and that is when it should be looked at.

"A replacement hard drive will bring back missing footage"

It will not. As one New York repair firm states, "Installing or formatting a new hard drive does not recover footage previously lost because of a failed drive." A new drive records from the moment it goes in.

What sometimes can be saved is video still on the old drive. That is why we copy what can be read from a failing drive before replacing it, and why the old drive is handed back to you rather than thrown away.

The real protection is alerts: a recorder that tells someone within hours when its drive starts failing loses days, not weeks.

"You need a permit to touch cameras on a landmarked house"

Not for a repair. The LPC's permit guidebook treats ordinary maintenance and repair of health, safety and utility equipment as work that needs no permit, and a dead camera replaced in the same place is that kind of work.

The guidebook's rules apply to new or moved equipment: cameras well spaced, no more than two per 25 feet of frontage on a small building, fixed at mortar joints or plain surfaces and never through cast iron or decorative elements, with little visible conduit.

The Department of Buildings is not involved either: the NYC Administrative Code says no electrical permit is required for wiring designed to operate under 50 volts.

"Camera repair costs about $100 to $300"

That range describes one consumer camera in an easy spot. HomeAdvisor's Brooklyn page puts the average surveillance camera install or repair at $1,416, with a typical range of $680 to $2,145 across job sizes, and published NYC diagnostic calls run $150 to $250 before parts.

On our Brooklyn list, a setting or connector fix is covered by the $150 service call or runs $150 to $350, a single camera replacement $250 to $600, and a recorder replacement $700 to $1,500. You see your number in writing first.

The expensive repairs are the ones done twice, such as a camera replaced when the backup was the problem. Testing in order avoids that.

On rainy nights the wet Belgian block in front of our Water Street house throws the street lamps back at our camera, and the picture streaks white. Is there a fix short of a new camera?
+

Usually, yes. Wet stone acts like a mirror, so a camera aimed low across the street sees a row of bright spots where the lamps reflect, and its exposure drops until everything else goes dark or streaks. We start with the aim and the settings: tilting the view so the shiny patch is a smaller part of the frame, turning on the backlight and wide dynamic range modes, and lowering the camera's own infrared, which also bounces off the wet surface. That work is normally covered by the service call. If the camera simply cannot handle the contrast, we price a replacement that copes better in low light.

While the dead camera on our landmarked Gold Street house is being replaced, we would like to add a second one. Does the Landmarks Commission limit how many cameras a narrow house front can carry?
+

It does set a limit. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's permit guidebook says cameras should be well spaced, with no more than two per 25 feet of frontage on a small building, attached at mortar joints or plain surfaces and not through decorative work, with very little visible conduit. A camera replaced in the same spot is ordinary repair, but a second camera is new work, so it has to meet those rules, and it is worth checking with the Commission before it goes up. Most Vinegar Hill houses are narrow enough that two cameras is the practical maximum on the front.

If there is ever another fire at the Con Edison site, what should we have checked on our cameras afterward?
+

Three things. After the 2012 incident at the plant, Brook Stanton of the Vinegar Hill Neighborhood Association said there was a lot of smoke damage to nearby homes, and smoke leaves a film on domes and lenses that looks like ordinary haze but does not wipe off with water. Second, if the power dipped or cut during the event, the recorder, drive and battery backup should be tested.
