The Neighborhood

Why NYC Properties Need GeoVision Camera Installation

Most cameras in New York City hang on buildings that were designed long before anyone imagined a camera. The Citizens Housing & Planning Council's housing-stock analysis says more than 80 percent of the city's housing was built more than fifty years ago, and that over half of all units, about two million, were built before 1947. The Rent Guidelines Board's 2026 Housing Supply Report puts it a slightly different way: 54 percent of units are in buildings constructed before 1947, out of 3,705,000 housing units the city counted in 2023. That age shows up in every camera job. Prewar walls are brick, terracotta block or plaster on lath. Hallways are narrow. Basements were built for coal, not for a network rack. A camera system in a building like that lives or dies on how the cable gets from the front door to the recorder, and on whether the person who installed it left anyone a map.

The building types are as varied as the boroughs. One investor guide to prewar housing sorts the old stock into four-to-six-story walk-up tenements built before 1929, eight-to-twelve-story elevator apartment houses from the 1910s and 1920s, and the larger prewar doorman buildings. CHPC's own summary runs from mansions in Riverdale to small homes in Midland Beach, garden apartments in Sunnyside to high-rises near Central Park, brownstones to tenements. A rental-market guide that sampled prewar listings found 47.7 percent of those buildings had fewer than ten apartments and 41.5 percent listed an elevator. Put plainly, a large share of the city's older apartment buildings are small walk-ups, where the cable from a rear-yard camera to a cellar recorder has to travel through masonry and up a stairwell with no drop ceiling to hide in. At the other end, the 2023 Housing and Vacancy Survey counted 829,200 units, 22 percent of the stock, in buildings of 100 apartments or more, where cameras run through risers and elevator traveling cables and the recorder may be watched by a security desk.

GeoVision is one of the camera and recording brands New York installers have been putting into those buildings for years. A Manhattan competitor's Upper East Side page lists GeoVision among the brands it partners with, alongside Sony and Axis, and AESS's own CCTV site lists GeoVision among more than a dozen brands it works on, from Dahua and Hikvision to Lorex and Rhombus. That matters because the GeoVision call we expect most often in New York is not a brand-new building. It is an owner, a managing agent or a co-op board that has inherited a GeoVision system along with the boiler and the intercom, and now needs to know whether it still works, whether it is safe to leave on the network, and what to put in when it is time to replace it.

That second question became urgent in late 2024. Security Affairs reported that researchers at the Shadowserver Foundation watched a botnet exploiting a zero-day flaw in end-of-life GeoVision devices, tracked as CVE-2024-11120, a pre-authentication command injection rated 9.8 on the CVSS scale. The same report counted roughly 17,000 internet-facing GeoVision devices exposed to it, with the largest share, 9,179, in the United States. Cybersecurity Help listed the affected models as the GV-VS12, GV-VS11, GV-DSP LPR V3 and GV-LX4C V2 and V3, all of them past end of life and no longer supported by the manufacturer. In May 2025, The Hacker News reported that the U.S. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency had added two GeoVision flaws to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, giving federal agencies until May 28, 2025 to apply fixes or stop using the products. We have no data on how many of those exposed devices sit in New York City buildings. We do know that a city full of old buildings and long-serving camera systems is exactly where unsupported recorders tend to stay plugged in.

Then there is the reason people buy cameras in the first place. In Astoria, CBS New York reported, a suspect used a screwdriver to disable a lobby door lock and then helped himself to the deliveries inside. In Harlem, at the Robeson on Lenox Avenue, CBS reported that furniture was stolen from a lobby where a key code is required to enter, and police said there was no forced entry. In Park Slope, CBS showed three suspects cutting open packages inside a building's lobby. A property manager interviewed by Brick Underground pointed out that face masks make people hard to identify on camera. None of those stories involves GeoVision equipment as far as the reports say, and we do not claim they do. They show the job a lobby camera in New York actually has to do: see the face at the door at a usable angle, record clearly enough under hallway lighting, and keep the footage long enough for a manager to find it after a tenant complains.

Finally, there are the rules. There is no general New York City law that requires cameras in apartment buildings, as one building-compliance guide puts it, though HPD may recommend them where crime-related violations keep recurring and many commercial insurers set camera coverage as a condition of theft and vandalism coverage. The rules that do exist are about the doors and the light. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires an intercom at the self-locking main entrance of every class A multiple dwelling of eight or more apartments built or converted after January 1, 1968. HPD's self-closing door inspections produced 3,151 violations in fiscal year 2024 and 2,937 in fiscal year 2025. The Multiple Dwelling Law also requires adequate lighting in vestibules, halls, stairs and courtyards, which is the difference between footage and a grey smear. A camera at the front door of a New York building works alongside all of that hardware, not instead of it.

We found no hyperlocal coverage in Patch, BK Reader or the Bronx Times about GeoVision equipment, and no Yelp complaints about camera installers that we could tie to this brand. Because so many New York buildings change hands, managers and supers more often than they change cameras, the most useful thing we can do on a first GeoVision visit is answer three questions: what exactly is installed, is any of it exposed to the internet, and which pieces are still worth keeping. Finding the end-of-life units first

The CVE-2024-11120 reports named specific models, and the first job is simply reading labels. In a building where the system has grown over the years, that can mean a recorder in the cellar, a video server on a shelf in the super's apartment, an older camera above the rear yard door and a newer one in the lobby, each installed at a different time. We photograph every label, note the firmware where the device still lets us log in, and list the results for the owner in plain language. If any device matches the end-of-life models in the reports, we unplug its network connection before we leave, even if we cannot replace it that day. BeyondMachines' summary of the advisory recommended isolating affected devices from the internet, performing factory resets, and ideally replacing them with supported models, and we follow that order. A factory reset is only done once the footage on the device has been dealt with, because it would erase settings and may make old recordings inaccessible.

Deciding what to keep

Not every older GeoVision piece has to go. A supported camera with a sharp picture, good cable and a sensible view can often stay on a new recorder, and keeping it saves the owner the cost of a new camera and the disruption of a new cable run through a plaster stairwell. Whether a given camera can talk to a given recorder depends on both models and their firmware, so we test it on site rather than assume. What we will not do is leave an end-of-life unit on the network, or recommend spending money to extend a recorder whose maker no longer supports it. When the honest answer is that a camera still works but the recorder behind it does not deserve to, we say so, and we price the replacement recorder separately so the owner can decide.

Handling the footage on an old recorder

Owners are often more worried about the footage on an old recorder than the recorder itself, especially if there is an open insurance claim, a tenant dispute or a police request. We never wipe a recorder we have just been asked to audit. If footage needs to be kept, we export the clips the owner identifies, by date and camera, to a drive the owner keeps, and we note where the original recorder and its hard drive are stored. Only after the owner has what they need do we reset, retire or remove the unit. If you are not sure what footage matters, ask your managing agent or lawyer before we arrive; we can wait, but we cannot undo an erased disk.

Rebuilding remote viewing without open ports

The common thread in exposed camera systems is a router port opened years ago so someone could watch the cameras from a phone. The GeoVision GV-Eye installation guide describes a different route, its GV-Relay function, which by default uses port 443, with port 9443 recommended when 443 is already in use by another service such as WebCam SSL or RTSP. Where the equipment supports a relay or similar method, we use it instead of forwarding ports straight to the recorder. Where an older unit only supports open ports, that is one more reason it belongs on the replacement list. Either way, the owner leaves with phone viewing that works, accounts with their own passwords, and a router that no longer advertises the recorder to the internet.

Decide what each camera is for

The most useful thing an owner can do before a site visit is write one sentence for each camera: "see who comes in the front door," "see the mailboxes," "see the rear yard gate," "see the register." That sentence decides the lens, the height and the angle. A camera meant to identify faces at a front door has to be close and roughly level with a person's head, while a camera meant to show whether a yard gate was left open can sit high and wide. In New York lobbies, where people often come in with hoods or masks, a second camera on the inside of the vestibule, looking at the inner door, often catches the face the outside camera missed. We agree those purposes with you on site and design around them.

Look at the light at night

Walk your building after dark once before we come. Note any hall where a bulb is out, any stairwell lit by a single fixture at one end, any courtyard that goes black when the street lights are behind a wall. Cameras with infrared night vision can see in darkness, but a bright fixture shining straight into a lens, or a glass door reflecting the lobby lights at night, can ruin a picture as easily as darkness. Because the Multiple Dwelling Law already requires adequate lighting in vestibules, halls, stairs and courtyards, fixing a dead fixture is usually the owner's job anyway, and it is far cheaper than a camera upgrade that cannot fix bad light.

Get the paperwork moving early

One installer guide notes that co-op boards, condo associations and commercial landlords usually want a formal scope of work and a compliant certificate of insurance before any contractor starts. In our experience the slowest part of a New York camera job is often waiting for that approval, not the work. Send us the managing agent's requirements, including the exact names they want on the insurance certificate, as soon as you start thinking about the job. We will send the scope of work in writing, listing each camera, each cable route and each wall penetration, so the board is approving the same job we will actually do.

GeoVision Camera Installation for NYC's Architecture

A camera system has to fit the building it is in. In New York, three things change from one building type to the next: how the cable gets from camera to recorder, what the camera is fixed to, and who has to approve the work. These are the building types the city's housing data and history describe, and how we approach a GeoVision job in each.

Prewar Walk-Up Tenements

The four-to-six-story buildings, mostly before 1929, often on lots about twenty-five feet wide. Masonry walls, narrow stairs, no ceilings to hide cable, and a cellar that becomes the equipment room.

Prewar Elevator Apartment Houses

Eight-to-twelve-story buildings from the 1910s and 1920s, with interior risers, service elevators and supers. Cable follows the risers; elevator cab cameras are wired through the traveling cable.

Co-ops and Condos

Board-run buildings where common areas belong to the corporation or association. Scope of work, certificate of insurance and board approval come before any drilling.

Rowhouses and Brownstones

The rowhouses that spread through Brooklyn, the Bronx and Queens as they were linked to Manhattan. Brick or brownstone fronts, stoops, rear yards, and often Landmarks review on the facade.

Large Postwar Complexes

Buildings of 100 or more apartments, which hold 22 percent of the city's units. Long cable runs, many entrances, security desks and network rooms on several floors.

Mixed-Use and Storefront Buildings

Shops at street level with apartments above, on commercial corridors in every borough. Two sets of owners' interests, store hours to work around, and sidewalk-facing cameras.

Walk-ups: the masonry and the stairs

The Lower East Side and East Village, as the Wikipedia history of the tenement laws describes, are still lined with five-story pre-law and six-story Old Law tenements, with bulkier New Law tenements on the corners. Greenwich Village Society's account of the 1901 Tenement House Act explains that the older buildings were squeezed onto lots usually twenty-five feet wide, while most New Law tenements sat on lots thirty-five feet wide or more. For a camera system that means two things. First, there is no drop ceiling and rarely any spare chase, so cable either travels in a neat surface raceway up the stair hall or follows an existing path such as an old dumbwaiter shaft or pipe chase. Second, almost every wall a camera is fixed to is masonry or plaster over masonry. We drill test holes, look at the dust, choose anchors suited to old brick, and set them in the brick face rather than the mortar joint wherever the camera base allows. In a five-story walk-up the recorder usually ends up in the cellar, near the building's internet line, inside a locked cabinet away from the boiler's heat and any water.

Elevator buildings: risers and traveling cables

Prewar elevator buildings from the 1910s and 1920s, and the larger doorman buildings that followed, give a camera system somewhere to run: the risers that carry other building services floor to floor. AESS's own apartment-building camera page describes the method we use there: drops pulled through the interior riser, rated penetrations fire-stopped, and the elevator cab camera wired through the elevator's traveling cable. That last part is coordinated with the building's elevator maintenance contractor, because the traveling cable is part of the elevator, not ours to modify freely. The Gowanus Houses participatory-budgeting proposal that asked for cameras "in elevators and near apartment doors" shows how often residents of large buildings name the elevator as the place they feel least safe, and it is a camera position we plan carefully: high in a corner, looking at the door, with nothing pointing at the control panel's keypad.

Co-ops and condos: the board's building

In a co-op, the hallway and lobby are common areas controlled by the board, as one New York apartment-camera guide puts it, so a shareholder cannot put hardware there alone. Building-wide camera replacements usually need board approval and must meet the building's own standards. HPD's 2021 Housing and Vacancy Survey found that only 12 percent of units in prewar buildings were co-ops or condos, about 70 percent of those co-ops, so many of the oldest board-run buildings in the city are prewar co-ops with old plaster, old wiring and exacting alteration rules. Our approach there is paperwork first: a written scope listing every camera position, every penetration and every hour of work, a certificate of insurance naming whoever the managing agent requires, and a schedule inside the building's permitted working hours. If the board wants the recorder in a particular room, or wants footage access limited to the managing agent, we design around that.

Rowhouses and brownstones: stoops, yards and Landmarks

The Architectural League's history of New York housing describes rowhouses spreading into Brooklyn, the Bronx and Queens as those boroughs were linked to Manhattan, and many of those streets now sit inside historic districts. A rowhouse camera system is usually small, a camera at the front door or vestibule, one at the rear yard, perhaps one at a garden-level entrance, but the facade is where the complications live. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's rules, in Section 2-20, cover health, safety and utility equipment, explicitly including security cameras, and its Permit Guidebook chapter on that equipment sets criteria such as size and finish under which staff can approve cameras without a full hearing. A real LPC staff approval we found covered nine security cameras at a building's ground floor, five on the west facade and three on the east, citing Section 2-20(c)(4). For an owner, the practical rule is to check status before drilling into a front facade, choose small cameras in a finish that suits the masonry, and favour positions inside the vestibule or under the stoop where they meet the purpose without changing the street face.

Large complexes: distance and many doors

Big postwar complexes bring a different problem: distance. Standard Ethernet runs have a length limit, and a camera at the far end of a garage or the back of a long courtyard may be beyond it from the main equipment room. In those buildings we plan intermediate network closets with their own PoE switches, connected back to the recorder, and we run cable on J-hooks across ceilings or in conduit in garages and service areas. Large complexes also tend to have many entrances, and each needs its own decision about what the camera should see. The Atlantic Plaza Towers dispute in Brownsville, which The Guardian reported on in 2019, is a reminder that residents of big buildings care intensely about how entry technology is used: the two high-rises already had cameras watching the grounds, doorways, elevators and halls, and it was the landlord's plan to add facial-recognition entry that tenants fought. We install cameras to record entrances and common areas, not to identify residents at their own doors.

Mixed-use buildings: two sets of interests

Where a store sits under apartments, the shop's cameras and the building's cameras are often owned by different people with different needs. The shop wants its register, doors and stockroom; the landlord wants the residential entrance, the mailboxes and the stairs. We usually keep those systems separate, on separate recorders and separate network connections, so each party controls its own footage and nobody's tenant ends up with a view of someone else's space. Where they must share cable pathways, such as through a basement, we label every run by owner as well as by camera.

Rules and practical limits for business cameras

  • No cameras in restrooms or changing areas. New York Labor Law §203-c bars employers from video recording in restrooms, locker rooms and changing areas, as one recording-law summary explains. We keep every view clear of those spaces, including views through a door that may open.
  • Analytics can trigger signage. A law firm's summary of NYC's biometric identifier law notes that ordinary CCTV appears to be exempt from its signage requirement as long as the business does not use software to analyze the footage for identifying features. If a business turns on facial recognition or similar analysis, the city's rules require a sign at every customer entrance at least 8.5 by 11 inches. We set cameras up without biometric analysis unless the owner asks for it and understands the rule.
  • Low-voltage cabling and the DOB. New power circuits are different; where a camera location needs a new outlet, that is an electrician's job and our quote says so.

  • Hidden cameras are a crime. Penal Law §250.45, as one apartment-law guide explains, makes covert recording in private spaces a class E felony. Business cameras are installed in plain view.

Field Notes: The First Hour With an Inherited GeoVision Recorder

We do not publish stories about a particular customer's building unless we can document them, so this is not a tale about one address. It is the routine our technicians follow when a New York owner or manager says, "There's a GeoVision box in the cellar and nobody knows anything about it."

We photograph the recorder before we touch it, front, back and label, so we have a record of how it was found. We note which cables are plugged into it and trace the network cable to the router. Only after that first hour do we talk about what to replace. In buildings where systems have outlived their installers, that order is what keeps evidence safe and stops us from replacing anything that still works.

Your Questions Answered

GeoVision Camera Installation in NYC: Questions Answered

Old GeoVision systems already in the building

Our recorder says GeoVision on the front and nobody has the password anymore. Can it be checked without losing what's recorded on it?

Usually yes, and that is the way we prefer to do it. We start by photographing the unit and its label, then look at what can be learned without logging in: the model, the cables, whether its network connection reaches the router and whether any ports are forwarded to it. If the recorder has to be reset to regain access, we first discuss with you whether its hard drive holds footage you need, because resetting a recorder can make older recordings harder to reach. Where footage matters, we arrange to export it or keep the drive before anything is reset. If the unit turns out to be one of the end-of-life models, the better path is often to retire it rather than spend time recovering access to equipment its maker no longer supports.

Is the old GeoVision video server in our cellar one of the models the botnet went after?

Read the label and compare it with the list. Cybersecurity Help's write-up of CVE-2024-11120 named the GV-VS12, GV-VS11, GV-DSP LPR V3 and GV-LX4C V2 and V3 as affected models, all past end of life and no longer supported by the manufacturer. If yours matches, disconnect its network cable and call us; the advice published with the vulnerability was to isolate affected devices from the internet, factory reset them, and ideally replace them with supported models. If yours does not match, it is still worth checking its firmware and whether it is reachable from the internet, because the same report counted about 17,000 exposed GeoVision devices, and an unpatched, internet-facing recorder is a risk whatever its model.

We have a mix of GeoVision and other brands from different installers. Can the new system tie them together?

Often it can, but only testing tells. Whether one maker's camera works on another maker's recorder depends on the specific models and firmware on both sides, and on which standard connection methods each supports. On site we connect each existing camera to the proposed recorder and check that it streams, records and plays back properly before we promise to keep it. Cameras that pass stay; cameras that do not, or that are themselves end-of-life, go on the replacement list with their own line on the quote. We would rather tell you that two old cameras need replacing than leave a system that records nine cameras reliably and the tenth only sometimes.

Permits, licenses and Landmarks

Does running camera cable through our building need a Department of Buildings permit?

For the low-voltage cable itself, usually not, provided the right person does it. Local Law 128 of 2024 added Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, which took effect on December 21, 2025 with the 2025 NYC Electrical Code and says an electrical permit is not required for low-voltage work under 50 volts when it is done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician, or a qualified person as defined in the code. Two things can change that answer: new line-voltage power for a camera, which is electrical work for an electrician, and cabling through fire-rated walls or floors in commercial buildings, which one compliance guide says may require a permit. We confirm the route with the building before quoting so you know which applies.

Does whoever installs our cameras in New York need a license, and which one?

Yes. The city's own business portal explains that installing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems requires a license from the New York State Department of State, and the state regulation behind it, 19 NYCRR 195.2(b), covers closed circuit television used, full or part time, to detect or monitor intrusion, break-in, theft, movement, sound or fire. That is the license AESS holds, NYS license #12000287431. A Long Island law firm has also reported that the Department of State treats a video intercom as a small CCTV system for licensing purposes. Before hiring anyone, ask for the license number and look it up with the Department of State; it takes a minute and tells you who you are letting into your building.

Our brownstone sits in a historic district. Are there limits on putting a camera on the front facade?

There can be. The Landmarks Preservation Commission says most exterior changes to the front and rear facades of buildings in historic districts require its review, and its 2018 rules, Section 2-20, cover security cameras along with other small safety and utility equipment, so staff can approve cameras that meet set criteria, including size and finish, without a public hearing. Its Permit Guidebook chapter on this equipment sets those criteria out, and we read it for the specific building before planning. To check whether your building is designated, call 311 and ask for the Landmarks Preservation Commission. Where the facade is protected, we look first at positions inside the vestibule, under the stoop or on the rear facade, which often meet the camera's purpose with fewer questions.

Owners, tenants and the footage

Are NYC apartment buildings actually required to have security cameras?

Not as a general rule. A building-compliance guide we reviewed states there is no general NYC law mandating cameras in apartment buildings, though HPD may recommend them where crime-related violations keep recurring. What the law does require is the hardware cameras work beside: an intercom at the self-locking main entrance of class A buildings of eight or more apartments built or converted after January 1, 1968 under Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a, self-closing doors that HPD actively inspects, and adequate lighting in common areas. Cameras are usually a choice owners make for theft, insurance, or tenant requests, and a well-placed system supports the required pieces rather than replacing them.

As a landlord, where in my building can I put cameras, and where should I not?

Common areas, entrances, lobbies, mailrooms, halls, stairs, laundry rooms, yards and roofs, are the usual places, and one apartment-camera guide describes hallway cameras as acceptable when they cover corridor traffic without seeing into apartments as doors open. Inside a tenant's apartment is a different matter; another guide calls landlord cameras inside rented living spaces generally illegal, and covert recording in private spaces can be a felony under Penal Law §250.45. In rent-stabilized buildings, a guide on tenant objections warns that courts and the state housing agency take seriously anything that looks like harassment. We aim every hallway camera along the corridor, not at a specific door, and we leave audio off unless you have taken advice on it.

Who should be able to see the footage from our building's cameras?

That is the owner's or board's decision, and the system should be set up to enforce it. The research we reviewed found no single NYC rule on footage access for ordinary common-area cameras, so most buildings decide by policy: typically the owner or managing agent, the super for live viewing of the entrance, and nobody else without a reason. We create a separate account for each person with only the rights they need, so a super can watch the lobby without being able to delete recordings. If the building uses fob or app entry tied to the cameras, HPD's Tenant Data Privacy Law limits what that entry data can be used for and how long it can be kept, which is worth discussing with counsel. None of this is legal advice.

Do we need to put up signs saying the building has cameras?

For a business using facial recognition or similar analysis, yes: NYC's biometric rules require a sign at every customer entrance, at least 8.5 by 11 inches. For ordinary building cameras, the picture is less clear. A summary of Penal Law §250.65 describes a protection for systems where a notice is posted conspicuously or the cameras are clearly obvious. One vendor claims a "Local Law 50" requires residential notice, but we could not confirm that on any city source, and sources we reviewed disagree about whether landlords must tell tenants in writing. Clear signs at the entrances are inexpensive and remove doubt, so we usually recommend them, and suggest owners confirm current requirements with their lawyer.

Houses and single apartments

I own a house in Queens. Can I put cameras on the outside of it facing my driveway and the street?

Generally, cameras on your own house, covering your own entrances, driveway and yard, are what most owners install, and a recording-law summary of New York rules treats exterior cameras on your own property as permitted when they do not look into places where neighbors have a reasonable expectation of privacy. In practice that means aiming at your door, your driveway and the stretch of sidewalk in front of it, and not into a neighbor's windows or yard. If the house is in a historic district, check with the Landmarks Preservation Commission before drilling into the front facade. We set camera views on site with you and use privacy masking where a view unavoidably catches a neighbor's window.

Can I mount a small camera outside my apartment door in a rental building instead of a doorbell camera?

Ask before you drill. The hallway outside your door is the building's common area, and sources disagree on how far a tenant may go: one legal Q&A says you need the landlord's permission and that the landlord can remove an unapproved camera, while an apartment-law guide says no state or city law forbids a camera on your own door but notes that hallway audio is legally unsettled. In a co-op, the board controls the hall. The option that raises the fewest questions, according to that guide, is a peephole camera that replaces existing hardware. A hard-wired GeoVision camera in a common hallway is a building system, so we install those for owners and boards, not for individual tenants without written approval.

The installation itself

What does putting in a GeoVision IP camera actually involve, start to finish?

The camera is the quick part. Each IP camera gets one Cat5e or Cat6 cable, which carries both its network connection and its power over PoE, so the real work is the cable route from the camera position to a PoE switch or recorder. We fix the camera base to the wall with anchors suited to the material, terminate and test the cable, connect the camera, give it a fixed address and a new password, and add it to the recorder. Then we set the image, aim, recording schedule and retention, check the picture at night as well as by day, and set up remote viewing safely. Following the manufacturer's setup guide for each model is part of the job, not something we skip.

How long does a GeoVision camera installation take in a typical NYC building?

It depends far more on the cable route than on the number of cameras. A small rowhouse system with short runs through a cellar can be a single visit. A walk-up where every run has to travel up a stair hall and through masonry takes longer, and a large building with risers, an elevator cab camera, a co-op board's working-hours rules and several equipment closets may take several days, sometimes split across visits to suit the building. An audit of an existing GeoVision system adds time at the start but often saves it later, because cable that already works does not need to be pulled again. AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About GeoVision Camera Installation in NYC

We could not capture Google's AI Overview or its People Also Ask boxes for this search from our build environment, so we will not pretend to quote them. What follows are claims we see repeated in quick online answers about camera installation in New York, and about legacy camera brands in general, set against the public record and our own field practice. Marketplaces such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish national and city cost guides that feed many of those answers. They are useful for a rough idea, but they are not a quote for your building.

"If the cameras still show a picture, the system is fine."

A working picture says nothing about whether a recorder is safe. The GeoVision units named in the CVE-2024-11120 reports could keep recording perfectly well while a botnet used them, which is exactly what makes old equipment dangerous: nobody notices.

Security Affairs, reporting on Shadowserver's work, counted about 17,000 internet-facing GeoVision devices exposed to the flaw, most of them in the United States, and the affected models were already past end of life.

Our test is not "does it show a picture" but "what model is it, is it supported, and can anyone outside the building reach it." In a city where camera systems often outlive their installers, those three questions come before anything else.

"A firmware update will fix any old recorder."

Updates only help on equipment its maker still supports. Cybersecurity Help's write-up describes the affected GeoVision models as end of life and no longer supported, which means there is no new firmware coming to close the hole.

When CISA added two GeoVision flaws to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, The Hacker News reported, federal agencies were told to apply fixes by May 28, 2025 or stop using the products. For unsupported models, stopping is the realistic option.

So we update supported equipment from the manufacturer's own downloads, and we take end-of-life equipment off the network and plan its replacement. We do not sell owners an "update" for a device that cannot receive one.

"Just open a port on the router to view cameras remotely."

This is the oldest advice in the camera business and the reason so many recorders end up exposed. A forwarded port puts the recorder's login page in front of the whole internet, and on an end-of-life unit with a pre-authentication flaw, the login does not even need to be guessed.

GeoVision's own GV-Eye installation guide describes a relay method instead, its GV-Relay function, which uses port 443 by default and port 9443 when 443 is taken by something like WebCam SSL or RTSP.

We set up phone viewing through methods like that wherever the equipment supports them, close old port-forwards on the building's router, and give each viewer their own account. Remote viewing is a convenience, not a reason to leave the front door of the network open.

"NYC requires cameras in apartment buildings."

It does not, as a general rule. A compliance guide we reviewed is explicit that there is no general NYC law mandating cameras in apartment buildings, though HPD may recommend them after repeated crime-related violations.

What the law does require is related hardware: Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a intercoms at the self-locking entrances of covered buildings, self-closing doors that HPD inspected into 3,151 violations in FY24 and 2,937 in FY25, and adequate lighting in common areas.

So the honest pitch for cameras in a New York building is theft, insurance, liability and tenant peace of mind, not a mandate. And a camera system that ignores a broken door closer or a dead hallway light has missed the point.

"Any electrician can install security cameras in New York."

Quick answers often mix up two separate questions: who may do low-voltage work in the city, and who may install security systems in the state. On the first, the new Administrative Code §28-105.4.7 lets licensed master electricians, special electricians and qualified persons do sub-50-volt work without an electrical permit.

On the second, the city's business portal says a license from the New York State Department of State is needed to install or maintain security or fire alarm systems, and 19 NYCRR 195.2(b) brings CCTV used for intrusion or theft monitoring within that definition. One vendor claims NYC requires licensed electricians for all wired camera installs; that conflicts with the state framework and we could not verify it.

AESS installs cameras under NYS license #12000287431. Whoever you hire, ask for the license number and check it.

"Average NYC camera installation costs are a reliable quote."

Angi publishes an average and a range for camera installation in New York, and Vertex publishes a per-camera range for commercial work. Those figures are real averages from real sources, but they average away exactly the things that decide a New York price.

Vertex itself names the drivers: union requirements in certain buildings, conduit in historic structures and after-hours work in active offices. Add a walk-up stair hall with no ceiling, an LPC facade review, an old recorder that has to be audited, or a twelve-story riser, and two four-camera jobs can look nothing alike.

That is why our price list is broken out by borough and by line item, and why we quote your building after looking at it rather than quoting an average.

"Facial recognition is just a camera setting."

In New York City it is more than a setting for a business. A law-firm summary of the city's biometric identifier law explains that ordinary CCTV appears exempt from its signage rule only if the business does not use software to analyze footage for identifying features. Turn that analysis on, and the city's rules require a sign at every customer entrance.

In residential buildings the issue is also trust. The Atlantic Plaza Towers dispute in Brownsville, where tenants objected to a landlord's plan for facial-recognition entry, and a Council Member's account of a Brooklyn tenant fighting a facial-recognition camera at his building's entrance, show how strongly residents react.

We set systems up to record entrances and common areas without biometric analysis unless the owner specifically asks for it, understands the rules, and has taken advice. Most buildings do not need it to solve the problems they actually have.

How to read a quote for a New York building

Picture two buildings that each want four cameras. The first is a rowhouse with a cellar, where the front-door camera, the rear-yard camera and two interior cameras all run a short distance to a recorder downstairs. The second is a five-story walk-up, where the cameras sit at the front entrance, the mailboxes, the top-floor roof door and the rear yard, and every run has to climb the stair hall in raceway and pass through masonry into the cellar. Both are "four-camera systems." On our borough price page the equipment lines are the same for both; the labor and pathway lines are not. Seeing those lines separately is what lets an owner understand why, and decide whether, for example, the roof-door camera is worth its long run this year or next.

If you are comparing quotes, ask every installer the same questions: which existing equipment are you keeping and why, where exactly does each cable run, which penetrations need fire-stopping, how long will the recorder keep video, and what will you leave us at the end. A quote that cannot answer those questions is a guess.

FAQ

NYC GeoVision Camera Installation FAQ

What does it mean when an installer calls itself GeoVision-certified, and should I insist on it?
+

A competitor's FAQ presents GeoVision certification as manufacturer training on the brand's products. We do not claim a GeoVision certificate on this page. What we suggest you insist on is the thing New York law requires, a NYS Department of State security installer license, which AESS holds as license 12000287431, plus a written audit of your existing equipment, a clear cable plan and a full handover. Ask any installer who mentions certification to show the certificate and the license.

Roughly what does a four-camera GeoVision system cost in New York City?
+

It depends on the borough and the building. The equipment for four cameras and a recorder is priced the same everywhere, but labor follows the borough rate and the cable route: four short runs in a rowhouse cellar cost far less than four runs climbing a walk-up stair hall through masonry. Our NYC GeoVision price list, linked above, breaks the lines out for each borough so you can see the difference before calling.

Is installation labor priced separately from the cameras themselves?
+

Yes. Equipment is listed at the same price across all five boroughs, and labor is listed separately at the borough rate. Brooklyn and the Bronx share the base labor rate, Queens and Staten Island are a little above it, and Manhattan is highest because of access and parking. That split also lets you see what you save when existing cable or cameras can be reused.

Can I watch our GeoVision cameras on my phone without opening ports on the building router?
+

On equipment that supports it, yes. GeoVision's GV-Eye installation guide describes a relay method, GV-Relay, that uses port 443 by default, or port 9443 when 443 is already in use by another service. We set up viewing that way where the recorder supports it and close old port-forwards. Older units that only work with open ports are a strong reason to plan a replacement.

Our store is on a busy shopping street. Can we let the NYPD see our cameras during an emergency?
+

Yes, if you choose to. NYC 311 explains that any business with a security camera can share live feeds with the NYPD during emergencies and register its camera locations at NewYorkCityConnect.org, and amNY has reported on a real-time sharing pilot aimed at retail theft. Joining is the owner's decision. We can set up the recorder so only the cameras you choose are shareable.

Can a tenant put their own cameras inside their apartment?
+

Inside the apartment itself, one New York camera guide says Wi-Fi cameras that need no drilling or wiring are generally allowed in most buildings, while hard-wired systems usually need landlord or board approval. A single vendor claims recent Real Property Law changes give tenants a specific right to Wi-Fi cameras, but we could not verify that. Hidden cameras that record anyone in private spaces can be a crime regardless.

Our building uses key fobs or an app to open the front door. Do extra city rules apply to the camera system?
+

They can. HPD's Tenant Data Privacy Law governs smart access systems in residential buildings of three or more units: data may only be used for granting access and monitoring entrances, exits and common areas, authentication data must be destroyed within 90 days, and owners may not require tenants to use smart access to enter their own apartments. Ordinary camera feeds not used for entry are generally treated separately, but ask counsel if your cameras and entry system are linked.

Will the cameras keep recording if the building's internet goes down?
+

Yes, with the way we install them. Cameras record to a recorder inside the building over their own cables, so an internet outage stops phone viewing but not recording. A power cut is different, which is why we put the recorder and PoE switch on battery backup in the rack, so a short outage does not leave a gap in the footage.

Can a camera in our shop's back area cover the staff bathroom door or the changing room?
+

Not inside those spaces, and we aim carefully near them. New York Labor Law section 203-c bars employers from recording in restrooms, locker rooms and changing areas. We keep views off those rooms entirely, including the view through a door when it opens, and use privacy masking on any camera whose frame comes close.

Our co-op board wants a scope of work and an insurance certificate before approving cameras. Do you provide both?
+

Yes. Send us the managing agent's requirements, including the exact names to list on the certificate, and we prepare the certificate of insurance and a written scope listing every camera, every cable route and every wall or floor penetration. We also schedule inside the building's permitted working hours and check in with the super or desk on arrival.

What happens to our old GeoVision recorder and the footage on it once the new system is in?
+

Nothing is erased until you say so. We export any clips you need first, then either leave the old recorder disconnected and stored where you choose, or remove it. Because hard drives can hold years of video of your residents and staff, we suggest keeping or securely destroying the drive rather than throwing the unit out whole.

Our hallway lighting is dim. Will new cameras fix that, or do we need better lights?
+

Usually better lights first. Infrared cameras can see in darkness, but uneven light, a single bulb at the end of a corridor or glare off a glass door can still spoil the picture. The Multiple Dwelling Law already requires adequate lighting in vestibules, halls, stairs and courtyards, so replacing dead fixtures is often the owner's job anyway, and it makes every camera work better.

Do you cover every borough, and what about addresses just outside the city?
+

Yes, all five boroughs, each at its own borough rate, dispatched from our Brooklyn office. We also install GeoVision systems on Long Island and in the Hudson Valley, where county rates apply. The address on the job decides which price page and rate you see.
