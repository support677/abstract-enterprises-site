Why Flushing Properties Need Smart Lock Installation Matched to the Door

Flushing is really several neighbourhoods stacked on top of one another, and each layer has its own kind of door. A smart lock that is right for one of those doors is often physically wrong for the next.

The people behind the doors matter as much as the hardware. The NYU Furman Center’s 2026 profile of Flushing/Whitestone (Queens Community District 7) counts about 252,000 residents in 2024 and puts the homeownership rate at 52.5 percent, far above the citywide 32.7 percent. Door details we measure on every Flushing job

The width of flat door beside the edge. Glass lights, raised panels and decorative mouldings decide which locks fit long before the brand does. Ordinary American keypad deadbolts mount through a round bore with a 2-3/8 or 2-3/4 inch backset; Korean-style mortise push-pull locks need much more. One North American distributor of Samsung locks lists an “absolute minimum of 4.5 inches of flat space from the edge of the door to any glass moulding, paneling, decorative beveling or moulding” for its mortise models. We measure that distance before anyone orders anything.

Door thickness and core. The same distributor lists its mortise locks for doors 1-9/16 to 3-1/8 inches thick, with a longer spindle for doors over 2-3/8 inches. Schlage lists its Encode Plus for doors 1-3/8 to 1-3/4 inches thick. A hollow-metal office door, a solid-core condo door and a thick original front door in Broadway–Flushing all sit in different parts of those ranges, and a hollow-metal door needs reinforcement where a lock body is mounted.

Glass that can’t be drilled. Tempered glass storefront doors without a frame cannot be bored for a lock. They take hardware made for the glass system — a patch lock in the bottom corner fitting, a bottom-rail lock into the floor, or a magnetic lock at the header. Each has different consequences for the floor, the fire alarm and the way people leave, which we work out door by door.

Frames, closers and latching. A smart lock that auto-locks is only as good as the door’s willingness to close. On shop doors we check the closer’s speed and latch stroke; on house doors we check that the strike sits where the bolt lands without being pushed. A motorised bolt that touches the strike edge strains the motor, drains the batteries and reports a jam.

Compliance callouts for Flushing business doors

  • One motion out. Door-hardware commentary on the model building codes is consistent that electrified locks must stay readily openable from the egress side, generally with one releasing motion and no key, code or special knowledge. Every shop, office and classroom door we fit opens from inside with a lever, a push bar or a push-to-exit button.
  • Fingerprints and customers. New York City’s Local Law 3 of 2021 requires covered commercial establishments — retail stores, food and drink establishments and places of entertainment — that collect customers’ biometric identifier information to post a sign at customer entrances, and bars selling or trading that information. A staff-only fingerprint door is a different situation from one customers use; ask your counsel which applies.
  • Interlocked vestibules. A two-door buzz-in vestibule is a special locking arrangement. Fire codes require a way out of every occupied space, and if the interlock is part of the exit path the hardware needs approved overrides. That design belongs to your architect and the city’s approvals, not to a lock catalogue.
  • Hands and heights. Door-hardware commentary on the 2010 ADA Standards sets two tests we apply to every public door: a person must be able to work the hardware without tight grasping, pinching or twisting the wrist, and it should sit 34 to 48 inches off the floor. Levers and keypads inside that band suit a wheelchair user and an elderly shopper equally.
  • Homes on the floors above. In a mixed-use building the apartment doors answer to the Housing Maintenance Code, and any smart access an owner offers tenants falls under the Tenant Data Privacy Act. We keep the shop’s codes and fobs entirely separate from them.

Your Questions Answered

Smart Lock Installation in Flushing: Questions Answered

These are the questions Flushing owners, tenants and shopkeepers bring to us before a lock goes on, grouped by the kind of door: tower apartments, hardware bought abroad or online, houses, and the shops, offices and classrooms of downtown. Prices, scheduling and coverage are in the FAQ further down. Nothing on this page is legal or compliance advice; where a law or agency is involved, we say which one so you can check with it directly.

Apartments in the new towers

Our condo is on a high floor downtown and my phone shows dozens of neighbours’ Wi-Fi networks — will a Wi-Fi smart lock keep dropping off?

It can, and crowded radio is the most common reason a lock in one of Flushing’s newer buildings shows “offline” in the evening and comes back by morning. Most Wi-Fi locks and lock bridges talk only on the 2.4 GHz band — Schlage’s product page for the Encode says plainly that it “requires 2.4GHz WiFi network” — and that is the same band every neighbour’s router, many older laptops and a long list of smart plugs and cameras use. In a tower with dozens of units per floor, the lock is competing with all of them, from inside a steel door at the far end of an entry hall, usually behind a concrete wall from your router.

There are four practical fixes, and we test them in this order. First, move the radio closer: a mesh node or access point near the entry, or a plug-in lock bridge in the hall closet — August’s support guidance, for example, puts its Connect bridge within 10 to 15 feet of the lock. Second, consider a lock that uses Thread through a border router you already own; Bold’s Matter guide describes Thread as a low-power, self-healing mesh, and both Bold and Yale say a Thread lock needs a border router, such as a recent smart speaker or hub. Third, keep the lock on Bluetooth only if you never need remote access. Fourth, if the building’s management runs a wireless network in the corridors, don’t join a lock to it unless management agrees in writing.

Whatever we choose, the test is the same: a phone on mobile data, standing in the lobby, locking and unlocking the door and receiving the alert, at the time of day when the building is busiest. A lock that passes that test at 7 p.m. will pass it at 2 p.m.

Locks bought abroad or online

A relative brought back a push-pull fingerprint lock from Korea — will it fit an ordinary American front door?

Sometimes, but not on the strength of the box. Push-pull digital locks of the kind sold in Korea and China are usually mortise locks: a rectangular lock body is let into a pocket cut in the edge of the door, and large escutcheons cover both faces. A typical American keypad deadbolt is the opposite — a cylindrical latch through a round bore, with a 2-3/8 or 2-3/4 inch backset, as Allegion’s door-preparation guidance for its Schlage locks spells out. The two systems use different holes in different places.

For its Samsung mortise models, one North American distributor asks for an absolute minimum of 4.5 inches of flat space from the edge of the door to any glass, panel or moulding, a door between 1-9/16 and 3-1/8 inches thick, and at least 70 mm of clearance where a storm door closes over the main door. Panelled wood doors, doors with a glass light near the latch side and narrow storm-door gaps fail those numbers more often than not. A door that already has two round holes for an American knob and deadbolt also has to be checked to make sure the new escutcheons will cover them.

If the door passes, we cut the mortise pocket, fit the body and the trim, and test the inside handle: a push-pull lock still has to open from the inside with one plain motion, without a code. We also check what you can and can’t get help with — the manual, voice prompts and support line may be in another language, and warranty claims may have to go back to the country of purchase. If the door fails, we will say so before cutting anything, and show you a North American lock that gives the same features on the door you have.

A no-name smart lock from an online marketplace costs a fraction of a Schlage — what are we actually giving up?

Mostly things you can’t see in the listing. Established makers publish which standard their lock was tested to; Schlage, for example, lists the Encode Plus as ANSI/BHMA A156.40 Grade AAA, the residential deadbolt standard’s top grade. Many marketplace listings name no standard and no grade at all, which doesn’t prove the lock is weak but leaves you with no way to know how its bolt, cylinder and housing were tested.

The second thing is the software behind it. A smart lock is half hardware and half service: an app, a cloud account, firmware updates and someone to answer when it misbehaves. Before buying, look for a company you can name, an app with recent updates, a privacy policy that says where account data is kept, and a published way to report security problems. If you can’t find those, assume the remote features may stop working one day and choose a lock that is still a good lock without them.

Third, the mechanical basics. Check that it has a real key override or a mechanical bypass, an inside thumb-turn that works without power, a backset that matches your door, and a battery cover that a grown-up can open without tools. We are happy to install a lock you supply; we will just tell you during the walkthrough if it falls short on any of those points, before it goes on your door.

Houses north and east of downtown

After October’s armed home invasion near the Horace Harding Expressway, should we stop unlocking our door from the app for people we can’t see?

It’s a sensible rule to adopt, though it helps to be clear about what happened. QNS reported that on October 11, 2026, four masked men forced their way into a home on the Horace Harding Expressway near 150th Street at about 2:30 a.m. That was a forced entry by armed men — not a remote unlock, a guessed code or a hacked app — and no lock setting would have made the difference that night.

Where a smart lock can make you less safe is in everyday habits. Unlocking from the app because someone at the door says they are a courier, a relative’s friend or a repairman you weren’t expecting is the digital version of opening the door to a stranger. A video doorbell or door camera beside the lock means you see the face before you press the button, and temporary codes with an end time are better than remote unlocking for deliveries and trades you have scheduled. At night, there is rarely a reason to unlock remotely at all.

The physical side still matters most. On houses we fit the strike with long screws that reach the framing, check that the bolt throws its full length, and talk about an alarm with door contacts and a panic button. A smart lock does its best work by making sure the side and rear doors are actually locked every night — and by telling you, on the phone by your bed, when one isn’t.

Shops, offices and classrooms downtown

Our tutoring centre fills with children after school — can the entrance keep strangers out without trapping the kids inside?

Yes, as long as the locking only ever applies to people coming in. Tutoring and test-prep centres are a fixture of downtown Flushing — a 2017 New York Times article on test-prep centres opens on Main Street — and many sit on upper floors behind a shared street door. The usual arrangement is a keypad or fob reader on the outside for staff and an intercom with a camera so the front desk can buzz in parents and visitors. On the inside, the door opens with a lever or a push bar, every time, without a code.

What most centres actually want is to know when a child leaves, and that is a job for an alert, not a lock. A door contact or an exit chime that sounds at the front desk whenever the door opens lets staff look up and see who is going, without ever making the door hard to open. Commentary on the model codes is clear that electrified locks must remain readily openable from the egress side, and delayed-egress or locked-exit arrangements are special locking systems that need approvals — not something to improvise for a classroom.

We also set credentials up so they can be taken away quickly. Part-time teachers get their own codes or fobs, which come off the system the day they finish; parents are buzzed in rather than given codes that spread; and the owner gets the log of who opened the door and when, which helps if there is ever a question about a pickup.

We prepare tax returns in an office above Main Street — does a keypad with an audit log help with the IRS’s data-security expectations?

It can be part of the answer, but the IRS doesn’t name door hardware. Publication 4557, Safeguarding Taxpayer Data, says that under the FTC Safeguards Rule, tax return preparers “must create and enact security plans to protect client data,” and among its basic steps it tells preparers to “limit access to taxpayer data to individuals who need to know” and to “implement a clean desk policy.” It points to Publication 5708 for building a written information security plan.

A keypad or fob lock with individual credentials gives you something concrete to put in that plan’s physical-security section: a list of who can open the office and the file room, a log of when they did, and a way to remove a seasonal preparer’s access the day the season ends. A shared four-digit code that every employee since 2019 knows gives you none of that. Where client files are kept on paper, a separate lock on the file room or cabinet room, with a shorter list of users, is worth more than a better lock on the front door.

We install the hardware and set up the user list and schedules; your accountant, your IT provider or your compliance adviser should decide what the written plan says. If you also want cameras or alarm contacts in the office, they can go in on the same visit.

Our jewellery shop wants a buzz-in vestibule where the inner door won’t open until the outer one closes — is that allowed?

Interlocked doors — often called a mantrap or sally-port — are used in jewellery stores, pharmacies and cash businesses, and they can be done legally, but they are not a lock purchase. A Locksmith Ledger article on mantraps puts the core rule plainly: “you can’t trap someone in a mantrap.” Fire codes require an emergency exit path out of every occupied room, and if there is no other exit from the secured area, the interlock hardware has to include approved free-exiting devices that override it.

The same article notes that where a mantrap sits on the means of egress, it may have to meet the rules for special locking arrangements such as delayed egress. In New York City that is a question for your architect or expediter and the Department of Buildings and FDNY, before any hardware is ordered. Electromagnetic locks are the usual way interlocks are built, which brings fire-alarm release and power-failure behaviour into the design as well.

Many small shops on Main Street end up choosing something simpler: a single entrance door kept locked during business hours, a camera at the door, and a release button behind the counter so staff buzz in customers they can see. We can fit either, but we only wire an interlock to a design that has been approved for your space.

Our storefront is a frameless glass door with a lock in the bottom corner fitting — can it go keyless?

Yes, but not with a deadbolt from a hardware store. Tempered glass can’t be drilled after it is made, so frameless glass doors take hardware designed for the glass system. One security integrator sums up the usual options well: a magnetic lock at the header, or a patch lock or bottom-rail lock made for that glass system. Some manufacturers now make an electronic lock built into a patch fitting; Codelocks, for example, describes its CL4500 GD as a medium-duty electronic lock with a patch lock for toughened glass doors, a backlit keypad and a key override.

Each route has a catch. A bottom-rail or patch lock throws into the floor, so the floor strike has to be cut into tile, terrazzo or stone, and the door has to swing true enough to meet it every time. A header magnetic lock needs power, a way to release from inside — usually a motion sensor plus a push button — and, where the fire code calls for it, release when the alarm sounds. Pairs of glass doors with a centre post or none at all add their own problems.

At the walkthrough we identify the glass fitting manufacturer, look at the floor and the header, and check how the door is used: whether the shop is staffed whenever it is unlocked, and whether the glass door is the only way out. Then we price the option that suits it, rather than forcing the same answer on every door.

If our staff door uses fingerprints, does the city’s biometric law mean we need a sign?

Possibly, depending on who uses the reader. New York City’s Local Law 3 of 2021, in effect since July 9, 2021, applies to “commercial establishments” — retail stores, food and drink establishments and places of entertainment — that collect, retain, convert, store or share customers’ biometric identifier information, and fingerprints are named in the definition. Those businesses must post a clear sign near all customer entrances, in the form the Department of Consumer and Worker Protection prescribes, and may not sell or otherwise profit from the data.

A fingerprint reader on a back door used only by employees is not collecting customers’ data, which is what the sign requirement is about. A fingerprint reader that members, students or regular customers use is a different matter. If you aren’t sure which side your door falls on, ask your lawyer before you enrol anyone; the DCWP publishes a template sign that covered businesses can use.

Whatever the legal answer, good practice is the same: tell staff what is enrolled and why, offer a code or fob to anyone who would rather not use a fingerprint, prefer locks that keep fingerprint templates on the device rather than in the cloud, and delete a person’s prints when they leave. We set the lock up that way by default.

Most of the Flushing businesses hit in last summer’s burglary spree had a door left unlocked — what actually closes that gap during business hours?

QNS reported in August 2025 that one man was suspected of burglarising a half-dozen businesses in and around Flushing from May to August. One rear door was forced on Sanford Avenue and a medical office’s locks were manipulated, but four of the six — a restaurant on Linden Place, a home-furnishing shop on College Point Boulevard, a furniture store on Northern Boulevard and the Flushing Tower Condominium — were entered through unlocked doors.

During opening hours the front door is meant to be open; the gap is the other doors. The fixes are unglamorous: a lock on the side, rear and office doors that relocks itself a few seconds after the door closes; a closer adjusted so the door actually latches instead of resting against the frame; a door-position sensor that tells the owner’s phone when a door has been propped for more than a few minutes; and a keypad on the outside of the delivery door, so staff stop propping it in the first place.

For the front door, a scheduled unlock that follows posted hours, and relocks itself if nobody opens up on a holiday, is better than relying on whoever closes to remember. And because a lock only protects a door that is shut, we look at the cash drawer, the office and the stock room as separate doors with their own, shorter lists of who can open them.

Our restaurant’s kitchen staff have wet, floury hands all shift — will a fingerprint reader on the staff door work?

Not reliably as the only way in. eufy’s support page for its smart locks says the fingerprint sensor “may not be working properly if your hand is wet, moisty, or dirty”, and other makers say much the same; anyone who has tried to unlock a phone with damp fingers knows the problem. A kitchen door used by cooks mid-shift, with gloves on and off and hands that have just been in a sink, is about the hardest place to put one.

The answer is to make fingerprints a convenience rather than the plan. Give every staff member a keypad code or a fob as their main credential, enrol two or three fingers for those who want to use the reader, and mount it at the entrance staff use when they arrive with clean hands, not at the door between the kitchen and the yard. Choose a reader that is rated for the location and can be wiped down.

Kitchens also wear hardware out faster: grease on the keypad, heat near the line, carts that hit the door. For the back-of-house door we usually prefer a sturdy keypad with physical buttons and an electric strike in the frame, which leaves the door’s own heavy-duty lever or exit device doing the work it was built for.

Burglars broke through the front doors of pharmacies on Kissena and Parsons — does a smart lock help when the door itself gives way?

Not on that door, and it is worth being honest about it. QNS reported that on December 20, 2025, a crew forced the front door of a medical business at 42-62 Kissena Blvd. at 6 a.m. and took cash and prescription medications, then broke into the pharmacy next door, and that a pharmacy at 45-12 Parsons Blvd. was entered by breaking the front door on December 30. When a door is broken through — and many storefront doors are mostly glass — the best lock in the world is only holding the part of the door the burglar didn’t need.

What helps is layering behind the glass. An intrusion alarm with door contacts, glass-break and motion detection tells you and the monitoring centre within seconds; cameras inside and out give the police something to work with; and a security gate or grille, where the building and city rules allow one, protects the glass itself after hours. None of those is a lock, and we say so.

A smart lock earns its place on the doors inside: the pharmacy’s stock room, the office with the safe, the back room with the medications. Those doors are usually solid, and a reader or keypad with a log there — opened only by the pharmacist and the owner — narrows what an intruder can reach and tells you exactly who has been in the room.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Smart Lock Installation in Flushing

Google’s AI Overview could not be opened from the environment this page was researched in, so nothing below quotes it. Instead, each heading is a claim that turns up again and again in lock marketing and listicles — the raw material answer engines summarise — followed by what Flushing news reports, maker specifications and real doors say.

“A smart lock stops break-ins”

A good lock that is actually locked protects the door it is on, and that is all. Two Flushing burglary stories from the past year show the limits from both sides.

In the 2025 spree QNS described, four of the six places entered had doors that were simply unlocked — a lock that relocks itself would have mattered there. In the December 2025 pharmacy burglaries on Kissena and Parsons Boulevards, the crew broke through the front doors, and no lock setting changes that.

So we sell smart locks for what they are good at — making sure doors are locked, showing who opened them, and ending the copied-key problem — and recommend alarms, cameras and sturdier doors for the rest.

“Fingerprint readers work for everyone”

Fingerprint unlocking is fast and hard to share, which is why owners like it. It is also the credential most affected by real working conditions.

Manufacturers’ own support pages, eufy’s among them, say the sensor may not work properly when a hand is wet or dirty. Kitchen staff, cleaners, mechanics and anyone who has just come in from the rain are exactly the people trying to open a door with damp hands.

We enrol several fingers per person and always pair fingerprints with a second credential — a code, a fob or a key — so a bad day at the reader is never a lockout.

“Any smart lock fits any door”

Product photos show locks on plain, flat, freshly painted doors. Flushing’s doors include panelled wood with glass lights, hollow-metal office doors, tower doors in steel frames and all-glass storefronts.

The published requirements say otherwise: Schlage lists the Encode Plus for doors 1-3/8 to 1-3/4 inches thick; a Samsung distributor asks for 4.5 inches of flat space beside the edge for its mortise locks; frameless glass doors can’t be drilled at all.

Measuring takes ten minutes. Returning a lock that was opened, or patching a door that was cut for the wrong one, takes much longer — so we measure first.

“Cheap and premium smart locks are the same inside”

Many budget listings show the same features as premium locks: fingerprints, codes, an app. What they rarely show is how the hardware was tested.

Established makers publish their certification; Schlage lists the Encode Plus as ANSI/BHMA A156.40 Grade AAA. A listing with no standard, no grade and no named company gives you nothing to compare.

A budget lock can be a reasonable choice for an interior door. On a street door or a door to a stock room, we suggest paying for a lock whose testing, maker and app you can actually identify.

“Wi-Fi smart locks just work in an apartment”

Set-up guides assume a router a few rooms away. In a downtown tower the router may be behind a concrete wall, the lock is inside a steel door, and the 2.4 GHz band the lock needs is shared with every unit on the floor.

Schlage states that its Encode requires a 2.4 GHz network, and August places its Connect bridge within 10 to 15 feet of the lock — both signs of how short these links are.

We test from the lobby on mobile data, at the building’s busiest hour, and add a node, a bridge or a Thread border router where the test fails.

“A fingerprint lock is just a convenience feature”

For a household, the privacy question is mostly about where the fingerprint templates are kept and who can see the log. For a business, New York City law can be involved.

Local Law 3 of 2021 requires covered commercial establishments that collect customers’ biometric identifier information, including fingerprints, to post a sign at customer entrances, and bars them from selling or profiting from that data.

We set locks up to keep templates on the device where the model allows, give everyone a non-biometric alternative, and point business owners to the city’s guidance before anyone is enrolled.

“Smart lock installation costs $250 to $400”

That range is real: Fixr’s January 2025 guide gives $250 to $400 nationally for installing a smart lock on an ordinary house door, with an average of $300. It describes a homeowner’s deadbolt swap, not a Flushing storefront.

A keypad or fob reader with an electric strike on a commercial door is a different job — wiring, a strike in an aluminium frame, an exit button — and at our published Queens rate it runs $1,575 to $2,625 installed. A push-pull lock that needs a mortise cut, or glass-door hardware, is quoted after we see the door.

Use national averages to sanity-check a simple house-door job, and use a written quote from someone who has measured your door for everything else.

Storefront Checklist

A Door Checklist for Flushing Shop Owners

Before a keypad goes on a Flushing storefront, go through these with us. Most problems we fix later started as one of these questions nobody asked.

  • Which doors does the public use, and which only staff? Public doors stay simple to leave through; staff doors get the credentials and the logs.
  • Who opens up, and on which days? A list of names and days turns into schedules, so the Monday cousin’s code doesn’t work on Saturday night.
  • What happens when someone leaves? Decide now who removes a departing employee’s code, fob or fingerprint, and make sure that person has the app.
  • Does every door actually latch? A door that rests against the frame defeats any lock. Closers and strikes get adjusted first.
  • Where is the cash, the stock or the medication? That room deserves its own lock and a shorter list of users than the front door.
  • Is anything glass? If the door or the panel beside it is glass, add an alarm contact and glass-break detection rather than relying on the lock.
  • Do customers ever use a fingerprint reader? If so, read up on the city’s biometric sign rule before enrolling anyone.
Staff Turnover

Taking Access Away the Day Someone Leaves

Turnover is the real reason most Flushing businesses go keyless. Copying a brass key costs a few dollars and takes minutes; collecting every copy when an employee leaves is impossible. A smart lock turns that into a deletion in the app — but only if someone actually does it.

We set businesses up so that removing a person is a single routine. Each person has one credential, named after them, never shared; codes for part-timers and seasonal staff are given end dates when they are created; at least two managers can remove users from their own phones; and the activity log is checked once a week for codes that shouldn’t be in use any more.

When someone leaves on bad terms, change the shared things too: any group code used for deliveries, the alarm code, and the mechanical override key if they ever held it. Then look at the log for the last two weeks of their employment. It takes ten minutes and usually settles any question about what happened after hours.

Connectivity

Getting a Signal to a Tower Unit Door

In Flushing’s newer buildings, the hardest part of a smart lock is often the radio, not the door. Unit doors are steel or solid core, set in steel frames, at the end of an entry hall; routers usually sit by the windows, and every neighbour’s network shares the same channels. A lock that works perfectly on day one can start dropping off when the floor fills up.

We choose from four setups. Bluetooth only keeps the lock working for anyone standing at it, with no remote features and nothing to fail. A bridge near the door relays the lock to your Wi-Fi from a hall closet or an outlet within a few feet. Thread through a border router uses a low-power mesh that many current smart speakers and hubs provide. Built-in Wi-Fi is simplest where the entry has a strong signal, often with a mesh node added near the door.

However it is connected, we finish the same way: from the lobby or the street, on mobile data, we lock and unlock the door and wait for the alert, then do it again in the evening. If the building runs its own fob or app system for the lobby and corridors, we keep your unit lock separate from it unless management asks otherwise.

Changing Hands

Handing Over a Smart Lock When a Flushing Condo or Shop Changes Hands

With condominiums now the most frequently sold property type in the neighbourhood, and storefront leases turning over on every block, a lock’s digital history changes hands far more often than its hardware. Whether you are buying, selling, moving in or taking over a lease, these are the things to get, or to give, on the day the keys change hands.

  • The administrator account. The seller or departing tenant should remove the lock from their app, or transfer ownership in the app, before closing or move-out. A lock still registered to someone else’s account can usually be reset, but only with physical access and sometimes the original setup code printed inside the battery cover or in the manual.
  • The paper that came in the box. Manuals, setup cards, spare keys for the override cylinder and any master or programming code. Imported push-pull locks in particular often need the original card to reset.
  • A list of what is enrolled. How many codes, fobs and fingerprints exist, and whose they are. If nobody knows, assume all of them should go.
  • Building credentials. In a tower, the building’s fobs for the lobby, elevator and garage belong to the building’s system and are handled by management, separately from the unit lock.
  • A reset and re-enrolment. On the first visit we factory-reset the lock where needed, update its firmware, set up two new administrators, enrol everyone fresh and change or re-key the mechanical override. Done in one appointment, it closes the door on every previous code at once.

Thickness, backset, the flat width between the edge and the nearest glass or panel, and the gap to any storm door get written down before anyone opens a box. In this neighbourhood, where so many locks arrive already bought, that step decides more jobs than any other.

Then the latch. We pull the door shut the way the busiest user does — a cook with a tray, a parent with a stroller, a teacher with an armful of folders — and watch whether it latches without help. If it doesn’t, the closer or the strike gets fixed before any electronics go on.

Then the way out. We stand inside, switch off the lights, and open the door with one hand. On a storefront, we check the push-to-exit button and the power-off behaviour too.

Last, the radio and the people. We test the connection from where the owner will use it, then enrol each person and watch them use their credential twice. A lock nobody can operate on day one never gets used properly.

Flushing Smart Lock Installation FAQ

What will a keypad-and-strike entry cost for a Flushing storefront or office?
+

Flushing is billed at our Queens rate, so one door with a keypad or fob reader and an electric strike comes to $1,575–$2,625 installed. A strike by itself is $263–$473; fixing a strike that has failed is $310–$509. For house, condo and apartment locks we give a flat figure once we have measured the door at the free walkthrough.

Do we need to arrange parking or loading space for your van downtown?
+

It helps. Tell us the address and the cross street when you book, and whether the building has a loading area or a rear entrance. On the busiest blocks around Main Street we plan the arrival time and carry the tools in, so a little notice keeps the appointment on schedule.

Can young children use the fingerprint reader, or should they have a code?
+

Many children can. Because a child’s hands keep growing, we enrol two or three fingers, re-enrol them if the reader starts refusing, and give each child a backup code or fob. A child who can’t open the door on a cold afternoon is a bigger problem than a slower method.

Will you adjust or replace the door closer so the shop door actually latches?
+

Yes. A closer that slams, drifts or stops short is the most common reason an auto-locking shop door is left unlocked. We adjust closers as part of the installation and, if one is worn out, quote a replacement before we fit the lock.

Do you cover Murray Hill, Queensboro Hill, Auburndale and College Point as well as downtown Flushing?
+

Yes. The same Queens crews cover all of Flushing and its neighbours, including Murray Hill, Queensboro Hill, Auburndale, East Flushing, College Point, Whitestone and Fresh Meadows, at the same Queens rates.

The fingerprint reader on our existing lock stopped recognising anyone — can it be repaired?
+

Often. We clean and test the sensor, update the firmware where the maker allows, check the batteries and re-enrol prints. If the reader itself has failed, we price a replacement lock or reader and fit it on the same door where the dimensions allow.

Is a smart lock with a built-in camera worth it at a shop entrance?
+

Sometimes. A camera in the lock shows who used the door, but it sees only a narrow view at hand height and depends on the lock’s batteries. For a shop entrance we usually suggest a separate camera covering the door and the counter, with the lock keeping the access log.

Can the staff door’s fingerprint reader double as our time clock?
+

We don’t recommend it. A lock’s log records that a credential opened a door, not who was working, and it isn’t designed for payroll records or overtime disputes. Keep a proper timekeeping system for hours and use the lock log for security.
