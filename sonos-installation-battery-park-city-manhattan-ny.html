The Neighborhood

Why Battery Park City Homes Need Sonos Installation

Battery Park City is land that did not exist sixty years ago. According to Wikipedia, it was created in the 1970s from more than three million cubic yards of soil and rock dug out for the original World Trade Center and other projects, together with sand dredged from New York Harbor, and the landfill was complete by 1976. The Battery Park City Authority, which New York State created in 1968 as a public-benefit corporation, still owns and manages the 92 acres. More than a third of that — about 36 acres — is parkland and open space. Nobody who lives here owns the ground under their building; condominium owners pay common charges plus a payment in lieu of taxes, and every building sits on a lease from the Authority.

The buildings followed a single plan. The 1979 master plan by Alexander Cooper and Stanton Eckstut treated the site as an extension of the Manhattan street grid, and The Cultural Landscape Foundation notes that its 26 parcels were designed independently by different developers to produce a varied neighbourhood. The first residential complex, Gateway Plaza, was completed in 1983; listings from CityRealty and StreetEasy describe it as six buildings with 1,712 apartments. The Authority built out the Rector Place neighbourhood and the river esplanade through the 1980s, and the Battery Place towers followed. North of Brookfield Place, construction began in the late 1990s and the last lots were finished in early 2011. By 2018, thirty residential buildings stood on the landfill and no more were planned.

For a Sonos installer, that history explains almost everything. Every apartment is in a managed high-rise built within the last forty-odd years. There is no lath to fish through and no rowhouse cellar to start from; floors and ceilings are concrete, interior partitions are usually steel studs and drywall, and the exterior wall on the river side is often mostly glass. A management office, a superintendent or a condominium board stands between you and any hole in the wall. And the Wi-Fi environment is a vertical stack of neighbours, plus whatever leaks in from across the Hudson.

The northern towers were built under rules no other Manhattan neighbourhood had. In 2000 the Authority published its Residential Environmental Guidelines, and the first building to follow them, The Solaire at 20 River Terrace, became the first high-rise residential building in the country to earn LEED Gold. The Verdesian followed as the first condominium in New York City to reach LEED Platinum. High Performing Buildings magazine describes The Solaire as a 27-storey, 293-unit tower whose apartments have MERV-12 filters changed by management every three months, whose windows are double glazed with a low-e coating, and whose materials were chosen for low emissions. Those details turn up in a Sonos plan: where the Amp can sit, what the glass does to sound and signal, and what we use when we patch a wall.

Then there is the quiet. Residents describe it again and again: on Reddit's r/AskNYC one wrote "Very quiet, close to everything by train/bus"; another listed "Pros: quiet, beautiful neighborhood. Right by the water. Very family friendly. Very dog friendly," then added, "Cons: quiet." That quiet is partly by design — the parks are governed by the Authority's own rules, which ban sound systems without a permit and any sound device in the parks between 10 p.m. and 8 a.m. — and it is about to be tested. The North/West Battery Park City Resiliency Project, a flood barrier running from the south esplanade up into Tribeca, started construction in 2026 and is scheduled into the next decade. A Sonos system here has to suit a quiet place that will be noisy outside for a while.

Battery Park City facts that shape a Sonos plan

  • 92 acres, 36 of them parkland: the whole landfill site and its open space (Wikipedia; The Cultural Landscape Foundation), managed by the Battery Park City Authority and its parks arm.
  • 1983: completion of Gateway Plaza, the first residential complex — six buildings and 1,712 apartments on South End Avenue (Wikipedia; CityRealty).
  • 30 residential buildings: the count by 2018, with no new construction planned (Wikipedia, citing The New York Times).
  • 2000: the year the Authority's Residential Environmental Guidelines appeared, leading to The Solaire and a run of LEED-rated towers on the north end (BPCA).
  • 10 p.m. to 8 a.m.: the hours when the parks rules forbid operating any sound reproduction device without a permit (21 NYCRR §9003.34).
  • 16,990 residents in 0.143 square miles: the 2023 population figure Wikipedia gives for the neighbourhood, in ZIP codes 10280 and 10282.

Taken together, the figures describe a neighbourhood that is newer, more regulated and more glass-walled than the Manhattan most Sonos advice is written for. None of that makes a good system harder. It means the plan starts with the building's rules and surfaces rather than the product catalogue.

The River Side of the Room

Glass, Light and Signal: Sonos Beside the Hudson Windows

For many households the reason to live here is the window: the river, the harbour, the Statue of Liberty, Jersey City lit up at night. The window is also the single biggest influence on how a Sonos system sounds and connects in these apartments, in three separate ways.

Glass reflects sound

A large pane is one of the hardest, flattest surfaces a room can have. Treble and the upper midrange bounce off it almost unchanged, so a speaker aimed across a room toward the window sends a strong second copy of the sound back at the listener a few milliseconds late. In a living room with a concrete ceiling, a stone or wood floor and a glass wall, that is what makes dialogue blur and music sound thin at higher volumes. The fixes are physical first: angle speakers along the window rather than at it, keep a soundbar's side-firing drivers clear of the glass, let curtains or shades and a rug do their share, and only then run Trueplay, which Sonos says "measures how sound reflects off walls, furnishings and other surfaces" and adjusts for them.

Coated glass blocks radio

Sonos's own interference guide rates ordinary glass as a low obstruction to Wi-Fi, below brick and far below concrete. Modern window glass is not ordinary glass. Energy-efficient towers use double glazing with a low-emissivity coating — High Performing Buildings magazine describes The Solaire's windows exactly that way — and the coating is a thin metallic layer. Graham Architectural Products, a window maker, warns that low-e coatings can attenuate electromagnetic signals in the bands cellular and Wi-Fi use, and a published research review lists measurements for double-pane low-e units of roughly 23 dB at 2.4 GHz and 28 dB at 5 GHz. Inside the apartment that barely matters. Between the living room and a terrace, or between your apartment and a neighbour's network across the courtyard, it matters a lot — which is one reason a portable taken onto a terrace can lose the system the moment the door closes.

The sun moves across it

Battery Park City's long edge faces west across the Hudson, so river-side rooms take the afternoon and evening sun. Sonos publishes an operating range of 32 to 104°F for the Era 100, and a dark speaker on a sill or a shelf in full summer sun behind glass can get warmer than the room around it. We keep speakers, Amps and Ports out of the strip of floor and sill where the sun lands, and suggest blinds that close automatically if the household is out on summer afternoons.

What we do differently in a window-wall room

  • Measure the room from the window back, not from the TV out, so the first decision is what the glass will reflect.
  • Prefer side or interior walls for the TV and soundbar, and keep any surround or music speaker angled along the glass rather than at it.
  • Run Trueplay with the shades in the position the household normally uses in the evening, and tell you how to re-run it in summer if the room changes.
  • Check Wi-Fi strength at every spot a portable will travel to, including the terrace, before deciding whether an access point is needed.
  • Keep every product out of the direct-sun band near the window, especially on west-facing floors.

The Museum of Jewish Heritage, the Skyscraper Museum, Stuyvesant High School and the neighbourhood's public schools, plus offices of the Authority and community groups. Background music in lobbies and event rooms, never in a classroom without the school's say.

Compliance notes for business music in Battery Park City

  • Music that reaches homes. The New York City noise code, as summarised by the Department of Environmental Protection, says commercial music from a store, restaurant or bar "shall not exceed 42 dBA as measured in any dwelling unit receiving the noise." On South End and North End Avenues the dwelling is often directly overhead.
  • Music on the parkland. The Authority's parks rules prohibit playing a sound system without a permit from its parks corporation, and prohibit operating any sound device "for advertising or commercial purposes" except as the rules allow (21 NYCRR §9003.34). A café's speaker aimed at the plaza is a parks question, not just a volume question.
  • Ceilings that carry air. Industry guides citing the National Electrical Code say cable run through an environmental-air space such as a return-air ceiling must be plenum-rated. Office and retail ceilings in the complex often work that way; we confirm with the building engineer and use rated cable.
  • Licensed music. Sonos plays whatever service it is signed into; personal streaming accounts are generally licensed for personal use. Shops and restaurants should choose a service licensed for business playback before opening day.
  • Hours. Many office and mall landlords limit noisy contractor work to before or after trading hours. We schedule around them and do the quiet setup during the day.

Sound that suits a small business by the water

A café on South End Avenue is not a bar in Midtown. Customers come in from the esplanade with strollers and dogs; the people upstairs come home early. The best systems we install here are two or three speakers spread across the room at low level rather than one loud box by the counter, with a volume limit set in the app so a busy shift can't push past it, and a schedule agreed with the manager so the music stops when the doors close.

Your Questions Answered

Sonos Installation in Battery Park City: Questions Answered

These are the questions Battery Park City households and businesses put to us that we don't hear elsewhere in the borough. Each answer reflects what we find in the buildings here.

The view, the glass and the sun

We want the sofa to face the river, not the TV — how do we arrange Sonos so the room works both ways?

Treat it as two listening positions rather than one compromise. The TV usually goes on a side wall, at right angles to the view, with the soundbar beneath it; when you watch, you turn the sofa or sit at the end. For music, which is what most people play while looking at the river, a pair of Era 100s or Era 300s set either side of the seating and aimed toward it gives proper stereo without anyone looking at a screen. Sonos lets the soundbar and the music pair be separate rooms in the app, so you can play one, the other or both grouped together. What we avoid is aiming the soundbar across the room at the window wall, which sends a bright reflection straight back at the sofa. On the quote we draw the room with both positions marked, so you can see where each product sits before anything is mounted.

Afternoon sun comes straight through our west windows onto the speaker shelf — can a Sonos speaker overheat there?

It can get warmer than it should. Sonos publishes an operating range of 32 to 104°F for the Era 100, and a black speaker sitting in full summer sun behind glass on a west-facing floor can climb above the air temperature of the room. The speaker will usually protect itself, but heat is still the enemy of electronics over years. We move speakers back from the sun band, choose a shelf or wall position the sun doesn't reach after about noon, and keep Amps and Ports out of sunny cabinets altogether. If the only good spot catches the sun for an hour, motorised or timed blinds solve it. We check this on the walkthrough by looking at where the floor is faded, which is usually a reliable map of where the sun lands.

Can a speaker bracket be fixed to the metal frame between our window panels, or to the wall just under the glass?

We treat both as off limits unless the building says otherwise in writing. The mullions and frames of a window wall are part of the building's exterior envelope; drilling them risks the seals that keep water and air out, and in a condominium the exterior wall is often a common element the board controls. The strip of wall below the glass often hides the heating and cooling unit, its pipes or the facade's anchors. Instead we mount on an interior partition or a column, use a floor stand, or put Era speakers on furniture near the window. If your board has approved fixings in that zone for something else, we ask for the approval and the drawing before quoting.

Green-built towers and building systems

Building staff change the air filter in our apartment's ventilation unit every few months — where can the Sonos gear go so they can still reach it?

Anywhere except in front of, on top of or inside that unit's access door. High Performing Buildings magazine describes The Solaire as having MERV-12 filters in each apartment that management changes every three months, and other towers on the north end have similar routines. An Amp, Port or router parked in the closet with the unit, or a speaker on a shelf across its panel, gets moved by whoever comes to service it, often unplugged. We find the access panel on the walkthrough, keep it clear by at least the width of the door swing, and put the Amp in another closet or a media cabinet with ventilation. Sonos asks for at least three inches of clearance around an Amp and two inches around a Port, so a shared cupboard rarely works anyway.

Our building was put up under Battery Park City's green guidelines — will you use low-VOC sealant and paint when you patch around the work?

Yes, if you or the building ask, and we ask first. The Authority's Residential Environmental Guidelines of 2000 led to buildings such as The Solaire, where the design team specified low-emission materials to protect indoor air. Most Sonos work leaves very little to patch — a few anchor holes behind a bracket, the edge of a wall plate — but where we fill, seal or touch up, we use low-VOC products and leave the building's own paint for the finish coat. Some managing agents keep a list of approved products or paint colours; send it to us and we'll match it. Plastering and repainting beyond small touch-ups are left to your painter, as on every Manhattan job.

The wall we want for the TV has pipes or ducts somewhere inside it — how do you know where it's safe to drill?

We don't guess. In the newer towers, interior partitions often carry the supply and return pipes or ducts for the apartment's heating and cooling, sprinkler branches and electrical runs. Before the visit we ask the super or managing agent whether the building has as-built drawings for your line of apartments; many do. On the day, we look at what is on the other side of the wall, locate studs, scan, and drill small inspection holes with a depth stop before any larger opening, as the Sonos in-ceiling manual's obstruction-survey advice suggests. If the safe zone for a bracket is not where the TV wants to be, we tell you and offer a floor stand or a different wall rather than taking the risk.

The parks and the waterfront

Are we allowed to play a Sonos portable on the lawn in Rockefeller Park or along the esplanade, and until what time?

Yes, within limits. The Authority's parks rules (21 NYCRR §9003.34) allow "the regular and customary use" of portable devices "so as not unreasonably to disturb other persons", but forbid any sound device in signed quiet zones, and forbid operating one between 10 p.m. and 8 a.m. without a permit. A sound system — something bigger than a portable — needs a permit from the parks corporation. So a Roam 2 or Move 2 at picnic volume on the Rockefeller Park lawn on a Saturday afternoon is fine; the same speaker at full volume on the esplanade after ten is not. We set the portable up so it leaves the apartment cleanly, joins your phone by Bluetooth when it is out of Wi-Fi range, and has a sensible volume limit for outdoor use.

Can our café play music to the tables we set out on the plaza in front?

Not just because the tables are yours. Much of the open space in front of Battery Park City storefronts is parkland or public plaza governed by the Authority's rules, which prohibit operating a sound device "for advertising or commercial purposes" except as the rules permit, and require a permit for any sound system. Whether your outdoor seating area counts depends on your lease and any licence for the seating, so ask your landlord and the Authority before anything points outside. Indoors, we can set a Sonos zone near the door at a level that stays inside, with a volume limit staff can't exceed, and the same 42 dBA limit for music reaching any apartment applies to the floors above.

Living beside the resiliency work

The flood-barrier construction is right outside our building — should we wait until it finishes before installing Sonos?

Usually not, but it changes what we do first. The North/West Battery Park City Resiliency Project began construction in 2026 and, under the current design, runs to around 2030, with some sections delayed by a redesign announced in July 2026. Indoor work is unaffected by the barrier itself. What the construction can affect is access to your building's river side, the noise level during the day while we tune, and anything you were planning to mount on a terrace or a wall shared with the work zone. We book indoor rooms now, run Trueplay at a quiet hour, and leave terrace or balcony speakers until the work in front of your building is complete.

Can vibration from the construction shake a wall-mounted soundbar or speaker loose?

It is unlikely with a correct fixing, but we plan as if it could happen. A local school's notice about the project warns families they may notice intermittent noise and vibration, and the Authority has agreed to put air and noise monitors on some buildings near the work. A bracket set into concrete with anchors rated for the load, or toggled into a steel stud, will not walk out under normal building vibration; one hung on drywall plugs might. We use rated anchors only, torque them to the maker's figure, and avoid shelves where a speaker could creep toward the edge. If you notice a bracket working loose during the construction years, call us and we'll re-check it.

Offices and shops

Our office ceiling is used as a return-air space — does the speaker cable above it have to be plenum-rated?

Yes, if it really is an air space. Industry guides citing the National Electrical Code say cable run in an environmental-air space, such as a ceiling used as a return-air plenum, must carry a plenum rating, such as the CMP marking on plenum communications cable. Many Brookfield Place and other commercial ceilings in the area are built that way. Plenum cable costs more and the building engineer usually wants to see its markings, so we confirm the ceiling type with the engineer before quoting, price the cable accordingly, and support it independently of the ceiling grid as the building's standards require.

Our shop is inside a mall-style complex — who decides how loud our music can be, us or the landlord?

Both, and the landlord has the last word. In a managed complex the lease and the landlord's tenant rules usually set limits on music heard in common areas, hours for contractor work and approval for anything fixed to the ceiling. AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Sonos Installation in Battery Park City

We could not view Google's AI Overview from our research environment, so this section does not quote it. Instead it tests seven assumptions that search answers, product marketing and cost guides commonly make, and that we hear from callers before they phone us about an apartment on the landfill.

Assumption one: "A wall of glass makes any room sound open and spacious"

Lifestyle photography sells river-view living rooms with a speaker on a sideboard and the Hudson behind it. The implication is that the space and the light come with good sound.

Acoustically, a window wall is a mirror. Hard glass sends treble straight back, and in an apartment with a concrete ceiling and a hard floor there is little to soak it up. The result in many Battery Park City living rooms is harsh, smeared dialogue at movie volume, not spaciousness.

Good sound beside glass comes from placement first: speakers that fire along the window instead of into it, soft furnishings doing some of the work, and Trueplay run with the shades where the household keeps them.

Assumption two: "A concrete building is soundproof, so a big subwoofer won't bother anyone"

Buyers moving from older buildings often hear that a concrete tower blocks noise, and conclude that bass is no longer a neighbour problem.

Concrete is excellent at stopping airborne voices and very good at carrying low-frequency vibration through the structure. A Sub standing on the slab or against a column couples its energy into the building, and the apartment below or beside can hear it as a hum. The neighbourhood is quiet; the Authority's own parks rules even set hours for sound.

We match the subwoofer to the room — often a Sub Mini, sometimes none — place it off shared walls, isolate it from the floor and set a volume limit for evenings.

Assumption three: "Luxury condos come with in-ceiling speakers, or can easily get them"

Design sites present flush ceiling speakers as standard for high-end apartments, and many buyers assume a speaker can be cut into any ceiling.

In Battery Park City the ceiling is almost always the underside of a concrete slab, with no cavity to cut into. Ceiling speakers are possible only where a soffit, a dropped hallway ceiling or a developer's bulkhead creates a void, and then only with the building's agreement.

Where no void exists, wall-mounted and free-standing products give better sound than a compromise ceiling would. We check the soffits on the walkthrough and tell you plainly which rooms can take ceiling speakers.

Assumption four: "You need the Battery Park City Authority's permission to install anything"

Because the Authority owns the land and its name is on the parks, the ferry terminal and the resiliency project, people often assume it approves alterations inside apartments too.

The Authority manages the site, the ground leases and the public spaces, and its parks rules govern sound outside. Work inside your apartment is a matter between you, your landlord or condominium board, and the city's codes — the same as anywhere in Manhattan.

So the people to ask before a Sonos install are your managing agent or board. The Authority comes in only if something is going to sound or be fixed outside, on the parkland or the esplanade.

Assumption five: "A range extender or a mesh kit fixes Sonos dropouts in a tower"

Forum answers and retail staff routinely suggest an extender or a mesh system for any wireless problem in an apartment.

Sonos lists wireless range extenders and Ethernet-over-power among unsupported setups, and says that in a mesh with a wired Sonos setup the Sonos product must be wired to the primary node. In a tower with dozens of neighbouring networks above, below and beside you, plus the low-e glass trapping and reflecting signal, adding more wireless hops usually makes things worse.

One cable from the router to a soundbar or an access point does more than any kit. Where the router is by the entry door, a short raceway run usually gets it there.

Assumption six: "Every terrace deserves outdoor speakers"

Outdoor speaker guides assume a private yard and a wall you can drill. Many people picture a pair on the terrace as soon as they see the river.

Not every Battery Park City apartment has outdoor space, and where it does, the condominium often controls the facade and terrace surfaces. The coated glass between the living room and the terrace weakens Wi-Fi, and music outdoors carries across the water and into the parks, where the Authority's rules prohibit operating sound devices between 10 p.m. and 8 a.m.

A charged Roam 2 or Move 2 that comes back inside after the evening is the simplest answer for most terraces here, and it needs no board approval at all.

Assumption seven: "Battery Park City lost power for days after Sandy, so the system should be built for outages"

Lower Manhattan's flooding in 2012 is well documented, and answers about the area often assume every building went dark.

Sandy flooded the ball fields and areas along West Street, according to Wikipedia. But the Tribeca Trib reported that Battery Park City "ended up faring the best of Downtown neighborhoods, with power remaining on in almost all the buildings," with only 400 Gateway Plaza losing power, for three days. The new flood barrier is meant to protect the neighbourhood from the next storm.

An apartment on the twelfth floor does not need a flood-proof Sonos plan. What it needs is a system that rejoins the network by itself after the router restarts, which we set up and test before we leave.

The Waterfront Rules

Parks, Quiet Hours and the Resiliency Years

No other Manhattan neighbourhood has a landlord for its parks the way Battery Park City does. The 36 acres of lawns, coves and esplanade are run by the Authority and its parks corporation under their own regulations, and since November 2025 a construction advisory committee of elected officials, agencies and residents has been meeting about the flood barrier going in along the edge. Both shape how music is used here.

What the parks rules actually say about sound

Section 9003.34 of the Authority's parks rules does four things. It bans "excessive, unreasonable, or unusually loud noise" in any park. It requires a permit from the parks corporation for any sound system, while allowing "the regular and customary use" of portable radios and similar devices as long as they don't unreasonably disturb others — except in signed quiet zones, where even that is prohibited. It forbids playing any instrument or sound device between 10 p.m. and 8 a.m. without a permit. And it bars sound for advertising or commercial purposes. Headphones are allowed everywhere. Separately, the Authority's permits page lists the activities that need a permit in advance, from organised gatherings to wedding ceremonies.

What that means for a Sonos household

For most families it means a portable is the outdoor speaker of choice, and it comes home before ten. We set a Roam 2 or Move 2 with its own volume limit, make sure it hands over cleanly from the apartment Wi-Fi to your phone's Bluetooth, and name it so it doesn't accidentally join a group still playing in the living room. A birthday party with a proper sound system on the lawn is a permit question for the Authority, not something a speaker setting can solve.

Living with the flood-wall construction

Wikipedia describes the North/West Battery Park City Resiliency Project as rebuilding North Cove and the south esplanade and installing flood barriers around South Cove, Rockefeller Park and the north side of Stuyvesant High School, with work slated for 2026 to 2031. The Broadsheet put the budget at about $1.6 billion; The City Reporter, in July 2026, described the wider project as a $2 billion effort and reported that the Authority had reached easement agreements with the Regatta, Gateway Plaza and Hudson Tower — including air and noise monitors on the buildings — but not with Liberty House or Liberty Terrace, forcing a partial redesign that will keep parts of the esplanade closed longer. The chair of Community Board 1 told the paper, "We're going to be living with this for five years."

For Sonos work that means three practical things. Book indoor rooms whenever suits you, and leave terrace speakers until the work outside your building is done. Expect daytime noise and occasional vibration on the river side, which is why we fix brackets with rated anchors and run Trueplay in the quietest hour of the visit. And expect the route to your building's door to change from month to month; we check the construction notices for your street end before the visit.

Quiet indoors, too

Habitat magazine reported in 2015 that the city's noise-complaint data placed the Battery Park City and Lower Manhattan area among the neighbourhoods with the most complaints, alongside the East Village and SoHo. Much of that area is the Financial District, not the landfill, and residents describe their own corner differently — one r/AskNYC commenter summed it up as "Lots of kids, and almost no nightlife. After a long day of work I love coming home to a quiet area." The point still holds for a tower: sound carries through concrete as vibration and through open windows across the coves. Sonos's per-product volume limit, a sensibly sized subwoofer and evening settings agreed at handover keep a good system from becoming a building issue.

A Battery Park City Apartment, Room by Room

Layouts vary from a Gateway Plaza studio to a four-bedroom on River Terrace, but most apartments here share the same parts, and each one asks something different of a Sonos system.

Before the apartment: the desk and the package room

In staffed buildings, Sonos deliveries usually wait at the front desk or in a package room, and contractors are generally signed in and sent up the service elevator. We ask you to tell the desk our name and the time, confirm whether the boxes have arrived, and bring them up ourselves so nothing is opened in the lobby. The entry and the router closet

Providers usually install the router where the line enters, often in a closet near the front door, which puts it at the far end of the apartment from the river-side living room. Concrete and steel between the two weaken the signal before the glass and the neighbours do. A raceway run along the hall to the soundbar, or a wired access point halfway, is usually the single most effective thing we do.

The living room on the river side

The biggest room, the most glass and the best view. The TV and soundbar go on an interior wall; a music pair handles listening toward the view; the Sub, if any, is a Sub Mini set back from shared walls. Everything stays out of the afternoon sun band.

The kitchen

Often open to the living room, with stone counters and tiled walls. One Era 100 on a shelf or counter end is usually right, grouped with the living room for parties and separate for the morning news. Keep it away from the cooktop and the sink, and out of a cabinet that traps heat.

The bedrooms

Smaller, quieter, sometimes with the ventilation unit in a closet. An Era 100 per bedroom with an alarm and a volume limit is enough, mounted high on a stud in a child's room or set on a dresser in the main bedroom. In park-facing bedrooms we keep the level modest with the windows open.

The home office

If someone works from home on the river side, where construction noise is loudest during the day, the office needs its own plan. A single speaker set to its own room, kept out of any group that might suddenly start playing, gives background music without surprising you on a call.

The terrace, if there is one

Board rules first, signal second, neighbours third. A charged portable that comes back inside is the answer for most; fixed outdoor speakers only with written approval and after the resiliency work outside the building is finished.

Our condo board wants music in the lobby, gym and children's playroom — can the building hire you directly?
+

Yes. For a building, the client is the condominium board or the managing agent, and the quote goes to them with each common area priced as its own zone. Our building signed an easement for the flood-barrier project — does that change what can be installed inside our apartment?
+

Not inside the apartment.
