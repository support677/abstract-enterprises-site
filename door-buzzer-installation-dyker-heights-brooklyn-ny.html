The Neighborhood

Why Dyker Heights Properties Need Door Buzzer Installation

Dyker Heights sits on the high ground of southwest Brooklyn, roughly between 7th and 14th Avenues from 65th Street down to the Belt Parkway, in ZIP code 11228 and Community District 10. It began in 1895 as a planned suburb of large houses on big lots, and the deed restrictions of that era set houses back from the street. A 2007 rezoning of 159 blocks, approved by the City Council 48 to 0, locked that pattern in: much of the neighborhood is now zoned for detached houses only, with detached and semi-detached houses allowed in the next band and rowhouses only at the edges. Homes.com sums up the result plainly, describing housing here as "a limited inventory of single- and two-family homes." For an entry installer, that is the starting point. A Dyker Heights front door is rarely a lobby door. It is a house door, often behind a yard, a fence and a gate.

That shape creates the first problem we are asked to solve. On a house set back from the sidewalk, a visitor who rings a plain bell at the door has already walked through the gate and up the path, so the gate has to be left unlatched for anyone to reach the bell at all. Owners who want the gate kept shut, for children, for dogs, for the bins and the car, or simply to keep strangers off the porch, need a way to talk to the visitor at the gate and open it from inside the house. Owners who live upstairs in a two-family want to answer without walking down two flights. And owners who have rebuilt or enlarged a house often find that the old bell wire went into the dumpster with the old plaster.

The second problem is packages. Residents here have complained for years about parcels disappearing from stoops and porches. In August 2019, Brooklyn Paper reported that one package thief had "plagued her neighbors for nearly 20 years," that a package was taken from a stoop by 78th Street and 11th Avenue that July, and that she had been arrested five times in Dyker Heights and Bensonhurst that year alone. One resident told the paper, "My neighbors and I try to take in packages for each other so as to not have her come stealing, but it's hard sometimes." Years later, an r/Brooklyn thread titled "Beware: Dyker Heights Package stealer" warned of a man with "large tote bags" who "will swoop in to steal after it's delivered." A door station does not stop a determined thief on its own, but the way it is set up decides whether a courier can talk to someone, whether the parcel can go somewhere locked, and whether the house has a clear picture of who came up the walk.

The third problem is new. A neighborhood with very few apartment buildings is starting to get some. New York YIMBY reported in October 2024 that permits had been filed for a three-story, 11-apartment building with a cellar and eight open parking spaces at 1302 83rd Street, and in January 2026 that permits had been filed for a five-story, 22-apartment mixed-use building with ground-floor commercial space at 7924 13th Avenue, on a corner lot by the 79th Street D station. Small new condo buildings have also come to market along 13th Avenue. Each of those buildings needs a real multi-apartment entry system, built to the city's intercom rules, on blocks where neighbors are used to a single doorbell.

Finally, a lot of houses here hold more than one household, and some hold more than their paperwork says. The NYU Furman Center counted 168 sales of two-to-four-family buildings in Bay Ridge and Dyker Heights in 2025, more than the 135 one-family sales. The Department of Buildings defines an illegal conversion as altering a building "to create additional apartment units without first obtaining the proper permits," for example "adding an apartment in the basement, attic or garage." We wire a door station for the households a house legally has, and we tell owners plainly when a request for an extra button would advertise an apartment the city does not know about. That conversation is better had before the job than after an inspector counts the buttons.

Repair or Replace in Dyker Heights

Need Repair in Dyker Heights? A Gate That Will Not Open From the House, an Upstairs Bell That Went Dead, or a Rebuilt House With No Buzzer at All

Repair calls from Dyker Heights fall into three patterns. The first is a working system with one dead part: a release button upstairs that hums but does not open the gate, a station on the second floor that rings but has no voice, a front door that releases and then will not latch again. The second is a house that changed and left its buzzer behind: the stoop was rebuilt and the station removed, the house was gut renovated and the old bell wire cut, or a second household moved upstairs and both families now share one bell. The third is a house that never had a buzzer, only a plain bell, and an owner who now wants to keep the gate shut.

Each one starts the same way: a call to (347) 934-8335 or photos on WhatsApp of the station, the door, the gate and the cellar wall where the wires come in. Where an existing system has to be diagnosed in person, that is the $250 service call booked online, and the $250 is applied toward the repair or toward a new system if replacing turns out to be the better value. You get both numbers and a plain recommendation. On a house with one or two households, a repair is often a single part, a new strike, a new power supply or a new indoor station, and we say so rather than selling a whole system.

The physical faults follow a pattern too. On houses here, the most common causes of a release that stopped working are a strike that has drifted out of line with its latch as the frame moved, a power supply in the cellar that has aged or been unplugged during other work, and wire damaged where it runs outside, along a fence, under a gate post or through a rotted sill. The part inside the walls is usually fine. When the gate station itself is dead, water is the usual reason, because a station on a gate pier takes rain and snow on its face all winter, and older units were not always mounted under a hood or sealed at the back.

When the house has changed and the buzzer went with it, the question is not repair but how to start again without opening finished walls. We cover that in the sections below: the route from the gate, the old intercom cable that some houses still have, and the cases where a wireless link or a two-wire system saves a renovated house from new holes.

The surviving 1890s houses: big rooms, many layers of old wire

The houses that survive from the first development were built for a single family with room to spare. A Brooklyn Eagle description from 1899, quoted in the neighborhood's Wikipedia entry, mentions first-floor ceilings of about ten feet, five rooms on each of the first two floors, four more on the third and a cellar under the house. That generosity is the challenge. The distance from the front door to a bedroom on the third floor is long, the walls are plaster on wood lath, and over a century most of these houses have had a speaking tube, a battery bell, a transformer bell, a room-to-room intercom and a wireless doorbell, often with bits of each left in the walls.

Our approach is to find a single clean path from the door to the cellar, because the cellar is usually open and is the easiest place to reach every floor from. From there, closets that stack, an old chimney chase or a disused laundry chute often carry new cable upward without opening finished rooms. Where none exists, a two-wire system that runs power, voice, video and release over one pair lets us reuse a single good old pair to each floor. Porches add one more decision: whether the station goes at the porch steps, at the door under the porch roof, or at the front gate, and each choice changes how far the visitor is from the household and how well the camera sees a face.

These houses also tend to have heavy original doors. A release on an old wood door has to be sized for the door's weight and for frames that have moved over the years, and we check the latch and strike line before quoting. A strike that is fitted to a frame that is out of square will buzz and do nothing.

Brick detached houses with side drives: two doors, a gate and a garage

The detached brick houses that fill much of the side streets were built with a car in mind. A typical lot has a front yard behind a low wall or fence, a walk to a front door that is formal and little used, and a driveway down one side to a side door near the kitchen and a garage at the back. In practice the side door is where the family comes and goes, and many deliveries end up there too.

That makes the first question which door the station belongs on, and the honest answer is often both, or neither, with the station at the gate instead. If the front gate is kept locked, the station goes at the gate and one release opens it. If the gate stays open by day and the family wants visitors at the side door, the station goes there, with a sign or a lit button at the front to send people round. If both doors get visitors, a two-door system with one indoor monitor that shows which door is calling avoids a second set of equipment.

Cable in these houses usually comes up from the cellar, which runs under the whole house. The side door is often a few feet above the driveway, with the cellar wall right behind it, which makes it one of the easiest doors in Brooklyn to wire. The front door, with a stoop and a porch, and the gate, out by the sidewalk, take more work, which is why the route matters so much on the quote.

Semi-detached pairs: one wall, two families, one walk

Semi-detached houses are the most common two-family stock in the neighborhood's middle zones. Each house in the pair is its own property, but the pair shares a party wall and sometimes a stoop, a walk or a front gate. Inside each house there are often two households, one per floor, with a single street door and an inner hall to two apartment doors.

The entry system in a pair has to respect two boundaries. The first is between the two houses: a station and its cable should stay on the owner's side of the wall, and a shared gate belongs to both owners, so either both agree on how it is controlled or each keeps its own key. The second is inside the house: each household needs its own button and its own way to answer, and the downstairs family should not hear every call for the tenants upstairs. A two-button audio or video station with one station on each floor covers that, and it is the job we do most often in two-family houses here.

The mistake we see is a single consumer video doorbell put on a two-family's shared door, with one household holding the account. It works until the household that owns the account moves out or stops sharing access, and then the other family has no way to answer at all.

Rebuilt large houses: new fronts, big doors, finished walls

Many lots in Dyker Heights now carry a newer, larger house. These houses tend to have masonry fronts, iron gates set between piers, wide or oversized entry doors and several finished floors, and they are often wired from scratch during construction. When the builder thought about the door station, these are the easiest houses in the neighborhood to finish: a pipe from the gate pier to the cellar, a cable to each floor and a box by the door. When the builder did not, they can be the hardest, because the walls are new, finished and no owner wants them opened.

The answer depends on what the walls already hold. New houses often have network cable to each floor for Wi-Fi or a television, and an idle pair in that bundle can carry a two-wire system. Where there is nothing, a wireless link from the gate to the house, or a video station that answers on the family's phones with one wired monitor near the stair, keeps holes to a minimum. Large entry doors add the second question: a pivot door or an oversized double door needs a release chosen for that door, which we cover in the questions below.

Attached rows at the edges: stoops at the sidewalk

At the edges of the neighborhood, where the 2007 rezoning mapped R5B rowhouse districts, houses sit in attached rows with stoops close to the sidewalk and small front areas. These look more like the rest of southwest Brooklyn than like the detached blocks, and the entry work is closer to a standard Brooklyn two-family: a two-button station by the door, a strike in the street door, a station on each floor and cable through the cellar and up an interior wall or a closet stack. The difference from the detached blocks is that there is rarely a gate, so the station goes at the door and the camera can see the visitor from a few feet away.

Small condos and walk-ups: shared doors and absentee owners

The small condo buildings and older walk-ups on and near 13th Avenue serve a handful of households behind one street door. StreetEasy lists 6605 13th Avenue, for example, as a 1931 mixed-use building with three stories and six units. In a building like that, the entry system serves people who may not know each other, owners who may rent out their units, and sometimes a store on the ground floor with its own door. A short directory panel with a button or a code per apartment, a strike that latches every time, and a closer that pulls the door shut behind every visitor are the basics. Who controls the directory and who pays for repairs should be written down when the system goes in, especially in a condo with a small board.

New rentals on former house lots: apartment rules on a house block

The new buildings filed at 1302 83rd Street and 7924 13th Avenue, with 11 and 22 apartments, are the clearest sign that Dyker Heights is adding apartment buildings to its house blocks. Buildings of eight or more apartments put up after 1968 must have an intercommunication system at a self-locking main door under the Multiple Dwelling Law, and a new building will be inspected against that. For these buildings we work from the drawings: where the main entrance and the rear or parking entrance are, where the equipment room is, where each apartment's station or app answering will be, and which trade pulls which cable. Getting that settled before the walls close is the cheapest entry system the builder will ever buy.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Stand at the Sidewalk, Walk to the Door, Then Go Down to the Cellar

These notes describe how our technicians approach a house in a neighborhood like Dyker Heights. They are our standard practice, not a story about one address.

Start where the visitor starts. The technician stands at the sidewalk first, not at the door. Is there a gate? Is it kept locked? Can a courier tell which door to use? Is the house number visible at night? A plan built from the front door outward often misses that the visitor never gets that far.

Walk the route and measure it. Next, the walk from the gate to each door visitors use, with the distance, the surface the cable would cross and anything in the way: steps, a paved walk, a planting bed, a driveway. That distance is the biggest single number on many Dyker Heights quotes.

Find the way down. Last, the cellar. Where do the old bell wires come through the wall? Is there an outlet for a power supply on a circuit the owner controls? Which closets or chases stack above it? On most houses here, the cellar is what makes the job clean or messy, and we would rather know that before writing a number than after.

Gate to Door

Thirty Feet From the Sidewalk: Putting the Station at the Gate, at the Door, or Both

The single most common Dyker Heights entry question is where the station goes. On a house that sits back from the street behind a fence, the answer decides how much cable has to be run, what kind of lock goes on the gate, how well the camera sees a face, and whether a visitor at night can find the button at all. There are three workable setups, and each one suits a different household.

Setup 1: the station at the gate, the gate locked

This is the setup for owners who want the yard closed. The station mounts on the gate pier or the fence post beside the gate, and the release is an electric lock or strike on the gate itself. A visitor presses the button, the household answers on an indoor monitor or a phone, and if they open, the gate unlocks for a few seconds and a closer or spring pulls it shut again. The front door keeps its normal key lock, because nobody needs to open it remotely: the household walks the visitor in or meets them at the door.

The work is mostly outside. Cable has to run from the gate to the house, and on a Dyker Heights lot that usually means across a front yard, under a walk or along the base of a fence. Gate locks need power and a gate that closes reliably; a sagging gate that has to be lifted to latch will defeat any release, so we check the hinges and the latch line first and say so if the gate needs work before the electronics go on. Iron gates also need a way to carry cable onto the moving leaf if the lock is mounted there, usually a protected loop at the hinge side.

The payoff is that the household decides who reaches the porch. For families with small children or dogs in the front yard, for owners who were burned by packages left on the stoop, and for houses on blocks that fill with visitors in December, this is the setup we recommend most.

Setup 2: the station at the door, the gate unlocked

This is the setup for owners who are happy to let people up the walk and want to answer at the door. The station goes beside the front or side door, a strike goes into the door frame, and the household can talk to the visitor and open the door from inside. The gate stays on a simple latch, or has no lock at all. It is the cheaper of the two outdoor setups because the cable path is short, often straight down into the cellar behind the door.

The downside is that a stranger gets to the door before anyone has spoken to them, and a parcel left there sits on an open porch. For many households that is fine, especially where the side door is under a carport or the porch is enclosed. Where the front door is far from the room the family lives in, we put an indoor monitor where people actually spend the day, usually the kitchen, and a second one upstairs.

Setup 3: a station at the gate and a station at the door

Some houses need both: a gate station so the yard can stay closed, and a door station because family members and regular helpers come up the driveway to the side door. One indoor monitor can answer both, with the screen showing which entrance is calling. This is also the right setup for a two-family where the downstairs household uses the side door and the upstairs household uses the front: each door gets its own station, and the gate station has two buttons.

Whatever the setup, a few details make it work on a Dyker Heights lot. The station needs a hood or a recessed mount where it faces the weather. The button should light, because a front walk under trees is dark by five o'clock in winter. The house number should be on or near the station, so couriers stop at the right gate. And the cable should be sleeved where it runs outside, because shovels, edgers and contractors' equipment find unprotected cable within a season.

Wired, wireless or a mix across the yard

Wired is still the most reliable way to connect a gate station to a house. Where the route crosses lawn or a planting bed, a shallow trench with cable in conduit is quick. Where it crosses a paved walk, a stone path or a driveway, the choices are to cut a narrow slot and patch it, to go around the paving along a fence line, or to bore under it. Each has a price, and the quote names the one we plan to use.

Wireless links between the gate and the house exist and work over the distances typical here, but they depend on batteries or a separate power feed at the gate, and on a clear signal through whatever stands between the gate and the house. A brick house with a metal-framed porch can be a poor path. We use wireless where trenching would damage something an owner cares about, such as a new paver driveway, and we test the signal at the gate before we commit to it. HomeAdvisor's cost guide prices wireless intercom systems at $150 to $1,500 and hard-wired ones at $1,000 to $15,000, but on a house the difference is mostly the labor across the yard, not the equipment.

Packages and Stoops

Twenty Years of Stolen Parcels: What a Door Station Can and Cannot Do for Dyker Heights Deliveries

Package theft is the complaint we hear most from Dyker Heights callers, and the local record explains why. Brooklyn Paper's 2019 report described a thief who had worked the stoops of Dyker Heights and Bensonhurst for close to two decades, who was arrested again and again, and whose penalty that year was five days of community service, because petty larceny is a misdemeanor and, the paper reported, judges could not weigh the many charges together at sentencing. One resident had caught the thief on her own surveillance cameras taking parcels off her porch, and she was arrested and came back anyway. The lesson residents drew was not that cameras failed. It was that a recording after the fact does not keep the parcel.

That is the right frame for an entry system too. A door station helps with packages in three specific ways, and none of them is magic. The first is conversation: a courier who rings and is answered can be told where to leave the parcel, whether behind the side gate, inside the enclosed porch or with the neighbor two doors down, instead of dropping it on the top step in view of the street. The second is a locked place to leave it: with a gate station and a release, the household can let the courier into the yard, or into an enclosed porch or vestibule, and the gate or outer door locks again behind them. The third is a clear picture of who came up the walk, taken at face height by a camera that was positioned for faces rather than for the whole street.

What it cannot do is replace someone being reachable. A release only works if a person answers. That is why, for households that are out all day, we pair the indoor monitors with phone answering, so the courier's ring reaches whoever is free, and why we set the release time on a gate or outer door to the shortest interval that still lets a courier with a box get through.

The neighbor network, done properly

The resident who told Brooklyn Paper that she and her neighbors take in each other's packages was describing the oldest package system in Brooklyn. A modern entry system can support that habit instead of replacing it. Many current video stations with app answering let an owner add another person's phone as a user, with or without the right to release the door, and remove that person again later. A neighbor who is home during the day can then answer a courier at your gate, see the courier on screen and let them leave the box inside your yard. We set those permissions up with the owner at handover so they are deliberate, named and easy to remove.

An enclosed porch or vestibule as the parcel room

Many Dyker Heights houses have an enclosed front porch or a vestibule between an outer and an inner door. That space can act as a small parcel room. The outer door gets a strike and the station; the inner door keeps its key lock. When a courier rings, the household releases the outer door, the courier sets the box down inside, and the outer door shuts and locks behind them. The house itself is never opened. Two details matter: the outer door must relatch reliably, which means a closer set for the door's weight and the wind on that side of the house, and the inner door must stay locked whenever the outer one is released, so the porch never becomes a path into the house.

Where the camera should look

The cameras built into door stations are set up to show the face of the person standing in front of them, not the sidewalk. On a gate pier that is exactly what you want: the person pressing the button, at arm's length, lit by the station's own light at night. A separate camera covering the porch, the steps and the walk is a different job, and on most houses we recommend both when packages are the concern, with the station camera for who rang and the porch camera for where the parcel went. Where the owner already has cameras on a recorder, we check whether the new indoor monitor or app can show them in the same place.

New Buildings

Eleven and Twenty-Two Apartments on a House Block: Planning Entry for the New Buildings Going Up in Dyker Heights

For most of its history Dyker Heights has had very few apartment buildings, and the Wikipedia entry for the neighborhood still describes its housing as mainly detached and semi-detached one- and two-family homes. That is changing at the margins. New York YIMBY's reports on the permits for 1302 83rd Street, a three-story building of 11 apartments with a cellar and eight open parking spaces, and for 7924 13th Avenue, a five-story corner building with 22 apartments over about 5,000 square feet of commercial space near the 79th Street D station, describe the kind of project we are now asked to quote. Small new condo buildings on 13th Avenue have also been listed for sale as brand new.

A building of that size is a different entry job from a house. It needs a main-door panel that can reach every apartment, a station or app answering in each unit, a release on a self-locking door, a second entrance from the parking area or the rear, a power supply and controller in an equipment room, and a written plan for who keeps the directory current as tenants come and go. It is also a building the law treats differently, which we explain in the rules section below. The best time to plan all of that is while the walls are open.

What the builder should decide before the walls close

Four decisions shape the cost of a new building's entry system more than anything else, and all four are cheap to make on paper and expensive to change later. The first is how residents will answer: a wall station in every apartment, app answering on residents' phones, or both. A wired station needs a cable or a pair to every unit, pulled during rough-in. App answering needs a reliable network at the panel and, ideally, a cellular backup. Doing both means pulling the cable anyway and adding the app on top.

The second is which doors get panels. The main entrance always does. A rear or side door from parking usually gets either a smaller station or a reader for residents only, so visitors are sent to the front. A package room, a trash room door or a roof door may need a release or a door-held-open alert. Each one is a conduit run and a box location to put on the drawings.

The third is where the brains live. The controller, the power supply and any network switch should sit in a dry, ventilated room the owner controls, on a circuit the owner controls, ideally with a battery backup. In a small building that is often a corner of the cellar or a ground-floor telecom closet. The fourth is the door itself: the main entrance should be specified with a frame and hardware that can take an electric strike or another approved lock, a closer strong enough to pull it shut every time, and a free-exit device on the inside. Changing a storefront-style aluminum door after the fact to fit a release is one of the most common avoidable costs we see.

Who pulls the cable

On a new building, entry cable can be pulled by the electrician during rough-in or by the low-voltage contractor. Either works, as long as one party owns it, the cable is the type the chosen system needs, every run is labeled at both ends, and the boxes are where the drawings say. What fails is the split where the electrician pulls a generic cable because the system has not been chosen yet, and the installer arrives months later to find the wrong cable to every apartment. If the builder brings us in at the drawing stage, we supply a one-page cable and box schedule that the electrician can price and follow.

The parking door and the store

The two new buildings above show the two complications that set Dyker Heights new construction apart from a standard walk-up. A building with open parking at the back has a second entrance that residents will use more than the front, often after dark, and that door needs to lock behind every person, be lit, and be reachable from the parking area without a long walk around. A building with a store on the ground floor has a commercial tenant whose deliveries, staff and customers must never be routed through the residential door, and whose own buzzer, if it has one, should be a separate system. Planning both at the start keeps the residents' entry simple.

The Rules

The Intercom Law, Free Exit and Legal Unit Counts: What a Dyker Heights Owner or Builder Is Responsible For

Most Dyker Heights houses are one- and two-family homes, and for those the city does not dictate what kind of buzzer an owner installs. The rules start to matter in three situations: a building with enough apartments to fall under the state Multiple Dwelling Law, any door that people must be able to leave by, and any house whose number of buttons does not match its legal number of apartments. This is a plain summary, not legal advice; a building owner with a specific question should check with the city agency or a lawyer.

Buildings of eight or more apartments

Section 50-a of the Multiple Dwelling Law says that every Class A multiple dwelling erected or converted after January 1, 1968, with eight or more apartments, "shall also be equipped with an intercommunication system" at a self-locking main entrance door, allowing a tenant to talk to a visitor and release the door from the apartment. Older buildings of that size must install self-locking doors and an intercom if tenants occupying a majority of the apartments ask in writing, with installation to begin within 90 days and finish within six months. Both new Dyker Heights buildings described above, with 11 and 22 apartments, fall on the required side of that line. An intercom that stops working in a covered building can be written up by HPD.

Buildings below eight apartments

For smaller buildings, such as the six-unit 1931 building StreetEasy lists at 6605 13th Avenue, that section does not require an intercom. A Brooklyn tenant on r/AskNYC put it bluntly about a four-unit building: the landlord "isn't required to put in a buzzer system or a self-locking front door." Many owners of small buildings install one anyway, because a street door that nobody can release from upstairs ends up propped open, and a propped street door is how strangers and package thieves get to the apartment doors.

Free exit, always

Whatever the building, the people inside must be able to leave without a key, a code or power. That is why we fit fail-secure electric strikes on most house and street doors: Allegion's guide explains that with a strike, "you can exit by turning the lever" whether the strike is fail-safe or fail-secure, and that fail-secure products stay locked from outside when power is removed. Magnetic locks are a different case. Allegion notes they "are only available fail safe," and on an egress door the building code requires a sensor on the inside, a manual push-to-exit button between 40 and 48 inches above the floor within five feet of the door, release on loss of power and release on a fire alarm. We use them only where a strike cannot work, and only with all of those parts.

Buttons and the legal unit count

The Department of Buildings treats an apartment added "in the basement, attic or garage" without permits as an illegal conversion, and a row of extra buttons by the door is one of the most visible signs of one. We wire stations for the households the house legally has. If an owner is legalizing a unit through the city's process, we can leave capacity for one more button and add it when the paperwork is done. We will not label a button for an apartment that does not legally exist, and we would rather explain that on the phone than on installation day.

Reach heights for the panel

For panels on new multi-apartment buildings and on businesses that serve the public, we mount buttons and controls within the 2010 ADA Standards' reach ranges, which set the high forward reach at "48 inches (1220 mm) maximum" and the low forward reach at 15 inches minimum. On a private house the owner chooses, and we usually mount at a height that suits both an adult and a child or a visitor in a wheelchair.

Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Installation in Dyker Heights: Questions Answered

Questions Dyker Heights owners, builders, condo boards and business owners ask us about new entry systems, grouped by the part of the job they concern. Each answer is the one we would give on the phone, before anyone has been out.

Gates, yards and doors

Getting cable from our front gate to the house means crossing a paved path and part of the driveway. Do you dig it up, cut a slot, or go wireless?

It depends on what the paving is and how much you care about it. Across poured concrete or asphalt, a narrow saw-cut slot with the cable in a small conduit, sealed over, is usually the cleanest and lasts as long as the paving. Across pavers or stone, we can often lift a single row, lay conduit beneath, and reset them, which leaves no visible patch. Where the paving is new, decorative or expensive to match, we look first for a route around it, along the fence and the side of the walk, even if that is a longer run.

Wireless is the fallback, not the first choice. A wireless link between the gate and the house avoids the paving entirely, but it needs power at the gate, either a battery that someone has to change or a small power cable that brings back the trench question, and it needs a clear signal through whatever stands between the gate and the inside unit. We test the signal at the gate before quoting it.

Whichever route we choose is written on the quote with its price, including the patching. If the ground is frozen hard in midwinter and a trench is the only route, we say so and offer the next workable date rather than patching a slot that will crack in the spring.

Our driveway gate already has its own motor and remote openers. Can the door station open it too, or does it need a separate intercom?

Usually the door station can trigger it. Most gate operators have an input for an external push button or a relay, the same input a wall button in the house would use, and a door station with a spare relay output can close that contact when someone at the station is let in. That lets one call open the walk gate, the driveway gate, or either one, from the same indoor monitor or phone.

Before we promise it, we need the make and model of the gate operator and a photo of its control board. Some operators are under a service contract with the company that installed them, and some newer ones are app-controlled with no spare input. In those cases the simplest answer may be a separate button on the indoor monitor wired to the operator, or leaving the car gate on its remotes and handling only the walk gate.

One safety point: a motorized gate must keep its own safety sensors and reversing functions. The door station only sends the same open command a wall button would. It never bypasses anything in the operator.

The lock on our front gate is on the swinging iron leaf, not on the post. How does a release get power on a gate that moves?

Through a protected cable loop at the hinge side, or by moving the electric part to the post. The usual way is a flexible armored loop that crosses from the fixed post to the moving leaf at the hinges, so the cable bends as the gate swings without being pinched. The release on the leaf is then wired through the gate's frame or along its rail.

Often it is simpler to reverse the arrangement: put an electric strike or keeper on the fixed post, where power is easy to reach, and keep the mechanical latch on the leaf. That works when the gate closes squarely against the post. On older iron gates that sag or bind, the gate has to be rehung or adjusted first, or no release will work reliably.

Either way, the station itself goes on the fixed pier or post, never on the moving leaf, so it is not shaken every time the gate slams.

Our rebuilt house has a tall pivot front door. Can it take an electric release?

Sometimes, but it needs a release chosen for that door. A pivot door turns on a pin near one edge instead of on hinges, and many have a long latch or a multipoint lock on the opposite edge. A standard strike does not fit those. The options are an electric strike made for the specific latch, a motorized multipoint lock from the door's own manufacturer, or, on a door that will never have one, leaving the front door on its key and putting the release on the gate or a side door.

We ask for the door's make and the lock's model before quoting. If the manufacturer offers a motorized lock for that door, it is usually the cleanest option, because it is designed for the door's weight and seal. Fitting a generic strike into a premium pivot door's frame risks damaging a door that cost far more than the entry system.

Our 1970s house still has a room-to-room intercom with speakers in several rooms. Can its wiring carry a new video system?

Often, yes. Old whole-house intercoms were usually wired with multi-conductor cable from a master unit to each room speaker and to the door. That cable, if it tests clean, can frequently carry a two-wire system, which needs only one good pair between the door station and each indoor station. Aiphone's upgrade guide puts it simply: "If the existing wires are in good condition, they can be reused."

The test is what decides it. We check each run for continuity, shorts and damage at both ends, and we find where each one actually goes, because cable in houses that age has often been cut, spliced or abandoned. Good runs get reused; bad ones get replaced or bypassed. The old master unit and room speakers come out and the openings are covered with plates or the new stations themselves, so you do not end up with dead speakers in the walls.

If the old cable is beyond saving, the fallback is a route through the cellar and up a stacked closet or chase, or wireless indoor units on the upper floors where running cable would mean opening finished walls.

Packages, neighbors and households

Our neighbors and we take in each other's deliveries. Can a neighbor's phone be set up to answer our gate for a courier, and taken off again later?

On many video stations with app answering, yes. The owner of the system can add another person as a user, choose whether that person can only see and talk to the visitor or can also release the door, and remove them at any time. A neighbor who is home in the daytime can then answer a courier at your gate, tell them to leave the box inside the yard, and open the gate for a few seconds.

We set this up deliberately at handover: who is on the list, what each person can do, and how to remove them. We also recommend that a neighbor's access be limited to the gate or the outer porch door, never the house door, so the arrangement covers parcels and nothing else.

Our dental practice uses the ground floor of the house our family lives in. Can patients be buzzed into the office during hours without being able to reach the stairs to our home?

Yes, and it usually takes two doors and two schedules. The practice gets its own station at its entrance, answered at the front desk, with a release on the practice door only. The family's door, or the inner door to the family's stair, stays on its own station and its own release, answered upstairs. Patients can be let in to the waiting room but have no way to open the family's door, and the family's visitors never ring the front desk.

Where the practice and the family share one outer door, the outer door gets a two-button station, one for the office and one for the house, and the inner doors do the separating. Many systems also let the office button be set to ring the front desk only during office hours and to ring the house, or nobody, after closing, so a late visitor does not reach an empty desk.

New buildings and small condos

We are building an 11-apartment rental on a side street. Which trade should carry the entry system in the bid package: the general contractor, the electrician or a low-voltage installer?

Any of the three can carry it, but one of them has to own it from the drawings to the final test. The common working split is that the electrician provides the 120-volt circuit and any conduit and boxes the plans call for, and the low-voltage installer supplies the system, pulls or approves the entry cable, terminates, programs and tests. The general contractor coordinates the door hardware so the main entrance arrives ready for a strike or an approved lock.

What causes trouble is leaving the system unchosen until late. Then the electrician pulls a generic cable to every apartment, the door is ordered without a prepared frame, and the installer arrives to find the wrong cable and a door that cannot take a release. If you bring us in at the drawing stage, we give the electrician a cable and box schedule and the door supplier a hardware note, and the bid prices line up.

Because a new building of 11 apartments falls under the Multiple Dwelling Law's intercom requirement, the system should be in, tested and documented before the building is occupied.

Our new building has open parking at the back, and residents will mostly come in through the rear door. Should that door have a full intercom panel too?

Usually not a full visitor panel, but it does need real access control. If visitors can ring at both the front and the back, they will choose whichever is closer to where they parked, and the rear door becomes a second lobby with nobody watching it. The better arrangement in most small buildings is a resident-only reader or keypad at the rear door, plus a small sign directing visitors to the front panel.

The rear door also needs the basics that the front gets: a strike or approved lock that relatches every time, a closer strong enough to pull it shut in wind, good lighting and a camera if the owner has one elsewhere. Where the owner does want visitors to use the rear, a compact station there that calls the same apartments keeps the directory in one place.

The store on our new corner building's ground floor will take deliveries after hours. Should its door be on the same entry system as the 22 apartments above?

No. Keep them separate. The apartments' system should serve residents and their visitors only, with a directory, codes and permissions managed for the building. The store's door should have its own station, answered at the counter or on the store manager's phone, with its own release and its own users. Mixing them means a store employee or a delivery driver ends up holding a code or a permission that opens the residents' door, and a tenant turnover at the store becomes a security question for the whole building.

Separate systems can still share infrastructure the owner controls, such as conduit space and an equipment room, but the store's equipment should be the tenant's to manage under its lease. We price the two as separate scopes so the owner can bill the commercial tenant if the lease says so.

The 2007 rezoning left our block zoned for detached houses only. Does that zoning limit what we can mount on the front of the house or on the gate?

Not for the entry system itself. Zoning decides what kind of building can stand on the lot, how big it can be and how it is used; CityLand's account of the 2007 rezoning describes districts limited to detached houses, others allowing semi-detached houses, and rowhouse districts at the edges. A door station on a gate pier, an electric strike in a door frame and a low-voltage cable in a trench are not what those rules govern.

Where something else can come into play is a new structure. If the plan is to build new masonry piers or a new fence to hold the gate station, that is construction, and the owner should confirm what is allowed for front-yard walls and fences before a mason starts. We mount on what is there; we do not build piers or fences. Separately, if a house is an individual city landmark, exterior changes go through the Landmarks Preservation Commission first.

The zoning does shape the job in one practical way: because so much of the neighborhood is detached houses set back from the sidewalk, the gate-to-house route described above is the normal case here rather than the exception.

"Audio-only intercoms and buzzer systems no longer meet the demands of today's residents"

ButterflyMX says this in its guide to video intercoms in New York City, and for a large rental building competing for tenants it may well be true. Seeing a visitor before opening the door is a real improvement, and we install far more video than audio.

But plenty of Dyker Heights households are well served by audio. An owner who wants a gate kept shut and a simple way to let the family's regular visitors in, without screens or apps, may be happiest with an audio station at the gate and a handset in the kitchen. It costs less, has fewer parts to fail and nothing to update.

The better question is what the household will actually do with the system. If people will want to see who is at the gate from upstairs or answer from work, buy video. If they will always walk to the window anyway, audio with a good release may be the smarter purchase, and video can be added later on most two-wire systems without pulling new cable.

"For a new development, choose a system without building wiring or in-unit hardware"

Also from ButterflyMX's NYC guide, aimed at developers watching their budgets. A panel that calls residents' phones over a cellular or internet connection, with no cable to the apartments and no stations on the walls, does save money at construction.

For a new Dyker Heights rental of 11 or 22 apartments, the trade-off deserves a hard look. The Multiple Dwelling Law requires an intercom that lets tenants talk to a visitor and release the door from the apartment. A phone-only system meets that only while every tenant has a working phone, a working app and a signal, and while the building's connection is up. Residents who prefer not to use an app, older relatives visiting from abroad, or a tenant whose phone is off are left without a way to answer.

Pulling a cable to every apartment during rough-in costs little compared with adding it after the walls are closed. Our usual recommendation for a new building here is to pull the cable anyway, choose a panel that supports both stations and app answering, and let the owner decide unit by unit.

"Most commercial and multi-tenant installations require NYC electrical or low-voltage permits"

Some installer blogs say this in their FAQ, and the instinct behind it, that entry work in a building is regulated, is sound. The details are where these summaries get vague.

What an owner can rely on is this. The entry system is low-voltage work, and in New York State it should be done by a licensed installer; ours is NYS license #12000287431. Any new 120-volt outlet or circuit for the power supply is electrical work for a licensed electrician. On a new building, the entry system is part of the work the building's own filings and inspections cover, which is why the builder's team should plan it.

If an installer tells you a permit is needed, ask which agency issues it and for which part of the work. A clear answer is a good sign; a vague one is worth a second quote.

"Allow one to two hours per station"

HomeAdvisor's intercom cost guide gives that figure for installing stations, and inside a house with open walls or reusable wire it is about right. Mounting an indoor monitor on a prepared box and connecting a pair really does take an hour or so.

It leaves out the parts that take time on a Dyker Heights job: the route from the gate, the strike that has to be fitted to an old frame, the door closer that has to be adjusted so the door relatches, the cable that has to be fished up three floors of a century-old house, and the testing. A gate-to-house video system with a station on every floor of a large detached house is often a full day for two technicians.

FAQ

Dyker Heights Door Buzzer Installation FAQ

Is a station on the front gate priced the same as one at the front door?
+

The station itself costs about the same, but the job usually does not. A gate station needs cable run across the yard or walk, a release suited to the gate rather than a door frame, and often a hood against the weather, so a gate job tends to sit higher in the same Brooklyn range than a door job on the same house. The quote shows the route and its cost separately so you can compare the two setups.

Our house has a front door, a side door off the driveway and a front gate. Can one indoor monitor answer all three?
+

Yes, on most video systems. Each entrance gets its own outdoor station and its own release, and the indoor monitor shows which one is calling and opens only that one. Many monitors accept several outdoor stations, so the family answers every door from the same screen in the kitchen or upstairs instead of having separate equipment for each.

Does a door buzzer system use much electricity?
+

Very little. The power supply runs low-voltage stations that sit idle most of the day, and indoor monitors draw more only while their screens are on during a call. The strike or gate lock draws power only for the few seconds it is released on a fail-secure setup. On a typical house the system's use is small next to everyday appliances.

Can a door buzzer be installed in an older house that still has knob-and-tube wiring?
+

Yes. The entry system is low-voltage and runs on its own cable, separate from the house's power wiring. The only point where it meets the house's electricity is the power supply, which plugs into an outlet or is wired to a circuit. If the nearest outlet is on old knob-and-tube wiring, we recommend a licensed electrician add a modern outlet for the power supply, and we tell you that before the job is priced.

Can the indoor monitor go on a tiled kitchen wall or a backsplash?
+

It can, though we usually prefer a painted wall nearby. Drilling tile is routine with the right bit, but the cable has to reach the spot from behind, and a backsplash often has cabinets, plumbing or outlets in the way. We pick the location with the owner during the walkthrough, at eye height where the family actually stands, and note it on the quote.

We salt the stoop and the walk all winter. Will that damage the door station?
+

Not if it is mounted with that in mind. Outdoor stations are built for rain and snow, but salt spray and slush thrown up from a walk are hard on any metal finish over the years. We mount stations above splash height, under a hood where they face the weather, and seal the back box so meltwater cannot run in behind. Wiping the face down after a heavy salting season helps it last.

Can the indoor monitor also show the driveway and yard cameras we already have?
+

Sometimes. Some video intercom monitors can display network cameras, and some camera recorders can show the door station in their app. Whether your cameras and the new station can share a screen depends on the specific models. Send us the make and model of your recorder before the visit, and we will tell you whether they can be combined or should stay on separate apps.

Can the menus on the indoor station be set to a language other than English for older relatives?
+

On many current monitors, yes, with several menu languages to choose from, though the list varies by model and maker. If a grandparent will be the one answering the door, tell us which language they read, and we will check the model's options before ordering and set it up at handover. For anyone who prefers no menus at all, a simple audio handset with one talk button and one door button needs no reading.

Does the camera on the door station record people walking past on the sidewalk all day?
+

On most intercom-style door stations, no. The camera shows a picture when someone presses the button and during the call, and many stations keep a snapshot of each caller, but they are not set up to record the street continuously. Consumer video doorbells are different and often record on motion. If you want continuous recording of the walk or the gate, that is a separate camera with its own settings.

Can a buzzer release go on the sliding glass door to our back yard?
+

Rarely, and we usually advise against it. Sliding doors lock with a hook bolt rather than a latch, so a standard strike does not fit, and the electric locks made for sliders are specialized and often clash with the door's own lock. For a back yard entrance, a release on the yard gate or on a hinged side door is almost always the simpler and more secure choice.

Can a station be put on a post at the driveway so a driver can ring from the car window?
+

Yes. A station on a short post or gooseneck pedestal beside the driveway, at car-window height, lets a family member, a caregiver or a contractor ring without getting out. It is usually paired with a motorized driveway gate, with the indoor monitor opening the gate through the operator's input. The post needs a footing and a cable run in conduit, both of which are priced on the quote.
