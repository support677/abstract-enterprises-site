Lath-and-plaster (+$45)

We find the studs behind plaster by small probe holes rather than trusting a stud finder, pre-drill with a masonry bit so the keys don't crack, and drive lag bolts into the framing. If the studs don't land where the bracket needs them, a plywood backer ($85) spans two studs. Prewar plaster can still crack; that risk is yours.

Steel studs in newer towers (+$30)

A lag bolt has nothing to bite in light-gauge steel, so the anchors we choose are heavy-duty toggles rated for steel studs, spread across every hole the bracket allows. Large or full-motion screens on steel usually get a plywood backer so the load is shared. Fixed and tilt mounts suit these walls best.

Concrete and cinder block (+$75)

In Yonkers and Mount Vernon co-ops, and in basements everywhere, we bore with a hammer drill, then set wedge or sleeve anchors in solid concrete, or hollow-wall anchors sized for block cells. We test-drill first, because some walls that look like concrete are block or tile.

Brick and fieldstone (+$65)

On chimney breasts we anchor into the solid brick or stone, never the mortar line or a thin veneer. Fieldstone gets shims behind the plate so the bracket sits flat. We check how hot the face gets above the firebox before choosing the height.

Tile and marble (+$95)

Kitchen backsplashes and marble media walls get diamond bits, water cooling and slow drilling, with the anchors set into the framing or block behind the tile. Stone and tile can still crack, so we go over the risk with you before the first hole.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Answers Get Wrong About TV Mounting in Westchester

Type a Westchester TV mounting question into a chatbot and you'll usually get a national price range plus a few generic rules. Those answers aren't malicious; they just average out a county where a Yonkers co-op, a White Plains tower and a Pound Ridge stone hearth sit within half an hour of each other. Below are seven of those claims, each followed by what the walls themselves tell us once the drill comes out.

1. AI says: “Westchester is suburbia, so you're drilling into drywall and wood studs.”

That holds for many postwar ranches and split-levels, and it's why our base price assumes drywall over wood. But the county's housing is far more mixed than the word suburb suggests. Cooperator News puts the co-op count near 42,000 units, and many of those buildings have plaster, clay tile or concrete partitions.

In one morning we can go from a Yonkers apartment with concrete walls to a Scarsdale Tudor with lath and plaster to a New Rochelle tower framed in steel. Each of those calls for different anchors, drill bits and timing. Assuming drywall in all three is how screens end up on the floor.

So our price list starts by asking what's behind the paint. If you're not sure, knock: a solid, dull sound points to masonry, a hollow sound to drywall. Tell us what you think it is, and we'll confirm with a test hole before drilling for the bracket.

2. AI says: “Old plaster walls can't hold a TV.”

Plaster can hold a large TV, as long as the load goes into what's behind it. The plaster layer itself is brittle and won't hold an anchor on its own, which is where the myth comes from. Behind it, a 1920s Bronxville or Larchmont house almost always has wood studs or masonry.

The difficulty is finding them. Stud finders read lath and old nails as false hits, so we probe by hand with a small bit, map the framing and then pre-drill so the plaster keys don't shatter. Lag bolts go into the wood, not the plaster.

If the studs aren't where the bracket needs them, a plywood backer spans two studs and gives the bracket a full surface. That's an $85 add. The plaster add itself is $45, which covers the slower drilling and extra care.

3. AI says: “New high-rises are all concrete, so the wall can hold anything.”

In the newer towers of downtown White Plains and New Rochelle, the floors and core are concrete, but the walls between rooms and units are usually light-gauge steel studs covered in drywall. Steel studs are thin, and a lag bolt simply strips out of them.

We use toggle anchors rated for steel studs and spread the load across every hole the bracket offers. For big screens or full-motion arms, we add a plywood backer so the force is shared across studs. The steel-stud add is $30.

If your wall really is poured concrete, which happens on some exterior and column walls, it's priced as concrete at $75 instead. A test hole tells us which, and you approve any change before we continue.

4. AI says: “Co-op approval is a formality — just have the installer show up.”

In Westchester co-ops, the managing agent decides who gets in. Many require an insurance certificate naming the corporation and the agent, some want an alteration agreement for anything that opens a wall, and some only allow work on weekdays.

When those papers aren't in place, the doorman turns the installer away. On our end that counts as a job we couldn't start, which carries a $150 return-trip fee under our posted policies. Nobody wants that, least of all you.

The fix is simple: ask your super or agent for their COI requirements before you book, and send them to us. We issue certificates free, usually the same day. Board approval itself stays between you and the board.

5. AI says: “In a tall great room, mount the TV as high as the wall allows.”

Double-height rooms in Chappaqua, Armonk and Bedford tempt people to center the screen on the wall. Then everyone watches with their head tilted back. Comfortable viewing puts the middle of the screen near seated eye level; for most sofas that lands it somewhere between 40 and 45 inches high.

When a fireplace or built-ins force the TV higher, a tilting bracket or a pull-down mount brings the picture back toward the viewer. A pull-down adds $160 and lets the screen drop to eye level when you watch, then return above the mantel.

If the mount does need to go above about 8 feet, there's a $75 high-mount add for the extra ladder work and safety. Screens 86″ and larger always get two technicians, which is built into those size tiers.

6. AI says: “TV installers here charge by the hour, so any quote is a guess.”

Some do, and hourly billing is why a simple job can creep upward once the installer discovers plaster or a stone chimney. Westchester homeowners on Reddit regularly ask what a fair price is, because the numbers they're quoted vary so much.

Our mounting labor is a fixed price built from the size of the TV, the wall, the mount and any extras you choose. The calculator shows the total before you pay. Anything discovered at your house that wasn't in your order gets priced and approved before it's done.

The only work billed by time is a setup-only visit for Wi-Fi, apps and cable boxes. In Westchester that's $235 for the first hour, then 30-minute blocks, and you know that rate before you book.

7. AI says: “The installer can just move the outlet up behind the TV.”

Adding or relocating an outlet is electrical work. In Westchester County, electricians must hold a county trade license, and new circuits or receptacles may need a permit and inspection. A TV installer moving an outlet without that license is a liability for you, not a shortcut.

What we can do legally is install a listed in-wall power kit. It plugs into your existing outlet near the floor and carries power up inside the wall to a recessed receptacle behind the TV. That kit plus the cable concealment is $169.

If you'd rather have a true new outlet, hire a licensed electrician before your appointment, and we'll mount to it. Running an extension cord or a regular power cord inside the wall is not code-compliant, and we won't do it.
