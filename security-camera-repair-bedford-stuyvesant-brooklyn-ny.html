The Neighborhood

Why Bedford-Stuyvesant Properties Need Security Camera Repair

Bedford-Stuyvesant runs from Flushing Avenue down to Atlantic Avenue and from Classon Avenue east to Broadway, and Wikipedia counts roughly 8,800 buildings in it that were put up before 1900. Most of the cameras we repair here are bolted to those buildings: to the brick beside a parlor-floor door, to the iron of an areaway gate, under a cornice, over a garden-level entrance tucked beneath a stoop. The houses were designed for gas light and coal furnaces, not for low-voltage cable. So almost every camera system in a Bed-Stuy row house was added later, usually by threading cable up an old chimney chase, along a cellar ceiling, through a window frame or across the back of the house to the yard. Those improvised paths are where most of the faults we find begin. A cable pinched by a window sash for five years, a connector left exposed to rain at the cornice, a power supply plugged into the same cellar outlet as a dehumidifier: each of those works fine for a while and then stops.

The second reason is how people live in these buildings. A great many Bed-Stuy houses are two- and three-family homes with an owner on one floor and tenants on the others, or brownstones divided into a duplex and a garden apartment. One recorder in the cellar often serves everyone, and the phone app is logged into by whoever happened to be there when it was set up, sometimes a former tenant, sometimes a contractor, sometimes the previous owner. When a camera goes dark in that kind of house, the question is never only technical. Someone needs to know who owns the system, who should be able to see which camera, and who still has access that they should not. A repair visit is the natural time to sort that out, and we treat it as part of the job.

The third reason is the street. Fulton Street is the neighborhood's main shopping street, crossed by Bedford, Nostrand and Stuyvesant Avenues, and the commercial stretches along Nostrand, Tompkins, Lewis, Marcus Garvey Boulevard, Malcolm X Boulevard and Broadway are lined with delis, pharmacies, restaurants, salons and cash businesses that depend on their cameras. In the past year, News 12 published surveillance video of a man scaling a wall and breaking through the ceiling of Fulton Pawnbroker on Fulton Street, where police say about $20,000 in merchandise was taken, and of four men dressed as police officers robbing a deli on Marcus Garvey Boulevard at gunpoint. Both stories were told through store camera footage. A camera that has been pointing at the wrong spot for months, or a recorder that stopped saving days ago, is only discovered on the day someone needs the video, and that is the worst day to find out.

The fourth reason is power. Bed-Stuy's underground electrical network has had a hard 2026. After Winter Storm Fern, the Brooklyn Eagle reported 786 customers in the neighborhood without power on the Monday, more than 990 by that night and about 300 still waiting on Wednesday morning, with Con Edison blaming snow and road salt for damaged manholes. In June, News 12 covered manhole fires at 1242 Fulton Street that left about 50 customers dark that evening and kept some Fulton Street stores without power into the next morning. Recorders, PoE switches and cheap plug-in power supplies do not like losing power suddenly and getting it back in surges. A lot of our Bed-Stuy calls start with the words "ever since the outage."

Urgent Repair in Bedford-Stuyvesant

Need Repair in Bed-Stuy? A Recorder That Won't Boot, a Camera Pulled Off the Wall, or Footage You Need Tonight

Urgent camera calls from Bedford-Stuyvesant come in three kinds, and each one has a right first move. The first is the whole system going dark at once: every tile on the monitor black, the app showing everything offline, the recorder silent or clicking. When everything fails together, the cause is almost never the cameras. It is power, the recorder, the switch that feeds the cameras, or the internet connection the app depends on. After an outage, it is very often a power supply or a PoE switch that did not survive the surge when power came back. The second kind is a single camera that is suddenly gone or pointing at the sky, usually the one nearest the street, the stoop or the store door. That points to physical damage, water in a connector, or someone who reached up and moved it. The third kind is the most stressful: something has happened, a package is gone, a car was broken into, a store was hit, and you need the video now, but you are not sure the recorder is even working.

For all three, the most important rule is the same: do not start pulling plugs and pressing reset buttons before someone has checked what footage is on the recorder. Most recorders keep recording in a loop and overwrite the oldest video when the drive fills. A factory reset can wipe settings and, on some models, the recording schedule, so the system comes back showing live pictures but saving nothing. A reboot of a recorder with a failing drive can be the reboot that it does not come back from. If you need footage from a specific night, tell us that first. Our technician's first job on arrival is to find and export that clip, and only then does the diagnosis begin.

There are safe things you can do while you wait. Check whether the recorder has power at all: a light on the front, a fan you can hear. Check whether the outlet it is plugged into works by plugging in a phone charger. If the cameras run from a separate power box, see whether its light is on. Look at the camera that failed from the ground and take a photo of it. Write down roughly when it stopped and what was happening on the block at the time. That is all. Do not climb a ladder to a cornice or reach over an areaway railing to a camera; the height and the iron spikes on many Bed-Stuy fences make that a bad idea, and a technician can tell more from a photo than you might expect. Then call (347) 934-8335 or send the repair request.

We book every request within 2 hours and give you a 2-hour window for the visit. If the property is a store that cannot open without its cameras, or a building where a tenant needs footage for a police report, tell us on the first call so the booking reflects it. We do not promise a fix on the first visit, because some repairs need a part, but the first visit always ends with the footage you need saved, the cause found and a written price for the fix.

Building Specialists

Security Camera Repair for Bedford-Stuyvesant's Architecture

The same fault looks different depending on the building. A camera that drops out on a brownstone cornice, on a three-family's back wall and over a Fulton Street shop door usually has three different causes. These are the building types we meet most in Bed-Stuy and what each one tends to break.

The single-family brownstone

A recorder in the cellar, cameras at the stoop, the garden-level door and the yard, and cable that climbs through four floors of plaster and brick to reach the front.

The two- or three-family house

An owner on one floor and tenants on the others, one shared recorder, and a phone app that too many people have logged into over the years.

The French flat or small walk-up

Four or five stories of 1890s apartments with a shared vestibule and hallway, where hallway and entrance cameras serve a whole building.

The storefront with apartments above

A shop camera system and sometimes a separate building system in the same structure, with cables that pass through the commercial ceiling.

The landmarked facade

Any house inside a historic district, where the camera's position, size and fixing on the front of the building follow Landmarks guidance.

The newer building

Mid-rise rentals and condos with network cameras on a managed switch, often in a locked telecom room run by a management company.

The single-family brownstone: long cable runs and a cellar full of hazards

In a brownstone kept as one home, the recorder almost always ends up in the cellar or a closet under the stairs, because that is where the internet comes in and where nobody minds a box with blinking lights. The cameras are three or four floors away. A run to a camera under the cornice can easily be one of the longest cables in the house, and it usually got there by the path of least resistance: up a chimney chase, along a dumbwaiter shaft, or out a back window and up the rear wall. Long analog runs lose signal, and long PoE runs that push past the limit for network cable give cameras that boot and then drop. When the front camera fails and the yard camera does not, the length and route of that one cable are where we look first.

The cellar itself causes trouble. Bed-Stuy cellars are often damp, and some take water in a heavy storm. A recorder sitting on the floor, or a power strip on the slab, is one leak away from a dead system. Dehumidifiers, boilers and sump pumps share outlets with camera power supplies, and a motor starting up on the same circuit can make a cheap supply flicker every time. One of the most common fixes we make in a brownstone has nothing to do with the cameras: we move the recorder and its power supply off the floor onto a shelf, give them their own protected outlet, and put a small battery backup between them and the wall.

The front of the house brings its own failures. Cameras mounted under a cornice catch water running off the roof; cameras beside a stoop get knocked by delivery carts and bicycles; cameras over the garden-level door under the stoop sit in a damp, sheltered corner where spiders build webs across the lens and the infrared light bounces back, turning night footage into a fog. That last one is not a fault in the camera at all. A cleaning and a slight change of angle fix it, and the parent Brooklyn list prices a clean and check at $80.

The two- or three-family house: whose system is it, and who can see it?

Two- and three-family houses are where the human side of a repair takes the most time. The owner may live on the parlor floor and rent the garden apartment and the top floor, or may live elsewhere and rent all of it. The cameras watch the shared front door, the yard everyone uses and sometimes the hallway, and the recorder is often in a part of the house only the owner can reach. When something fails, the tenant usually notices first, because the tenant is the one who gets the package that disappears. But the tenant cannot authorize work on equipment they do not own, and we will not open a landlord's recorder on a tenant's say-so alone. If you rent and the cameras in your building are down, the first step is a message to the owner, and we are glad to give you the words to put in it.

The access question goes the other way too. Over years, the phone app on a shared system collects logins: a tenant who moved out, the contractor who installed it, a relative who set it up, the previous owner who sold the house. Every one of those people may still be able to watch the front door. A repair visit is the right time to fix that. We change the administrator password, remove accounts that should not be there, and, where the recorder supports it, set up separate users so each household sees the cameras that are relevant to them and nothing else. That last part depends on the recorder. Many older units only have one or two accounts, and if separate viewing matters to you, we will tell you whether your recorder can do it before you pay for anything.

Footage in a shared house raises one more question. Brick Underground, writing about a tenant who wanted to see building footage to identify a package thief, quoted attorney Jennifer Rozen saying landlords are not obligated to show tenants footage outside a legal process, and that city buildings are not required to have working cameras at all. Attorney Catherine Grad said a tenant could argue the landlord should review footage when theft keeps happening: "I don't think that it's such an arduous duty to impose on the landlord." None of that is legal advice, and owners and tenants should get their own. What we can do is make sure the system actually records, so that when someone asks the question, there is an answer to look at.

The French flat or small walk-up: a building system, not a house system

LPC's designation report for the Expanded Stuyvesant Heights district describes four- and five-story French flats from the 1890s mixed in among the row houses, and there are many more small walk-ups scattered through the rest of Bed-Stuy. In these buildings, cameras cover a shared vestibule, the hallway, the mailboxes, the stairs, the back door and sometimes the roof door. The recorder usually lives in the cellar or a utility closet, and the person responsible for it is a managing agent or a super rather than someone who lives in the building. That changes the repair in practical ways. Access needs to be arranged, the managing agent may need a certificate of insurance, and the cameras on each floor often hang on cable runs that go up through the stair hall, where tenants have painted, hung decorations or knocked into them with furniture on moving day.

Hallway cameras in walk-ups tend to fail at the connector or the power. Many older systems power every camera from one central box with a fuse or a small circuit for each output, and when a single camera shorts, from water or damage, it can take its neighbors down with it. We test each output, find the camera or cable that is pulling the others down, and isolate it so the rest of the building comes back while the bad one is repaired. In a building of this size we also check the lighting. A camera in a dark stairwell records nothing useful even when it works perfectly, and a burnt-out bulb on the landing can look like a camera fault on the monitor.

The storefront with apartments above: two systems sharing one building

Much of Fulton Street, Nostrand Avenue and Broadway is made of buildings with a shop on the ground floor and apartments above. The shop usually has its own system, installed by the business, with cameras on the register, the door, the aisles and the stockroom. The building owner may have a separate system for the residential entrance. The two often share a cellar, and their cables cross through the same ceiling. When a shop's camera fails, the first job is making sure we are looking at the right system. We have traced plenty of "broken shop cameras" to a cable that the building's contractor cut while working on the apartments upstairs.

Storefront systems in Bed-Stuy take more abuse than residential ones. Ceiling cameras get hit by stock being moved, door cameras get knocked by hand trucks, outside cameras get turned, covered or ripped down. The parent Brooklyn list prices vandalized camera repair at $400 to $900 per camera plus cable repair, because a camera that has been pulled down usually takes its connector and some cable with it. After a break-in like the one at Fulton Pawnbroker, where News 12 reported the man came in through the ceiling, a store often wants more than a repair. We look at where the cameras were pointing during the incident and tell you honestly whether moving one or adding one would have shown more, and the repair and the change can happen on the same visit.

The landmarked facade: repairs are easy, moves take care

If your house is inside one of Bed-Stuy's historic districts, the Landmarks Preservation Commission's Permit Guidebook is the document that governs cameras on the outside of the building. It says no permit is needed for "Ordinary maintenance, repair, and retrofitting of health, safety, and utility equipment." Swapping a dead camera for a similar one in the same spot is ordinary repair. For an in-kind replacement the guidebook says the application does not need elevation drawings, though manufacturer cut sheets are still required. What does need care is moving a camera, adding one or changing how it is fixed.

The guidebook's standards are specific. It says equipment "must be attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood, or metalwork," and "must not be attached through cast iron or on any decorative surfaces." It says security cameras "must be well spaced to avoid a cluttered, haphazard appearance," and that small residential or commercial buildings may have no more than two security cameras for every 25 feet of street frontage. It wants equipment as small as possible and painted to blend in, conduit concealed through plain masonry where possible, and no more than about ten inches of visible conduit if it cannot be. On a narrow row-house front, that usually means two front cameras is the practical limit. We plan every facade repair around those rules, so a repair does not turn into a violation.

The newer building: managed networks and locked rooms

Bed-Stuy also has newer rental and condo buildings mixed in among the older stock. Their cameras are usually network cameras on a PoE switch in a telecom room, sometimes on a cloud platform the management company pays for. The faults are different: a switch port that died, a firmware update that broke the connection to the recorder, a cloud subscription that lapsed, an IT contractor who changed the network and did not tell anyone. The access is different too. We will need the management office to open the telecom room and, often, someone who knows the network passwords. If you are a board member or a manager of one of these buildings, the most useful thing to have ready is the name of whoever installed the system and any login information they left.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The First Twenty Minutes in a Bed-Stuy Cellar With a Dead Recorder

These notes describe how our technicians work on systems in buildings like Bed-Stuy's. They are our standard practice, not a story about any one address.

Ask before touching. The technician starts with questions, not tools: when did it stop, did anything happen on the block that day, has anyone reset or unplugged anything, and is there footage from a particular day that must be kept. If there is, finding and exporting it comes before anything else.

Look at the power path. In a cellar, the technician follows the recorder's power cord and the camera power cable back to the wall. Shared outlets, daisy-chained power strips, adapters lying in damp corners and supplies whose light is flickering are all noted. After an outage, a blown supply or a switch that will not power its ports explains more failures than anything else.

Read the recorder. The recorder's screen shows which channels have video, whether the drive is detected, how much is recorded and whether the clock is right. A clock reset to a date years ago is a sign the recorder lost power and its settings with it. A drive that reports errors or is missing tells us the next step on its own.

Test, do not guess. For a single dark camera, the technician tests the cable at the recorder end, then at the camera, with a cable analyzer or a PoE tester, and traces any unlabeled run with a tone generator. The result is written down for each run, and every cable gets a label.

Price, then fix. Only when the failed part is known does the technician write a price. You see it before the repair starts.

Power and Outages

After the Outage: Why Bed-Stuy Camera Systems Come Back Wrong, and How to Stop It Happening Again

Power failures do more damage to camera systems than most owners realize, and Bed-Stuy has had a run of them. The Brooklyn Eagle's account of the outages after Winter Storm Fern put the count at 786 customers out on the Monday, more than 990 that night and about 300 still without power Wednesday morning, with Con Edison saying snow and road salt had damaged several manholes and parked cars had slowed the repairs. In June, News 12 reported manhole fires around 1242 Fulton Street that left about 50 customers dark that evening, and a separate News 12 report said rows of businesses on Fulton Street lost power, with some stores still without it the next morning. Con Ed said heat was "almost certainly the cause." For anyone with cameras in those blocks, each of those events was a test their system may not have passed.

Here is what a sudden loss of power does. A recorder that is writing to its hard drive when the power cuts can damage the file system on the drive, and the next time it boots it may refuse to record, or record and then lose everything. A recorder's internal clock battery, if it has run down, lets the time and date reset, and footage recorded after that is stamped with a date from years ago, which makes it hard to find and harder to use as evidence. When power comes back, it often comes back with a surge, or flickers on and off several times while crews work. Cheap plug-in power adapters and the power supply boxes that feed analog cameras are the first things to fail. PoE switches can lose individual ports, so some cameras come back and others never do. Cameras themselves sometimes come back on default settings and lose their connection to the recorder.

The pattern we see afterward is easy to misread. An owner sees most cameras working on the monitor and assumes the system came through. Weeks later, after a package disappears from the stoop, they discover the recorder has not saved anything since the night of the outage, or that the drive is full of footage stamped with the wrong year. That is why we tell every Bed-Stuy customer: after any outage, check two things, not one. Is every camera showing live video, and does playback show recordings from today with today's date? If the second answer is no, the system is not working, whatever the live view looks like.

What actually protects a system from the next outage

The single most useful upgrade for a Bed-Stuy system is a battery backup, an uninterruptible power supply, between the wall outlet and the recorder. It does two jobs. It keeps the recorder running through short flickers so the drive is never cut off in the middle of a write. And for longer outages, a good unit gives the recorder time to keep recording on battery, and the power coming out of it is cleaner than what comes from the wall when service is restored. If the cameras are PoE, the switch that powers them should be on the same backup. If the cameras run from a separate power box, that box should be too, or the recorder will stay up while every camera goes dark, which is not much help.

The second protection is boring but matters: a dedicated outlet. Recorders in Bed-Stuy cellars are regularly plugged into the same strip as a boiler control, a sump pump or a dehumidifier. Motors starting and stopping on that circuit make power dip, and over months that wears out cheap supplies. Moving the recorder to its own outlet, on a shelf off the floor and away from water, is often part of a repair. The third is a quick check of the recorder's clock settings. Where the recorder supports it, we set it to update its time automatically from the internet, so a reset clock corrects itself instead of stamping weeks of footage with the wrong date.

There are limits. A battery backup is not a generator, and a long blackout across several blocks will eventually take down any small system. We do not do electrical work, so if your cellar needs a new circuit or the outlet itself is faulty, that is a job for a licensed electrician, and we will say so. What we can promise is that the parts of the camera system we touch will be set up to survive the next flicker on your block better than they survived the last one.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Repair in Bedford-Stuyvesant: Questions Answered

These are the questions Bed-Stuy owners, tenants and shopkeepers bring to us most, grouped by the situation they come from. Each answer is short; the sections above have more detail.

Landmarked houses and facades

Our house is in the Stuyvesant Heights historic district. Do we need Landmarks approval just to replace a dead camera with a new one?

Usually not. The LPC Permit Guidebook says ordinary maintenance, repair and retrofitting of health, safety and utility equipment needs no permit, and a similar camera in the same spot falls under that. If you want to move the camera, add one or change the conduit on the front, the guidebook's standards apply, and staff can issue permits for cameras that meet them. We tell you which case you are in before the visit.

The old camera on our brownstone was screwed straight into the carved stone beside the door. Can the new one go in the same holes?

If the house is landmarked, we would rather not. The guidebook says equipment must be attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, and not on decorative surfaces. A replacement is a chance to move the fixing into a mortar joint nearby and leave the old holes to be patched by a mason. On a house that is not landmarked, it is your choice, but the same practice protects the stone.

We want a third camera on the front of our row house. Is that allowed?

On a landmarked house, the guidebook limits small residential and commercial buildings to two security cameras for every 25 feet of street frontage, so on a typical narrow front a third camera is unlikely to be approved. Often the better answer is a wider-angle replacement for one of the two you have, or a camera inside the vestibule looking out.

Outages and power

Since the winter outages, the recorder in our cellar restarts itself every few days. Is it the drive or the power?

It can be either, and sometimes both. A recorder that restarts on its own is often getting unstable power from a tired adapter, or is struggling with a drive damaged when the power cut out mid-recording. We test the adapter and the outlet, read the drive's health and check the recorder's logs, and the answer decides whether the fix is a power supply, a drive or both.

Should a battery backup go on the recorder, the switch or both?

Both, plus the camera power box if there is one. A backup on the recorder alone keeps it running while every camera goes dark. For PoE cameras, the switch is what powers them, so it needs to stay up too. A modest unit on a small home system is enough to ride through the flickers that do the most damage.

Our store on Fulton Street got its power back after the June manhole fires, but four cameras never came back. Where do you start?

With whatever powers those four. If they share one PoE switch or one power box, that part is the prime suspect, since one failed component can take several cameras with it. If they run from different sources, we test each run and camera separately. Either way, we export any footage you need from before the outage first.

Footage after something happens

A package was taken from our stoop last night. How do we keep the clip from being recorded over?

Export it now if you can: find it in playback on the recorder or in the app and save it to a USB drive or your phone. If the system is acting up or you cannot find it, call us with the date and rough time and do not reset anything. Our technician exports that clip before touching anything else on the visit.

Our tenant asked to see the footage from the hallway after a theft. Do we have to show it?

That is a legal question for your own lawyer. Brick Underground quoted an attorney saying landlords are not obligated to show tenants footage outside a legal process, but must provide it if police request it or a court orders it. Practically, saving the clip right away keeps every option open, and we can export it for you.

A detective from the precinct wants a copy of our video. Do we have to hand over the recorder?

Usually a copy is what is needed, not the box. We export the clip to a USB drive in a format that plays on an ordinary computer, and we check that the recorder's clock matches real time first, because a clock reset after an outage can stamp footage with the wrong date.

Shared houses and stores

We bought a two-family and the previous owner's phone still gets alerts from our cameras. How do we cut them off?

The fix is on the recorder and the account behind the app, not the phone. We set a new administrator password, remove accounts and linked devices that should not be there, and, where the system uses a cloud account, move it to an account you own. The garden-floor tenant and the family upstairs share one recorder. Can each household see only its own cameras?

On many recorders, yes: separate user accounts can be limited to chosen cameras. Some older models only allow one or two accounts with no camera limits. We check your model before you pay anything and tell you whether it can be done on the equipment you have.

Someone climbed in through the ceiling at a shop near us. Can our cameras be changed to cover the roof hatch and ceiling?

Yes, and a repair visit is a good time to do it. News 12 reported the Fulton Pawnbroker break-in came through the ceiling. Usually move it, and sometimes swap the type. A dome or turret mounted to the ceiling away from the shelf edge is harder to knock than a bullet camera on an arm. If the camera itself is damaged, replacing it on the same cable is a single-camera repair on our list.
