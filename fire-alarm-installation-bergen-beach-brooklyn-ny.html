  • Houses, not towers. Community Board 18 describes the area as predominantly one- and two-family homes, and none of our sources found walk-ups, co-ops or high-rises. Most jobs are interconnected smoke, heat and CO alarms in a house, not a building fire alarm system filed with the FDNY.

Same-Day Service in Bergen Beach

Need Repair in Bergen Beach? Chirping at Night, a Kitchen Alarm That Wakes the Tenant, or Detectors That Quit After the Basement Got Wet

Not every call is a new installation. Many Bergen Beach owners call because something they already have has started misbehaving. The most common is the chirp: one alarm, often in a hallway or a basement, giving a short beep every minute or so, usually at night when the house is coolest and a tired battery's voltage drops furthest. The second is the nuisance alarm, where cooking or a steamy bathroom sets off an alarm that is too close to the stove or the shower, and in an interconnected two-family that wakes both households. The third is particular to a neighborhood that floods: alarms that began chirping, sounding at random or going silent after water or damp reached the basement. The fourth is the quiet one, which nobody notices: alarms more than ten years old that still have a green light but no longer sense smoke as well as they should.

Call (347) 934-8335 and tell us which of those you have. If an alarm is sounding and you are not certain there is no fire, leave the house and call 911 first. Everything else can wait for us. Many repairs are finished on the first visit, often within one to two hours of the technician arriving, because replacement smoke alarms, CO alarms and common interconnect parts travel on the van. What We Install

Fire Alarm Installation Services in Bergen Beach

The list below covers what Bergen Beach owners actually ask us for. Each starts with the same question: who sleeps where, and how would they hear an alarm that starts two floors away?

Interconnected smoke alarms on every level

Alarms in each sleeping area, outside bedrooms and on every floor from the basement up, linked so that when one senses smoke, all of them sound. In a three-story two-family, that link matters more than any single device.

Carbon monoxide alarms near boilers and bedrooms

CO alarms on each level with fuel-burning equipment and near sleeping areas, including the basement where most Bergen Beach boilers and water heaters sit. Combination smoke and CO units are used where they fit the layout.

Basement apartment detection

Alarms in a basement studio or one-bedroom unit, with its own separate entrance, tied into the alarms upstairs so a fire on either level reaches both households.

Heat detection for boiler rooms and garages

Heat alarms where exhaust, dust or cold make a smoke alarm a poor choice, such as a boiler closet or an attached garage, interconnected with the rest of the house.

Panel systems for larger properties

Conventional or addressable control panels for shops, offices and condo common areas, designed and filed with the FDNY before installation where the rules call for it.

Replacing aging and mismatched alarms

Houses where alarms of four different brands and ages have been added over the years. We replace them with a matched set that interconnects, and remove dead hardwired bases that were simply capped off.

Shop and tenant-space detection

Detection and notification for stores on Avenue N and Avenue T, and coordination with a landlord's building system for tenant spaces in larger centers on Ralph Avenue.

Monitoring through partners

Where an owner wants a central station watching the system, especially for a house that sits empty for stretches, we connect it to monitoring through our partners.

Device maps and handover

A written list of every device, its location, type and install date, with a walk-through for each household on testing, silencing and replacement dates.

Building Specialists

Fire Alarm Installation for Bergen Beach's Architecture

Six kinds of property make up nearly every Bergen Beach fire alarm request. They differ in how many households share a roof, what is underneath them and how the wiring got there, so the first questions on the phone are always about the house rather than about alarm brands.

The detached stucco two-family

An owner's duplex above a rental studio or a one-bedroom unit, over a finished basement, with stucco outside and a driveway to the side. Two households, two schedules and often a separate basement entrance.

The semi-detached brick two-family

Brick houses sharing one wall with a neighbor, on lots such as the 26-by-100 example in the listings. Narrower floor plans, a shared party wall and stairs at one side.

The three-family house

Larger houses like the three-family on Bergen Avenue, with three households stacked over a basement. The most people under one roof of any common Bergen Beach house type.

The post-war one-family and cape

Detached houses such as the 1955 cape in the sold listings, with bedrooms under a sloped roof, a basement and often an attached garage.

The older house

The handful of houses that predate the 1960s building wave, from the 1899 house hlres lists on Avenue T to the 1930 detached house on a 40-by-100 lot. Older framing, older wiring and the most surprises in the walls.

The gated condo and townhouse

Units in the Georgetown section's gated communities, including Mill Harbor, and condo addresses on East 72nd and East 73rd Streets. Association rules, shared walls and controlled access to the grounds.

The detached stucco two-family: an owner upstairs, a tenant below, and a basement in between

The layout Homes.com shows again and again in Bergen Beach is an owner's large duplex on the upper floors, a smaller rental unit or studio on the ground or basement level, and a finished basement with its own door to the yard or the driveway. Fire alarm planning in these houses begins with a simple fact: the two households do not live on the same clock. The owner's family may be asleep upstairs at 11 p.m. while the tenant cooks a late dinner, and the tenant may be asleep at 2 p.m. after a night shift while the owner's children are home from school. An alarm that sounds only on the floor where the fire starts protects whoever happens to be on that floor. An interconnected set protects everyone, which is why we treat the link between the units as the core of the job rather than an extra.

The link can be made with wire or by radio, and in a detached two-family the choice usually comes down to how the house was finished. Where a basement ceiling is open or a dropped ceiling can be lifted, we can run interconnect wire along the joists and up through a closet or a chase beside the stair. Where every ceiling is finished drywall and the owner does not want it opened, interconnected alarms that link wirelessly are often the cleaner choice, provided we test the signal from the basement unit to the top floor through the floors and walls of that particular house before we commit to them. Stucco over block or frame can make a difference to range, and a basement surrounded by earth on three sides is the hardest place in the house for a radio signal to leave. We test first and decide second. We also mark which alarms belong to which household, so a tenant who moves out does not take the link with them by removing a unit they thought was theirs.

The semi-detached brick two-family: one shared wall and a narrow plan

A semi-detached house has one wall in common with the house next door, and that shapes both the risk and the work. The party wall is usually solid masonry, which is good for slowing a fire between houses but means no wiring can run inside it. In a narrow plan, the stair is typically against one side, bedrooms are front and back, and the hallway between them is short. That makes alarm placement fairly simple: one outside each sleeping area, one inside each bedroom, one at the head of the basement stair and one in the basement itself. The challenge is getting interconnect wire from floor to floor in a house with little spare space. We look for closets stacked above each other, the space beside a chimney or the back of a linen cupboard before we consider anything visible.

The three-family house: three households and one escape stair

The three-family on Bergen Avenue that Redfin lists is a reminder that some Bergen Beach houses hold three separate households over a basement. More people means more bedrooms, more cooking and more chances that a fire starts while someone on another floor is asleep. In these houses we pay particular attention to the common stair, because in most of them it is the only way out from the upper floors, and smoke climbs it faster than people can descend. We put alarms at the head and foot of the stair on each level as well as inside and outside the bedrooms of every unit, and interconnect the whole house so the top floor hears the basement.

A house of three units also raises a question that the owner, not the installer, has to answer: whether the property is treated as a one- or two-family house or as a multiple dwelling under the city's housing rules, which affects what the owner is required to provide and maintain. That depends on the building's legal use and its certificate of occupancy, not on how it is advertised. We do not decide it, but we will design to the stricter reading if the owner is unsure, and we will tell you which devices are there because a code requires them and which because we recommend them. Where a property has a fire alarm control panel or the owner wants one, the FDNY's rules for fire alarm systems come into play, and we explain those under the commercial section below.

The post-war one-family and cape: bedrooms under the roof and a garage at the side

The 1955 cape in Homes.com's sold listings is typical of a type found across southeast Brooklyn: a story and a half, with one or two bedrooms tucked under a sloped roof, a living floor below, a full basement and often a garage. The sloped ceilings change where an alarm can go. Smoke rises to the highest point of a sloped ceiling but can collect in a dead-air pocket right at the peak, so manufacturers give mounting guidance that keeps the alarm a short distance down from the apex. We follow the maker's instructions for each model on each ceiling. The garage is the other issue. Car exhaust, cold, dust and humidity make a smoke alarm in a garage prone to false alarms, so we usually fit a heat alarm there, interconnected with the house, and put a smoke alarm on the house side of the door between the two.

The older house: from the 1899 building on Avenue T to the 1930s detached house

Bergen Beach has very few houses from before its 1960s building wave, but those few deserve care. Hlres lists 6710 Avenue T, built in 1899, as the oldest building in the neighborhood, and the sold listings include a detached house from 1930 on a 40-by-100 lot. Forgotten New York's history explains where they came from: before full landfilling, there was a small cluster of houses along Avenue U. Houses this old have usually been rewired at least once, often in pieces, and an owner may not know what is behind the plaster. We do not assume. Before adding hardwired alarms we look at what feeds the existing ones, check whether old alarm bases are live or dead, and find out whether there is a circuit we can use. Any new line-voltage circuit or change to the electrical service is work for a licensed electrician under the Department of Buildings' rules, and we coordinate with one when a house needs it.

In many older houses, the most practical design is a mix: sealed long-life battery alarms that interconnect wirelessly in rooms where opening plaster would cause real damage, and hardwired alarms where a ceiling is already open or an accessible attic or basement gives a clean route. A house of this age often has more nooks than a post-war plan: a back stair, a sleeping porch closed in decades ago, an attic finished as a bedroom. We walk every one of them. A bedroom that was not a bedroom when the house was built is the room most likely to have no alarm at all.

The gated condo and townhouse: association rules and controlled access

The Brooklyn Eagle describes the Georgetown section as having numerous gated communities, including Mill Harbor, which Homes.com calls one of the newest projects in Bergen Beach. Redfin's condo listings sit at addresses such as 1070 and 1072 East 73rd Street and 1091 and 1093 East 72nd Street. In a condo or townhouse community, the first question is who owns what. The alarms inside a unit are usually the unit owner's responsibility, while anything in a shared space, such as a common hallway, a garage under the building or a clubhouse, belongs to the association. If the association has a fire alarm system for common areas, the board and its contractor control it, and we do not tie a unit's alarms into it without their approval. We ask the owner for the association's rules on contractors, insurance certificates and working hours before we book.

Field Notes

Field Notes: How a Technician Decides Where the First Bergen Beach Alarm Goes

This is not a story about one job. It is how our technicians work through the three houses most likely to stand on the same Bergen Beach block: an owner-over-tenant two-family with a basement unit, a semi-detached brick house, and a townhouse inside a gated section of Georgetown. The checks are the same in every house. What changes is where the technician starts, because each type hides its biggest gap in a different place.

The two-family: start at the bottom and ask who sleeps there. In a two-family with a finished basement, the technician goes down first. He looks at the boiler and water heater, notes where the smoke pipe or vent runs, checks whether there is a CO alarm on that level, and looks for signs of past water, such as staining at the foot of the walls or a sump pump. Then he asks the question that decides the design: does anyone sleep down here? A basement used for storage needs an alarm. A basement that holds a rented studio needs alarms inside the sleeping area, outside it and in the boiler space, all linked to upstairs. He then presses the test button on whatever alarm the basement already has and listens for the top floor. If the top floor stays silent, interconnection becomes the first item on the written scope.

The semi-detached house: start at the stair. In a narrow semi-detached house, the stair is the escape route and the chimney for smoke. The technician stands at the top of it and looks at what is there: usually one aging alarm in the hall, sometimes none. He checks each bedroom, then follows the stair down to the basement and back up to see where a wire could travel between floors without touching the party wall. Closets stacked above each other are the prize. If there are none, he plans for wireless interconnect and tests the signal from the basement to the top bedroom before he writes anything down.

The gated townhouse: start with what the builder left. In a newer townhouse, the alarms are often hardwired and interconnected from construction, so the technician begins by finding out what is there and how old it is. He reads the date on each alarm, identifies the brand and model, and checks whether the interconnect wire is present at every base. If the alarms are near the end of their life, he looks for a replacement that is compatible with the existing interconnect, so the new set links without new wiring. If any common-area device sits on a wall shared with the association's space, he leaves it alone and notes it for the board.

Every house: the same last test. However the day starts, it ends the same way. The technician tests each alarm in turn while someone listens on every other floor, including inside the basement unit with its door closed, because a door can make a big difference to how loud an alarm sounds on the other side. He tests the CO alarms with the maker's test function, checks that every device is labeled with its location and install date, and goes through the device map with each household. Then he shows the tenant and the owner separately how to test and silence their own alarms, because the person who does not know how to quiet a nuisance alarm is the person who eventually takes the battery out.

Compliance notes for Bergen Beach homes and businesses

  • FDNY review before installation. FDNY Fire Code Chapter 9 requires design and installation documents for fire alarm systems to be submitted to the FDNY for review and approval before installation. This applies to fire alarm systems, the kind with a control panel found in shops, offices and larger buildings, not to the standalone smoke and CO alarms in most Bergen Beach houses.
  • Repair versus a change. Industry guidance on the NYC code treats replacing detectors, pull stations or control panels with identical parts and no design change as a repair. Moving or replacing a fire alarm control panel or similar control units requires new plans to be filed with the FDNY. In a shop that is renovating, that difference decides the timeline.
  • Two stories and fewer. The 2022 NYC Building Code does not require a manual fire alarm system in buildings of two stories or fewer where each dwelling unit is separated by one-hour fire partitions and has a direct exit to a public way, exit court or yard. That describes many Bergen Beach two-families. Smoke and CO alarms are a separate requirement, and we tell owners which devices are required for their house and which we simply recommend.
  • Line voltage. The Department of Buildings requires a licensed electrician and a permit for electrical work. Where hardwired alarms need a new circuit or a change to the panel, an electrician does that part and we coordinate with them.
  • Visible signals in public spaces. Where a business or assembly space has an alarm system, the 2010 ADA Standards call for visible alarms alongside audible ones in public and common-use areas, which matters in a store, a restaurant dining room or a parish hall.
  • No landmark review. We found no landmark district in Bergen Beach. A shopping center lease, a condo association or an insurer may still set their own rules on contractors, hours and documentation.
Above the Waterline

Three Basins, One Basement: Where Fire Alarm Equipment Belongs in a Bergen Beach House That Can Flood

Every Bergen Beach owner who lived through Hurricane Sandy, or bought a house from someone who did, knows the basement can take water. Homes.com's neighborhood guide puts it plainly: most residences here are at extreme flood risk because the neighborhood is surrounded by three basins, and more than 200 properties were affected during Sandy. Fire alarm design usually ignores water. In Bergen Beach it cannot. The basement is where the boiler, the water heater, the laundry and often a tenant's bedroom are, so it needs detection. It is also where electronics fail first when the water comes. The way through is to separate what has to be in the basement from what only happens to be there.

What has to be in the basement. The sensing devices do. A smoke alarm or heat alarm has to be where a fire would start, and a CO alarm has to be on the level with the fuel-burning equipment. Fortunately, these devices go on the ceiling or high on a wall, which is the right place for them anyway, because smoke and heat rise. A ceiling-mounted alarm in a basement with an eight-foot ceiling sits well above the level of most basement water, though no placement is a guarantee in a severe storm. We never mount a basement alarm low on a wall to make it easier to reach, and we never put a plug-in CO alarm in a low outlet in a basement that has flooded before. If a plug-in model is the right choice, we use an outlet as high as the circuit allows or choose a battery or hardwired model instead.

What does not have to be in the basement. In a house with a simple set of interconnected alarms, there is nothing else to place. In a larger property with a control panel, a power supply, a communicator for monitoring or a battery backup, those pieces can usually go somewhere higher: a ground-floor utility closet, the back of a hall closet, a garage wall above the level where water reached in past storms. Many people assume panels belong in the basement because that is where the electrical service is. For a Bergen Beach property, we start from the opposite assumption and put control equipment on the lowest floor that has never taken water, unless a code or a design requirement makes that impossible. When we ask an owner where water reached in Sandy, it is not small talk. The answer sets the line below which nothing with a circuit board goes.

How wires should travel. In a house that floods, the route a wire takes matters as much as where the device ends up. We run basement wiring along the joists and come down to a device, rather than running along the foot of a wall and climbing up. Where a wire has to pass through a block or concrete wall, we seal the opening afterward so it does not become a path for water or smoke. Junction points stay high. In a finished basement where the ceiling is closed, wireless interconnection often makes more sense than in other neighborhoods, because it means there is no wire in the wall at the level water reaches. We still test the radio signal from the basement upward before relying on it.

After water reaches the basement. Any alarm, base or wire that has been underwater or sat in a damp basement for days should be treated as suspect, even if it seems to work. Water and salt leave residue that can corrode contacts slowly, and an alarm that tests fine the week after a flood can fail months later. After a flood, we replace affected devices rather than drying them out, check every interconnect connection on the lowest level, and test the whole house again. Owners rebuilding a basement after water damage should plan alarms into the rebuild, which we discuss in the FAQ, rather than putting the old ones back on new drywall.

What a storm does to power. Coastal storms bring power cuts as well as water. Hardwired alarms have battery backup for exactly this reason, and sealed long-life battery alarms do not depend on house power at all. During a power cut, households in Bergen Beach have used generators, space heaters and candles, and each adds its own fire or CO risk. A generator run in or near a garage or basement is a serious carbon monoxide danger. This is not a reason to buy any particular product. It is a reason to make sure CO alarms on every level are working and in date before storm season, not after.

What we will not promise. No placement makes equipment flood-proof, and no alarm survives being submerged. What a careful design does is keep the parts that cost the most and take longest to replace above the water in an ordinary flood, and keep the sensing devices where they belong, on the ceiling. If your basement has taken water more than once, tell us when you call, and we will plan the system around it.

Basements and boiler rooms

We rent out the basement studio, which has its own entrance from the driveway. Should the alarms down there be linked to the ones in our part of the house?

Yes, and this is the single most important thing to get right in a Bergen Beach two-family. A fire that starts in the basement studio, at the boiler or in the laundry can fill the stair with smoke before anyone upstairs smells it, and a fire that starts upstairs at night can trap a tenant who never heard it. Linking the alarms means that when any one senses smoke, every alarm in the house sounds, in the studio and in your rooms. The link can be wired, where the basement ceiling or a closet gives a route, or wireless, where the ceilings are finished and you would rather not open them. Either way, we test it from inside the studio with its door shut, because a closed door between the units is exactly the condition under which it has to work. We also suggest a short conversation with your tenant about what the alarms are for, so nobody disconnects a unit thinking it is a nuisance, and we label which alarms are in which household's space.

Our boiler sits in an unfinished corner of the cellar next to the water heater. What kind of detector belongs there?

Usually two devices, doing different jobs. A carbon monoxide alarm belongs on that level, because the boiler and water heater burn fuel and a cracked flue, a blocked vent or a failing burner can push CO into the house without smoke or smell. For fire, a smoke alarm right next to a boiler can be prone to false alarms from dust or combustion particles at start-up, so a heat alarm close to the boiler, with a smoke alarm nearby in the open part of the basement, is often the better arrangement. All of them should be interconnected with the rest of the house so the bedrooms upstairs hear them. The January 30, 2026 all-hands at 2276 East 73rd Street, which the nycfire.net forum recorded as a reported boiler-room fire in the basement of a private dwelling, is a local reminder that this corner of the house matters. We keep a clear space around the equipment, mount devices on the ceiling according to the maker's spacing guidance, and leave a note on the device map saying what each device near the boiler is for.

The cellar took water in Sandy and has flooded in heavy storms since. Is it safe to put any alarm equipment down there?

The sensing devices have to go down there, because that is where a fire or CO leak would start, but they belong on the ceiling, which is also the right place for them technically. Anything else, such as a control panel, a power supply, a monitoring communicator or a backup battery, should go on the lowest level that has never taken water. Tell us where the water reached, and we will draw the line there. We run basement wiring along the joists rather than along the bottom of the walls, keep junctions high, and seal any opening where a cable passes through a foundation wall. Plug-in CO alarms do not go in low basement outlets. If water does reach the alarms in a future storm, treat every device it touched as needing replacement, even if it still tests, and have the whole house tested again before you rely on it.

Two households under one roof

Our tenant's cooking sets off the alarms and now both floors go off every few nights. How do we stop the false alarms without losing protection?

The answer is almost never to remove an alarm or take out its battery, which is what usually happens after the fifth false alarm. Start with where the alarm is. An alarm a few feet from a stove, toaster or oven door, or just outside a bathroom where shower steam escapes, will sound with ordinary cooking and bathing. Moving it further away, within the limits the maker sets, often solves the problem. Second, look at the type. Some sensing technologies are more prone to cooking nuisance than others, and many alarms have a hush button that silences them for a few minutes without disconnecting them. In some kitchens, a heat alarm is a better fit near the cooking area with a smoke alarm further away. Third, make sure the tenant knows how to hush the alarm in their own unit, so the whole house is not woken while someone searches for the right button. We can look at the layout and fix the placement in one visit, and we keep the interconnect intact, because the false alarms are annoying but a disconnected alarm is dangerous.

I live upstairs and rent out the lower apartment. Who should install and look after the alarms in the tenant's unit?

In practical terms, the owner installs and the tenant tests and reports. You own the house and the system, and an interconnected set only works if it is designed as one, so it makes sense for you to choose, buy and place every device, including the ones in the tenant's space. The tenant lives with the alarms in their unit every day, so they are best placed to press the test button, notice a chirp or report a unit that has gone quiet. The exact legal duties depend on how the house is classified and what your lease says, and that is a question for your own attorney or the city, not for us. What we can do is install a set that covers both units, label which devices are in which space, leave the tenant a short written guide to testing and silencing, and give you a device map with install dates so you know when replacements are due.

Garages, batteries and the waterfront

We charge an e-bike and a scooter in the attached garage overnight. What detection should be out there?

A heat alarm interconnected with the house is usually the right device for the garage itself, because car exhaust, cold and dust make smoke alarms in garages prone to false alarms, and an alarm that keeps false-tripping gets disconnected. On the house side of the door between garage and house, a smoke alarm gives early warning if smoke gets in. Because the garage is attached, a CO alarm in the rooms next to it is sensible too, especially if a car is ever started inside. The FDNY has said battery-caused fires were up about 70 percent this year, according to Firehouse.com. That report was citywide and the fatal fire it described was in Crown Heights, not here. Still, the safest habits are the ones the FDNY repeats: charge batteries where you can see them, not overnight unattended, and away from the only exit. If you do charge in the garage, an alarm that reaches the bedrooms is what gives you time to get out.

Our house backs onto East Mill Basin and we have a detached shed and a dock out back. Can the alarm system cover the outbuildings?

It can cover a detached shed or garage, with limits. A dock itself is not a place for indoor fire alarm devices, which are not built for spray, salt and open weather. Usually, by mixing methods. No. We turn off only the circuit that feeds the alarms while we connect them, usually for a short period. If an electrician needs to add a new circuit, that work is scheduled with you and the electrician tells you how long any outage will be. Refrigerators and other circuits normally stay on.

How should we get the house ready before the technician arrives?
+

Clear a path to the boiler, the basement ceiling and the garage, and move furniture away from spots under existing alarms. Let any tenant know which rooms we need to enter and when, and if you live in a gated section, arrange gate access. Keep pets in a closed room and have the brand of any existing builder alarms handy if you know it.

We are rebuilding our basement after water damage. At what point should the alarms go in?
+

Plan them before the new ceiling goes up. With the joists open, interconnect wire can be run cleanly and boxes placed where the alarms will go, then the alarms are mounted after the ceiling and paint are finished. Do not put the old, water-exposed alarms back up on new drywall. Replace them and test the whole house once the basement is done.

Does the laundry area with the dryer in our basement need its own detector?
+

The basement needs coverage, and a laundry area is part of it. Dryer lint is a known fire hazard, but lint and steam can also trip a smoke alarm placed too close. We usually put a smoke alarm in the open basement space at a sensible distance from the dryer and washer, or a heat alarm closer in, both linked to the rest of the house.

Do the alarms need wifi or a phone line to work?
+
