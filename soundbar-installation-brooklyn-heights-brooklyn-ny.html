The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Heights Homes Need Soundbar Installation

Brooklyn Heights is the part of the city where the house itself is usually older than every idea of home entertainment, and that is what a soundbar installer has to plan around here. The neighbourhood sits on the bluff across the East River from Lower Manhattan, bounded by Old Fulton Street near the Brooklyn Bridge to the north, Cadman Plaza West to the east, Atlantic Avenue to the south and the Brooklyn-Queens Expressway and the river to the west. Wikipedia calls it New York's first commuter suburb, and most of the rowhouses that line its streets were built before the Civil War. Wikipedia's neighbourhood article counts more than six hundred pre-Civil War houses, one of the largest groups of that housing anywhere in the United States.

Those houses are why Brooklyn Heights became the first historic district New York City designated. The Landmarks Preservation Commission held its hearing on November 17, 1965 and designated the district six days later, on November 23. The Historic Districts Council describes what it protects: nineteenth-century brownstone and brick homes, churches and carriage houses in the Federal, Greek Revival, Gothic Revival and Anglo-Italianate styles, with the Greek Revival work singled out as particularly notable. The Brooklyn Heights Association, founded in 1910 and described by the New York Preservation Archive Project as the oldest continuing neighbourhood association in the five boroughs, had pushed a "historic zoning" resolution as early as 1959.

For a soundbar, the landmark status matters less than people fear and the age of the walls matters more than they expect. The Commission regulates work that needs a Department of Buildings permit, work on the inside that affects the outside, and designated interior landmarks; mounting a bar on an inside wall and running low-voltage cable within the room falls outside all three, as long as nothing is fixed to the facade, a rear wall that the street can see, or the roof. The walls themselves are another story. Brownstoner's survey of the oldest houses in the Heights lists 84 Willow Street and 43 Willow Street in Flemish-bond brick, and 68 Hicks Street, 30 Middagh Street and 24 Middagh Street as wood-frame houses, all dating to the 1820s. A wall like that has no predictable stud spacing, the plaster may be laid on split lath or straight onto brick, and a stud finder built for 1980s drywall is close to useless.

The rooms are generous and they are full of things you cannot replace. Brownstoner's 2022 write-up of 20 Remsen Street, a Greek Revival house in place by 1857, lists original plasterwork, a curving stair, pierced plasterwork in the entry and dining room and marble mantels throughout. Its 2023 profile of 116 Pierrepont Street, a 25-foot-wide 1840s Greek Revival house, mentions black marble mantels in the parlor and dining room and crown molding in the front parlor. A 2014 6sqft listing of 192 Columbia Heights, built in 1859, describes 14-foot ceilings on the parlor floor and an operable dumbwaiter up to the parlor level. Every one of those details limits where a TV can hang and how its cables can travel.

Then there is the Heights that isn't a single-family house at all. Many rowhouses have been divided into floor-throughs and duplexes. Pre-war apartment buildings and co-ops line Columbia Heights, Montague Street and the blocks near the Promenade. The Hotel St. George complex on Clark Street, eight buildings put up between 1885 and 1930 and once the largest hotel in New York, is now divided into student housing, co-op apartments and commercial space. The 14-storey Hotel Bossert on Montague Street, built in 1909, was bought in May 2025 for conversion to apartments, with work reported to have begun in July 2026 and residential completion planned for 2028. Each of those buildings brings a managing agent, house rules and paperwork that a rowhouse owner never sees.

And on the western edge, the neighbourhood sits on top of a highway. The Promenade that gives the Heights its view of Lower Manhattan is the roof of the triple-cantilever section of the expressway. In 2019 the Brooklyn Eagle reported residents near Middagh and Willow Streets describing shaking, loud bangs and rattling shutters from heavy truck traffic, with vibration running from the early morning until evening. A soundbar doesn't change any of that, but it is one more thing on the wall that can buzz, and in those houses we set the bracket and the bass with that in mind.

Brooklyn Heights in figures, for anyone planning a TV wall

  • 25,092 residents in Brooklyn Heights at the 2020 census, in a neighbourhood whose primary ZIP code is 11201 and which falls within Brooklyn Community District 2 (Wikipedia).
  • More than 600 pre-Civil War houses: one of the largest concentrations of such housing in the country, most of them rowhouses (Wikipedia).
  • November 23, 1965: designation day for the Brooklyn Heights Historic District, the first district the Landmarks Preservation Commission named anywhere in the city (LPC map, Historic Districts Council).
  • 31.2% homeownership in 2024 across Fort Greene/Brooklyn Heights, against 32.7% citywide; the same district's median household income was $143,880 (NYU Furman Center, district BK02, updated May 2026). Most households here rent, which shapes who signs off on a drill hole.
  • 1,826 feet: the length of the Brooklyn Heights Promenade, from the west end of Remsen Street to the west end of Orange Street. Its southern half opened in October 1950 and the northern half in December 1951 (Wikipedia, The Cultural Landscape Foundation).
  • About $4 billion over ten years from 2030: the city's August 2026 plan to repair the city-owned BQE including the triple cantilever, with a temporary two-level bypass on Furman Street below the Promenade (NYC Mayor's Office).

Taken together: old houses, mostly rented, often divided, wrapped in a landmark district and, on one edge, perched over a highway that will be under construction for most of the next two decades. A good soundbar job in the Heights is quiet in every sense. It leaves no mark the Commission would notice, no hole a future owner will curse, no cable along a cornice and no bass that comes through the party wall at midnight.

Six Kinds of Heights Home

What the Wall Is Made Of Depends on the Decade

Before we quote anything in Brooklyn Heights we ask two questions: roughly when was the building put up, and has the room been renovated? The answers narrow the wall down to a handful of possibilities, and that tells us which fixings to bring, how long the job will take and which price lines will appear on the bill.

The 1820s Federal House

The survivors around Willow, Middagh and Hicks Streets. Some are brick in Flemish bond, some are clapboard wood frame two and a half storeys high. Rooms are lower and smaller than later houses, and framing was cut and spaced by hand, so we locate solid timber by pilot-drilling rather than by assuming a sixteen-inch rhythm.

The Greek Revival Rowhouse

The 1830s and 1840s houses that give the district its character: brick or brownstone fronts, wide parlors, marble mantels, crown moldings. Party walls are solid brick. Inside walls are usually plaster, sometimes on lath and sometimes straight on masonry, and the TV wall is very often the party wall.

The Italianate Brownstone

The 1850s houses with taller ceilings, the 14-foot parlor at 192 Columbia Heights among them. Height is the issue: a TV hung over a mantel ends up high, and a bar hung above roughly eight feet carries the high-mount line. We usually suggest the bar sits lower, on the TV mount or a bracket just under the screen.

The Carriage House

Converted stables on Hunts Lane, Grace Court Alley, Love Lane and College Place, two or three storeys of brick with heavy timber floors. The living room is often upstairs over a garage or workshop. Walls may be bare brick, brick with furring and drywall, or new framing put in during conversion.

The Pre-War Apartment House or Co-op

Columbia Heights, Montague Street and the blocks near the Promenade. Plaster on block or clay tile partitions, steam heat, a managing agent who wants a certificate of insurance and set working hours, and neighbours above, below and beside. The board's house rules decide more of the job than the wall does.

The Converted Hotel

The Hotel St. George complex on Clark Street, now co-ops, student housing and shops, and the Bossert on Montague Street, under conversion to apartments. Rooms that began as hotel suites can have unusual partition layouts and limited outlets; we ask for a photo of the wall and the outlet before quoting.

There is a seventh type that cuts across all six: the house somebody has already renovated. A gut renovation may have replaced the plaster on one wall with drywall over new studs, left the party wall as exposed brick and closed up an old opening with plywood and board. In those rooms we read each wall separately, because the TV wall and the wall beside it may need completely different fixings.

Building Specialists

Soundbar Installation for Brooklyn Heights' Architecture

The Heights is unusual even by Brooklyn standards because it mixes the city's oldest construction methods within a few blocks. Here is how each wall is handled, what tends to surprise people, and where the extra lines on the price list come from.

Flemish-bond and common brick

The earliest brick houses in the district were laid in Flemish bond, where headers and stretchers alternate in every course; later rowhouses mostly used plainer bonds. For a soundbar the bond pattern matters less than the condition. Nineteenth-century brick is softer than modern brick and the mortar between it is often a lime mix that crumbles under a drill. We drill a test hole at the bracket line, look at the dust that comes out, and set the anchors in the face of solid brick rather than in the joint. A bar weighs far less than a TV, so even soft brick will hold it if the anchor is right and the hole hasn't been over-drilled. The brick or stone line on the Brooklyn list adds $65.

Lime plaster on lath, and plaster straight on masonry

Inside walls in a pre-Civil War house are usually plaster. On a partition it is laid over wood lath nailed to studs; on the party wall it is often applied directly to the brick. You can't always tell which from the room, so the first small hole tells us: a puff of grey grit and then wood means lath on timber, red dust means brick behind. Plaster is scored and drilled slowly with a sharp masonry bit so it doesn't chip, and fixings pass through it into what's behind, never into the plaster alone. The plaster line adds $45.

Hand-framed walls in the earliest houses

In the 1820s frame houses, studs, posts and braces were cut to the builder's own measure. Spacing varies, some framing members are much heavier than modern lumber, and the wall may lean. We find the real framing with small pilot holes behind where the bracket will cover them, and when no member lands where the bar needs to sit, we fit a painted backer board across two of them. The backer line is $85. On walls that lean or bow, we level the bar to the TV rather than to the wall, so the two read as one piece.

Tall parlors and the marble mantel

Parlor ceilings in the later rowhouses run very high, and the instinct is to hang the TV over the mantel. That puts the screen above eye level and the bar higher still. If you want the TV there, we usually fix the bar to the TV or its mount so it stays just under the picture instead of drilling a second set of holes in the chimney breast. Mounting above about eight feet carries the $75 high-mount line. Marble surrounds are never drilled without your explicit yes; marble and glazed tile can chip even at low speed, and that line is $95.

Carriage-house brick and heavy timber

The carriage houses on the lanes were built to hold horses and carriages, not people, and the conversions vary enormously. Citylore's profile of Grace Court Alley describes two- and three-storey carriage houses from the mid-nineteenth century, nearly all still carrying their hay cranes, converted first to garages and chauffeurs' quarters and later to apartments. Inside, we find bare brick, furring strips with drywall over the brick, or entirely new partitions. Floors are often heavy timber, which carries a subwoofer's thump down into a garage or the room below more than a concrete slab would, so the sub usually goes on an isolation pad and away from the beam lines.

Pre-war co-op partitions

Apartment buildings from the early twentieth century usually have plaster partitions on a masonry backing and solid outer walls. The fixing is a masonry anchor; the paperwork is the bigger task. We send the certificate of insurance free, confirm the building's working hours and elevator booking, and keep the drilling short. Hidden in-wall wiring is rarely allowed in these buildings, so a painted cord cover or a cable run along the baseboard to the cabinet is the usual answer.

Original features are irreplaceable, and we treat them that way. If the only sensible position for the bracket is through a decorative plaster band, an original mantel surround or a carved door casing, we stop, explain the options and wait for your decision. Our pick is nearly always a TV-attached bracket or console placement over a hole in something that can't be remade.

Commercial life in the Heights is concentrated on a few streets, and almost all of it happens in old buildings: ground-floor shops under apartments, offices in former banks and townhouses, and churches whose halls are rented to community and arts groups. A soundbar is usually the right tool for a single screen in a room where people are close to it.

Montague Street

The neighbourhood's main shopping street, with a concentration of bank buildings and a business improvement district that has run a weekend Open Street since 2020. DOT's 2024 design concept for the corridor reports that 71% of more than 2,000 respondents to a 2023 survey supported pedestrian improvements. Shops here often have a TV by the counter or in the window; we keep the sound inside the door.

Clark Street and the St. George

The former Hotel St. George complex now mixes co-op apartments, student housing and commercial space, including a gym in part of the old pool area. Fitness and studio rooms usually want speakers rather than a bar; a single lounge or front-desk TV is a bar job.

Henry and Hicks Street Storefronts

Small shops, cafes and offices on the ground floors of rowhouses, with apartments overhead. The bar goes on the inside wall, the bass is kept modest and the sub, if any, stays off the floor that the upstairs tenant lives on.

Churches and Their Halls

St. Ann & the Holy Trinity on Montague Street has leased school, office, rehearsal and concert space to community and arts groups, and has run The Forum at St. Ann's arts series since 2012. Parish halls and meeting rooms often want a TV with clear speech for talks and screenings, without anything fixed to historic fabric.

Offices Near Borough Hall

The eastern edge of the Heights runs along Cadman Plaza West toward Court Street, where professional offices occupy older buildings. Conference and waiting rooms usually want a bar for presentations and a fixed volume the staff can't push too high.

Atlantic Avenue Edge

Atlantic Avenue marks the southern boundary of the neighbourhood. Shops and restaurants on the Heights side of it occupy the same old row buildings, with the same mix of brick party walls and apartments above.

Rules that apply to business rooms

  • Noise from a business: the city's noise code summary from DEP limits commercial music between 10 PM and 7 AM to no more than 42 dB inside a nearby home and no more than 7 dB over ambient on the street 15 feet or more away, with a separate limit for pervasive bass. In a shop under an apartment, the subwoofer is usually the problem, not the bar.
  • Passageways: in a corridor or waiting room where people walk past the screen, the ADA limits how far anything mounted between 27 and 80 inches off the floor may project. We keep the TV and bar flat, high enough, or behind furniture that holds people back.
  • Insurance certificates: a shop or office landlord will usually ask for one before the first hole. It costs you nothing.
  • Exterior and storefront: anything fixed to the outside of a building in the historic district, including a speaker over the door, is work the Landmarks Commission reviews. Our bars stay inside.
Your Questions Answered

Soundbar Installation in Brooklyn Heights: Questions Answered

Most soundbar questions have the same answer in every borough. These are the ones that come up because of what Heights houses are and where they sit: hidden voids inside very old walls, a landmark review that some owners are already in the middle of, and an expressway that shakes part of the neighbourhood.

Inside the walls of a pre-Civil War house

A disused dumbwaiter shaft runs beside our parlor — can the soundbar's cables drop down it to the equipment in the cellar?

Often, yes, and it can be the neatest cable path in the house. Some Heights townhouses still have their dumbwaiters; the 1859 house at 192 Columbia Heights was listed in 2014 with one still working up to the parlor. Where the car and ropes are long gone, the shaft is a ready-made vertical chase from the kitchen level to the parlor and sometimes above.

What we check first: whether the shaft is truly empty and dry, whether anything else already runs in it (old gas pipe, plumbing, an abandoned electrical feed), and whether the shaft opens anywhere near the TV wall. HDMI and network cable can be lowered down the shaft on a fish line and fixed to its side with clips, then brought out through a small plate low on the parlor wall. That lets the cable box, streamer or receiver live in a cabinet or on the lower floor, with only the soundbar and its power under the screen. HDMI length is the limit: passive cables are reliable for a modest run, and beyond that we use an active or fibre HDMI lead, which we price before starting.

What we won't do is put mains power down the shaft. A new outlet behind the TV, or power carried between floors, is electrician's work, and in a landmarked house it's also the kind of job that may need a Department of Buildings permit and therefore Landmarks sign-off. Low-voltage signal cable is ours; anything carrying 120 volts belongs to the electrician.

There may be old gas-light pipes in the wall where the TV goes — how do you drill safely?

This is a real risk in nineteenth-century Brooklyn houses, and it is one reason we pilot-drill instead of simply driving in a long anchor. Brownstoner's history of row-house lighting explains that when electricity arrived, the gas pipes that fed the fixtures were capped and left in place, and that in many Brooklyn homes they are still active even though they are capped and not used. A typical parlor had a gasolier hanging from the middle of the ceiling medallion, and halls and back rooms had wall sconces, so pipe can run up a wall where a sconce once was or across a ceiling toward the medallion.

On a Heights TV wall we look for the clues first: a capped stub or patched circle where a sconce once hung, a medallion overhead, a run of plaster that sounds different. We use a scanner that detects metal, keep pilot holes short and shallow, and place the bracket so its fixings avoid any line between an old sconce position and the floor or ceiling. If we find or suspect pipe, we move the bracket or switch to a TV-attached bracket rather than drilling near it.

If anyone in the house ever smells gas, leave and call Con Edison or 911 from outside. Whether an old line is live, and capping it properly at the source, is a job for a licensed plumber, not a low-voltage installer. Owners in Brooklyn who found live lines in their walls have described exactly that on Brownstoner's forum, and the advice there was the same: get a plumber to check it.

Already in front of the Landmarks Commission

Our renovation is already filed with the Landmarks Preservation Commission — can the TV-wall wiring be added to that approval, or does it need its own?

The Commission's own guidance is the place to start. LPC says a permit for interior work is needed when the work requires a Department of Buildings permit, when interior work affects the exterior, or when the interior is a designated interior landmark. NYC Business's guide for landmarked properties adds that interior renovations needing a DOB permit generally receive a Certificate of No Effect, because they don't change protected architectural features.

Low-voltage cable for a soundbar, by itself, usually falls outside all three triggers. The question becomes practical: if your architect is already opening walls under a DOB permit and a Certificate of No Effect, the cheapest moment to run HDMI, network cable and a recessed box behind the TV is while those walls are open, as part of the contractor's scope. Ask your architect whether to list the low-voltage work in the renovation documents; we can supply a description of what we would run and where. Any new electrical outlet behind the TV is electrical work and goes through the electrician under that permit.

Where it changes is outside. A speaker, cable or dish fixed to a facade, a visible rear wall or a roof is exterior work and comes under Landmarks review. This is general guidance, not legal advice; the Commission's Preservation Department will tell you whether a particular piece of work needs a permit, and your architect or expediter should confirm what belongs in your application.

Living over the expressway

Trucks on the expressway make our house shake — how do we tell a rattle from the soundbar from one in the building?

Do one simple test with the TV off. If the rattle comes when a heavy truck passes and nothing is playing, it's the house: a loose shutter, a window sash, a picture frame or a glass shelf. The Brooklyn Eagle's 2019 report on the triple cantilever quoted residents near Middagh and Willow Streets describing shaking, bangs, rattling shutters, broken glasses and cracked walls, with vibration from early morning into the evening. That isn't something a bracket can fix.

If the buzz only happens with sound playing, and particularly at certain low notes, it's the system. The usual culprits are a bar touching the wall or the TV, a loose bracket screw, a subwoofer sitting on a hollow floor or against a cabinet door, or something on the console resonating. We play a slow low-frequency sweep through the sub with you in the room, find the note that sets things off, and fix the cause: felt pads between bar and wall, a re-tightened bracket, the sub moved or put on an isolation pad, and the bass trimmed a little in the app.

If both happen, we deal with the system and tell you what we noticed in the house. In the streets nearest the expressway we also check the bracket fixings again before we leave and suggest you look at them after a few months; constant vibration is a good reason to choose a bracket with locking screws and to avoid relying on plaster alone.

What AI Gets Wrong About Soundbar Installation in Brooklyn Heights

Search for soundbar installation and an AI summary will give you a confident national answer. Some of it is fine. Some of it doesn't survive contact with a Greek Revival party wall, a co-op board or a capped gas pipe. Here is where the generic answer and a Heights job part ways.

"Soundbar installation costs about $58 to $231"

That range comes from Homewyse's national estimate, and it is a fair figure for a bar on drywall in a suburban house. It assumes studs every sixteen inches, a hollow wall to hide the cable and nothing fragile nearby.

In the Heights, the base mount is $129 and the wall decides the rest: $65 for brick, $45 for plaster, $85 if the framing needs a backer board, $95 for marble or tile. A bar on a party wall with a Sanus bracket and a painted cord cover is $337.99, which sits above the national range for honest reasons.

The number you should compare is the written total for your wall, not a national average. We send it before anyone drills, and if the wall turns out different from what was booked, you see the new figure first.

"Hide the wires inside the wall for a clean look"

Generic advice treats in-wall wiring as the default. In a rowhouse whose TV wall is the brick party wall, there is no hollow to run cable through, and cutting a chase into a shared masonry wall is a much bigger and dustier job than any soundbar warrants.

Where the wall is hollow, usually a later partition or a renovated wall, the $169 in-wall kit works well. Where it is brick or plaster on brick, a slim cord cover painted to match the wall or the baseboard is the honest answer, and it can be removed later without damage.

The third option, often overlooked, is to use a void the house already has: a closet behind the TV wall, a dumbwaiter shaft, or the space under a built-in. We look for those before suggesting anything cut.

"Find a stud and mount it"

AI summaries describe stud finders as if every wall has studs. In the oldest Heights houses, the framing was cut by hand and the plaster can be thick enough to blind an electronic finder, and on party walls there are no studs at all.

We locate fixing points by pilot-drilling where the bracket will cover the hole, reading the dust and the resistance. Wood, brick and hollow lath each tell you something different, and we only commit the anchors once we know which we have.

The same discipline is what keeps us away from hidden hazards. The Brownstoner history of row-house lighting notes that old gas pipes were capped and left in the walls, and some are still live; a short pilot hole with a metal scan first is the safe way to work.

"Landmark rules mean you need permission for any change"

Some AI answers overstate the Landmarks Commission's reach, telling Heights owners they need approval for anything at all. LPC's own guidance lists three cases for interior work: work that needs a DOB permit, interior work that affects the exterior, and designated interior landmarks.

A soundbar on an inside wall with low-voltage cable inside the room meets none of those. What does need review is anything fixed to the outside: a speaker on a rear terrace wall the public can see, a cable run along the facade, a dish on the roof.

Co-op boards, condo associations and landlords have their own rules, and those apply regardless of landmark status. For the specifics of your building, the Commission's Preservation Department and your managing agent are the people to ask.

"Mount the soundbar directly below the TV, at ear level"

Dolby's placement advice is sound: mount the bar below the TV and keep the two close together, aiming for ear level where you can. The trouble in tall Heights parlors is that the TV often goes over the mantel, well above ear level, and the bar follows it up.

We don't pretend a high bar sounds like a low one. If the TV must stay over the mantel, we fix the bar to the TV mount so it hugs the bottom of the screen, angle it slightly toward the seats where the bracket allows, and set dialogue enhancement so voices hold up.

Often the better answer is moving the TV. A screen on the wall opposite the windows, or beside the chimney breast rather than over it, gives you a lower picture, a better seat and a bar at a sensible height, and leaves the mantel alone.

"Any handyman can mount a soundbar"

Thumbtack's national figure for TV wall mounting runs $121 to $234, and plenty of handymen will hang a bar for something similar. For a bar on drywall, that might be all you need.

In a house in the first historic district in New York, with lime plaster, soft brick, a neighbour on the other side of the party wall and possibly a live gas line in the wall, the job is about knowing when not to drill. That judgement is what a licensed low-voltage contractor with old-house experience is for.

Ask any installer three questions: what anchor they will use in your wall, how they will stop the drill reaching the neighbour's side, and who carries the insurance certificate your building or landlord will want. The answers tell you most of what you need to know.

"Just use Bluetooth if the cables are a problem"

Bluetooth is convenient for phones and terrible for TV sound. It adds delay, which makes lips and voices drift apart, and it compresses audio.

For the TV itself we always use HDMI eARC or ARC, or optical as a fallback, so the picture and sound stay in sync and the TV remote controls the volume. Wireless is fine for the link between the bar and its own subwoofer and rear speakers, which are designed to pair with each other.

In a parlor where the cable box can't sit near the TV, the answer is a longer HDMI run, a dumbwaiter or closet chase, or a cable along the baseboard to a cabinet, not Bluetooth between the TV and the bar.

Above the BQE

Columbia Heights, Willow and Middagh: Setting Up Over a Highway

Few neighbourhoods we serve have a highway built into the bluff beneath them. When Robert Moses planned the Brooklyn-Queens Expressway along the western edge of the Heights in the 1940s, the Brooklyn Heights Association opposed a wider road, and what was built instead was a stack: two decks of traffic cantilevered off the bluff, with the Promenade on top. The Promenade itself was designed by Clarke & Rapuano in 1946 and runs 1,826 feet from Remsen Street to Orange Street.

For the houses closest to it, that structure is a neighbour. The Brooklyn Eagle reported in 2019 that the expressway carries more than 150,000 vehicles a day, and residents near Middagh and Willow Streets described vibration intense enough to rattle shutters and break glasses. A Brooklyn Eagle notice about overnight repairs described weeks of nighttime jackhammering on the triple cantilever, with concrete and rebar work at the Clark Street fan plant area and the deck at Grace Court.

The future is more of the same, then better. In August 2026 the city announced a roughly $4 billion, ten-year rehabilitation of the city-owned BQE, including the triple cantilever, with construction to begin in 2030 and a temporary two-level bypass on Furman Street below the Promenade that the city says will not block it. Environmental review starts this fall, with the impact statement expected in summer 2028.

What that means for a soundbar on the western streets

  • Brackets with locking hardware. In houses that shake, we prefer brackets that lock the bar in place rather than letting it hang on two hooks, and we fix them into brick or timber, never plaster alone.
  • Felt and spacing. A bar that touches the wall or the TV can buzz at the same frequencies as the passing traffic. A few millimetres of felt and a correct gap fix most of that.
  • Dialogue settings for a noisy backdrop. With windows open toward the river side, traffic noise competes with voices. Dialogue enhancement and a modest centre-channel boost help more than turning the whole system up.
  • Night mode for neighbours. The same houses are rowhouses with party walls. Night mode compresses loud passages so a quiet film doesn't need to be turned up until the explosions shake the wall next door.
  • Surge protection on one strip. Every component goes on one surge-protected strip in a reachable spot, which is good practice anywhere and makes life easier during construction-season outages.

None of this is exotic. It is simply the difference between a job done for a quiet suburban living room and one done for a house that has had a highway at the foot of its block since the 1950s.

Party Walls and Neighbours

Good Sound in a Rowhouse Without Losing the Neighbours

Nearly every house in the Heights shares at least one wall with the house next door, and many share floors and ceilings with tenants as well. Bass is the part of a film that travels through all of them. High and middle frequencies stay mostly in the room; the low thump of a subwoofer passes into brick, timber joists and plaster and comes out on the other side. That is why we spend more time on where the subwoofer sits than on where the bar goes.

The city takes neighbour noise seriously. In 2025 the 311 system logged about 636,000 noise complaints, 463,349 of them residential, according to a New York Post analysis, and the 311 portal lists loud television among the neighbour-noise complaints it accepts. The noise code itself, in section 24-218, treats sound measured at 7 dB(A) or more above the ambient level inside a receiving property between 10 PM and 7 AM as unreasonable. You don't need a meter to stay well clear of that; you need a sensible setup.

What we do in a shared house

  • Keep the sub off the party wall. A subwoofer in a corner formed by the party wall excites that wall more than anywhere else in the room. We start it on an inside wall or under a side table and move it only as far as the room needs.
  • Decouple it from the floor. On timber floors over a garden apartment or a neighbour's ceiling, an isolation pad or a pair of dense foam blocks under the sub cuts the thump carried into the joists.
  • Calibrate, then trim. Room calibration from the seat sets a sensible baseline. We then trim the sub a few steps below the app's default, which most people never notice in the room and the neighbours notice a great deal.
  • Night mode on the remote. Most bars compress loud passages in night mode. We show you where it lives so it's one button after 10 PM, not a menu hunt.
  • Bracket off the wall surface. A bar hard against a party wall can pass vibration straight into it. Felt pads and the bracket's own spacing keep a small gap.

If you're the one hearing the neighbour's television, a soundbar won't fix that. What helps is the same advice in reverse: a polite note suggesting the sub moves off the shared wall usually does more than any complaint.

Renovating

When the Walls Are Open: Planning the TV Wall in a Heights Renovation

Heights houses are renovated constantly, and a renovation is the one moment when hiding a soundbar's cables costs almost nothing. Once plaster is back on the wall and the cornice is restored, the same job means surface channels or careful fishing through old cavities. A few decisions made at the drawing stage save a lot later.

  • Decide the TV position before the electrician's rough-in. The screen's centre and height decide where the outlet and the cable box go. Mark them on the drawings, not on the day the TV arrives.
  • A recessed box behind the screen. A recessed power-and-data box lets the TV hang flat and gives the bar a hidden path for HDMI. The power side is the electrician's work under your permit; the low-voltage side can be ours or your contractor's.
  • Empty conduit to the cabinet or cellar. A smooth, empty conduit from behind the TV to the cabinet, or down to the cellar where the network equipment lives, lets cables be changed for decades without opening the wall again.
  • Solid blocking at the bracket line. Where a new partition is framed, a strip of solid timber between the studs at the bracket height means the bar can go anywhere along it.
  • Leave the party wall alone. Chasing cable into a shared masonry wall is rarely worth it. Plan the TV for an inside wall if you can, or accept a neat painted channel on the brick.
  • A wired network point. Thick brick and plaster block Wi-Fi between floors, so a network cable ending behind the TV keeps the bar and the streamer on a steady connection.

If your architect is already working through Landmarks under a Certificate of No Effect, none of this changes the protected exterior. Send us the drawings and we will mark up what we'd run and where, so your contractor can include it in their scope.

FAQ

Brooklyn Heights Soundbar Installation FAQ

Can the soundbar play the narrated audio-description track for a family member who is blind or has low vision?
+

Yes. Audio description is a secondary audio track with extra narration, usually switched on with the SAP button or the TV or streaming app's accessibility menu. Once the TV or app is playing that track, the TV sends it to the bar like any other sound. If a cable box decodes the channel, it is turned on in the box's settings instead. We set it up and show whoever needs it how to switch it on and off.

We're subletting our apartment for the summer. What should be signed out of the soundbar before the subtenant arrives?
+

Sign your streaming services out of the TV and any streamer, and remove the bar from your own app account or invite the subtenant as a guest if the app allows it. Turn off any voice assistant linked to your accounts and note the Wi-Fi network the bar uses. A setup-only visit ($195 first hour) can do this and set it back when you return.

What would bars in both the parlor and a top-floor den of a Heights townhouse add up to?
+

Taking a common pairing: the parlor bar on the brick party wall with its own bracket and a painted cord cover is $129 + $65 + $59 = $253. The den bar on plaster is $129 + $45 = $174, and if the den is on the third floor or higher with no elevator the $40 walk-up line applies, making $214.
