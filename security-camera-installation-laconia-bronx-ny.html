The Neighborhood

Why Laconia Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Laconia is a neighborhood of front steps. Wikipedia treats it as the northern part of Allerton, drawn between Boston Road, East Gun Hill Road, the Dyre Avenue subway line, Allerton Avenue and Bronxwood Avenue, while the CityNeighborhoods guide pushes the western edge out to Williamsbridge Road, and listing sites cannot agree whether its ZIP code is 10467 or 10469. Forgotten New York explains the name as a made-up one from an 1880s development that disappeared long ago. What the sources do agree on is the kind of street you find inside those lines. CityNeighborhoods describes detached brick homes, two- and three-family houses and low-rise apartment buildings, many put up between the 1920s and the 1950s, when Italian-American, Jewish and Irish families made it a model of Bronx middle-class life. Neighborhoods.com describes whole blocks of duplexes beside quadruplexes with a few single-family homes mixed in. City-Data's housing counts make the 1950s the biggest decade, with about 940 units, ahead of roughly 748 from 1939 or earlier and 298 from the 1940s, plus about 445 units added between 2010 and 2019. A local broker quoted by Homes.com put it simply: Laconia is pretty strictly residential. For a camera plan, that means most jobs here are about a house, its doors and the few yards of sidewalk in front of it, not a lobby.

The houses themselves shape where the cameras go. A typical listing, 2450 Laconia Avenue, is a brick two-family built in 1940; another, at 2546 Laconia Avenue, is a fully detached brick legal two-family; a third, on Hone Avenue, stacks a three-bedroom apartment over a two-bedroom with a full basement below. Wikipedia notes that across this part of the Bronx many private homes have basement studios even when the house is classified as one-family. Put those together and a single Laconia house can have a front door shared by two apartments, a second door for the upper floor, a side door down to a basement unit, a back gate, two side yards and a car at the curb. Each of those is a place where something can happen with nobody watching, and each belongs to someone different in the house. The porch thefts News 12 covered in neighboring Pelham Gardens show the pattern: the homeowner's own video caught the person taking his packages, and even after he asked drivers to use the back gate, they still left boxes at the front door. On these blocks the front step is where a camera does its first and most important work.

The bigger systems nearby do not change that. New York Focus reported that NYCHA runs more than 20,000 cameras in indoor and outdoor common spaces and that the NYPD is linking those feeds to its own systems over the Big Apple Connect network. Those cameras watch public housing grounds, such as Eastchester Gardens at 1130 Burke Avenue, where residents once lobbied for city money for cameras and a new door system and where the current renovation lists security improvements in its scope. They do not watch the porch of a private two-family on Paulding or Tenbroeck Avenue. Cars are another concern on these streets. Norwood News reported that 19 of the 252 thefts of cars, tires, rims and catalytic converters charged in a 2026 case were in the 49th Precinct, which serves Allerton, and ABC7 reported a thief who lifted a garage door on Thwaites Place in January 2025 and went through 23 vehicles. We are not ranking any block, and these cases do not describe every house. What they show is that the front step, the side and back of the house, and the car at the curb are where a Laconia camera plan earns its money.

The rules matter as much as the hardware. Outdoor positions on a house are the owner's decision, and a renter needs the landlord's permission for anything in a shared hall or on the outside walls, as Brick Underground has pointed out to readers more than once. Public housing tenants answer to the housing authority's lease, and the Bronx tenant at Parkside Houses who was served an eviction notice for breaching her lease by installing cameras is a reminder to ask first. Privacy goes both ways. New York's Civil Rights Law section 52-a lets owners and tenants sue over a neighbor's camera aimed into their yard, and a camera inside a tenant's living space is out of the question. Licensing matters too: course providers for the state's installer exam stress that security systems, including cameras, fall under the state license, and Seneca Security notes that if Buildings Department permits are pulled, the work must be done or supervised by a licensed master electrician or licensed low-voltage contractor. We frame every outdoor view to stop at the property line, keep indoor cameras out of rented apartments, and do the work under NYS license #12000287431.

Laconia facts that shape a camera plan

  • Mostly houses, mostly older: City-Data counts more housing units from the 1950s than any other decade, and more than a thousand built in 1949 or earlier, so most cameras here mount on brick and run cable through basements, not drop ceilings.
  • More doors than a house seems to have: two-families with a shared front door plus an upper-floor entrance, four-families on duplex blocks, and basement studios with their own side door all need views of their own.
  • The porch is the target: News 12's Pelham Gardens report shows packages taken off a front porch four times in one season, with drivers ignoring a request to use the back gate.
  • Public cameras watch public housing: NYCHA's more than 20,000 cameras and the NYPD link cover developments like Eastchester Gardens, not private houses across the street.
  • A neighborhood still being built: New York YIMBY reports permit filings in 2026 for a 13-story building with 97 homes at 3374 Boston Road, a six-story building with 60 homes at 3365 Wilson Avenue and a five-story building with 12 homes at 3038 Wilson Avenue.
  • Cameras that get knocked down first: Bronx Times reported that burglars at the Eastchester Gardens Community Center broke a window, knocked down the security cameras and ripped out a phone line, which is why we mount and wire cameras so a single blow does not end the recording.

What Laconia owners often add next. A house that starts with a porch camera often comes back asking for a video doorbell on the upstairs entrance, or for an alarm sensor on the basement door that ties into the same phone alerts. Store owners on Boston Road usually ask next about an alarm for the back door or a camera view showing on the intercom. We leave a spare port at the recorder and a labeled cable end in the basement for that next step, so nobody has to drill the brick twice.

A Laconia house is small enough that a few cameras cover it, and busy enough that each one has to be in exactly the right place. The front step takes deliveries and visitors for two or more households. The side yards on a detached house are narrow strips nobody looks down. A basement studio may have a door that only its tenant uses. The back gate opens onto a yard the street cannot see. And the car sits at the curb all night. Packages that land at the front door anyway

The Pelham Gardens homeowner in the News 12 report did what most advice tells people to do. He asked delivery drivers to use his back gate, and they kept leaving boxes at the front door. On Laconia's blocks, where houses sit close to the sidewalk and the porch is a few steps up, a box at the door is visible to everyone walking by. A camera cannot make drivers follow instructions, but it can do three things that help. A face view at the door, set at head height and recording continuously, shows who took a box clearly enough to describe. A second view looking down at the step shows the box arriving and leaving, with the time on the clip. And because the recorder keeps every clip with an accurate clock, you can show the delivery company exactly when and where its driver left the package, which matters when you ask for a refund. We also set the alert to fire only when someone stops at the step, not every time a neighbor walks past.

Two or three front doors on one brick house

Many Laconia two-families have a shared vestibule for both units, while others give the upper apartment its own door at the side or the top of an outside stair. Four-family houses on the duplex blocks can have two front doors, each serving a pair of apartments. However the doors are arranged, each entrance needs its own camera, aimed so that whoever stands at that door faces the lens, and named on the recorder by the floor it serves. That way, when the tenant on the second floor asks whether a visitor came for them, the owner can find the clip by door and time in a minute, without scrolling through the other apartment's comings and goings. Where tenants want to see their own door from their phone, the owner can give each one a limited account that shows that door alone. The yard, the curb and the other apartment's entrance stay on the owner's account.

The basement studio's own door

A studio below a Laconia house usually has its door down a short flight of steps at the side or the back, out of sight of the street and of the upstairs apartments. That makes it the door most worth watching and the hardest to watch well, because the steps are narrow, the light is poor and the camera has to sit high enough to be out of reach without staring at the top of everyone's head. We mount the camera on the wall above and slightly to one side of the steps, so a person coming down faces it, use a camera that keeps a color picture in low light, and add a small motion light if the steps are dark. The tenant is told the camera is there and what it covers. It shows the steps and the outside of the door, never the inside of the studio, and the tenant can be given a view of their own entrance if the owner agrees.

The car at the curb overnight

Car break-ins and thefts of tires, rims and catalytic converters tend to happen in a few minutes in the middle of the night, at the curb in front of a house. A camera on the front corner of the house, looking along the curb rather than out across the street, keeps the car close enough to the lens to show a person crouched beside it. It records continuously, because motion triggers can miss someone working low and slowly, and it sits high enough that it cannot be reached from the sidewalk. Streetlights help, but they also throw glare, so we check the view after dark during the walk-through and choose a camera that copes with bright points of light in a dark frame. What a single house camera will not do is read the plate of a car passing at speed in the street; we say so before anyone buys a camera hoping it will.

Free-standing brick houses: two narrow yards and a long way to the back

The detached brick two-families on streets such as Laconia Avenue have something attached houses do not: a narrow yard on each side, usually just wide enough for a path, bins or a driveway. Those strips lead straight to the back of the house, and from the street nobody can see down them. One camera mounted high on each front corner, looking down the yard toward the back, records anyone walking in. A camera on the rear wall faces the back gate and the yard, and covers the rear windows from outside. Because the house is detached, the recorder in the basement can be reached through short, straight cable runs, one small hole per camera through the brick, sealed afterward. The one thing we check carefully is the neighbor's side wall, often only a few feet away: the camera is aimed down the yard, not at their windows, and the infrared light is set so it does not wash the frame white.

Three-over-two on a full basement: the basement does most of the work

A house laid out like the Hone Avenue listing, a larger apartment above a smaller one on a full basement, is one of the easiest camera jobs in the neighborhood, because the basement ceiling runs from the front wall to the back. Cable for the porch, the side yard and the back gate drops straight into the basement close to each camera, runs along the joists and reaches the recorder without anyone opening a finished wall upstairs. The recorder goes into a locked cabinet in a dry corner of the basement, away from the boiler's heat, the laundry and any basement door that opens to the outside. Where the basement holds an apartment, the recorder goes into a utility area the owner controls, never inside the tenant's space, and the cable stays above the utility ceiling. Both apartments' doors get their own views, and each tenant can be given a limited account for their own door.

Duplex and four-family blocks: shared fronts and property lines

On the blocks Neighborhoods.com describes, where duplexes stand beside quadruplexes, two owners may share a stoop wall, a walkway or a driveway. A camera on your half of the front is your decision; a camera on a shared wall or aimed down a shared driveway is a conversation with the neighbor first. We mount on your side of the line, frame the view so it covers your door, your stoop and your stretch of curb, and use privacy masking on the recorder to black out any part of the picture that falls on the neighbor's door or windows. Where the two owners want to cover the shared walk together, each keeps their own recorder and decides what to share. Cable never crosses into the neighbor's basement without written agreement, even when the houses share a wall and it would be shorter.

Prewar houses, low-rise apartment houses and the new mid-rises

In the houses built before 1940, the brick is often older and the mortar softer, so we anchor into sound brick rather than crumbling joints, keep mounts away from the edges of openings and seal every hole. Their basements tend to hold the most abandoned wire, which we trace before cutting anything. Low-rise apartment houses add a vestibule, mailboxes, a stair and a cellar, and we treat them much like a small building: a face camera inside the vestibule, a mailbox view, cameras at the top and bottom of the stair, and a recorder locked in the cellar. The new mid-rises filed near the Gun Hill Road station are a different job again: concrete and block, riser closets, lobbies, retail below and parking, with cable run above corridor ceilings and openings through block cored and sealed. Those get a written plan with the developer or managing agent before the first tenant moves in.

Rules and limits for business cameras in Laconia

  • Private places stay off camera. Restrooms, changing areas, treatment rooms and any room where people expect privacy are never recorded, and we check that mirrors and glass do not show them by reflection.
  • The landlord decides outside the store. The facade, sign band, gate housing and shared back belong to the building owner, and we ask for written agreement before mounting there.
  • City property is off limits. Nothing private goes on lampposts, street signs, bus stops or the subway structure; sidewalk views come from your own wall.
  • Audio stays off. Business cameras are set to record video only, which avoids the consent questions that come with recording conversations.
  • Openings stay sealed. Every hole through a rated wall or floor gets rated firestop, and every exterior hole is sealed against water.
  • Exits stay free. Nothing mounts on a self-closing door, its closer or its frame in a way that could stop it latching, and no camera or cable narrows an exit path.
  • Permits when the work calls for them. New electrical circuits, conduit in common areas or connections to fire alarm or access control equipment can bring permit requirements, and we check before work starts.
  • The installer should be licensed and insured. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431 and carries insurance, which landlords and insurers usually ask to see first.

Field Notes: What We Check First at a Laconia House

On a house in this neighborhood, the walk-through starts on the sidewalk, not the basement. The technician stands where a delivery driver stands and looks at the porch the way the camera will: how high the step is, where the porch light throws glare, whether the door is set back far enough that a face ends up in shadow. Then the walk goes around the house in order. Each entrance gets counted, including the upstairs door some owners forget to mention and the basement steps at the side. Each side yard gets walked to the back gate, with a note of how close the neighbor's windows are. At the back, the question is where the rear wall can hold a camera that sees the gate without staring into the yard next door. Only then do we go downstairs, find the electrical panel and the internet box, trace the old wire across the basement ceiling, and pick a dry, out-of-the-way corner for the recorder. The last check is the one owners appreciate most: we ask who in the house should see which camera, and write it down before quoting.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Laconia: Questions Answered

Houses and the people in them

We asked delivery drivers to use our back gate, and they still leave boxes at the front door. Can a camera show which driver did it and when?

Yes, if the camera is placed for it. A face camera at the door, at head height, records the driver clearly as they step up; a second view looking down at the step shows the box being set down and, later, whoever picks it up. Recording continuously, with the recorder's clock kept accurate, gives you a clip with the exact time, which is what a delivery company asks for when you dispute a missing package. A camera at the back gate does the same for boxes left there. Keep the clips for a few weeks in delivery season. If the same carrier keeps ignoring the instruction, a clip showing the driver walking past a back-gate sign is a stronger complaint than a phone call.

Our detached two-family has open yards on both sides of the house. Which side should get a camera first?

Usually the side that leads most directly to the back gate or to a basement door, because that is the one someone would use to get behind the house unseen. If both sides lead to the yard, start with the side with the driveway, bins or meters, since people already have a reason to walk there and nobody looks twice. One camera on the front corner, mounted high and looking down the yard, covers the whole strip. The second side can follow later on the same recorder; we run its cable at the same visit so adding the camera means a short trip, not new holes. Either way the view is framed down the yard, away from the neighbor's windows.

A handyman offered to mount cameras we bought ourselves. Does New York's installer license apply to that kind of job?

The sources do not agree, and you should know that before deciding. Course providers for the state's installer exam say security systems, including cameras, fall under the state's security and fire alarm installer license, with heavy fines for unlicensed installers. The Department of State's own application, though, says installing passive, non-detecting devices does not need the license. What is clear is that new electrical circuits need a licensed electrician, and that any permits pulled through the Buildings Department need a licensed master electrician or licensed low-voltage contractor. Ask whoever does the work which license they hold and whether they carry insurance. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431, insured, and installs customer-bought equipment as labor-only work.

What should a written camera quote for a Laconia house list, line by line?

Each camera by location and model, each mount and what it fixes to, each cable run and roughly how it gets to the recorder, every hole through brick and how it will be sealed, the recorder and how many days it stores, the battery backup, any off-site clip storage, each user account to be set up, and the setup and testing time. It should also say what is not included, such as electrical work or patching inside, and what happens if a hidden obstacle turns up in the basement. A quote that says only "installation" in one line leaves you no way to compare it with another, or to know what you are paying for.

New buildings, lots and garages

Our new building has both an open parking lot and an enclosed garage. Should the two be on one recorder?

Usually yes, with one important condition. The filing for 3374 Boston Road, for example, shows both open spaces and enclosed spaces, and a building like that is easier to manage when every camera, lobby, garage and lot, sits on one recorder in a locked room with one set of accounts. The condition is distance: lot cameras far from the building can exceed what one cable run carries, so they may need a switch in a weatherproof enclosure or an outdoor-rated link back to the building. The garage, with headlights and dim corners, needs different cameras from the lot, which faces open sky and streetlights. On the recorder, we name and group them separately, so the super can pull up "garage" or "lot" in seconds.

Which cameras should a new twelve-apartment building have working on the day the first tenants move in?

The ones that cover how people get in and where their things wait. For a five-story building with a dozen homes, like the one filed at 3038 Wilson Avenue, that means a face camera inside the street door, a vestibule or lobby view, a mailbox and package view, the rear or service door, the cellar door and the roof door. Stairs and halls can follow on the same recorder once the budget allows, with cable already in place. Move-in weeks bring a steady stream of movers, deliveries and strangers, so those first cameras should be recording continuously from day one, with tenants told in writing where the building's cameras are.

Stores, kitchens and late nights

Our bakery starts baking hours before dawn with the gate half up. Which camera watches the staff door while the front of the shop is dark?

A face camera just inside the staff door, at head height, so each person who comes in is recorded clearly, and an alert during the early hours that goes to the owner's phone. A half-raised gate deserves its own view from inside, looking at the gap, because that opening is the easiest way in while staff are busy in the back. A camera that keeps a color picture in low light shows the dark front without the owner switching on every light. Staff working in the back can have a small monitor showing the front door and the gate, so they know who is at the front without leaving the ovens.

Our pool hall stays open late with tables spread across one big room. Where do the cameras earn their keep?

At the door, the counter and wherever money and cues change hands. The entrance camera gives a clear face of everyone coming in, which matters most on busy nights. The counter view shows the register, payments and the rack where equipment is handed out. One or two high overviews show the room as a whole, so an argument at a far table can be followed from start to finish. Pool rooms are often dim over the floor with bright lights over each table, which confuses ordinary cameras, so we choose ones that handle bright and dark areas in the same frame and set them during evening hours, not in the afternoon.

Our laundromat is open long hours with machines down both walls. Can cameras help settle arguments over lost laundry or machines that took someone's money?

Often, yes. A camera looking down each row of machines from a high corner shows who loaded and unloaded which machine and when, which settles most "someone took my clothes" disputes. A tighter view of the change machine or payment area shows whether a customer paid and what happened next. The door camera records faces as people come and go. The cameras should be set to show machines and floor, not to linger on customers folding clothes, and a sign telling customers that cameras are in use is good practice. Steam and lint coat lenses, so we place cameras away from dryer vents and show staff how to wipe them.

We run the store downstairs and live upstairs. How do our employees see the shop cameras without seeing our home?

With one recorder and separate accounts, or two recorders, depending on how strictly you want them apart. On one recorder, the employees' account shows only the shop cameras: door, counter, floor and stock room. Your family's account sees the house entrance, the stair and the back yard as well. If you would rather the home cameras never share a box with the shop at all, a second small recorder upstairs keeps them fully separate on their own app. Either way, the shop monitor at the counter shows shop views only, and the home entrance camera is framed so it does not show customers waiting outside the store door.

When something stops working

One camera out of six on our property went dark. Is it usually the camera, the cable or the recorder?

When only one camera goes dark and the others keep working, the recorder is rarely the problem. On outdoor cameras on a house, the usual causes are water in the cable connection at the camera, a cable damaged where it enters the brick or runs low on the wall, a tripped port on the switch, or a camera that simply failed after years in the weather. The quick checks are whether the camera's infrared lights glow at night and whether its port on the recorder or switch shows a light. We test the cable end to end with a meter, which tells us in minutes whether to replace the camera, re-terminate the cable or run a new length.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Laconia

Quick answers from search tools, profile sites and cost calculators tend to treat Laconia as a generic Bronx neighborhood. These are claims we see repeated, and what the local sources and the work itself say instead.

"Laconia is a neighborhood of apartment complexes and high-rises."

One profile site, NeighborhoodScout, describes Laconia's housing as mostly apartment complexes and high-rise apartments plus townhomes. Another, Homes.com, calls it a community of single-family homes. Both are summaries built from broad data, and they contradict each other.

The sources that describe the streets themselves point somewhere in between. CityNeighborhoods names detached brick homes, two- and three-family houses and low-rise apartments; Neighborhoods.com describes blocks of duplexes beside quadruplexes; the listings on Laconia and Hone Avenues are brick two-families. Eastchester Gardens and the new mid-rises near the station are real, but they are the exception, not the street pattern.

For camera work that difference matters a great deal. A high-rise plan starts in the lobby. A Laconia plan usually starts at a porch, counts every door on a house and runs cable through a basement, which is why we ask what the property actually is before suggesting anything.

"A video doorbell is all a house needs."

For a single-family house with one door and nothing at the side or back, a good doorbell can be a reasonable start, and plenty of advice stops there.

A Laconia two-family often has two or three entrances, a basement door, two side yards, a back gate and a car at the curb. A doorbell on the main door sees one of those. Battery doorbells also tend to record only when they sense motion and to send clips to one person's app, which leaves the owner and tenants arguing over who saw what.

The more useful setup is a doorbell or face camera at each entrance plus a few wired cameras for the sides and back, all recording to one recorder in the basement, with each person's account showing what they need.

"Public housing cameras and the police already cover the area."

It is true that public cameras have grown around here. New York Focus reported that NYCHA has more than 20,000 cameras in its common spaces and that the NYPD is linking those feeds to its own systems, and Eastchester Gardens' renovation includes security improvements.

Those cameras are aimed at NYCHA buildings and grounds. They were never pointed at the porch of a private house on Burke or Radcliff Avenue, and their footage is not yours to pull when your package goes missing or your car is damaged at the curb.

So a Laconia camera plan treats public cameras as somebody else's system. Your doors, your yard and your curb are recorded on a recorder you control, and you decide whether to share a clip.

"Nobody needs a license to put up cameras."

Some answers say camera installation is unregulated in New York. Others say any camera installer must hold a state license. Both appear in sources we read, and the Department of State's own application adds a third position: that installing passive, non-detecting devices does not require the license.

What is not in dispute is narrower. New electrical work belongs to a licensed electrician, and if Buildings Department permits are pulled, the work must be done or supervised by a licensed master electrician or licensed low-voltage contractor.

Owners do not have to sort out the theory. Ask the installer which license they hold and for proof of insurance. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431 and carries insurance, and says on the quote when a job needs an electrician.

"Any installer listed for the Bronx is local."

Search results for camera installers near Laconia look local. When we checked Yelp's results for nearby Pelham Gardens, most of the companies listed were based in Forest Hills or Fresh Meadows, in Queens.

There is nothing wrong with a company from another borough doing good work, and Yelp also carries plenty of praise for Bronx installers. But distance affects how fast someone can come back when a camera goes dark or a clip has to be saved tonight.

AESS dispatches Bronx work from 460 E Fordham Rd, books every request within 2 hours, and handles follow-up visits from the same place.

"Camera installation has one average price."

Cost sites publish a statewide average with a wide range around it, and AI tools repeat those figures as if they described any house.

On a Laconia house, the total depends on things an average ignores: how many entrances the house has, how far the back-gate camera is from the basement, whether the cable crosses brick or runs through an open basement ceiling, whether there is a basement unit to work around, and how many separate accounts the owner and tenants need.

That is why we quote each Laconia job in writing after photos or a walk-through, at our Bronx rate, with every camera, mount, cable run and task on its own line, so you can see where the money goes.

"Wireless cameras are always the easy choice."

Wireless cameras are easy to buy and quick to stick on a wall, and for a renter's indoor camera they are often the right answer.

Outside a brick house, Wi-Fi has to travel from a router somewhere inside through masonry to a camera at the back gate or the far side yard, and in our field experience that is where signals weaken and clips drop. Battery models also need charging on a ladder and usually record only on motion, which can miss a slow, low movement beside a car.

Wired cameras fed from the basement get power and picture over one cable, record continuously and keep working when the Wi-Fi is busy. For most outdoor positions on a Laconia house, that is the more reliable choice.

Laconia Security Camera Installation FAQ

Is the estimate for a Laconia house really free, and does the written price hold if we take time to decide?
+

Yes, the estimate is free, whether it comes from photos you send or from a walk-through. The written quote lists every camera, mount, cable run and task, and it stays valid for the period printed on it. If you wait past that date, equipment prices can change, so we confirm or update the quote before ordering anything. Nothing is ordered and no work is booked until you approve the quote in writing.

We want copies of important clips kept away from the house. How does that work without uploading every camera all day?
+

The recorder in the basement keeps everything, and only selected events are copied off the property. Typically that means motion at the doors and back gate during the hours the house is empty or asleep, plus any clip you mark yourself. Those copies go to a cloud account or another location you control, so if the recorder is stolen or damaged the important moments survive. Copying events rather than full days keeps the upload small enough for an ordinary home internet line.

Burglars at the Eastchester Gardens Community Center knocked the cameras down before taking anything. How are cameras placed so that does not end the recording?
+

A camera that is knocked down has usually already recorded the person who reached for it, so long as the footage is stored somewhere else. That is the first rule: the recorder sits away from the cameras, out of sight, with key clips copied off site. Second, cameras are mounted high, in hard-cased housings, with cable entering straight into the wall behind the mount so there is nothing to yank. Third, cameras watch each other where possible, so one camera records anyone attacking another.

Our store shares a rear lot with the other businesses in the row. Can we put a camera on our back wall that looks over the lot?
+

Usually, if your landlord agrees in writing and the camera is framed around your own back door and the part of the lot your business uses. The lot often belongs to the building owner, not to any one tenant, so the owner decides what can be mounted and where it may point. Ask whether the other tenants already have cameras back there; aiming yours to meet theirs leaves fewer gaps. Your recorder stays yours, and you decide what to share with neighbors after an incident.

Can a camera in our basement warn us about a burst pipe or a leak while we are at work?
+

Not by itself. A camera can show you water on the floor if you happen to look, but it is not a leak detector and will not reliably alert you to a slow drip. For that, add a water sensor on the floor near the boiler, the water heater and the washing machine, which sends an alert to your phone. A camera aimed at the same corner then lets you look at what the sensor found before you rush home. We can add both on the same visit.

Can the front camera let us know when the kids get home from school?
+

Yes. The recorder can send a phone alert when someone is detected at the front door during a set window, for example mid-afternoon on school days, with a snapshot attached so you can see who it is. Outside that window, the alert can stay quiet. Some families give a teenager a view-only account so they can check the door camera before opening it. Settings that delete clips or change alerts stay on the parents' account.

We are selling our two-family. What happens to the camera system and its accounts at closing?
+

Decide first whether the cameras stay with the house, and put that in the contract. If they stay, export any clips you want to keep, then remove your phone accounts, change or hand over the administrator password, and give the buyer the camera map and the recorder's model details. If the tenants have their own limited accounts, tell the buyer so they can decide whether to keep them. If the system comes with you, we can take it down and seal the holes before the closing.

Spiders keep building webs across our porch camera every summer. Why, and what helps?
+

The camera's infrared lights and the nearby porch light attract insects at night, and spiders follow the insects. A web across the lens catches the infrared light and fills the night picture with a bright haze, and a moving strand can set off motion alerts all night. Moving the camera slightly away from the porch light helps, as does using a camera with a separate or external light so the glow is not right at the lens. Wiping the housing every few weeks in summer keeps the picture clear.

The previous owner ran camera cables through gaps in our window frames. Is that a problem?
+

Often it is. Cable squeezed through a window frame lets in drafts and water, keeps the window from closing and locking properly, and wears through where the sash rubs it. It also leaves an easy cable for anyone outside to cut. The better path is a small hole through the brick near each camera, sealed on both sides, with the cable running inside the basement or behind trim to the recorder. We can reroute the old runs, remove the leftovers and seal the gaps in the frames.

Our front door sits behind an iron storm door with a decorative grille. Can a doorbell camera work behind it?
+

Not well. A camera behind a grille sees the bars first and the visitor second, and motion detection tends to fire on the grille's shadows. The usual fix is to mount the doorbell or face camera on the frame or wall just outside the storm door, at head height, so it sees the visitor directly. If there is no room outside, a small camera above the storm door looking down at the step is the next best view, paired with the existing bell.

We travel abroad for several weeks most winters. What should be set on the cameras before we go?
+

Check that the app opens on your phone over cellular data, not just home Wi-Fi, and that alerts reach you from outside the country. Set alerts for the doors and back gate at all hours while you are away, and confirm that event clips are being copied off the property. Make sure the battery backup is working and the recorder's drive holds enough days. Give a trusted neighbor or relative a limited account for the front door, so someone nearby can check when you call.
