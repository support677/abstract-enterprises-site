The Neighborhood

Why Greenwood Heights Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Greenwood Heights is a neighborhood that the cemetery built twice. Green-Wood Cemetery, founded in 1838 and now a 478-acre National Historic Landmark, came first, and the low, leafy, mostly residential blocks north and west of it filled in later in the century. Neighborhood histories describe Irish, Italian, Polish and Norwegian immigrants, dockworkers, factory hands and tradespeople working at the Erie Basin and the Brooklyn waterfront, who built and rented modest brick rowhouses and wood-frame two- and three-family homes; by the 1890s the area was largely built out. Then the cemetery gave the neighborhood its name. The blocks were folded into Sunset Park in the 1960s and had their own name again by the 1980s, either because real estate agents wanted to set them apart from Sunset Park, as one guide says, or because residents wanted to distinguish themselves from Park Slope and Sunset Park, as another puts it. Boundaries vary by source: one runs from the Prospect Expressway south to 39th Street and from the Gowanus Canal east to Eighth Avenue, another from 15th to 36th Street and Third to Eighth Avenue. None of that changes the price, which is our Brooklyn rate. What it explains is the building stock, and the building stock is what decides how a cable gets from the cellar to a desk two floors up.

That stock is unusual for this part of Brooklyn. Brownstoner's Building of the Day put it plainly: Greenwood Heights is better known for its wood framed houses, both restored and covered with aluminum siding. Homes.com notes that its colorful, vinyl-sided houses set it apart from South Slope and Park Slope to the north, and a local real estate guide describes a mix of wood frame, vinyl sided and brick homes. Public records bear that out house by house. PropertyShark lists 347 20th Street as a two-family frame house built in 1899 with aluminum or vinyl exterior walls, 310 22nd Street as a two-family frame house from 1901 with composition walls, and 204 22nd Street as a two-unit frame building from 1910. A Reinvent NY guide describes most rowhouses as two to three stories over a garden level and often converted into two- or three-family configurations rather than kept as single homes. For a cabling crew, that adds up to a house with hollow wooden walls, a cellar under the whole footprint, two or three separate households, and an outside skin of siding that nobody wants punctured carelessly.

The other half of the neighborhood is new. The Department of City Planning's 2005 rezoning of South Park Slope, adopted by the City Council on November 16, 2005, lowered density on side streets while treating the major avenues, especially Fourth Avenue, as suitable for more. The Architect's Newspaper reported the side streets went from R6 to R6B, capping street walls at 40 feet. Real estate guides describe the result as an influx of condominium apartments into an area of mainly one- and two-family homes. New York YIMBY has tracked the newest wave: permits for an eleven-story mixed-use building at 179 20th Street, a four-story building with seven residences at 321 21st Street, four condominium units at 218 23rd Street due in spring 2026, a five-story residential building at 275 20th Street with security cameras among its amenities, and a three-story building at 156 21st Street with 3,468 square feet of manufacturing space. Earlier reports covered thirteen apartments at 645 Fifth Avenue. These buildings have poured concrete or plank floors, drywall partitions, sometimes a telecom closet in the cellar and sometimes none. Adding a cable in one after the walls are closed is a completely different job from fishing one through a frame house.

The reason people call is usually Wi-Fi, and in these houses the complaint has a physical cause. Each floor of a converted rowhouse is its own apartment, with its own door, its own router and often its own provider, and a router in a front room has to push its signal through plaster, brick party walls, floors and, in a sided house, sometimes a layer of metal. Reviews.org quoted a Reddit user in a prewar building who needed an extender and said the walls are very heavy. Garden apartments, half below the sidewalk, tend to be the worst-served rooms in the house. Home offices on the top floor, a TV in a back parlor, a camera at the rear yard, a printer in the cellar: each one is easier to serve with a cable than with another wireless box. The answer is rarely a stronger router. It is one or two cables placed where the house will let them run, ending at an access point or a jack where the signal is needed.

Finally, the neighborhood keeps changing hands. PropertyShark put the median home sale price in Greenwood Heights at $1.3 million in the third quarter of 2026, down 4.9 percent from a year earlier, and a long-time resident told Homes.com that many families have been here generation after generation. Both groups end up calling us: new owners who are gutting a frame house and want cable in before the walls close, and long-time owners who finally want the second-floor tenant's internet to stop depending on a cable stapled up the stairway. Small businesses on Fourth and Fifth Avenues, the workshops and warehouses toward Third Avenue, and the studios next door in Industry City round out the calls. Every one of them starts with the same question: where can cable travel in this building without damaging it?

Six Greenwood Heights facts that shape a cabling plan before the first hole is drilled

  • Much of the housing is wood framed. Brownstoner calls Greenwood Heights better known for wood-frame houses than brick, many covered in aluminum siding. Hollow stud walls and wooden joists make concealed runs possible in ways a brick-and-plaster house does not.
  • Most houses hold more than one household. Reinvent NY describes rowhouses often converted to two- or three-family use. Each apartment usually wants its own network, and the cellar becomes the shared starting point.
  • Garden apartments are common. Local guides note the basement apartments found in many rowhouses. Half-below-grade rooms are where Wi-Fi from upstairs is weakest and a wired access point helps most.
  • The avenues are concrete. Since the 2005 rezoning, new buildings on and near Fourth and Fifth Avenues have gone up with poured floors and closed partitions, where cable added after construction often has to travel in surface raceway.
  • There is no historic district on the residential blocks. We found no Landmarks Preservation Commission district covering Greenwood Heights' homes; the landmarks are individual sites such as the cemetery's gates, its chapel and the Weir Greenhouse.
  • Some service arrives by antenna. NYC Mesh, the volunteer network, opened a supernode on the roof of the DataVerge data center in Industry City, and a mesh install includes an Ethernet cable from the rooftop antenna down to the apartment it serves.

Structured Cabling for Greenwood Heights' Architecture

Six kinds of building account for nearly every cabling call from Greenwood Heights. They differ in what the walls are made of, whether there is a cellar and a roof space to work in, how many households live inside, and who besides you gets a say. Those four things set the route long before anyone picks a cable category.

The sided frame house

Wood-framed rowhouses from the 1890s and 1900s under aluminum, vinyl or composition siding, like the two-family houses PropertyShark lists on 20th and 22nd Streets.

The restored frame house

Frame houses whose owners have stripped the siding and brought back clapboard, trim and original woodwork, where nothing visible may be added to the front.

The brick and brownstone row

Masonry rowhouses in Italianate and Neo-Grec styles, such as the Queen Anne rows on 18th Street, with brick party walls and plaster on the inside.

The converted two- and three-family

Any of the above split into separate apartments, often with a garden unit below the stoop and a shared cellar under everything.

The post-rezoning building

Concrete-floored condos and rentals on and near Fourth and Fifth Avenues, from four-unit buildings like 218 23rd Street to eleven stories at 179 20th Street.

The avenue shop and the workshop

Storefronts with apartments above on Fourth and Fifth Avenues, and manufacturing and warehouse buildings toward Third Avenue like the one planned at 156 21st Street.

The sided frame house: hollow walls behind a skin you should not puncture

A wood-framed house is the friendliest building in Brooklyn for cable, as long as you respect two things. Inside, its walls are mostly hollow bays between studs, and a cable can often drop from one floor to the next inside a bay with no visible opening except the jack itself. Its floors are wooden joists, so a cable can cross a cellar ceiling or the space between floors through small holes drilled in the right places. That is the good news. The first thing to respect is the siding. Aluminum, vinyl and composition siding on a Greenwood Heights house sits over the original wood sheathing or clapboard, and every hole through it is a path for water to get behind the siding and into the wall. When a cable has to come in from outside, from a provider's drop, a rooftop antenna or a yard camera, we bring it through one planned point with a sleeve, a drip loop and a sealed, flashed cover, rather than a fresh hole wherever it happens to arrive.

The second thing to respect is what the framing is doing. In a house built around 1900, the studs and joists carry the floors above, and some of them have already been notched by a century of plumbers and electricians. We drill rather than notch, keep holes small and near the middle of a joist's depth, stay away from the ends where joists bear on walls, and use the spaces that are already open: the gap where a closet stacks above a closet, the chase beside an old chimney, the bay where a previous trade already ran a pipe. Before drilling down from one floor to the next, we check for fire blocking, old gas lines and wiring in the bay, and if a stud bay is blocked midway, we decide with you whether to open a small access hole and patch it or move the route. The quote tells you which rooms will have any opening at all.

The restored frame house: wiring a house whose owners took the siding off

Brownstoner's description of the neighborhood names both halves of the story: frame houses covered with siding and frame houses that have been restored. Owners who have spent years stripping vinyl, repairing clapboard and remilling trim do not want a single cable clip on the front of the house, and usually not on the trim inside either. In these houses we treat the exterior as off limits except for the one service entry point the provider already uses, and we look harder for interior routes. Stacked closets, the back stair, the chase beside a chimney breast, built-in cabinetry and the space behind baseboards all become candidates, and jacks are placed low on walls or inside cabinets where a box will not interrupt original woodwork.

Restoration also changes the timing. A house in the middle of restoration, with plaster down in some rooms and siding off one wall, is the cheapest moment to place cable that will never be seen. We have no interest in making a restoration wait, so we fit our work into the gaps between trades: cable pulled while walls are open, terminated after the plasterer and the painter are done, tested at the end. For any opening in original fabric, a doorframe, a baseboard, a floorboard, we show you the exact spot first. Restorers tend to have strong opinions about every square inch of their house, and they are usually right.

The brick and brownstone row: plaster on brick and nothing hollow to fish

Not every house here is wood. Brownstoner's look at 207A to 209 18th Street describes Queen Anne rowhouses from before 1888 on a block of wood-frame rows, and the neighborhood mixes brick and brownstone fronts among the frame houses. In a masonry rowhouse, the party walls and often the front and back walls are solid brick with plaster applied directly to it or on furring strips. There is no hollow bay to drop a cable through, so the vertical route has to come from somewhere else: a closet stack, a dumbwaiter shaft if the house still has one, an old chimney flue that has been properly decommissioned, or the cavity beside a stair. Interior partitions in these houses are often wood stud and plaster, which can be fished like any frame wall.

The horizontal runs follow the floors. Cable crosses under each floor through the joists, which in a brick row run between the party walls, and comes up to jacks on interior partitions or through the floor at the baseboard. Where a jack has to sit on a brick wall, it goes in a low-profile surface box fed from the floor directly below, or the cable runs behind a baseboard. Where cable has to pass through a brick wall, between front and back buildings, from cellar to yard, we core a clean sleeve rather than breaking out a ragged hole, and seal it afterward. The whole route is drawn for you before the quote, because in a brick house the drawing is the job.

The converted two- and three-family: one cellar, several households

The most common Greenwood Heights cabling job is not a single-family house at all. It is a rowhouse split into a garden apartment, a parlor-floor or duplex unit for the owner, and one or two apartments above, each with its own lease, its own provider and its own idea of what good internet means. In these houses the cellar is the hub. The provider's lines usually arrive there, sometimes one per apartment, and the cleanest design keeps every household's equipment either in its own apartment or in a locked, labeled spot in the cellar, with its own cable bundle running up to its own floor. Nobody's network passes through anybody else's router, and a tenant moving out never takes another apartment's connection with them.

The garden apartment needs special attention. It is the unit closest to the cellar and the furthest, in signal terms, from everyone else's router, and it is often the one with the least natural light and the thickest walls. A single cable from the cellar to a ceiling access point in the middle of the garden unit usually does more than anything else on the job. Because the cellar is shared and sometimes part of a tenant's lease, we also plan access: which door we use, which days the tenant is home, and whether any equipment in the cellar needs a lockable cabinet so the owner, the tenants and the next provider technician can each reach only what is theirs.

The post-rezoning building: concrete, closed walls and a board with rules

After the 2005 rezoning set higher density on the avenues and lower density on the side streets, new buildings went up mostly on and near Fourth and Fifth Avenues, and lately a few on the side streets themselves. New York YIMBY's reports give a sense of the range: four condominium units at 218 23rd Street, seven residences at 321 21st Street, a five-story rental at 275 20th Street that a commenter described as technically an office-to-residential conversion, thirteen apartments at 645 Fifth Avenue and an eleven-story mixed-use building planned at 179 20th Street. In these buildings the floors and ceilings are concrete, the walls are drywall on metal studs, and the developer's original wiring is whatever the developer chose. Some units have a structured media panel in a closet with a jack in each room; some have a single coax outlet.

Adding cable to a finished unit here means working with what the building allows. Drywall partitions can be fished, but a concrete ceiling cannot hide a cable, and many condo boards forbid cutting or coring the slab. A Yelp review from a Sunset Park electrical contractor search describes exactly that: the contractor was not allowed to cut the concrete to recess wiring and had to install surface raceway. We plan around those rules rather than fighting them. Surface raceway painted to match, cable inside the soffits that already hide ductwork, runs along the top of kitchen cabinets and inside closets, and the building's existing sleeves between the unit and the telecom closet are the usual tools. If a building is still under construction, the developer is the one to talk to, and the time to talk is before the drywall goes up.

The avenue shop and the workshop: storefronts, light industry and the floor below somebody's apartment

Fourth and Fifth Avenues carry most of the neighborhood's commerce, with smaller clusters along Prospect Avenue, and guides name places like the Greenwood Park beer garden in a former gas station, Baked in Brooklyn on Fifth Avenue, Minnie's Bar on Fourth Avenue and Freddy's Bar and Backroom at 627 Fifth Avenue; Mexico En Tu Casa at 776 Fourth Avenue and Ron's Deli at 926 Fourth Avenue opened in August 2025. We do not name any of them as customers. A storefront network on these avenues is small but crowded: card terminals, a register, a kitchen printer, an office computer, a camera recorder and Wi-Fi for staff and guests, all fitted into a back room or under a counter, often in a building where the apartments above share the cellar and the landlord controls both.

West of Fourth Avenue and toward Third, the neighborhood turns light industrial. New York YIMBY reported permits in September 2026 for a three-story building at 156 21st Street with 3,468 square feet of manufacturing space and four enclosed parking spaces, and Industry City, just to the south between 32nd and 41st Streets, holds artist studios, restaurants and coworking space. In workshops and small factories the cabling concerns change: long runs across open floors, cable that has to come down to a workbench or a machine without being snagged, motors, welders and heavy power cable that data cable should stay well away from, and dust that gets into anything left open. We cover those floors with conduit and planned drops, and keep office cable in the office.

Field Notes

Field Notes: What a Technician Checks in a Greenwood Heights House Before Drawing a Route

These notes describe how our technicians survey houses and buildings like those in Greenwood Heights. They describe our practice, not an account of any particular address.

Go to the cellar first. The provider's lines almost always land there, and the cellar ceiling shows how the house was built: which way the joists run, how deep they are, what has already been drilled through them and where the plumbing stacks rise. It also shows the history. Old phone wire, abandoned coax and the remains of earlier internet installs tell us which routes upstairs other trades have already used.

Find the stack. The technician walks every floor looking for something that lines up vertically: closets one above another, a chase beside a chimney breast, a bathroom wall above a bathroom wall, the back stair. In a frame house, a closet stack from cellar to top floor is often the entire riser for the job, with no visible opening anywhere else.

Look under the roof. Many houses here are two or three stories over a garden level, and the top floor often has a low space between its ceiling and the roof, reached through a hatch or not at all. If it is reachable, it is the easiest place to cross from front to back on the top floor. If it is not, the technician notes it so nobody promises a top-floor route that does not exist.

Knock on the outside walls. The technician checks whether the exterior is siding over wood, restored clapboard or brick, because that decides how any outside cable enters and whether a route can run up the back of the house. The rear of a sided frame house sometimes offers a straight, protected route between floors that the inside does not.

Ask who lives where. In a converted house, the owner, the garden tenant and the upstairs tenants may each have their own provider and their own expectations. The survey records which apartments need their own network, which rooms we must enter and what each household should be able to reach.

Leave a sketch behind. Before leaving, the technician sketches the route floor by floor with you, marking every jack and every opening, so the written quote describes a plan you have already seen.

Pathways

Cellar to Cockloft: How Cable Climbs Through a Greenwood Heights House

Every structured cabling system has the same parts: an entrance where service arrives, a spot where the main equipment lives, cable between that spot and any other equipment points, cable from there to each jack or access point, and the jacks themselves. In a Greenwood Heights rowhouse, the entrance and the main equipment usually share the cellar, and the whole job comes down to one question: how does the cable get up? Here is how each part of the house answers it.

The cellar ceiling. The joists over the cellar are the horizontal highway of the house. Cable for each floor gathers here from the equipment spot, travels along or through the joists to the bottom of the vertical route, and turns upward. We support it on its own hooks or straps, keep it off water and gas pipes and away from the electrical panel and its feeders, and group each apartment's cable together so it can be identified without a tracer later. In cellars that are also somebody's storage, laundry or workshop, the cable stays high and out of reach of shelving.

The vertical route. In a frame house the best vertical route is often a single stud bay that runs clear from the cellar to the top floor, found by checking from below with a drill and a camera before anyone opens a wall. More often the route is a series of closets, each with a small hole through its floor into the closet below, which costs nothing in finished space and stays hidden. Where neither exists, a chase beside a chimney breast, the wall behind a stair or, in some sided houses, a protected run up the rear exterior can do the same job. In brick rows the closets and chases do nearly all the work, because the masonry walls offer no cavity.

Each floor. Once a bundle reaches a floor, each cable travels the last few feet to its jack: down a hollow partition to a box at outlet height, under the floor to come up behind a baseboard, or along the top of a closet to an access point on the ceiling. In a frame house most jacks end up flush in the wall with nothing else showing. In a brick house, jacks on masonry walls sit in a slim surface box fed from below. Access points go where the signal is needed, usually the middle of the floor's ceiling, not where the router happened to land.

The top floor and the roof space. The top floor is where routes most often get stuck, because there is no floor above it to come down through. If the house has a reachable space under the roof, cable can cross there and drop into the top-floor walls. If not, the top floor is fed from below, from closets or wall bays, and the jacks are placed where that is possible. When a rooftop antenna is involved, the cable comes down from the roof into that same space, through a sealed opening.

Openings that get closed again

Every hole through a floor or a fire-rated wall is a hole in something that slows fire and smoke. In a one-family frame house the floors between stories are not usually rated the way a large apartment building's are, but every hole still gets closed around the cable with the proper fire-rated sealant, because an open hole from cellar to bedroom is a chimney for smoke whatever the code says. In the post-rezoning buildings, where unit walls, shafts and floors are rated, sealing penetrations with listed firestop is required and usually a condition of the board's approval. We seal as we go, not at the end, and mark each sealed opening on the drawing.

Keeping data apart from power and pipes

Old houses are crowded behind the walls. A century of plumbers, electricians and heating contractors have used the same bays and joist spaces, and some of what they left is still live. We keep data cable separated from electrical wiring, cross it at right angles where the two must meet, keep it away from steam and hot water pipes and never strap it to gas lines. If an existing electrical cable or old gas pipe sits in the bay we hoped to use, we pick another bay rather than squeeze past it. That kind of discipline is invisible on the day the job is finished, and it is what keeps a network cable working for decades in a house that keeps being renovated around it.

Service Entry

Where the Internet Comes In: Provider Boxes, Separate Apartments and Antennas on the Hill

Cabling inside a house only helps if it starts in the right place. In Greenwood Heights, that place is almost always the cellar, but the details vary more than people expect, especially in houses with more than one apartment and on the higher blocks near the cemetery.

The provider's box. When a cable or fiber provider connects a house, its technician brings the line to a demarcation point, usually a box on the cellar wall, sometimes a box on the outside of the house or in a hallway. Everything from that point to your devices is yours. In older installs, providers often ran their own cable from that box straight up the outside of the house or through a window frame to wherever the customer wanted the modem, and many sided houses here still carry those cables. Part of a good cabling job is landing the provider's line in a sensible spot in the cellar and running clean, labeled cable from there to each floor, so the next technician has an obvious place to connect and no reason to drill another hole.

One house, several providers. In a two- or three-family house, each household may have its own provider and its own line. That is fine, and it is common. What causes trouble is when each provider's installer runs a new line by a new route. We plan the cellar as a small, shared entry point: each provider's line lands in its own labeled spot, each apartment has its own cable bundle running upstairs, and the owner knows which is which. If an apartment changes providers, the new line connects at the cellar and the cable upstairs stays exactly where it is.

Antennas and mesh networks. Greenwood Heights sits high. Battle Hill, inside the cemetery, is the highest point in Brooklyn at about 216 feet, and guides note the neighborhood's views of Upper New York Bay and the Manhattan skyline. NYC Mesh, the volunteer community network, opened a supernode on the roof of the DataVerge data center in Industry City, and a Brooklyn community board's minutes describe a mesh install as an antenna with an Ethernet cable running from it to the user's apartment. Whether a mesh connection or any fixed-wireless service suits a particular rooftop is for the network and the owner to work out. Getting a cable from the antenna mount down through the house to the router, through a sealed opening and with proper grounding where it enters, is cabling work, and we plan it like any other run.

Where the equipment should live in a rowhouse

In a single-family house, the router and switch can live anywhere central, and a closet on the parlor or second floor is often better than the cellar because it sits in the middle of the house. In a multi-family house, each household's equipment should usually live in its own apartment, fed from the cellar, so a tenant can restart their own router without going downstairs. The cellar then holds only the provider handoffs and a small panel where each apartment's cable starts. Cellars here can be damp in summer, so anything that does stay downstairs goes on the wall, off the floor and away from the water heater and the washing machine.

Garden apartments and the signal problem

The garden apartment is half below the sidewalk, wrapped in foundation walls and often under a floor that carries the parlor's heavy joists. Wi-Fi from a router upstairs has to fight through all of it, and cellular signal is frequently weak at that level too. A single cable from the cellar to an access point mounted on the garden apartment's ceiling solves the Wi-Fi side, and modern phones can carry calls over a good Wi-Fi connection when the carrier signal is poor. This is one of the cheapest, most noticeable improvements we make in Greenwood Heights houses, and it is a good first phase if a fuller job has to wait.

Rules and Licenses

Permits, Licenses, Landmarks and Boards: What Governs a Greenwood Heights Cabling Job

We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. It is what the published rules say and how we apply them, so you know what to ask before work begins.

The low-voltage permit rule. The city's Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, which took effect on December 21, 2025, says an electrical permit is not required to install, alter, maintain or repair wiring designed to operate below 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data transmission circuits, provided the work is done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person. The Department of Buildings lists low-voltage electrical work under sections 28-101.5 and 28-105.4.7 on its electrical requirements page. For most data cabling in a house or small building, that means no electrical permit filing. We should be honest that not every source agrees: one industry site still says low-voltage work requires DOB permits. Where a job is large or unusual, we confirm with the Department of Buildings rather than rely on any website, including this one.

Fire alarm wiring is separate. One NYC cabling guide notes that fire alarm wiring falls outside the low-voltage exemption and requires specialized licensing and additional permits under New York State rules. In the post-rezoning buildings, which have building fire alarm systems, our data cable never shares a fire alarm raceway, and we coordinate with the fire alarm contractor where both trades are working.

Licensed security work. Cameras, door access and burglar alarms are licensed work in New York. AESS holds NYS license #12000287431, so when a cabling job includes a camera at the back yard or a reader at a building door, the same licensed company handles the cable and the device.

Landmarks. We found no Landmarks Preservation Commission historic district covering Greenwood Heights' residential blocks. The designated landmarks here are individual sites tied to the cemetery: its gates, designated in 1966; the Weir Greenhouse, designated in 1982; and the Fort Hamilton Parkway Gate and the cemetery chapel, designated in 2016. For an ordinary house on 20th or 22nd Street, that means the Commission is unlikely to be involved in a cable entering the building. It does not mean anything goes on the facade; neighbors and owners here care a great deal about how their houses look, and we treat the front of a restored house as untouchable whether or not a rule says so.

Boards, co-owners and tenants. In a condo building, the board's alteration rules usually govern any opening in walls, floors and ceilings, and many forbid cutting into concrete. In a two- or three-family house, the owner decides, but tenants have rights to quiet enjoyment and notice before anyone enters their apartment. We send a certificate of insurance to any board or managing agent before the crew is booked, and in rental houses we schedule entry into each unit with the owner and the tenant together.

Old paint. Houses built before 1978 may have lead paint on trim, floors and walls. Drilling a small hole for a cable disturbs very little, but we treat painted surfaces in old houses with care: we drill through protective sheeting, collect dust at the bit and clean with a vacuum rather than a broom. If a route would require opening a larger area of old painted surface, the federal lead-safe rules for pre-1978 housing come into play, and we tell you before that happens rather than after.

Your Questions Answered

Structured Cabling in Greenwood Heights: Questions Answered

These are the situations Greenwood Heights owners, tenants and businesses bring up when they call: siding that should not leak, cellars with century-old joists, top floors with no way up, garden apartments with no signal, antennas on the hill, condos with concrete ceilings and workshops full of motors. Each is answered here once and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Frame houses and their siding

Our frame house is covered in vinyl siding over the old clapboard. If a cable has to come in from outside, how do you keep water from getting behind the siding?

By making one planned entry instead of several improvised ones. Siding on a Greenwood Heights frame house sheds water on its face; a hole through it lets water reach the sheathing underneath, where it rots wood out of sight. When an outside cable has to enter, from a provider's drop, a rooftop antenna or a yard camera, we choose a single point, ideally under an eave or on the rear wall where it is sheltered, and bring the cable in through a sleeve sloped slightly downward toward the outside, with a drip loop on the cable so water runs off before it reaches the opening. The siding gets a proper mounting block or entry cover sealed to it, and the sleeve is sealed inside and out. If old provider cables already enter through window frames or random holes, we can consolidate them through the new entry and seal the old holes.

Could the cable between floors run up the back of our frame house instead of through finished rooms?

Sometimes, and on some sided houses it is the cleanest route available. The rear wall of a rowhouse faces the yard, out of sight from the street, and a cable run in a small, painted, outdoor-rated raceway up that wall can reach each floor through one sealed entry per floor, with no openings in finished interior rooms. It is a good answer when the inside has no stacked closets and the owner does not want plaster opened. It is a poor answer on a restored clapboard house or where the rear wall gets heavy weather, and it adds exterior entries that need maintaining. We weigh it against the interior options on the survey and show you both on the drawing, with the reasons, so you choose with full information.

Does aluminum siding on our house block Wi-Fi to the yard and to a camera at the back fence?

It can weaken it noticeably. Aluminum siding is a sheet of metal wrapped around the house, and metal reflects and absorbs radio signals far more than wood or vinyl does. A router inside a house sided in aluminum often loses much of its signal at the exterior walls, which is why the back yard, a garage or a camera at the fence can be out of reach even when the indoor Wi-Fi seems fine. The fix is to stop asking the signal to get through the wall. A cable to an outdoor-rated access point under the rear eave covers the yard directly, and a cable to the camera itself removes Wi-Fi from the equation for that device. Both enter through a single sealed opening.

We are stripping the siding to restore the clapboard. Is that the right moment to run cable, and what should go in?

It is one of the best moments you will get. With siding off and before new clapboard goes on, the outside of the wall cavities is easier to reach, exterior entry points can be built into the new skin instead of cut through it later, and any old provider cables that ran across the facade can be removed for good. We would place a single sleeved entry for future provider lines, cable to any exterior camera or doorbell positions you think you might want, and, where the wall bays are reachable, cable down inside them to the floors that need it. Coordination is everything: our cable goes in after the carpenter repairs the sheathing and before the clapboard and trim are installed, and nothing is left loose for the painter to discover.

Cellars, top floors and old fabric

The joists in our cellar are well over a hundred years old and already full of plumbers' holes. How do you get across without weakening them?

By drilling small holes in the strongest part of the joist and using spaces that are already open. A joist carries most of its load at its top and bottom edges, so holes go near the middle of its depth, stay well away from the ends where it rests on the walls, and stay small. We never notch the bottom edge, which is how many old joists were weakened in the first place. Before drilling, the technician looks for joists that are already cracked, sistered or heavily notched and routes around them, and where the cable can run parallel to the joists in the bay between two of them, it does, supported on its own straps without any holes. If a joist looks damaged, we tell you; that is a carpenter's job, not ours.

Our top floor sits under a low roof space with only a small hatch. Can cable get across up there to reach the front bedroom?

If the space is reachable and safe to work in, it is often the easiest route on the whole floor. Many Greenwood Heights rowhouses are two or three stories over a garden level, and the top floor commonly has a shallow cavity under the roof. A technician checks through the hatch for clearance, insulation, old wiring and any roof leaks, and if the space allows it, cable crosses there and drops down into the top-floor partitions to jacks or a ceiling access point. If the space is too tight, too hot to work in safely in summer or packed with insulation that should not be disturbed, we feed the top floor from below instead, and say so before quoting rather than discovering it on the day.

Our house was built around 1900 and almost certainly has lead paint on the trim. What happens when you drill through painted woodwork?

We assume the paint is lead-based and work accordingly. A cable needs only a small hole, and we drill it through plastic sheeting taped below the work, with a vacuum attachment collecting dust at the bit, then wipe and vacuum the area rather than sweeping it. Where possible we avoid painted trim altogether, coming up through the floor behind a baseboard or into a closet rather than through a door casing. If a route would require opening a larger area of old painted surface, the lead-safe renovation rules for pre-1978 housing apply, and we would tell you before starting so you can decide whether that route is worth it. In houses with young children we are especially strict about cleanup each day.

Can a jack go on the chimney breast in our parlor, where the TV is going?

Usually, though we treat the chimney with respect. A chimney breast is masonry, and an active flue inside it is no place for cable. If the fireplace is decommissioned and the flue sealed, the chase beside the breast is sometimes a good vertical route, and a jack can be set low on the breast in a surface box fed from the floor below. If the flue is still in use, cable stays outside the masonry entirely and comes up through the floor beside the breast, or behind the baseboard, to a jack at the side of the TV location. Mounting above a fireplace carries its own add-on on our price list, and we check the mantel and wall before agreeing to it.

Garden apartments, tenants and roofs

The garden apartment in our house gets almost no cell signal. Will a wired access point down there help with phone calls?

It usually helps a great deal, though not by fixing the cell signal itself. A garden apartment sits half below grade behind foundation walls, and both Wi-Fi from upstairs and cellular signal from outside struggle to reach it. A single cable from the cellar to an access point on the garden apartment's ceiling gives the unit strong Wi-Fi, and most current phones can place and receive calls over Wi-Fi when the carrier signal is poor, once Wi-Fi calling is turned on. Cellular signal boosters are a separate product with their own rules, and we do not install them. For most garden tenants, a wired access point is the change they notice first.

Our cellar is part of the garden tenant's lease. Where should the network equipment for the upstairs apartments go?

Out of the tenant's space as much as possible. If upstairs households' routers sit in a cellar that only the garden tenant can enter, every restart becomes a knock on a tenant's door. The better design keeps only the provider handoffs and a small labeled panel in the cellar, in a lockable box on the wall near the entry, and runs each apartment's cable up to that apartment's own router. The owner keeps a key to the box, providers connect there, and the garden tenant is not hosting anyone's equipment. Entry into the cellar during installation is scheduled with the tenant in advance, and the lease, not us, decides who may access the box later.

We are thinking about an antenna on our roof on one of the higher blocks. What does the cable coming down need besides a hole in the roof?

Three things: a weatherproof entry, protection where it comes inside, and a sensible route down. Cable from a rooftop antenna is outdoor-rated and sunlight-resistant on the roof, enters through a flashed and sealed opening rather than under a roof edge, and is bonded to the building's grounding where it comes inside, with surge protection at the entry, which the electrical code expects for antennas and outside cable on a building. From there it follows the same vertical route as the rest of the house to the router. NYC Mesh and fixed-wireless services set their own requirements for the antenna and mount, and the building owner decides whether anything goes on the roof at all. We handle the cable from the mount to the router.

New buildings and workshops

We bought a unit in one of the new buildings and the board will not let anyone cut into the concrete ceilings. Can we still add jacks?

Yes, by working with what the building already has. The partitions in a new unit are usually drywall on metal studs and can be fished; the concrete overhead cannot. We trace the developer's existing cable back to the unit's media panel or telecom closet, use any spare sleeves or conduit the building provided, and run new cable inside partitions, through soffits that already hide ducts, inside closets and along cabinet tops. Where a stretch has to be visible, it goes in a slim surface raceway painted to match, which is what a Sunset Park contractor's Yelp review describes doing when concrete could not be cut. We send the board our scope and route before work starts, along with the certificate of insurance.

Our workshop near Third Avenue runs big motors and a welder. Will that interfere with the network cable?

It can if the cable is careless about where it goes, and it is easy to prevent. Large motors, welding equipment and heavy power cable create electrical noise, and data cable laid alongside them for long stretches can pick it up, which shows up as errors and dropped connections. We keep data cable well apart from power feeders and machine wiring, cross them at right angles where they must meet, keep data runs away from welding stations entirely, and use steel conduit where cable comes down near machinery, which shields it and protects it physically at once. Where a run has to pass very close to heavy equipment over a long distance, shielded cable or fiber may be the better choice, and the survey tells us which.

Our electrician is rewiring the house during the renovation. Can the network cable just go through the same holes as the new power wiring?

Not the same holes, but the same visit can work well. Data cable should not share a bored hole or a box with power wiring; running them together invites noise on the data side and complications for the electrician's inspection. What works is coordination: while walls are open, we plan our route through separate holes in the same bays or neighboring ones, keep a sensible distance from the power runs, and cross at right angles where needed. If the electrician is installing new boxes, we can agree on where data jacks sit next to outlets, so the finished room looks planned. Tell us the electrician's schedule when you book and we fit our pulls in between their rough-in and the drywall.

FAQ

Greenwood Heights Structured Cabling FAQ

Should we move furniture away from the walls before the crew comes?
+

It helps to clear about three feet in front of each wall that will get a jack and to empty the closets on the route, since closets are often where cable travels between floors in Greenwood Heights houses. Heavy pieces can stay where they are; the crew will move what it needs with your permission and put it back, but fragile items and anything valuable are best moved by you.

Do we need to arrange parking for your van on our side street?
+

No, finding parking is our job. It does help to tell us about alternate-side days, a driveway we may use or a hydrant-heavy block, and if the house sits on a steep stretch of one of the numbered streets, we plan to carry material from wherever the van can stop.

If the job runs into a second day, will open holes be left overnight where a cat could get in?
+

No. Any opening into a wall bay, a floor or the space under the roof is covered at the end of each day, and closets we are working through are closed. Tell us about pets when booking so the crew knows which doors to keep shut while they work.

Can the old telephone jack boxes in our rooms be reused for the new data jacks?
+

Often, yes. If an old phone box sits in a sensible spot and its wall cavity connects to the route, we can pull new cable to it and fit a data faceplate, which avoids a new opening. If the old box is surface-mounted on a baseboard or the old wire was stapled along the outside of the wall, we usually remove it and patch rather than reuse it.

Can the faceplates match the trim and hardware in a restored old house?
+

Usually. Faceplates come in several colors and finishes, and in restored rooms we often place jacks low near the baseboard or inside a cabinet so they barely show. Tell us what you would like it to match and we will bring options, or install plates you have chosen yourself.

How do you get a cable up through restored pine floorboards without damaging them?
+

We avoid the open floor and come up at the edge of the room, either through the bottom plate of the wall from the cellar or through a small hole right at the baseboard where it is hidden by the trim. Before drilling we check from below for joists, pipes and wiring, and if a floor is especially precious, we look for a route through a closet instead.

Will our electricity need to be turned off while you work?
+

Generally not. Data cabling does not involve your electrical panel, so the house stays powered. If a new jack sits beside an existing outlet and we need to open that wall, we may ask to switch off one circuit briefly for safety, and we tell you before doing it.

How do you protect refinished floors and stair runners while carrying ladders and cable through the house?
+

We lay floor protection on the path between the door, the cellar stairs and each work area, use drop cloths under every ladder and work spot, and carry ladders rather than drag them. Stair runners get a cover on the treads we use, and anything we touch is cleaned before we leave each floor.

Our bar on Fifth Avenue has a back garden for customers. Can we get good Wi-Fi out there?
+

Yes, with a cable to an outdoor-rated access point mounted on the rear wall or under an overhang facing the garden. The cable enters through one sealed opening, and the access point can run the guest network only, kept separate from the payment terminals and the office computer inside.

We may sell our house in a couple of years. Is there anything we should keep from the cabling job?
+

Keep the port map and the test results. We cannot tell you that wiring changes what a buyer pays, but a clear record of which jack goes to which room, how each floor is fed and where the provider's line enters is something a buyer's inspector and the next owner can use, and it saves them from tracing everything again.

Is the quote free, and what does the written quote include?
+

Talking through your photos and preparing the quote costs nothing. The written quote lists every jack and access point, the cable type, the route floor by floor, each opening through siding, brick or floors, any equipment and the payment terms, all at our Brooklyn rate.
