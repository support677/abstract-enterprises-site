Fieldston is a neighborhood of houses rather than apartment buildings. Inside the Fieldston Historic District, which the Landmarks Preservation Commission designated in 2006, roughly 250 houses stand on winding, tree-lined private streets laid out around rock outcroppings, the oldest of them from the 1910s, in Tudor, Medieval, Colonial, Dutch Colonial, Craftsman and Mediterranean Revival styles, with a later group of mid-century modern houses among them. Many of these houses are large, many have been added onto, and most have more stairs, wings, cellar rooms and attic rooms than a typical Bronx house. On the eastern edge, along Broadway and Cayuga Avenue, a short row of co-ops holds most of the neighborhood's apartments. That mix decides the fire alarm work here. In the houses, the job is nearly always a whole-house plan of linked smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, so that a fire in a kitchen wing, a cellar or a finished attic is heard in a bedroom at the far end of the second floor with the door shut. In the co-ops, it is building systems, apartment alarms and the line between them. This page explains how we plan both in old plaster-and-masonry houses, when a job crosses into an FDNY filing, what two recent house fires reported by News 12 suggest about planning, and what the work costs at our standard Bronx rate.

The Neighborhood

Why Fieldston Properties Need Fire Alarm Installation

For fire safety, what matters is what that history left inside the houses. A house built in 1913 and enlarged in 1919 and again in 1927, like the Tudor Revival at 4401–4403 Waldo Avenue, has three building campaigns under one roof, each with its own framing, its own wiring and its own idea of where the bedrooms belong. Brick Underground's 2023 profile described Fieldston's single-family houses as 50 to 100 years old and said they usually need renovation, which matches what an installer finds: wiring updated room by room over decades, alarms added by different owners at different times, and attic or cellar rooms that were turned into living space long after the original plan. These are often big houses with long halls, a main stair and a secondary stair, a kitchen in a service wing, and bedrooms on two or three floors. A battery alarm in each hall, each one working on its own, can sound loudly in an empty corridor while the people it is meant to wake sleep a floor away behind a closed door.

Six Fieldston facts that shape a fire alarm plan

  • Almost every lot in the district holds a one- or two-family house. NYC PLUTO property records for the historic district show about 256 residential lots, all classed as one- or two-family buildings, and CultureNow describes the district as 257 houses and related structures. Fire alarm work here is therefore mostly household smoke and carbon monoxide alarms rather than filed building systems.
  • The houses span a century of styles and building methods. Records and local histories name a 1911–12 Craftsman at 4418 Waldo Avenue with a stone base and fieldstone chimneys, a 1918–19 Dutch Colonial Revival by Baum at 331 West 250th Street, a 1918 one-family house at 4680 Fieldston Road, a 1928 Medieval Revival at 4405 Waldo Avenue built by Baum for Albert Wheeler, and a 1928 Mediterranean Revival at 4621 Waldo Avenue. Each builds walls, ceilings and attics differently.
  • Additions are part of the record. The Tudor Revival at 4401–4403 Waldo Avenue dates from 1913 with additions in 1919 and 1927. In houses like it, the alarm plan has to cross the joints between building campaigns, where walls are often thickest and ceiling heights change.
  • The streets and the landscape are protected too. Wikipedia describes the neighborhood as owned by a private association that maintains the streets, and the Natural Area rules mean landscape changes need City Planning review. Fire alarm work that stays inside the house rarely touches either, but anything that crosses the yard to a garage or outbuilding should be planned with both in mind.
  • Exterior changes in the district need Landmarks review. The LPC requires permits for alterations that affect the exterior of a building in a historic district, and says most exterior changes to front and rear facades need review. Its Fieldston master plan and 2023 rule amendments list additions, outbuildings, windows, heating, venting and air conditioning, but do not name fire alarm devices.
  • The apartments sit on the eastern edge. PLUTO records place the neighborhood's apartment buildings on a short strip along Broadway, Cayuga Avenue and Post Road between West 242nd and West 250th Streets, about eight buildings and roughly 480 apartments, including The Fieldston at 6035 Broadway, a six-story, 103-unit co-op built in 1961 with a doorman and a live-in super, and a 79-apartment co-op from 1930–31 at 4410 and 4414 Cayuga Avenue.

Need Repair in Fieldston? A Chirp Nobody Can Find, a Linked Set That Will Not Stop Sounding, or Alarms Lost in a Renovation

The repair calls we expect from a neighborhood of large older houses fall into a few patterns. The first is the chirp nobody can locate: a low-battery or end-of-life warning from one alarm among ten or twelve, on a floor nobody uses, echoing down a stair so it seems to come from everywhere. The second is a hardwired, linked set that sounds all at once with no smoke anywhere, often because one alarm in the chain has failed, has been painted, or sits too close to a bathroom or a steam radiator. The third is a carbon monoxide alarm near the cellar boiler that has reached the end of its life and announces it with a pattern of beeps the family does not recognize. The fourth follows a renovation: alarms taken down when a ceiling was opened and never put back, or put back on a circuit that is now switched. In a co-op on Broadway, it may be a panel in the lobby showing trouble that the super cannot clear. Our first question is always the same: is anyone sleeping in a part of the house that has no working alarm tonight? If so, protection comes back first, even if that means a temporary alarm in a bedroom while a wiring fault is traced.

Standard smoke and CO alarms travel on the van, so a single failed or expired alarm is often replaced on the first visit, frequently within an hour or two of the technician arriving. The early Craftsman house: low attics, deep porches and wiring added later

The first Fieldston houses went up in the years just after the street plan was drawn, and the plan itself tells us that electricity arrived later than water, gas and sewers. A house from 1911 or 1912 has therefore been rewired at least once and often several times, and the alarms in it usually reflect whoever did the last job. What we look for first is the attic. Craftsman houses often have a low roof space over the bedrooms, sometimes reached only by a hatch, sometimes partly finished into a room under the slopes. That space can hold the house's oldest wiring, abandoned circuits and decades of stored belongings, and it sits directly over the bedrooms. Where a roof space is finished and used, it gets its own smoke alarm linked to the rest of the house. Where it is storage, we look at whether a heat alarm suited to an unconditioned space belongs there. A stone base and fieldstone chimneys, like those recorded at 4418 Waldo Avenue, also mean that some walls on the lower floor will not take cable easily, so the plan usually runs links through the floor framing above the cellar or uses wireless interconnect for the alarms on the masonry side of the house. Porches that were enclosed and turned into rooms get the same question as any other room: does anyone sleep there, and does anyone close the door?

The Tudor and Medieval Revival house: thick walls, steep roofs and three houses in one

The picturesque houses that the district's early guidelines encouraged are the hardest to cover with a single, simple plan. Steep roofs create tall attic spaces and bedrooms tucked under the slopes. Stone and brick walls, especially between the original house and a later wing, stop cable and weaken wireless signals. Stairs turn, landings change level, and the service side of the house often has its own stair from the kitchen up to rooms that were once for household staff. A house like 4401–4403 Waldo Avenue, built in 1913 and enlarged twice, may have bedrooms in three different parts of the building, each reached by a different route. Our plan for a house like this starts with a floor-by-floor drawing that marks every sleeping room, every stair and every wall that separates one building campaign from the next. Smoke alarms go outside each sleeping area and in or near each bedroom as the layout requires, at the top of every stair, and on every level including the cellar and any used attic room. Then we decide, wall by wall, how the links travel. Where a run has to pass through masonry, we look for an existing chase, a closet stacked over a closet, or a route through the cellar ceiling before we consider drilling. Where none exists, a wireless link with a repeater placed to carry the signal around the thick wall usually solves it without opening anything, and we test that link under the conditions it will face at night.

The Colonial and Dutch Colonial Revival house: center halls and the stair as the escape route

The Colonial and Dutch Colonial Revival houses are more regular inside. A center hall rises to a second-floor hall with bedrooms on both sides, and a gambrel or gabled roof creates a third floor or a large attic above. Their regularity makes them easier to plan, but it also concentrates risk in one place: the main stair is usually the way out for everyone upstairs, and a fire in the center hall, the living room or the dining room on the ground floor sends smoke straight up it. We put a smoke alarm at the top of that stair and in the upstairs hall outside the bedrooms, link both to the alarms below, and make sure the ground floor rooms that open onto the hall are covered at the points where smoke would reach the stair. Where the house has a back stair to the kitchen, it gets attention too, because it may be the second way out. On the third floor or in a finished attic, we look at how far the rooms are from the stair and whether the doors stay closed. For a house of this size, the 1918 one-family at 4680 Fieldston Road is a fair picture, the work is usually completed in one visit, with alarms placed so the ceiling medallions, cornices and plasterwork that define these rooms are left alone.

The Mediterranean Revival house: stucco, tile and masonry inside as well as out

Mediterranean Revival houses such as the 1928 Baum design at 4621 Waldo Avenue often carry their masonry character indoors: plaster on masonry, tiled floors, arched openings and heavy beams. Cable is hard to hide in walls built this way, and surface raceway would spoil rooms whose finishes are the reason the owners bought the house. That makes these houses strong candidates for sealed long-life battery alarms with wireless interconnect, or for a hybrid in which alarms on the frame-built upper floor are hardwired along joist bays while those on the masonry ground floor link by radio. Low tile roofs can mean shallow attic spaces that are hard to reach, so we look at what is up there through any available hatch before deciding whether a device belongs in it, and we note on the device list how it would be reached for testing. Many of these houses also have courtyard-facing rooms and terraces, so we check that a bedroom on the far side of a courtyard is not cut off from the alarms in the rest of the house by the very walls that give the building its look.

The mid-century modern house: open plans, flat roofs and few halls

CityLand notes that the district's guidelines were relaxed in the 1950s, and modern houses followed, with open living areas, flat or low roofs, large windows and fewer internal doors. The usual alarm rules still apply, but the ordinary landmarks of a plan, such as a hall outside the bedrooms or a stair at the center, may not exist. A modern house can have a living area that flows into the kitchen and dining room, bedrooms down a gallery rather than a hall, and a sloped ceiling that peaks over the main room. Smoke alarms in an open space have to be kept away from the kitchen's cooking smoke, placed correctly on a sloped ceiling, and located so the gallery to the bedrooms is covered even though there is no door between it and the living space. Flat roofs usually mean the roof space is a shallow cavity rather than an attic, so cable routes are planned along the joists from the few points where they can be reached. Large glass walls also mean fewer wall surfaces for anything, so ceiling-mounted alarms with discreet profiles are usually the only option, and we show the owner each proposed location before mounting.

The Broadway and Cayuga co-op: the building's system and the shareholder's alarms

The co-ops along the eastern edge are a different job entirely. A building like The Fieldston at 6035 Broadway, six stories with 103 apartments, a doorman and a live-in super, or the 79-apartment co-op at 4410 and 4414 Cayuga Avenue from 1930–31, may have building fire alarm equipment in its common areas, while the smoke and carbon monoxide alarms inside each apartment are a separate matter. Our first visit to a co-op is a survey: what panel is there, if any, what it watches, what condition its standby batteries are in, whether it reports to a monitoring company, and what paperwork the board holds. ASM Integrators' summary of the rules sets the boundary that decides the rest: replacing detectors, pull stations or control panels with identical parts and no design change is a repair, while moving or replacing a control panel with different equipment needs new plans filed with the FDNY. For shareholders, the work is usually in-unit alarms placed for the apartment's bedrooms and kitchen. For boards, it is usually a documented survey, a list of what the building's equipment does and does not cover, and a plan for any repair that keeps the work in the repair category where that is honestly possible. Because owners are expected to keep inspection and maintenance records for at least three years, we leave a device list, test results and a short guide to the panel's messages that the super can keep at the panel.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Walking a Large Fieldston House Before Choosing a Single Alarm

Start at the bedroom farthest from the kitchen. The technician asks which bedroom is the most remote, goes there first, closes the door, and notes every turn, stair and wall between that room and the kitchen, the cellar door and the attic. The whole plan is built backward from the person who would hear a fire last.

Find the joints between building campaigns. In a house enlarged more than once, ceiling heights change, floors step up or down, and walls thicken where an exterior wall became an interior one. Those joints decide where cable can travel and where a wireless signal may need help.

Follow the chimneys. Every chimney is traced from the cellar to the roof, with a note of which floors it passes through and which appliances vent into it. Carbon monoxide planning follows the flues, not only the boiler.

Read every alarm already on a ceiling. Each existing alarm is recorded with its date code, its type, its power source and whether it links to anything. Smoke alarms carry a manufacture date, and any past the age the maker specifies goes on the replacement list.

Ask what the family actually does at night. Who sleeps with the door shut, who uses the attic room, whether the cellar playroom becomes a guest room, where phones, bikes and tools are charged. A plan drawn for the house as it is lived in protects better than one drawn from the floor plan.

Compliance notes for Fieldston houses and buildings

  • Most houses need alarms, not a building system. Built Engineers' summary notes that for one- and two-family homes a central fire alarm system is typically not required, but smoke and carbon monoxide alarms may still be required. That describes nearly every house in the district.
  • HPD's rules for rented one- and two-family homes. For one- and two-family homes that are not owner-occupied, HPD guidance calls for approved, working detectors in each dwelling unit, including a carbon monoxide detector within 15 feet of the entrance to each sleeping room. CO alarms must have an end-of-life signal, and owners must replace detectors at the end of their useful life.
  • Apartment buildings carry extra duties. In Class A multiple dwellings, which include apartment buildings like the Broadway co-ops, a detector that fails within a year of installation because of a defect must be replaced within 30 days.
  • Filed systems start with the FDNY. Where a building needs a fire alarm system, plans go to the FDNY for review, the NYC Business portal says they must be approved by a New York State registered professional engineer or architect, the fire alarm plan involves a licensed Master Electrician and an S97 certificate holder, and the FDNY issues a Project Authorization before installation. We install, label and pre-test; we do not act as the design professional.
  • Inspection after installation. The FDNY's Fire Alarm Inspection Unit inspects a filed system once it is 100 percent complete and pre-tested.
  • Repair versus redesign. Identical replacement of detectors, pull stations or panels with no design change is a repair; moving or replacing a control panel with different equipment needs new plans filed with the FDNY.
  • Landmarks for anything outside. The LPC requires permits for alterations that affect the exterior of a house in the historic district and says ordinary repairs such as replacing broken glass, matching repaints and recaulking do not need one. The sources we read do not address interior-only fire alarm work, so our standing practice is to keep every device and cable inside and to raise anything that would show outside with LPC staff before it is installed.
  • Landscape is City Planning's. Because Fieldston is in a Special Natural Area, landscape changes need City Planning review, and the LPC does not regulate its mature trees. A cable trench to a detached garage is a landscape question as well as an alarm question.
Before Dawn on West 238th Street

What an Early-Morning House Fire Says About Planning Alarms in a House Full of Sleepers

The first floor is where fires start and nobody is. At 6 a.m. the kitchen, the living rooms and the library are empty. A fire there may grow for some time before smoke climbs the stair. In a large Fieldston house the first floor can be long, with rooms opening off a hall that runs the length of the house and a kitchen in a separate wing. Detection on the first floor has to be placed where smoke from those rooms would gather on its way to the stair, not only in the hall where the alarm was easiest to mount years ago.

The upper floors are where the warning has to arrive. An alarm that hears the fire is only useful if every sleeping person hears the alarm. Linking every alarm in the house means the one in the downstairs hall sounds in the third-floor bedroom too. In a big house with closed doors, thick walls and an air conditioner running, we test that link from the farthest bedroom and add an alarm inside a bedroom where the hall alarm is not clearly audible.

Several sleepers, several rooms. The resident in the News 12 report had to wake his roommates himself. In a family house, the same thing happens when one person is a light sleeper and the others are not, or when children sleep two floors from their parents. A linked alarm in each sleeping room means nobody's safety depends on someone else waking first.

A morning check of the plan. Our technicians do the final test with the household in their usual sleeping rooms, doors closed, and trigger an alarm on the floor where a fire is likeliest to start. If anyone cannot hear it clearly, the plan changes before we leave.

Houses Opened Up for Work

Palisade Avenue and the Weeks When a Renovation Leaves the Floors Open

Openings are paths. When ceilings are down, floorboards are lifted, walls are stripped to the studs and new chases are cut for plumbing or ductwork, the barriers that normally slow smoke and fire from one floor to the next are gone. A fire in the cellar of a house under renovation can reach the attic far faster than it could in the finished house.

Construction removes alarms exactly when the risk rises. Alarms come down when ceilings come down, dust from sanding and plaster demolition can damage the ones that stay, and a contractor's power tools, heaters and work lights add sources of ignition. If the family is living in part of the house, there needs to be a plan for the weeks in between: temporary alarms in every occupied sleeping area and on every occupied level, kept clear of the dust, linked where possible, and checked by someone each week.

The best time to run cable is while the walls are open. For owners who want hardwired, linked alarms in a house with plaster or masonry walls, a renovation is the one moment when cable can travel freely. We coordinate with the general contractor so cable and boxes are in place before walls close, and the alarms themselves go up once the dust has settled.

Vacant periods need a plan too. A house left empty between owners or during a long renovation has nobody inside to notice smoke. If it will be empty for long, an owner should decide whether detection should report to someone outside the house, and we can add partner central station monitoring at $19.99 a month if wanted.

Big houses, many floors and far wings

The original stone house and the later wing are separated by a thick masonry wall. Will wireless interconnect signals get through it?

Sometimes, but we never assume it. Wireless interconnected smoke and CO alarms talk to each other by radio, and stone, brick, plaster on metal lath and the steel in some older floors all weaken that signal. A wall that was once the exterior of a house and became an interior wall when a wing was added, as happened in houses like 4401–4403 Waldo Avenue, can be two feet of masonry. Before we rely on a wireless link across a wall like that, we place the alarms, link them and test the signal from both sides with the doors closed. If the link is weak or intermittent, there are three remedies, in the order we prefer them: a repeater or bridging alarm placed where it can see both sides, often in a doorway or stair that connects the two parts of the house; a hardwired link through a route that already exists, such as the cellar ceiling or a stacked closet; or, as a last resort, a single drilled path in the cellar. What we will not do is leave the house with a link that works on the day and fails on a damp night. The test results for each link go on the device list.

Our house has a main stair and a narrow back stair from the kitchen to the old staff rooms on the top floor. Does each stair need its own alarm?

In most houses with two stairs, yes, because each stair is both a route for smoke and a possible way out. A back stair from the kitchen is a particular concern: the kitchen is where many house fires start, and a back stair rising directly from it can carry smoke to the top floor before the main stair shows anything. We put a smoke alarm at the top of each stair and link both into the house's set, so smoke rising either way sets off every alarm. Where the back stair opens into the kitchen itself, the alarm at its foot is placed so ordinary cooking does not trigger it, which may mean a heat alarm in the kitchen and a smoke alarm a little way up the stair or on the first landing. The rooms at the top of a back stair, often former staff rooms that are now guest rooms, studies or a bedroom for an au pair or a teenager, get their own alarms and the same audibility test as the main bedrooms. We also note on the plan that the back stair is a second way out, so the family knows it exists and keeps it clear.

The 238th Street fire started on a first floor before dawn. Where does a detector have to be so a ground-floor fire is caught before the smoke reaches the bedrooms?

On the path the smoke will take, and as early on that path as the rooms allow without nuisance alarms. Smoke from a ground-floor fire rises to the ceiling of the room where it starts, spreads along it, flows out through the top of the doorway into the hall, and then climbs the stair. A smoke alarm in each main ground-floor room and in the hall outside them sees it at the first two stages; an alarm at the foot of the stair and another at the top see it at the third. In a big house we cover all three stages, because a fire in a library at the end of a long hall might take minutes to reach the stair, and those are minutes the people upstairs should have. Kitchens are handled with heat alarms or carefully placed smoke alarms nearby, because an alarm that sounds every time the toast burns gets disabled. Every one of these is linked to the alarms near the bedrooms, so the warning reaches the upper floors at the moment the first alarm senses smoke, not when the smoke itself arrives. We never claim to know what happened inside the house on West 238th Street; this is simply how we would plan any house where everyone sleeps upstairs.

Our mid-century house is one open space under a flat roof, with glass walls and very few interior doors. Where do the alarms go when there are no halls?

They go where the halls would be, in function if not in name. The code and the manufacturers' instructions place smoke alarms in the area outside sleeping rooms, in each sleeping room, and on each level. In an open-plan house, the area outside the bedrooms might be a gallery, the edge of the living room, or a corridor formed by built-in cabinets. We identify it on the plan and put the alarm there. The open living area then gets coverage of its own, placed away from the kitchen so cooking does not cause false alarms, and away from the strong air currents near sliding doors and large windows, which can push smoke away from an alarm or keep it from reaching it. Sloped and vaulted ceilings, common in modern houses, have specific placement rules near the peak that we follow from the alarm's instructions. Because there are few walls to hide wiring in and the roof is flat, the cable routes are usually limited to the joist bays above the ceiling, reached from a closet or the edge of the roof structure, and in many modern houses wireless interconnect is simply the better answer.

The finished room under our Tudor roof gets very hot on summer afternoons. Can a smoke alarm up there cope with that heat?

Most household smoke alarms are designed for the temperature range of a lived-in room, and each one lists its operating range in its instructions. A finished room under a steep roof with little insulation can, on a July afternoon, exceed what some alarms are rated for, and the same room can be cold in January if the heating up there is poor. Alarms pushed past their range can false alarm, fail early or chirp. Before choosing a device for a room like this, we look at whether the room is actually used for sleeping, how it is heated and cooled, and how hot it gets, and we pick an alarm whose rated range covers it. If someone sleeps there, it needs a smoke alarm, and the answer may include improving the room's ventilation or insulation, which is work for your contractor rather than ours. If the space is storage, a heat alarm suited to an unconditioned area may be the better choice, linked to the rest of the house. Either way, the alarm is placed on the ceiling at the distance from the peak that its instructions require, not tucked into the hottest point at the top of the slope.

Restoration, renovation and additions

We will live in the house while the second floor is stripped to the studs. What keeps us protected while the walls and floors are open?

A temporary plan, agreed with your contractor before the first wall comes down. The second floor's permanent alarms will come off with the ceilings, and the ones left on other floors are at risk from dust. We put sealed battery alarms with wireless interconnect in every room the family still sleeps in and on every level that stays occupied, linked to each other so a fire on the open floor sounds where you sleep. Alarms near dusty work are kept at a distance or protected only as the manufacturer allows, and never simply bagged and forgotten. The contractor agrees to keep the stair and a second way out clear, to unplug heaters and chargers at the end of each day, and to tell us when ceilings are about to close so permanent cable can go in first. The Palisade Avenue fire in Riverdale is the reminder: construction openings let that fire move between floors. In a house you are living in, someone should walk the open floor at the end of each workday and check that nothing is left smoldering. When the work is done, the temporary alarms come down and the permanent plan goes up as one linked set.

We are applying to Landmarks for a rear addition under the Fieldston master plan. At what stage should the fire alarm be planned?

At the design stage, before the drawings are final, for two reasons. First, the addition will change the house's sleeping rooms, stairs and routes, so the alarm plan for the whole house needs to change with it; an addition that puts a new bedroom suite beyond a masonry wall is exactly where a link can fail. Second, if any part of the fire protection would show on the outside, it should be in the drawings your architect presents rather than added afterward. The LPC's materials show its Fieldston rules covering additions, outbuildings, windows, heating, venting and air conditioning, and LPC review applies to most exterior changes to front and rear facades, but fire alarm devices are not named. In practice, almost all household fire alarm work stays inside, and we design it that way. What we need from the architect is the floor plan of the addition, the location of any new mechanical equipment and flues, and the route the electrician will use to feed the new space. With those, we can place alarms, plan the links to the original house and run cable while the addition's walls are still open.

Special rooms and old heating systems

Our library holds rare books and the walls are hung with paintings. Can fire detection protect those rooms without any risk of water?

Yes. Smoke and heat alarms detect and warn; they do not release water, chemicals or anything else into the room. A household alarm system protects a collection by giving the earliest possible warning so that people can act, and, if it is monitored, so that the fire department is called even if nobody is home. Sprinklers are a different system installed by licensed sprinkler contractors, and whether a collection room should have them, or some other suppression method, is a decision for the owner, the insurer and a fire protection designer. For the alarms themselves, we place smoke detection in the library so it sees smoke early, which in a room with tall bookcases and a high or coffered ceiling means following the placement rules carefully. We keep devices off the ornamental surfaces where possible and mount them so their removal later leaves the smallest mark. If the collection matters to your insurer, ask whether they want the house's detection to be monitored and what documentation they expect, and we can arrange partner central station monitoring at $19.99 a month.

The steam radiators in our house hiss and vent vapor in winter. Can that set off a smoke alarm mounted above or near them?

It can. Many smoke alarms cannot distinguish dense water vapor from smoke, and a radiator air vent that spits steam as the system heats up, or a radiator with a failing valve, can put enough moisture in the air nearby to trigger an alarm placed directly above it or in a small room where it sits. The heat rising off a radiator can also carry dust onto an alarm's sensor over time. When we plan a house with steam heat, we note every radiator and keep alarms out of the direct column of air rising from them, while still meeting the placement rules for the room. If a radiator vent is spitting water, that is a job for your heating contractor, and fixing it helps both the heating and the alarms. If an existing alarm has been false alarming every winter morning when the heat comes up, moving it a few feet, within the spacing the manufacturer allows, often solves the problem without reducing protection. What we will not do is remove an alarm from a sleeping area because of steam.

Our boiler flue and two fireplace flues share one old chimney stack. Which floors need carbon monoxide alarms because of it?

Every floor with sleeping rooms needs CO alarms anyway, and a shared chimney is a reason to place them with that chimney in mind. In older houses the boiler often vents into a chimney that also served fireplaces, and over a century that masonry can crack, liners can fail or be missing, and a blockage at the top can push combustion gases back into the house. Carbon monoxide that escapes from a damaged flue may enter the house on any floor the chimney passes through, not only in the cellar. So we place a CO alarm near the boiler, CO alarms on every level with sleeping rooms within the distance of the bedroom doors that HPD guidance describes for rented homes, and we make sure a bedroom that shares a wall with the chimney is covered. We also recommend that the owner have the chimney and flue inspected by a qualified chimney or heating professional, especially if the house was converted from one fuel to another. A CO alarm warns of the problem; it does not fix it, and an alarm that keeps sounding near a chimney is a reason to call that professional and get out of the house.

Our au pair sleeps in a former maid's room off the back stair, far from our bedrooms. What does that room need, and how will she know when an alarm elsewhere is sounding?

It needs what any sleeping room needs: a smoke alarm inside the room, a smoke alarm in the area outside it, carbon monoxide protection as close to the entrance as the guidance for sleeping rooms calls for, and a link to every other alarm in the house. Rooms like this were often built at the back of the top floor, over the kitchen, reached by the back stair and separated from the family's bedrooms by a door and sometimes a heavier wall. That is why linking matters more there than anywhere: if the fire is in the kitchen below her, she needs to hear the alarm first, and if it is in the main part of the house, the alarm near her room must sound too. We test audibility from her room with the door shut. We also make sure she knows both ways down, the back stair and the main stair, and what each alarm sound means, since someone new to a house will not know that a single chirp means a battery and a loud pattern means get out. A short written note with the escape routes and alarm sounds, left in the room, helps anyone who stays there.

Co-ops on the eastern edge

A shareholder's new smoke alarm failed after eight months. Does our co-op have to replace it, and how quickly?

If your building is a Class A multiple dwelling, which an apartment building like the co-ops on Broadway and Cayuga Avenue is, HPD's notice on smoke detecting devices says a detector that fails within one year of installation because of a defect must be replaced within 30 days. Who is responsible for the replacement depends on who installed it and on your proprietary lease and house rules, which the board and its counsel interpret; if the corporation supplied the detector, it is generally the owner's obligation. What we can do is make the question easy to answer. When we install apartment alarms for a co-op, we record each one's location, model, manufacture date and installation date on a list the managing agent keeps. If one fails early, that list shows when it went in and whether it falls within the one-year window. We replace a failed alarm promptly, note the cause if we can tell it, such as a defect, paint or damage, and add the replacement to the list. For alarms a shareholder bought and installed, the manufacturer's warranty is usually the first place to turn.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Fire Alarm Installation in Fieldston

We could not capture Google's AI Overview for this topic in our build environment, so nothing below quotes it. These are claims that appear often in generic online answers and national cost guides, paraphrased, each followed by what Fieldston's houses, its co-ops and the public rules actually show.

"One smoke alarm on each floor protects a house"

Short answers about home fire safety often reduce the rule to an alarm per level, as if square footage and layout did not matter.

The placement rules call for alarms in each sleeping room and in the area outside sleeping rooms as well as on every level, and a large Fieldston house can have bedrooms in two or three separate parts of the same floor, reached by different stairs and divided by masonry.

One alarm in a long second-floor hall cannot be relied on to wake someone behind a closed door at the far end of a wing. In a house this size, the count follows the bedrooms and the routes, and every alarm is linked.

"Wireless interconnected alarms work anywhere in a home"

Product descriptions and summaries tend to present wireless linking as a universal solution that removes every wiring question.

Radio links lose strength through stone, brick, plaster on metal lath and heavy floors. A house built in 1913 and enlarged in 1919 and 1927 can have exactly those walls between its parts.

Wireless interconnect is often the right answer here, but only after each link is tested in place. Where a link is weak, a repeater or a short cable route makes it reliable.

"Old stone and brick houses are less likely to burn"

It is common to read that solidly built older houses are inherently fire-resistant because of their masonry.

The walls of a revival house may be masonry, but its floors, roof framing, stairs, trim, paneling and contents are mostly combustible. The Palisade Avenue fire in Riverdale reached the basement, both main floors and the attic once construction openings gave it paths.

Masonry can even work against early warning by containing smoke in one part of a house. Linking alarms across those walls is what lets a fire behind them be heard.

"You can expect to pay about the national average for fire alarm installation"

National cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish averages drawn from many places and house types, and AI summaries tend to repeat them as if they fit any address.

An average cannot account for a three-floor house with two stairs, an addition beyond a masonry wall, a shared chimney and a finished attic, or for the care needed around original plaster and woodwork.

Our standard Bronx list starts at $600 for a basic residential system, sets complete home protection at $2,500, and runs $2,000 to $6,000 for a custom hardwired system, with the written price set after a walk-through.

"Carbon monoxide only comes from gas stoves and kitchens"

Many answers frame CO as a kitchen issue tied to gas ranges.

In Fieldston's older houses the main fuel-burning appliance is usually a boiler or furnace in the cellar, often venting into a chimney built a century ago that may also carry fireplace flues and pass through every floor.

CO alarms belong near the boiler and on every level with sleeping rooms, and a cracked or blocked flue can leak gas on a floor far from the cellar, which is why we trace the chimneys.

"Apartments in buildings with a doorman and a building fire alarm don't need their own alarms"

Some summaries suggest that a building's staff and central equipment make in-unit alarms unnecessary.

Smoke and carbon monoxide alarms are required in almost all residential occupancies whether or not a building has a central system, and HPD rules for Class A multiple dwellings set duties for in-unit detectors.

In a co-op like 6035 Broadway, a doorman and a live-in super help in an emergency, but the alarm that wakes a shareholder at night is the one inside the apartment.

"Hardwired alarms are always better than battery ones"

General advice often ranks hardwired alarms above battery alarms without qualification.

Hardwired alarms are excellent where a sound circuit is available and cable can travel without damaging the house. In a Mediterranean Revival with plaster on masonry or an open mid-century house with few walls, running cable can mean surface raceway or destructive openings.

Sealed long-life battery alarms with tested wireless interconnect can give a house like that full, linked protection. The right choice depends on the walls, the wiring and whether a renovation is opening them anyway.

FAQ

Fieldston Fire Alarm Installation FAQ

Can new alarms go in the same spots as the old ones so we do not end up with extra holes in the ceilings?
+

Often, yes, where the old spots meet the placement rules. We check each one first. Older alarms were frequently mounted too close to a wall or corner, right outside a bathroom door, or in a hall that no longer leads to the bedrooms after a renovation. Where an old location is sound, the new alarm uses it and its mounting plate covers the old marks. Where it is not, we explain why and patch the small opening left behind.

How do you test smoke alarms without filling the house with smoke?
+

We use the alarm's own test function to confirm its sounder and its links, and where the manufacturer allows, a listed aerosol tester that checks the smoke sensor without producing real smoke. Each alarm is triggered in turn while someone listens from the farthest bedroom, so we prove both that the alarm senses and that every linked alarm in the house responds. Heat and CO alarms are tested according to their makers' instructions.

Do you take on fire alarm work for the schools and the university around Fieldston?
+

Campus systems at schools and universities are designed by fire protection engineers and managed by the institutions' own facilities teams, usually through formal bidding. We can respond if invited to bid on installation work, but this page is written for the houses and co-ops of Fieldston, and we make no claims about the systems at Horace Mann, Ethical Culture Fieldston School or Manhattan University.

Will the technician walk the second way out of each upper floor with us?
+

Yes, if you would like. On the walk-through we note the main and any secondary stair, and we point out where a second route from an upper bedroom depends on a window or a back stair that is blocked by storage or furniture. We do not design escape plans or sell escape equipment, but knowing the routes helps us place alarms so the warning reaches each bedroom before smoke reaches its way out.

Does a walk-in closet or a dressing room off the main bedroom need its own alarm?
+

Usually not, as long as the bedroom it opens from and the area outside it are covered. A closet or dressing room is not a sleeping room. The exception is a large dressing room with its own lighting, appliances or a separate door to a hall, which can behave like a room of its own. We look at how it is used and wired and include an alarm there if it makes sense for that house.

I see clients in an office inside my house. Does that make the fire alarm a commercial job?
+

In most cases a small home office stays part of a residential house, and we plan its detection as part of the house's linked alarms. If an office has a separate entrance, regular visitors and space that has been formally converted, its occupancy could be treated differently, and that is a question for your architect or a registered design professional. We tell you if anything we see suggests you should ask.

Is a plug-in carbon monoxide alarm good enough, or should CO alarms be mounted?
+

A plug-in CO alarm with battery backup can work if it is installed where its instructions say and is never unplugged for a vacuum or a lamp. The trouble in practice is that outlets are rarely in the best spot near a bedroom door, and plug-in units tend to get moved. In a whole-house plan we usually prefer mounted or combination alarms that stay put and link to the rest of the house.
