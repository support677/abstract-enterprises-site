The Neighborhood

Why Crown Heights Properties Need Alarm Installation

Ask ten people where Crown Heights starts and you will get several answers, so here is the one we use, taken from Wikipedia’s article on the neighborhood: Washington Avenue on the west, Atlantic Avenue on the north, Ralph Avenue on the east and Empire Boulevard on the south. That is about 1.4 square miles. Prospect Heights lies to the west, Bedford–Stuyvesant to the north, Brownsville to the east, and Flatbush, Prospect Lefferts Gardens and East Flatbush to the south. The neighborhood was once known as Crow Hill; a Brownstoner history of Franklin Avenue notes the Crown Heights name was in use by 1907.

Eastern Parkway is the spine. Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux conceived it in 1866, and NYC Parks says it was built between 1870 and 1874, running 2.2 miles from Grand Army Plaza to Ralph Avenue with four rows of trees and a central carriage drive between pedestrian malls. For an alarm installer the parkway matters for a plainer reason: it is the line between two police precincts. NYPD’s own pages put the 77th Precinct at 127 Utica Avenue, serving northern Crown Heights and part of Prospect Heights, and the 71st Precinct at 421 Empire Boulevard, serving southern Crown Heights, Wingate and Prospect Lefferts. A monitoring station that dispatches police does it by address, so a correct address on the account matters more here than in most places; we check it on the day of installation.

The housing is what makes the work interesting. The Crown Heights North Historic District, as described on Wikipedia, covers 1,019 contributing buildings put up between about 1853 and 1942: rowhouses, townhouses, two-family and semi-attached houses, freestanding houses, flats, apartment buildings, churches and commercial buildings, in styles from Greek Revival and Italianate through Romanesque Revival and Beaux-Arts. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the district in phases, in 2007, 2011 and 2015, and Brownstoner reported 472 buildings in the first phase, centered on Dean Street between Bedford and Kingston Avenues. A separate, tiny Park Place Historic District, 13 row houses at 651 to 675 Park Place between Franklin and Bedford, was designated in 2012; Brownstoner notes that at least four of those single-family houses had become multiple dwellings by the end of the 1950s.

That history of conversion is something we see inside the houses. Many Crown Heights row houses were cut into apartments decades ago, and some have since been put back together by owners who want the whole house again. Either way, the house tends to have more doors than a family needs, old apartment entrances on the landings, a stair hall that was once common space, and a garden floor with its own street door under or beside the stoop. A good alarm plan starts by counting those doors, not by counting rooms.

Then there are the apartment houses. Brick buildings of five and six stories line the avenues and side streets, many with fire escapes on the front or rear and windows onto narrow air shafts. The burglary pattern that police described in this part of Brooklyn is plain enough. In November 2013, DNAinfo reported 49 burglaries in the 77th Precinct between October 6 and November 10, up from 33 a year earlier. Captain Eddie Lott put it down to residents leaving fire-escape windows open in unseasonably warm weather, and said a burglar who pries at ten windows only needs one to give. He added that much of the command goes to work from 7 a.m. to 7 p.m., which leaves apartments empty through the day. In July 2016, the same outlet reported two women arrested after an officer saw them on the fire escape of a large brick apartment building on New York Avenue, using a hammer on a window around one in the afternoon.

Basements and shops round out the picture. A Brooklyn Paper item, which gives May 26 as the date but no year, described a thief near the Brooklyn Museum who tried a building’s front door around 12:46 p.m., failed, then forced the basement door and took a $450 bike stored there. And in late November 2023, News 12 reported two businesses burglarized on consecutive days, one on Franklin Avenue and one on Lincoln Road, with $900 and $8,650 in cash taken. None of this makes Crown Heights unusual for Brooklyn. It does tell you where to put sensors: on the windows people open, on the doors nobody watches, and around the cash.

Six facts about Crown Heights that change an alarm plan

  • Two precincts, split at the parkway. The 77th covers the north side, the 71st the south (NYPD). The account address and cross street must be exact.
  • Historic district rules apply to the outside. More than a thousand buildings sit inside the Crown Heights North district phases or the Park Place district, where exterior work falls under the Landmarks Preservation Commission.
  • Lots of converted houses. Row houses split into flats leave extra doors, landing entrances and separate bells that need a decision, one by one.
  • Fire-escape windows were the entry point police named. The 2013 spike in the 77th was tied to open fire-escape windows in warm weather (DNAinfo).
  • Daytime is the risky time. The same report linked burglaries to apartments left empty from 7 a.m. to 7 p.m.
  • Commercial streets are mixed-use. Franklin, Nostrand, Kingston and Utica Avenues, plus Atlantic Avenue on the edge, are the commercial streets Wikipedia lists; most shops have apartments above them.

Crown Heights Alarm Repair the Same Day: Beeping Keypads, Zones That Will Not Clear, Panels That Went Quiet

Not every call is a new system. A lot of Crown Heights houses already have an alarm, put in by a previous owner or by a company that has since disappeared, and a lot of apartments inherited a keypad from the last tenant. The calls we get most are simple: the keypad chirps every minute because a sensor battery is dying; one window zone shows open when the window is shut, usually because a wired contact has loosened in an old frame or a magnet was painted over; or the panel has stopped reporting, often because its communicator used a phone line or cellular network that no longer exists.

A technician can usually find the fault within the first hour. Batteries and failed contacts are replaced on the spot. Wired contacts in century-old window frames are tested at the panel first, so we know whether the problem is the device or the run of wire behind the plaster. A panel that cannot report is fitted with a new cellular communicator if the rest of the system tests sound. A siren that sounds and will not stop is silenced and then traced: New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building burglar alarm’s audible signal to shut off automatically within fifteen minutes, and an old panel that keeps ringing past that is set back inside the limit.

If you do not have the installer code, we can still help on most common panels, though a full reset sometimes means reprogramming every zone. Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Crown Heights’ Architecture

The building decides where the wire can go, which openings matter and what can be mounted where people will see it. These are the six types we meet most often between Washington and Ralph Avenues.

Limestone row houses

Rows such as the seven houses at 1187 to 1199 Bergen Street, designed around 1894 by Albert E. White with rough-faced limestone fronts under a continuous red tile roof (Brownstoner). Deep masonry walls, tall parlor windows, and a top floor that shares a roofline with the neighbors.

Brownstone and brick row houses

Three- and four-story houses with a stoop to the parlor floor and a garden-floor door below. Interior plaster, original woodwork and, often, an old alarm’s wired contacts already hidden in the window frames.

Row houses that were split into flats

Single-family houses later divided into apartments, as Brownstoner records for several Park Place houses by the 1950s. Extra doors on landings, separate bells, sometimes a second stair, and in many cases a rental floor still occupied.

Freestanding and semi-attached houses

Fewer in number, but the historic district counts them. Windows on three or four sides, side yards and driveways, and doors a row house never has, so the perimeter list is longer.

Prewar apartment buildings

Brick walk-ups and elevator buildings of five or six stories, with fire escapes, air shafts, a lobby the tenants do not control and a basement shared by everyone. The unit door is the main perimeter; the windows on the fire escape and shaft are next.

Shops with apartments above

The commercial-residential buildings Brownstoner dates from the late 1880s to the early 1930s on Franklin Avenue, and their cousins on Nostrand, Kingston and Utica. A storefront below, a residential entrance beside it, and a back yard or cellar shared between them.

How the wiring changes by building type

Limestone and brownstone fronts. Nothing goes on the face of the stone if we can help it. Contacts sit inside the frames; any outdoor device is placed at the rear or under a cornice where it does not read from the street, and cable comes inside the house as soon as it can. When a run has to cross masonry, it passes through a mortar joint rather than the face of a stone, because a joint can be repointed and a cracked stone cannot be put back.

Brownstone interiors with original plaster. Where an old system left wire inside the walls, we test and reuse it. Where it did not, wireless devices take the upper floors and wired zones take the cellar and garden floor, where joists are open from below. Keypads go on plain wall surfaces, never on carved trim or a door casing.

Converted houses. Before mounting anything we walk the house with the owner and mark each extra door: in use, locked out, or a night boundary. Locked-out doors still get a contact, because an unused door is exactly the one nobody notices is open.

Freestanding houses. More windows at ground level means more glass-break coverage, and driveways and side yards are watched from inside by detectors aimed away from the street so passing cars and pedestrians do not trip them.

Apartment buildings. Everything inside the unit is wireless and removable unless the owner of the building is the client. A cellular communicator does the reporting, because tenants change internet providers and building wiring belongs to the landlord.

Mixed-use buildings. The shop and the apartments get separate systems unless one owner runs both. Where one owner does, the panel is partitioned so the store can be armed at closing while the family upstairs comes and goes.

Row Houses

Alarming a Historic Row House Without Changing How It Looks

Owners in the Crown Heights North district phases and on the Park Place block often worry that an alarm means a permit fight. In our experience the alarm itself rarely touches anything the Landmarks Preservation Commission regulates, because nearly all of it lives indoors. What the Commission does care about is exterior equipment. Its permit guidebook chapter on health and safety work, which we read for these pages, says equipment should not go on or in front of a primary facade unless there is no other option, should be placed discreetly, should not be drilled through cast iron or decorative surfaces, and should run concealed through plain masonry, with only short visible lengths treated as staff-level work. We plan to stay inside that guidance by default, which in practice means three habits.

First, no siren box on the front. An interior siren in the stair hall is loud enough to drive out an intruder and wake a household. If an owner wants an outdoor sounder, it goes at the rear, where the garden or yard side of the house is less formal and usually less visible. Second, nothing is screwed into the face of the limestone or brownstone. Third, cable from the cellar to the upper floors travels inside, through closets, chases and the stair wall, so nothing appears on the front of the house at all.

Inside, the questions are about floors and people. A typical four-story row house has a garden floor with a street door and a yard door, a parlor floor with tall front windows and often a rear extension, and two bedroom floors above. The garden floor is the one to watch: two doors, low windows, and in many houses a yard that backs onto other yards. We put contacts on both garden doors and on every garden window, a motion detector in the garden-floor hall, contacts on the parlor windows at the back, and glass-break detection in rooms where the windows are too large or too old to take a contact cleanly.

Bedroom floors are protected at the stair rather than room by room. At night the household arms the lower floors and leaves the bedrooms off, so a child walking to the bathroom does not set anything off, while a door or window opening below does. Where a house still has its original pocket doors between front and back parlors, those can mark the night boundary too: a contact on the pocket door lets the front parlor stay armed while the back parlor is in use.

Roofs are the last item. Rows like the one on Bergen Street share a continuous roofline, so the hatch or skylight at the top of the stair is reachable from the neighbors’ roofs. It gets a contact on the hatch and, where a skylight lights the stair, a glass-break detector that can hear it.

Apartment Buildings

Fire Escapes, Air Shafts and Window Air Conditioners in Crown Heights Apartment Buildings

Inside a prewar apartment house the alarm is the tenant’s or the unit owner’s, not the building’s, and it has to work around three things the household does not control: the lobby door, the fire escape and the basement. The front door of the unit is the main perimeter, and its contact is the one that should never fail, which is why we use a contact rated for heavy daily use rather than the thinnest one on the market.

The fire escape is next. FDNY guidance on apartment fire safety allows only approved window gates that open without a key, so a fire-escape window cannot simply be locked shut. That is exactly why it was the route police described in 2013, and it is why it gets the most attention. We fit a contact to the sash so the alarm knows when it opens, a glass-break detector in the room so it knows if the pane is smashed without the window ever moving, and, if there is an approved gate, a contact on the gate as well. None of it slows the window down as an exit. Because the window has to be usable in a fire, we set it to sound immediately with no entry countdown: nobody comes home through the fire escape.

Air shafts are less obvious. Bathroom and kitchen windows in many prewar layouts open onto a narrow shaft shared with the next building. Most cannot be reached from the ground, but some sit beside a neighbor’s window, a ledge or a pipe, and the bottom-floor shaft windows are often reachable from a back yard or areaway. We look out of each one before deciding. If nothing outside can bear a person’s weight within reach, a contact is enough. If something can, glass-break is added.

Then there are window air conditioners. Through most of a Crown Heights summer, many windows have an air conditioner sitting in them, held by the lower sash. A contact on that sash cannot do its job while the unit is in place, because the sash is already open a few inches. The answer is to protect the room rather than the sash: a glass-break detector for the pane above the unit and a motion detector or curtain detector that covers the floor beneath the window, so anyone pushing the unit in and climbing through is seen. In the autumn, when the units come out, the sash contact goes back on duty.

The basement is the last part of the building. Bikes, strollers and storage live down there, often in cages, and the Brooklyn Paper item about the bike taken from a basement near the Brooklyn Museum is the kind of theft we hear about most from apartment tenants. A tenant’s alarm can cover a storage cage with a wireless contact and a small detector, provided the landlord agrees and the signal reaches; we test the signal from the cage to the panel upstairs before promising anything, and if it does not reach we say so.

A one-bedroom on the fourth floor, opening by opening

  • Unit door: heavy-duty contact, keypad inside the door, entry countdown long enough to get through the door with groceries.
  • Living-room window on the fire escape: sash contact, glass-break detector, gate contact if a gate is fitted, no countdown.
  • Bedroom window on the fire escape: same as the living room, plus the option to leave it off at night while the rest stays armed.
  • Kitchen window on the air shaft: contact; glass-break only if a ledge or neighbor’s window is within reach.
  • Street-facing windows: usually nothing, unless a cornice, sign or scaffold gives access.
  • Window air conditioner: glass-break above, motion or curtain detector below, through the cooling season.
  • Basement storage cage: wireless contact on the cage door if the landlord agrees and the signal tests good.

Daytime Arming

Empty From Seven to Seven: Arming Habits That Match How Crown Heights Households Live

When the 77th Precinct’s captain explained the 2013 burglary spike, he made two points: open windows, and empty apartments during the working day. Police then shifted patrol hours toward the evening, DNAinfo reported, to cover the time people come home. The alarm side of that lesson is simple. A system that is only armed at night protects you from the less likely burglary. The point of the panel is that it is armed when the place is empty, and in many Crown Heights homes that means from breakfast to dinner.

The hard part is habit, not hardware, so we set systems up to make arming automatic. Every person in the household gets a phone login and the panel can remind the last person out if it is still disarmed when everyone’s phones have left. A key fob on the key ring arms the system on the way down the stairs. For households that leave at the same time every weekday, a schedule can arm the system automatically at a fixed time if nobody has disarmed it, and cancel itself on holidays. A missed arm becomes a push notification instead of an unprotected day.

Night arming is the second habit. In a row house the lower floors are armed at bedtime and the bedroom floors are left off. In an apartment, every window that opens onto the fire escape is on, except the one by the bed if the household wants it open for air, and the unit door is on. The motion detectors are bypassed so the household can move around. Both settings get one button on the keypad and one on the app, so nobody has to remember a sequence.

The third habit is telling the alarm who else comes in. Dog walkers, cleaners, a nanny, a relative who checks on the plants: each gets a personal code, limited to the days and hours they actually come, and the app shows each arrival by name. When the arrangement ends, the code is deleted from the phone in seconds. That is better than a spare key under a planter and much better than a shared master code.

Finally, the alarm should know what “home” means in summer. When windows are open for air, the sensors on those windows are bypassed for the evening, and the panel says so out loud when you arm it, so you never leave the house thinking a bypassed window is protected.

Before the Visit

Three Things a Crown Heights Household or Shop Can Sort Out Before We Arrive

Walk the back of the building

Open every rear window, every window onto a fire escape or shaft, and the door to the yard. Look at what is right outside each one: an extension roof, a ledge, a neighbor’s fire escape, a fence, a garbage enclosure. Those are the openings the plan will be built around, and if you know them before we arrive, the visit is faster and the price is firmer.

List everyone who needs to get in

Write down who lives there, who visits on a schedule, and who in a building or business needs access at odd hours. For a rental floor or a store, include the tenant or the employees. Each name becomes a code with its own hours, and knowing the list in advance means programming is done on the day of installation, not afterwards over the phone.

Find the paperwork

If there is an old alarm, look for the installer’s name on the panel door or a sticker in the window, and any manual or code sheet. A co-op or condo apartment may need the managing agent’s approval or a certificate of insurance, and a rented store may have lease terms about alterations. Ten minutes with those papers can save an hour of guesswork on the day.

Common Fixes

Four Alarm Layouts We Often Correct in Central Brooklyn Row Houses and Walk-Ups

1. The front door protected, the garden floor forgotten

An older system often has contacts on the stoop door and parlor windows and nothing at all on the garden floor, which is where the yard door and the low windows are. We add them first; it is usually the cheapest improvement in the whole house.

2. A fire-escape window with no glass-break

A contact on the sash catches a window being opened. It does not catch a pane being smashed and someone climbing through the frame, which is what the hammer on New York Avenue was for. Glass-break detection in the room closes that gap.

3. A converted house treated like a one-family

When an old apartment door on a landing has no contact because “nobody uses it,” the house has a hole in it. Every door to the outside or to a former separate unit is either on the alarm or permanently secured; usually both.

4. A shop alarm that nobody knows was armed

A store with one shared code and no closing report cannot tell the owner whether the last employee armed it. Personal codes and an alert when the store is still disarmed at closing time fix that for the price of an afternoon of programming.

What a Crown Heights store or restaurant alarm usually includes

  • Gate and door contacts. A heavy-duty contact at the foot of the rolling gate and on the shop door behind it, so the alarm knows which one moved.

  • Cash. A hold-up button where money is counted, a contact or vibration sensor on the safe, and a motion detector over the office where the day’s takings wait for the bank.
  • People. Personal codes for each manager and employee, opening and closing reports, and an alert if the store is still disarmed half an hour after closing.
  • Reporting. A cellular communicator so the system still reports if the shop’s internet is cut, with internet as a second path.

Rules that shape every Crown Heights alarm

  • Fifteen-minute cutoff. Under § 24-238 of the City’s Administrative Code, an audible building burglar alarm has to stop by itself no later than fifteen minutes after it starts. Most storefronts here have apartments overhead, so we program a shorter run.
  • Licensed installers. New York’s General Business Law Article 6-D requires businesses that install security or fire alarms to be licensed by the Department of State; ask any installer for the license number before work starts. Ours is #12000287431.
  • Fire-escape windows stay usable. FDNY guidance allows only approved window gates that open without a key; no alarm device we fit ever blocks one.
  • Landmarked exteriors. Inside the historic districts, exterior equipment is LPC territory. Our default is to keep everything off the facade.
  • Smoke and carbon monoxide. Detector rules for apartments and rental units come from HPD and the fire code; if a burglar alarm also monitors smoke or CO, it supplements the required detectors rather than replacing them.

A medical, dental or accounting office on the garden or parlor floor of a Crown Heights row house is a small business and a home at the same time. Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Crown Heights: Questions Answered

These are the questions that come up in Crown Heights more than anywhere else we work. Shorter general questions are in the FAQ near the bottom of the page.

Row houses in and around the historic districts

Our row house still has the separate apartment doors and doorbells from when it was divided into flats. Now that one family lives in the whole house, should those inner doors be on the alarm?

Yes, most of them, but not in the way people expect. An interior apartment door on a landing is not a way into the house from outside, so it does not need a contact for burglary on its own account. What it can do is divide the house. If a former third-floor apartment door now opens onto a bedroom floor, a contact on it lets you arm everything below that door at night and leave the bedrooms free. The doors that do need perimeter sensors are the leftovers that open outside: an old second street entrance under the stoop, a service door to the yard, a door from a former rear apartment onto an extension roof. Those are the ones that stay shut for years and are never checked. Old doorbells and intercom buttons for vanished apartments are worth disconnecting at the same time, because a caller ringing an empty “apartment 3” and getting no answer is a useful test of whether anyone is home.

There is an old dumbwaiter shaft running from the cellar kitchen up to the parlor floor. Could anyone use it to get in, and should it be on the alarm?

A disused dumbwaiter is a hole between floors, so it deserves a look even though nobody climbs up a dumbwaiter shaft for fun. The question is where the bottom of the shaft opens. If it starts in a cellar room that is itself on the alarm, with its door and windows covered, the shaft adds nothing new: anyone at the bottom has already tripped a sensor. If the bottom sits in a part of the cellar the system does not cover, or the old service door beside it opens to the areaway, then the hatch at each floor gets a small contact, and the cellar door gets one too. Where the doors on the shaft have been nailed or painted shut for decades, a contact on the one at the parlor level is usually enough, because that is where a person would have to come out. We do not wire through the shaft itself; old shafts often hold the remains of pulleys and ropes that should be left alone.

The house still has its original pocket doors between the front and back parlors. Can the alarm use them to split the floor at night?

It can, and it is one of the neater tricks in an old row house. A small concealed contact is fitted at the top of the pocket door’s opening where it meets the jamb, so it is invisible when the doors are open. With the doors closed, the front parlor and its tall street windows can stay armed while the family uses the back parlor and the kitchen. The motion detector in the front parlor is then assigned to the same night setting. If the pocket doors are rarely closed, the same result comes from aiming the front parlor’s motion detector so it watches the front windows and not the passage to the back.

Prewar apartment buildings

We rent the top-floor apartment, and the door to the roof is at the end of our hallway. Does the roof matter for a unit like ours?

More than for anyone else in the building. On a row of attached prewar buildings the roofs often meet at the same height, so a person who gets onto one roof can reach the windows and skylights of every top-floor apartment along the row, and the windows facing the roof or a rear setback become ground-floor windows in all but name. We check what your windows look onto from the roof side: a parapet, a bulkhead, a neighbor’s roof at the same level. Those windows get contacts and a glass-break detector, and a skylight over a stair or bathroom gets glass-break coverage too. The roof door itself belongs to the landlord and has to stay usable as an exit, so we do not alarm it from a tenant’s system; if it is propped open or its alarm bar is broken, that is a repair request to the managing agent, and worth putting in writing.

How long should the entry countdown be at our apartment door, and should the fire-escape window have any countdown at all?

The countdown at the unit door should be just long enough for the slowest person in the household to unlock, step in and reach the keypad or the phone, with shopping bags. In most apartments that is around thirty seconds. Longer countdowns give an intruder who comes through the door more time to find and damage the keypad, and modern panels with a cellular communicator report a tamper during the countdown anyway. The fire-escape window should have no countdown at all. Nobody in the household comes home that way, so any opening there is treated as an alarm the instant it happens. The same goes for air-shaft windows and a roof hatch.

A burglar was seen on a New York Avenue fire escape swinging a hammer at a window. If the glass breaks but nobody climbs in, does the alarm still go off?

Only if the room has a glass-break detector. A sash contact reports the window moving, not the glass, so a pane smashed out of a closed window leaves the contact quiet until someone tries to open the sash or climbs through the frame. In a room with a glass-break detector, the sound of the pane breaking trips the alarm straight away, and an intruder standing on the fire escape hears a siren before ever setting foot inside. In our experience that is when most give up. On fire-escape rooms, we treat glass-break as standard, not an upgrade.

Our bathroom window opens onto a narrow air shaft shared with the next building. Can anyone actually come in that way?

It depends on what is in the shaft. We look out of the window and up and down before deciding. If the shaft walls are sheer brick, the window is several floors above the bottom, and the next window is out of reach, the risk is low and a contact covers it cheaply. If there is a ledge, a pipe, a neighbor’s window within arm’s reach, or a fire escape that crosses the shaft, a person could step across, and glass-break detection is added. Windows at the bottom of a shaft are the ones most often reachable, because the shaft floor may connect to a yard or areaway. A frosted or wired-glass bathroom window still breaks, so it gets the same treatment as any other.

Our windows hold air conditioners all summer. How is a window with an AC unit in it protected?

Not by the sash contact, because the lower sash is already open around the unit. During the cooling season the room itself becomes the sensor. A glass-break detector covers the upper pane, and a motion detector or a narrow curtain detector watches the floor in front of the window, so somebody pushing the unit in and climbing through is caught within a step. The sash contact stays mounted and comes back into use when the unit is removed in the autumn. We also suggest the brackets and side panels that hold a unit firmly in the frame; a unit that cannot be pushed in from outside without real noise and effort is part of the protection.

Shops and restaurants on the avenues

Our Franklin Avenue bar has a back garden that customers use until closing. How is a yard like that alarmed once the last table is cleared?

The garden itself is usually left out of the burglar alarm, because outdoor motion detection in a yard full of cats, leaves and neighbors’ lights is a recipe for false alarms. Instead, the alarm draws its line at the building: a contact on the door to the garden, glass-break for any windows onto it, and a motion detector inside the back room, aimed away from the garden door so it does not see through the glass. Furniture and heaters left outside are better covered by a camera than by an alarm sensor. If the garden has a back gate to a lane or a neighbor’s yard, a contact on the gate can be added on its own zone and set to report only after closing, so staff carrying out bins do not trigger it.

Our takeout place stays open past midnight and opens again at seven. Is there any point arming for such a short closed stretch?

Yes, because those few hours are when the shop is most clearly empty. A store that closes at one and opens at seven has five or six dark hours with the gate down and cash, equipment and stock inside, and that is the window most commercial break-ins happen in. Arming takes seconds: the closing employee enters a code or taps the app, the panel reports who armed it, and a reminder goes to the owner if nobody has armed by a set time. The morning employee disarms with their own code. Both events are logged, which also settles any argument about who opened and closed.

Burglars hit a Franklin Avenue business and one on Lincoln Road on consecutive days in 2023. When businesses near us get broken into, should we change anything about our alarm that week?

A few small things, none of them expensive. Check that the system really is armed every night by looking at the closing reports in the app. Walk the back of the shop and test each sensor with the keypad in test mode, especially the rear door and any roof hatch. Make sure the call list at the monitoring station is current, so the person who answers at 3 a.m. can actually come. If your alarm is self-monitored, consider a short-term professional monitoring plan for a month or two; a plan without a long-term contract can be dropped later. And if the cash usually waits overnight on the premises, that is the week to bank it the same day.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Crown Heights

We could not open Google’s AI Overview, People Also Ask or similar live search panels from the environment this page was researched in, so we are not quoting them. What follows are claims that show up again and again in generic online answers and in sales pitches from national companies and lead-generation sites such as Angi and HomeAdvisor, set against what we see in Crown Heights buildings.

“Cover the doors first; burglars use doors.”

Doors matter, and the unit door or street door is always on the system. But the burglary spike the 77th Precinct described in 2013 came through fire-escape windows, and the 2016 arrest on New York Avenue was of two women on a fire escape with a hammer. In prewar apartment buildings, the window onto the fire escape is at least as important as the door.

A plan that spends its sensor budget on the front of the apartment and leaves the fire-escape room with a single contact is a plan built for a different building type. We start at the back.

The same goes for row houses: the stoop door is the most visible entrance and the least used by intruders, who prefer the garden floor and the yard.

“Landmarked houses can’t have alarms without a permit.”

The Landmarks Preservation Commission regulates exterior work in historic districts. Its permit guidebook deals with how and where security equipment goes on a building, and it asks for discreet placement and nothing on a primary facade unless there is no other option.

An alarm installed entirely inside the house, with an interior siren and cable run indoors, does not add anything to the outside. That is our default in the Crown Heights North district phases and on Park Place.

If an owner wants an outdoor siren, camera or other exterior device, it is worth checking with the Commission first, and we plan its location with the guidebook’s preferences in mind.

“Wireless alarms don’t work in old brick buildings.”

Thick brick and plaster do shorten wireless range, and a big row house or a basement three floors down can be at the edge. But current wireless sensors and panels reach through most Crown Heights houses and apartments without trouble.

The real answer is to test. We check the signal from every sensor location to the panel before mounting anything, and if a spot is weak we move the panel, add a repeater, or wire that zone.

Hybrid systems exist for exactly this case: wired where the cellar makes wiring easy, wireless where plaster and woodwork make it costly.

“A sensor on every window is the safest setup.”

More sensors is not the same as better coverage. A fourth-floor street window with nothing beneath it but a sheer wall does not need a contact; a window onto a fire escape needs a contact and glass-break; a window with an air conditioner in it needs neither on the sash for half the year and a detector in the room instead.

Over-sensoring raises the price and adds batteries to change without making anyone safer.

We price by opening, after looking out of each one, which is why a Crown Heights one-bedroom often costs less than a generic “starter package” assumes.

“Alarm installation costs $1,000 to $3,000 plus monitoring.”

Ranges like that circulate widely, and they are not wrong as national averages. But the Brooklyn list we work from starts lower and runs higher: a basic residential system from $600, a typical wireless apartment system $600 to $1,200, complete home protection from $2,500, and a custom hardwired system $2,000 to $6,000.

Partner monitoring is optional, costs from $19.99 a month, and can be cancelled without a multi-year commitment.

The real price for a Crown Heights house depends on the number of openings that matter, not the square footage, and a visit fixes it in writing.

“Alarms only matter at night.”

The 2013 report from the 77th Precinct tied burglaries to apartments left empty during the working day, 7 a.m. to 7 p.m. An alarm that is armed only at bedtime is off during exactly those hours.

We set systems up so daytime arming is automatic: reminders when everyone has left, a schedule that arms if nobody does, a fob on the key ring.

Night arming still matters, but it is the second setting, not the first.

“Anyone can install an alarm.”

Anyone can stick a sensor to a window frame. Installing alarm systems as a business in New York requires a license from the Department of State under General Business Law Article 6-D, with qualifying education, an exam and fingerprinting, according to the Department’s licensing page.

Licensing also matters for insurance. An insurer that offers a discount for a monitored alarm may ask for a certificate from the installer or monitoring company, and a co-op board or landlord may ask for the installer’s license and insurance before allowing work.

Ask for the license number from whoever you hire. Ours, #12000287431, is on every page and every invoice.

Our Installation Process

Six steps cover a single apartment and a four-story house with a rental floor alike. The Crown Heights part is the second step: before choosing a single device, the technician opens the rear windows and looks at what is outside them.

1. Floor, photos and keys

You tell us which floor you are on and what kind of building it is, send photos of the rear windows and the front door, and list who else has a key or needs a code.

2. Look out of every rear window

From the rearmost room forward: each fire escape, shaft, extension roof, yard wall and hatch is inspected, and any opening a person could reach from outside goes on the list.

3. Decide door by door, then price it

Each opening is assigned a sensor or none, each leftover door in a converted house gets a decision, the areas for owner, tenant or shop are drawn, and a written Brooklyn price follows, usually that day.

4. Panel, power and signal test

The panel goes where it is out of sight from the windows, near a dependable outlet, with battery backup and a cellular communicator; every planned sensor position is signal-tested before anything is mounted.

5. Mount without marking the house

Contacts go inside frames, nothing goes on the face of the stone, cable runs indoors, renters get removable mounts, and fire-escape windows get sash, gate and glass-break coverage with no countdown.

6. Codes, schedules and a walk-through

Everyone gets a personal code and app login, daytime reminders and schedules are set, the siren is limited well inside the City’s fifteen minutes, every sensor is tripped and confirmed, and each person is shown how to arm, disarm and read the app.

Twice a year: a short alarm check for Crown Heights homes

In spring, before the air conditioners go in, test each window contact and glass-break detector from the keypad’s test mode, and decide which windows will hold units so the room detectors can take over. In autumn, when the units come out, test the sash contacts again and replace any sensor battery the app flags as low. Field Notes

Field Notes: Count the Doors Before You Count the Rooms

This is how our technicians approach a Crown Heights row house, described as practice rather than as the story of any one job. The first walk-through is about doors. Start at the cellar and work up, opening every door that leads outside or used to lead into a separate apartment, and write each one down: the areaway door, the yard door, the garden-floor street door, the stoop door, the door from a rear room onto the extension roof, the old apartment doors on the landings, the hatch at the top. A house that has been divided and joined back together can have twice as many as the owner remembers.

Only then do the windows get counted, from the back: which ones face a fire escape, which an extension roof, which an air shaft, which a yard wall a person could stand on. The front windows of upper floors usually come last and often drop off the list entirely.

The last thing to check is the old system, if there is one. Wired contacts from an alarm of thirty years ago are often still in the window frames, and if they test good on the meter they save the owner a great deal of work. If they do not, the wire stays in the wall and wireless contacts go on beside them. Nothing on the front of the stone, ever.

If an older system’s wiring tests sound, a panel replacement at $800 to $1,500 plus added sensors can cost less. Sales tax is added, and the written figure follows one visit.

Can the keypad be mounted low enough for someone who uses a wheelchair?
+

Yes. A keypad can go at any height that suits the household, and we place it where it can be reached from a seated position within the entry countdown, usually beside the door on the latch side. A second keypad or a wireless one can be added elsewhere, and arming from a phone or a key fob means nobody has to reach a wall at all. Tell us at booking so the mounting height is agreed before the technician drills.

How many door and window sensors does a typical prewar one-bedroom actually need?
+

Often fewer than a boxed kit suggests. A typical one-bedroom needs a contact on the unit door, contacts on each window that opens onto the fire escape or a reachable air shaft, a glass-break detector in each fire-escape room and one motion detector covering the hall. That usually comes to four to six contacts, which is why most fall within the basic residential system from $600. High street windows with nothing below them are left off.

Can a contact go on a steel apartment door, or does the metal interfere with it?
+

It can. Ordinary magnetic contacts can lose range on steel doors and frames, so on metal-clad apartment doors we use contacts built for steel, either recessed into the frame where a drill can reach or surface-mounted with a spacer that keeps the magnet clear of the metal. The contact is tested with the door closed, slightly ajar and fully open before we leave, so it reports exactly when the door leaves the frame.

Can the keypad and the app name each door and window, so we know which one opened?
+

Yes. Every sensor is named during programming, in the words the household actually uses, such as garden yard door, parlor rear window or fire-escape bedroom window. The keypad shows that name when the sensor trips or is left open while arming, and the app lists every opening by name and time. Clear names also let the monitoring station tell you exactly what tripped when they call.

Can the alarm warn us when the front door has been left unlocked, not just open?
+

Yes, with the right lock. A door contact only knows whether the door is open or shut. Pairing the system with a compatible smart deadbolt, or adding a bolt-position sensor inside the frame, lets the app show whether the bolt is thrown and send a reminder if the house is armed with the door unlocked. On a row-house stoop door with an original mortise lock, a bolt sensor is usually the less intrusive choice.

Can we add sensors ourselves later, for instance when we finish the back rooms?
+

On most of the wireless panels we install, yes. We can leave spare sensors programmed and ready, or show you how to enroll a new one from the app or keypad. Wired zones and anything that changes the monitoring account are better left to us. If you add a sensor yourself, test it in test mode and tell the monitoring station, so the new name appears on their screen.

How do we hear the alarm on the top floor when the panel is down in the cellar?
+

With a second sounder upstairs. The panel can sit out of sight in the cellar or a closet, but the sound has to reach the people: a siren in the panel’s own room is easily muffled by three floors of brick, joists and closed doors. We add an interior sounder on the bedroom floor, wired or wireless, and set the entry chime to play there as well, then test it with the bedroom doors shut. The result is an alarm you hear clearly at the top of the house without making the cellar any louder.
