The Neighborhood

Why South Williamsburg Properties Need Alarm Installation

South Williamsburg is the part of Williamsburg south of the Williamsburg Bridge approaches, usually described as the blocks between Division Avenue, Broadway, Flushing Avenue and the East River, though some sources draw the line at Kent Avenue or at Heyward Street and the Navy Yard. It sits in the 90th Precinct and Community District 1, its ZIP codes are 11206 and 11211, and its population was estimated at about 47,000 in 2020. Most of it is home to one of the largest Hasidic communities in the country, centered on Lee and Bedford Avenues, alongside a long-established Puerto Rican and Latino community toward Broadway and the Los Sures blocks. For an alarm installer, the details that matter are simpler than the history: households are large, buildings are attached, and many homes and institutions follow a weekly rhythm in which the building is either very full or very quiet.

That rhythm is the first thing we plan around. In a big family the front door is opened by children, guests, older relatives and helpers from early morning until well past midnight, and a system that has to be disarmed every time someone comes home soon stops being armed at all. On Shabbos and Yom Tov many residents will not touch a keypad, open an app or carry a fob, so whatever the alarm does in those hours has to be set beforehand and has to work without anyone handling it. And because most of the activity happens at home, the hours when the house is truly empty are fewer than in other neighborhoods; the hours that need protecting are the ones when everyone is asleep.

The most recent local warning makes the point. In September 2026, Yeshiva World News reported a Williamsburg Shomrim alert about a man who entered several homes through unlocked doors at about five o’clock on a Shabbos morning, while families slept, until a young father woke to find him inside and called for help. The same man was believed to have burglarized another home weeks earlier, taking a significant amount of money. Shomrim’s advice to residents was to lock backyard, basement and secondary entrances, to check that doors are secured before going to sleep “even when home,” to keep entrances and hallways lit, and to make sure cameras and alarms work, that their timestamps are accurate “and alarms are activated.” Almost every item on that list is something a properly planned alarm either does for the family or reminds them to do.

The second pattern is the institution that is busy, not empty, when it is hit. In February 2023 a man walked into the Satmar matzah bakery at 423 Broadway at about 9:50 in the morning, with the bakery working five weeks before Passover, went through the jackets on a coat rack, climbed to the second floor, made himself coffee and forced open the locked office, leaving with more than $60,000 in cash and more than $34,000 in checks, according to the Brooklyn District Attorney. In 2016, CBS New York reported that a man entered a synagogue on Hewes Street near Harrison Avenue at about 3:30 on a Sabbath morning, searched the coats and the prayer room and took the cash box from the basement office. In both cases the building’s front door had been passed, and the money was in an office that nobody was watching. That is why so many of our South Williamsburg plans put a separate, always-armed zone on one office door before they worry about anything else.

The numbers show a neighborhood where burglary has fallen a long way but has not gone away. The NYPD’s CompStat report for the 90th Precinct for the week of September 7–13, 2026, counted 196 burglaries in 2026 to date, against 181 at the same point in 2025, an increase of 8.3 percent, even though burglary is down 38.8 percent over 16 years and 79.5 percent over 33 years. Robbery was down 19.6 percent for the year, and grand larceny, theft without a break-in, was down 5.1 percent but 48 percent above its level of 16 years ago. A family does not need a fortress here. It needs the doors that are actually used at night to report when they open, the rooms that hold money to report when anyone goes in, and a call list of people who answer.

An Alarm Nobody Arms, a Keypad Nobody Can Read or a Panel That Calls Nobody: Alarm Repair in South Williamsburg

A large share of the alarm calls we get from this neighborhood are about systems that already exist. A family moves into an apartment off Bedford Avenue and finds a keypad by the door with a code nobody knows. A bakery or a wholesale business on Broadway discovers, after a theft, that its panel had been reporting to a monitoring account in the name of a previous tenant who stopped paying years ago. A shul’s office has contacts on the street door and nothing on the office itself. Most often, the system works perfectly well and is simply never armed, because arming it means a code at the door every time one of a dozen people comes home, and somewhere along the way the household gave up.

Each of these is fixable, usually in one visit. A panel whose wiring and sensors test sound can usually keep them, with a new communicator or a new panel on the existing wires, which is the panel-replacement line on our price list rather than a new system. A system nobody arms usually needs a different arming plan, not new equipment: fobs instead of codes for the children, perimeter-only arming at night, and in some homes an arming schedule set in advance. If a siren will not stop, we talk you through silencing it over the phone first, because in an attached building it is waking more than one family. What We Install

Alarm Installation Services in South Williamsburg

Night arming for big households

The street door, the yard door, the basement and the reachable windows armed while the family sleeps, with motion detectors left off so children and guests can move around inside, and a soft chime instead of a siren when someone in the house opens a door by mistake.

Fobs and simple codes for many people

A fob on the key ring for each teenager, a short personal code for each adult, a separate code for a helper or a cleaner that can be switched off in seconds, and a log that shows which of them armed or disarmed the system.

Office and cash-room zones

One door in a bakery, shul, yeshiva or wholesale business that stays armed while the rest of the building is open, with a contact, a motion detector inside and an alert to the manager the moment it opens without the right code.

Store-and-apartment buildings

For the walk-ups on Lee Avenue and Division Avenue with a shop at street level: the store armed at closing on its own area, each apartment upstairs on its own, and the shared stair, roof door and cellar on a common area that cannot be disarmed by one tenant for everyone.

Schedules set before Shabbos and Yom Tov

For households and institutions that want it, arming and disarming entered in advance, so the alarm does what the family decided before candle lighting without anyone touching it until after Havdalah. We program what the household’s own rav has approved; we do not rule on it.

Apartment alarms for renters

For tenants in walk-ups and mid-rises: wireless sensors on your own door and windows, a panel and keypad inside the apartment, nothing fixed to the landlord’s hallway, and everything removable when you move.

Smoke, CO and water on the same panel

Monitored smoke, carbon monoxide, heat and water sensors that report to the family’s phones and, if monitored, to the central station, including the carbon monoxide detection HPD requires in rented homes with gas appliances. A building fire alarm stays a separate system.

Takeovers, resets and repairs

Adopting a system a previous owner or tenant left behind, clearing lost master codes, moving monitoring into the right name, replacing communicators that no longer reach anyone, and re-planning alarms a family stopped arming because it was too much work.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for South Williamsburg’s Architecture

Most of South Williamsburg was built close together and has been added to ever since. The side streets off Lee and Bedford Avenues are lined with modest attached brick buildings of three and four stories, many with a store at street level; newer family-size apartment buildings have filled lots between them, with big kitchens, many bedrooms and staggered balconies; Kent Avenue has gone from factories to glass towers and converted lofts; and the public housing campuses near the river hold thousands of apartments. What drives an alarm plan is less the age of the building than how many doors the household really uses, what it shares with the neighbors, and where the money is kept. These six building types cover nearly every request we get.

The walk-up with a store downstairs

A three- or four-story brick building with a shop at street level, a narrow residents’ door beside it, a single stair to the apartments and a door to the roof at the top. One owner, several families and a store tenant share it, so the alarm has to be divided before it is designed.

The narrow row house

An attached house on a side street, often enlarged at the back or the top, with a door to a small yard, a basement with its own door and windows the yard and the fire escape can reach. Usually one large family, sometimes with a rented floor or a married child’s apartment below.

The family-size mid-rise apartment

An apartment of four, five or more bedrooms in a newer elevator building, with a corridor door, a balcony door and sometimes a second door to a service stair. The building controls the lobby; the family controls everything behind its own door.

The Kent Avenue loft or tower unit

A converted factory loft or an apartment in one of the glass buildings near the river, with large windows, open plans and a building staff that keeps its own rules about contractors and hours.

The tower apartment on a managed campus

An apartment in Independence Towers, the Taylor–Wythe Houses or a similar campus, where the management office approves what may be installed and the alarm has to live entirely inside the unit.

The shul, yeshiva or gemach building

A building that is full at certain hours and empty at others, often with a ground-floor hall, classrooms or a beis medrash, and one small office or storeroom where donations, tuition payments or donated goods are kept.

The walk-up with a store downstairs: divide it before you wire it

A walk-up with a store underneath is really three or four premises sharing one front wall. The store opens early and closes in the evening and usually closes for Shabbos; the families upstairs keep their own hours; and the owner, who may live in one of the apartments or somewhere else entirely, is responsible for the street door, the stair, the cellar and the roof. If one panel treats all of that as a single area, somebody will eventually disarm it for everyone or set it off for everyone, and within weeks it will be left off. So the first question on these buildings is how many areas the alarm needs and who holds the codes to each.

The arrangement we use most often is one panel with an area for the store, an area for each apartment that wants one, and a shared area for the cellar, the roof door and, where the owner asks, the residents’ street door. The store’s staff arm and disarm their area with their own codes; a family upstairs arms its apartment with its own fobs; the shared area can be armed by whoever is responsible at night. A child who forgets a fob cannot disarm the store, and a store employee cannot see the apartments’ history. Because the roof door at the top of the stair is often the one opening nobody checks, it gets a contact on the shared area and an alert to the owner whenever it opens after dark.

The narrow row house: the back of the house at night

The row houses on the side streets are where the September 2026 Shomrim warning applies most directly. The doors Shomrim named, the backyard door, the basement door and other secondary entrances, are exactly the ones a large family uses all day and forgets to lock at night: the yard door the children run out of, the basement door a delivery came through, the side door to a married child’s apartment downstairs. The front door is usually locked and often has a person nearby. The plan for these houses starts at the back.

Every door gets a contact, including the basement door and the door to any rented or family apartment downstairs, and every window that can be reached from the yard, the fire escape or a lower roof gets a contact or is covered by a glass-break detector. At night the whole perimeter is armed with the motion detectors off, so the family can move freely inside; if any door opens from outside, the panel sounds and reports. Where a household wants it, a separate area covers the downstairs apartment, so its residents can come home late through their own door without disarming the main house. A light at the yard door, tied to the same app, makes the most of Shomrim’s advice to keep entrances lit.

The family-size mid-rise apartment: one door to the corridor, one to the stair

Newer apartments for large families are built around a big kitchen and dining room, with many small bedrooms and, in many buildings, a balcony off the main room. The building handles the lobby door, the elevator and the cameras in the common areas, which leaves the family responsible for what happens behind its own door. On upper floors that usually means the corridor door, any door to a service or fire stair that opens into the apartment, and the balcony door where a balcony connects to a neighbor’s or sits low over a setback roof. Higher windows that nobody can reach usually need nothing at all.

Because these apartments hold so many people, the arming plan matters more than the sensor count. Each adult gets a code, each older child a fob, and the system is armed in a night setting that watches only the doors. The keypad goes inside the apartment, never in the corridor, and the panel is placed where it is not the first thing someone sees on entering. Most of these jobs fall on the $600 to $1,200 wireless line of our price list, with a second keypad or extra fobs added for very large households.

The Kent Avenue loft or tower unit: building rules first

Lofts and tower apartments near the river come with management rules about when contractors may work, which elevator they may use and what may be fixed to the walls or window frames. We ask for those rules before scheduling, send any insurance paperwork the building requires, and work inside the hours it allows. The alarm itself is usually simple: a contact on the entry door, contacts on any terrace or balcony doors, glass-break coverage for large windows that face a terrace or a low roof, and one or two motion detectors placed so the open plan does not leave a blind corner by the door.

The business burglaries on Kent Avenue in January 2023, when front-door locks were cut and a door was broken with a sledgehammer, were at street level, and they matter most to the shops and cafés in these buildings rather than to the apartments above them. A ground-floor business in a tower is planned like any store, with its own panel or its own area, and kept entirely separate from the building’s systems.

The tower apartment on a managed campus: everything stays inside the unit

In Independence Towers, which moved to private management under the city’s PACT program beginning in 2020, and in the Taylor–Wythe Houses, the management office decides what a tenant may install. A wireless apartment alarm that is screwed or taped only inside the unit, uses no building wiring and comes off cleanly when the tenant leaves is the kind of system offices most often allow, but the written answer has to come from them. We help the tenant describe exactly what will be mounted and where, and nothing is installed until that approval is in hand.

Inside the apartment, the plan is short: the entry door, any window that a fire escape or a lower roof reaches, a motion detector in the hall between the door and the bedrooms, and a smoke and carbon monoxide detector that reports to the family’s phones. These apartments almost always fall on the basic residential line, from $600.

The shul, yeshiva or gemach building: protect the room, not just the building

An institution’s front door is open to many people for many hours, and it is usually not where the loss happens. In the 2016 Hewes Street theft the money was taken from a basement office; in the 2023 matzah bakery burglary it came from an office on the second floor. So the alarm for a shul, a yeshiva, a bakery attached to a community institution or a gemach starts with a list of the rooms that hold cash, checks, records or valuable goods, and each of those rooms gets its own zone: a contact on the door, a motion detector inside and an alert to a named person whenever it opens outside agreed hours.

The rest of the building is armed at closing, or on a schedule agreed in advance by the institution and its rav, with the beis medrash, hall or classrooms left as they need to be. We program what the institution decides and record exactly what each zone does, so the gabbai, the administrator and whoever answers the alarm at night all know what to expect.

Asleep at Home

Five in the Morning, Everyone Asleep, a Door Left Open: Planning a South Williamsburg Alarm for the Hours Before Dawn

Most alarm advice is written for a house that is empty during the day. The burglar Shomrim warned about in September 2026 did the opposite: he came at about five in the morning, on Shabbos, when families were home and asleep, and he went in through doors that had been left unlocked. That is a hard case for an ordinary alarm, because the ordinary way to use one is to arm it when you leave and disarm it when you come home. A family of ten that goes to bed at different times, with guests leaving late and someone always up early for a minyan, often does not arm anything at night at all.

The fix is a night setting planned around how the house actually sleeps. Every exterior door and every window that can be reached from outside is armed; the motion detectors inside are not, so children can go to the kitchen and parents can check on the baby without setting anything off. If a door opens from outside while the night setting is on, the system does not wait the usual thirty or forty-five seconds that it gives someone coming home with a code. It sounds and reports at once, because at that hour nobody in the family should be walking in. The person who leaves first in the morning, for shul or for work, needs a way to go out without a code: a short exit delay on one chosen door, or a fob, depending on what the household is comfortable with.

Unlocked doors are part of the same plan. A contact tells the panel whether a door is open or shut; it does not know whether it is locked. Before the night setting can be turned on, the panel checks that every door and window is closed and says which one is not. In homes where the family wants more, a smart lock or a lock-status sensor on the main door can tell the app that the door is shut but unlocked. Shomrim’s own list, to check that doors are secured before sleeping even when home, is easier to follow when the panel refuses to arm until they are.

Shabbos changes how the setting is turned on, not what it does. For a family that will not touch a keypad, the choices are to arm before Shabbos and live with the setting for twenty-five hours, or to have a schedule entered in advance that switches the house into the night setting at an agreed hour and out of it in the morning, or, in some homes, a delayed-arming key of the kind Rav Eliezer Melamed’s Peninei Halakha discusses, in which turning a key causes the system to arm itself several minutes later. Peninei Halakha allows that method only in a case of great need, and halachic opinions on alarms, motion sensors and schedules differ. We set up the hardware for whichever approach the household’s own rav has approved, show the family exactly what it will and will not do, and never tell anyone what is permitted.

The last part of a night plan is what happens when the alarm does go off. Shomrim and the NYPD told residents not to confront an intruder, because the person “could be armed and dangerous,” and to get to a safe place and call. So the siren is loud enough to be heard in the bedrooms, the panel’s keypad or a panic button is placed where a parent can reach it from the bedroom hallway, and the call list starts with adults in the household and, if the family wants a central station, ends with a dispatch to the 90th Precinct. Many families also keep the Shomrim number handy, and an alarm that wakes the house quickly gives them the minute they need to use it.

The Office Upstairs

Busy Downstairs, Money Upstairs: Alarm Zones for Bakeries, Shuls, Yeshivas and Wholesale Offices in South Williamsburg

The most expensive burglary we found in South Williamsburg’s recent record did not happen at night. On February 28, 2023, at about 9:50 in the morning, a man walked into the matzah bakery at 423 Broadway while it was working, looked through the jackets on the coat rack, went up to the second floor, made himself a cup of coffee and forced open the locked office. The Brooklyn District Attorney put the loss at more than $60,000 in cash and more than $34,000 in checks. He was caught, pleaded guilty to burglary and was sentenced to two and a half to five years; District Attorney Eric Gonzalez said he “was caught red handed and will now be held responsible.” But nothing in the building told anyone, while it was happening, that a stranger had gone upstairs.

A conventional alarm would not have helped, because a conventional alarm is off when a business is open. The tool for that situation is a zone that stays armed while the rest of the building is in use. In a bakery or a wholesale business, it is the office door and the stair to it; in a shul, the office, the room where the pushkas and the membership payments are kept, and the basement storeroom; in a yeshiva, the administrator’s office and the room where tuition payments are handled. That zone has its own contact, usually a motion detector inside the room, and its own short list of people who may disarm it. When it opens without one of their codes, the manager’s phone rings in seconds, during business hours or not.

The 2016 Hewes Street case shows the same thing in a different form. A man entered the synagogue at about 3:30 on a Sabbath morning through a front door that members said is locked overnight, searched the coats and the prayer room, where there were no cameras, and went down to the basement manager’s office for the cash box. “Sabbath night, nobody is in the synagogues,” one member told CBS New York. An office zone that stays armed from Friday afternoon until after Shabbos, set in advance and with a monitoring station and a non-observant contact on the call list where the institution’s rav approves, would have reported the moment that office door opened.

Two details make these zones work in practice. The first is the list of people: an office zone with forty codes on it is not protecting anything, so we keep it to the few people who really need to go in and give everyone else access only to the areas they use. Before Pesach, when bakeries and food businesses take on extra hands, the extra staff get codes for the shop floor and the loading door, not for the office. The second is the alert: it goes to a person who will answer the phone during the working day, not to a shared email box, and the message names the room, so the manager knows immediately whether to walk upstairs or to call the police.

A camera pointed at the office door and at the stair completes the plan, because the alert tells the manager something has happened and the camera shows who. We add one on the same app where the business wants it, priced on the cameras-with-alarm line of our Brooklyn list, $1,000 to $2,500, and keep the recorder in a room that is itself on the alarm.

Commercial Alarms

Business Alarm Installation in South Williamsburg

Commercial South Williamsburg is a mix of small family stores, food businesses that work through the night before holidays, wholesale and distribution space near the old industrial edge, and the cafés and shops of the Kent and Wythe Avenue blocks. Many businesses close for Shabbos and Yom Tov, so the same shop that is busy all week can sit dark for twenty-five hours or, around Rosh Hashanah and Pesach, two or three days. Burglaries here have tended to come in sprees that hit several businesses in a night, which makes fast reporting matter more than a loud bell. Flushing Avenue and the old industrial blocks

Warehouses, distribution businesses and workshops along the southern edge, near the Navy Yard and the former Pfizer plant, where the city approved a rezoning for new housing in 2017. Large roll-up doors, loading areas and empty weekends define the alarm plan here.

Stores that close for Shabbos and Yom Tov: arm by plan, not by habit

A Lee Avenue store that closes on Friday afternoon is empty from before candle lighting until well after dark on Saturday, and over a three-day Yom Tov it is empty much longer. During those hours nobody from the business will look at an app, and on the street there is less traffic than at any other time of the week. That makes the arming routine the single most important part of a store alarm here. The panel is armed at closing by the last person out, with a code or a fob; if the store is not armed by an agreed time on Friday, the owner gets a reminder in time to come back; and the call list for the closed hours names people who will answer, which for many stores includes a manager or a neighbor who is not observant, or a monitoring station that dispatches the police directly.

The sensors are chosen for a store that will be dark for a long time. A contact on the gate or the shop door, glass-break coverage behind the gate, a contact on the back door and on any door to the cellar, and motion detection inside, aimed so that holiday decorations or a stack of boxes left by the door will not block it. Because the store is often below apartments, the siren is set to stop well within the city’s fifteen-minute limit, and the families upstairs are told what it means if they hear it.

Food businesses before the holidays: the busiest weeks are the riskiest

Bakeries and food businesses here work long hours before Pesach, Rosh Hashanah and Sukkos, with extra staff, deliveries at odd hours and more cash than usual. The 2023 matzah bakery burglary happened in exactly that period, five weeks before Passover. During those weeks we keep the alarm plan the same but tighten the edges: the office zone stays armed all day, the extra workers get codes only for the areas they use, the loading door has its own contact with an alert if it is left open after the last delivery, and the owner can see on the app who armed and disarmed the store each night.

Warehouses and workshops near Flushing Avenue: big doors, empty weekends

Distribution businesses along the southern edge of the neighborhood usually have one or two roll-up loading doors, a personnel door, a small office and a roof with a hatch or skylights. They are busy on weekdays and closed over Shabbos and Sunday mornings, and they often have no residential neighbors to hear a siren. We put heavy-duty contacts with wide gap tolerance on each roll-up door, a contact on the personnel door and the roof hatch, motion detection across the floor and a separate zone on the office, and we rely on cellular reporting to a monitoring station or to several phones rather than on noise.

Compliance notes for South Williamsburg homes, stores and institutions

  • The siren must stop on its own. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building’s audible burglar alarm to shut off automatically within fifteen minutes. In attached buildings full of families, we set the cutoff well inside that limit.
  • The installer must be licensed. New York’s Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers under Article 6-D of the General Business Law, with 81 hours of qualifying education, an exam and fingerprinting. Our license number is #12000287431, and any installer’s license can be checked with the Department of State.
  • Rented homes need the right detectors. HPD requires smoke detectors in each unit of a multiple dwelling and carbon monoxide detectors in rented one- and two-family homes with gas or fuel-burning appliances, within fifteen feet of the entrance to each sleeping room. Monitored detectors on an alarm panel can meet that requirement and report to the owner at the same time.
  • Exits stay exits. Nothing we install may lock, slow or block a required exit, a roof door used as a way out, or a fire escape window. Contacts report open and closed; they do not lock anything.
  • Fire alarms are a separate system. A burglar alarm can carry monitored smoke, heat and carbon monoxide detectors for a home or a store, but it does not replace a fire alarm system a school, hall or larger building may need, and it is never tied into one without that system’s fire alarm contractor. AESS does not service kitchen hood fire suppression systems.
  • Managed buildings decide what goes on their walls. In public housing, PACT developments, condominiums and rentals, the management office or the board has the final word on what a tenant or owner may mount, and we work from their written approval.
Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in South Williamsburg: Questions Answered

These are the questions families, store owners and the people who run shuls, yeshivas and gemachs bring to us, grouped by where they come from. General questions about cost, monitoring and timing are in the FAQ further down.

Nights, Shabbos and large households

Shomrim’s September 2026 alert told families to make sure their alarms are activated and their timestamps accurate. How can we tell, without opening an app, that our panel really armed last night and that its clock is right?

The panel keeps its own history, and we set it up so that you do not have to go looking for it. Most of the panels we install announce the arming state out loud and show it on the keypad, and the app can send one adult a short morning message saying when the house armed and disarmed overnight; where the adults in the household do not use apps, that history is read from the keypad screen or by us on a visit. The clock is set from the cellular network, so it does not drift the way an old panel’s does. It matters because when something happens, the police and Shomrim match the alarm’s times to camera footage, and a clock that is twenty minutes off sends everyone to the wrong part of the video.

After our Friday-night seudah the last guests leave close to midnight, and nobody in our home will touch a keypad until after Havdalah. Can the doors downstairs arm themselves once the house is asleep?

On most current panels, yes. An arming schedule entered before Shabbos can switch the house into its night setting at an hour you choose, for example one in the morning, and back out again before the first person leaves for shul. If a door is still open at that hour the panel cannot arm that door, so we program it to skip the open opening and arm everything else rather than refuse to arm at all. Whether a schedule like this suits your household on Shabbos is a question for your rav; some families prefer to arm before candle lighting and leave the setting alone for the whole of Shabbos, and the hardware supports both.

If our alarm goes off at five in the morning, Shomrim says not to confront anyone. What should the alarm do in that first minute so that we do not have to?

It should make enough noise inside the bedrooms that every adult wakes at once, tell you which door or window opened so you know which part of the home to stay away from, and start the call list without anyone pressing anything. We place the siren where the bedrooms hear it, put a panic button or a keypad in the bedroom hallway so a parent can call for help from there, and, if the house is monitored, have the central station dispatch police for a night-setting alarm without waiting for someone to pick up. That leaves the family free to gather the children in one room and call Shomrim or 911, as the alert advised.

Our rav allows arming with a delayed key in a case of real need. What does that hardware look like, and can we see it work before Shabbos?

It is usually a key switch or a programmable input wired to the panel, set so that turning it starts a countdown and the panel arms itself several minutes later, in the way Peninei Halakha describes. We install it, set the delay your rav specifies, and test it with you during the week, as many times as you like, so that you know exactly what happens and how long it takes. We do not decide whether it may be used, or when; we make sure it does precisely what your rav approved and nothing more, and we write down how it is set so that nobody changes it by accident.

The basement door of our row house is used during the week by the boiler man and by deliveries. How do we let them in without handing out the code the family uses?

Give them a code of their own that only works on the basement’s area, only during the hours you allow, and that you can switch off from the app or the keypad as soon as the job is done. The rest of the house stays armed while they are downstairs, and the history shows exactly when their code opened the door. For a one-time visit, a code that expires the same evening is the simplest choice; for someone who comes every month, a permanent limited code saves you from giving out a new one every time.

Shuls, yeshivas, gemachs and offices

The thief at the Broadway matzah bakery went through the coats downstairs before he went up to the office. Can the stair itself tell us that someone is going up during working hours?

Yes. A contact on the stair door, or a motion detector at the top of the stair, can be put on its own zone that stays active while the bakery or office floor is open. It can do one of two things: sound a soft chime downstairs so staff know someone has gone up, or send an alert to the manager’s phone, or both. In a building where staff use the stair all day, we usually set it to chime and to alert only when the office zone at the top is opened without the right code, so the phone rings when it matters and not every time someone goes for supplies.

The man who robbed the Hewes Street shul in 2016 searched a prayer room that had no cameras. Should a shul alarm cover the beis medrash over Shabbos, or only the office?

For most shuls, the office, the storeroom and any room where money or seforim of value are kept should be covered, and the main rooms should be decided by the shul’s leadership and its rav. A beis medrash that is open for early minyanim and late learning is hard to arm without the alarm going off on the people it is meant to serve, so many shuls leave it unarmed and protect the doors that lead from it to the offices and the basement. Where the shul wants the large rooms watched overnight, a schedule set in advance can arm them between the last and first minyan.

Our gemach keeps donated cash and goods in one basement room of an apartment building. Can that room be alarmed on its own without involving the rest of the building?

Yes, as long as the building’s owner agrees to what is mounted in the room. A small wireless panel with a contact on the room’s door, a motion detector inside and a cellular communicator can protect that one room without any wiring in the hallways and without touching the building’s other systems. The volunteers who run the gemach get their own codes, the alerts go to two or three of them, and the siren, if there is one, is kept quiet enough not to wake the building. It is priced as a basic system, from $600 on our Brooklyn list.

Shops and apartments

At two of the Kent Avenue businesses hit in January 2023, the burglars cut the locks on the front door and took a sledgehammer to a door. Should a store’s sensors be on the door itself, or is a motion detector inside enough?

Both, for different reasons. A contact on the door reports the moment it opens, and a shock sensor on the door or the frame reports while someone is still cutting or hammering, before it opens at all. A motion detector inside is the second layer, for the case where someone gets in another way. Relying on the motion detector alone means the alarm only speaks once a burglar is already inside, which on a night when several stores are being hit is a few minutes later than it needs to be.

Our apartment in a newer family-size building has a door from the kitchen to the service stair the building uses for trash. Does that door need a sensor if it is always locked?

Yes. A door that is always locked is also a door nobody watches, and service stairs in larger buildings are used by staff, contractors and neighbors at all hours. A contact on that door costs little, arms with the rest of the apartment at night and tells you if it is ever left open. If the building does not allow anything to be fixed to the door, a small adhesive contact on the inside of the frame is usually acceptable, but we confirm that with the management first.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in South Williamsburg

Ask a chatbot or a search summary about alarms for a home or a business here and the answer is usually written for a small family in a detached house that is empty all day, with prices averaged from lead sites such as HomeAdvisor and Angi. These are seven claims we hear repeated, phrased the way they usually appear, and what we see on jobs between Division Avenue and Flushing Avenue.

“An alarm protects your home while you are away.”

It does, and that is the smaller half of the job in this neighborhood. The intruder in the September 2026 Shomrim alert came at five in the morning while families were asleep, and he got in through doors that were not locked. An alarm that is only armed when the house is empty would have done nothing that night, because the house was not empty.

The setting that matters most for many South Williamsburg families is the one used while they sleep: doors and reachable windows armed, motion detectors off, and an instant alarm if anything opens from outside. We plan that setting first and the away setting second.

It also changes how we judge a system. The question is not how many sensors it has, but whether the family can turn on the night setting every night without thinking about it. If it takes more than one step, it will not be used.

“Arm and disarm from your phone, wherever you are.”

Most summaries assume every adult has a smartphone and will use an app at any hour. Some adults here carry basic phones that cannot run alarm apps, and on Shabbos and Yom Tov many residents will not use any device at all. An alarm designed around an app is, for those households, an alarm that is off for a day a week and more.

So our plans here lean on keypads, fobs, voice announcements and schedules entered in advance, with the app as one option for the adults who want it. Alerts that do need to reach a person can go to a phone call or a text, or to a monitoring station.

We also ask, before anything else, who in the family will actually arm the system, and design around that person’s habits.

“Put valuables in a safe and the sensors on the front door.”

In the two largest institutional thefts we found here, the front door was not the problem. At the matzah bakery on Broadway the man walked in during working hours and forced the office upstairs; at the Hewes Street shul he came through a front door that was supposed to be locked and went to the basement office. In both, the loss was in a room nobody was watching.

A front-door sensor would not have helped, because in one case the building was open and in the other the door was passed without force. What helps is a zone on the office itself that stays armed whether or not the rest of the building is.

For businesses and institutions, we start the plan with a walk through the rooms that hold money, and only then look at the doors.

“Burglary is falling in New York, so you need less security than before.”

Over the long run that is true here too: the 90th Precinct’s burglary count is down 38.8 percent over 16 years and 79.5 percent over 33, according to the NYPD. But in 2026 it was going the other way, with 196 burglaries through mid-September against 181 a year earlier.

Grand larceny, theft without a break-in, was 48 percent above its level of 16 years ago. Taken together, the numbers describe a neighborhood with far fewer burglaries than a generation ago, and with the ones that remain concentrated in homes where a door was left open and in businesses where cash is kept.

Our answer is a smaller, better-placed system rather than a bigger one: the doors used at night, the rooms that hold money, and nothing that will be bypassed because it is a nuisance.

“Most alarm systems have a Sabbath mode.”

Some appliances do, and AI answers often assume alarms work the same way. In practice, alarm manufacturers mostly offer pieces rather than a single mode. A thread on an alarm retailer’s support forum about a Qolsys panel noted that a sensor’s indicator light could be turned off in programming, but said of the panel’s voice and beeps that “it is not possible to schedule settings changes like that.” A NAPCO support page suggests powering motion sensors through a relay module and labeling that relay group “Sabbath Mode.” Alarm.com’s scheduled arming rules can arm and disarm at set times on supported panels.

So the right question is not whether a panel has a Sabbath mode, but which of its chimes, lights, voices, schedules and sensors need to behave differently for your household, and whether the panel can do that.

We answer that by testing the actual panel with the family before Shabbos, not by reading the brochure.

“Walking past a motion detector on Shabbos is fine” (or “is forbidden”).

Search summaries tend to give one answer. The sources do not. The Jewish Link reported that, according to the Star-K, walking past a motion detector is not a melacha if nothing perceptible happens, and in the same article the author’s own rav disagreed. Peninei Halakha treats entering a room where a sensor turns on a light as forbidden.

Those differences are for each household and its rav to settle, not for an installer or a chatbot. What we can do is give the family every option: motion detectors that can be left off for Shabbos, sensors without indicator lights, and a night setting that relies only on doors.

Whatever the family decides, we record it, so that the next technician does not undo it.

“Alarm installation costs about $1,119 in Brooklyn.”

That is HomeAdvisor’s Brooklyn average, with a typical range of $450 to $2,198 and an overall range of $200 to $3,000. It is a fair midpoint for a simple apartment alarm, and it is far too low for a walk-up with a store, two apartments and a shared roof door, or for a bakery with an office zone and cameras.

Our Brooklyn list starts at $600 for a basic residential system and runs $1,500 to $3,000 for a standard small business, with complete home protection from $2,500 and per-unit setups for landlords at $400 to $800 per apartment. Installing a kit you already own is $200 to $400.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Ask Who Comes Home Last

The first question we ask in a South Williamsburg home is not how many windows it has. It is who comes home last at night, who leaves first in the morning, and which door each of them uses. In a household of ten or twelve, those two people decide whether the alarm will be armed every night or abandoned in a month. If the last one in is a teenager with a key, the night setting has to start with a fob, not a code; if the first one out leaves for a six o’clock minyan through the yard, that door needs a short exit delay and the others none. Only after those answers do we walk the windows. In a business or a shul, the matching question is who holds the office key, because that short list becomes the office zone’s code list, and everyone else gets less.

South Williamsburg Alarm Installation FAQ

How does an alarm in a South Williamsburg home send its signal to the monitoring station?
+

Over a cellular communicator built into the panel, which does not depend on the home’s internet or a phone line. Where a family wants a second path, the panel can also report over Wi-Fi. We measure the cellular signal where the panel will sit before installing, because a basement office or an inner room behind brick can be weaker than the front of the apartment.

When a monitored alarm goes off, how quickly does someone react?
+

The signal reaches the central station within seconds, and the station starts the call list or dispatches at once, depending on how the account is set up. How fast officers from the 90th Precinct arrive depends on the NYPD and on what else is happening that night, and no alarm company can promise that. What we control is that the signal goes out immediately and reaches people who answer.

Why might our quote list only six or seven sensors when the apartment has twenty windows?
+

Because a window nobody can reach from outside does not need one. On upper floors of a mid-rise or a tower, only windows next to a fire escape, a balcony, a setback roof or a neighbor’s roof are real entry points. Every sensor on the quote has a reason written beside it, and if you want more coverage than we recommend, we add it and price it.

How often should the alarm be tested, and who does it?
+

We test every zone with you on installation day and again at the two-week review. After that, a monthly test is a good habit: put the account in test mode with the monitoring station, open each door, and check that the keypad and the alerts show it. The panel also tests its own battery and communicator and reports a trouble condition if either fails.

What should we have ready on the day of the survey?
+

Access to every door, including the basement, the yard and the roof door, an adult who knows how the household uses each one, and in a rented or managed building, the landlord’s or management office’s approval if you already have it. For a business or institution, the person who holds the office key should be there, because the office zone is planned around them.

Can just one adult in the household be able to add or delete codes?
+

Yes. The master code, which can add, change and delete other codes and change schedules, can be held by one or two adults, while everyone else gets a user code or a fob that only arms and disarms. Codes for helpers and tradespeople can be limited to certain areas and hours, and the history shows who used which code.

If the siren goes off on Shabbos, will it keep sounding until somebody turns it off?
+

No. City law requires a building’s audible burglar alarm to stop on its own within fifteen minutes, and we set the cutoff well inside that, often a few minutes in an attached building full of families. The panel keeps reporting to the call list and stays armed for the other doors after the siren stops, so nobody has to touch the keypad for the noise to end.
