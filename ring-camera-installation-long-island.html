The Neighborhood

Why Long Island Properties Need Ring Camera Installation

Long Island's housing was built for the car, the driveway and the front step, and that is where most of its trouble now arrives. When Levitt and Sons started work in 1947 on what became Levittown, Encyclopedia.com records, the site was 1,300 acres of potato fields; by 1951 it covered more than 5,000 acres and some 17,500 single-family homes. Before that, History.com notes, William Levitt had already built 1,080 homes in Westbury and 2,250 in Roslyn. The pattern spread across Nassau and then pushed east into Suffolk: a detached house, a lawn, a driveway, a door facing the street. A Newsday analysis of nearly 12,000 home sales in 2026, reported in an excerpt the paper posted, found that more than 5,000 of the houses sold had been built in the 1950s and 1960s. That is the stock a Ring doorbell and a pair of floodlight cams are being added to today.

The Census numbers show how complete that pattern is. Of Nassau County's 477,860 housing units, 364,529 are one-unit detached houses, and the median year built is 1955. Suffolk has 579,790 units, of which 463,095 are detached, with a median year of 1970. In practical terms, three out of four Long Island homes are a single house with its own front door, its own wiring and its own owner making the decisions. That is good news for a Ring install, because there is no building intercom to work around and no co-op board to satisfy on most jobs. The challenge is the age of what is already in the walls. A doorbell transformer installed when the house was new was sized to ring a mechanical chime, not to run a camera, a WiFi radio and an infrared light all night. One Ring owner put it plainly in a product review: he had to upgrade his 35-year-old doorbell transformer to get enough power to run his Ring Pro. On a 1955 house, the original transformer may be more than twice that age, if it has not already been replaced with whatever was on the hardware store shelf.

The second reason is what is happening on the front steps and in the driveways. In November 2025, CBS New York reported that the Suffolk County District Attorney had charged 14 men in a porch-theft scheme that ran from October 2023 to February 2025 across 31 communities, including Brentwood, Central Islip, Shirley and Deer Park. Prosecutors said the group hacked systems to learn when Verizon and AT&T devices were being delivered, then took more than 200 phones and tablets, leading to 50 felony charges. Smaller cases keep turning up on News 12 Long Island: four iPhones taken from a porch on Walters Avenue in Wantagh and caught on a Ring doorbell, a suspect trying to grab a package mid-delivery on Atlantic Avenue in Massapequa Park. CBS New York aired a clip from Rider Avenue in Patchogue of a thief who waved at the camera around 5:45 in the evening. In Nassau, LI Life & Politics reported that burglaries rose 5 percent, from 234 to 245, in January through May of 2026 compared with 2025, even as overall property crime fell. And when ABC7 New York covered a multi-agency burglary and car-theft task force in January 2024, police said the burglars were attracted by cars in people's driveways, and that break-ins while people were at home had gone up.

The third reason is the one people forget until a camera fails: the weather and the grid. ABC7 reported that Tropical Storm Isaias in August 2020 left almost half of PSEG Long Island's 1.1 million customers in the dark at the peak. In August 2024, the New York State Department of Financial Services described the first-ever flash flood emergency for Suffolk County, the failure of the Stony Brook Mill Pond dam, and more than 2,000 residents and business owners with flood damage. Superstorm Sandy did $150 million in damage to the city of Long Beach alone, according to Wikipedia's account of the storm, with voluntary evacuations ordered south of Sunrise Highway. A Ring device that runs on WiFi needs both power at the camera and a working router and internet connection to save anything to the cloud. When the power goes, a hardwired doorbell goes with it, and a battery camera keeps watching only as long as the router stays up. That is why a Long Island Ring install has to plan for the outage, not just the sunny day.

Finally, there is the question of who else might see the video. Long Island has a long, public relationship between Ring and the police. In December 2019, City & State New York reported that Nassau County police would work with the Ring Neighbors app to catch package thieves using footage from smart doorbells, if customers gave access. Consumer Reports later explained that police requests on Neighbors were switched off in 2024 and then came back as a program called Community Requests, which owners can turn off in the Neighbors settings. In 2026, Variety and TechCrunch reported that Ring cancelled a planned partnership with Flock Safety after a backlash over its Super Bowl ad. Out in Southold, The Suffolk Times quoted a resident at an April 2026 meeting about Flock cameras asking whether there was "any place you can go that somebody doesn't have a camera on their porch." Our job is not to take a side in that debate. It is to install your system so that you, not a default setting, decide what is shared, and so your camera watches your property and not your neighbor's bedroom window.

We could not see Google's live People Also Ask boxes or AI Overview for this search. We found no official ruling on whether a Long Island town or village requires a permit for a low-voltage doorbell camera, only an installer's claim that it does not. Where this page talks about what we find in 1950s transformers or slab ranches, it draws on the age and type of the housing in the public record and on how houses of that age are generally built, not on a survey of Long Island homes. Nassau: older, denser, and divided into many villages

Nassau is the smaller county, 453 square miles, with almost as many people as Suffolk packed into a fifth of the area. Its government is a patchwork: ny.gov lists two cities, three towns, 64 incorporated villages and more than 60 unincorporated hamlets. For a Ring install that matters in two ways. The first is rules. A village may have its own building department and its own view on what can be attached to a house, while a hamlet answers to its town. We check the address, not just the town name on the mail, because a mailing address and a hamlet line do not always match. The second is the age of the houses. With a median build year of 1955, a Nassau doorbell circuit is more likely to be original, undersized or patched. We plan on testing and often replacing the transformer, and we bring the parts to do it in the same visit.

Nassau is also where the lots are tighter. A Ring floodlight cam over a garage on a 1950s block will see the neighbor's driveway and side yard almost by default. That makes privacy zones and careful aiming part of every Nassau job. New York Civil Rights Law §52-a, as a Yahoo News explainer described it, makes it unlawful to install or use video surveillance where a person has a reasonable expectation of privacy, and installers such as Vertex Security recommend digital privacy masking on exterior cameras so they do not capture a neighbor's backyard. On a narrow Nassau lot, we would rather turn a camera five degrees and mask a window than argue later about what it saw.

Suffolk: bigger lots, longer driveways, more distance

Suffolk covers 2,373 square miles in 10 towns, more than five times Nassau's area. The houses are newer on average, but the property is bigger, and the distances are the problem. A colonial in central Suffolk may sit well back from the road, with a garage, a shed or a pool house the owner also wants watched. Ring devices send their video over WiFi, so a camera at the end of a long driveway is only as good as the signal that reaches it. In Suffolk, more of our time goes into the network: moving the router, adding an access point, or running a cable to an outbuilding so the camera there has a strong connection. The East End adds seasonal homes that sit empty for months, and that brings its own planning, covered further down this page.

Suffolk also has two county programs that touch Ring owners. The Suffolk County Police Department runs a free Camera Registry, in which residents and businesses record where their cameras are. The department says plainly that it does not get access to registered cameras; it may ask an owner to check video for a particular date and time, and retrieval is at the owner's convenience. Separately, if you add Ring Alarm and it is set up to trigger a police response, Suffolk's Alarm Management Program requires a registration, with a fee for residences and a higher one for commercial premises. The department's page says its online portal is down and applications go by mail. We explain both when we set up a Suffolk system.

Chime, Router, Driveway: What to Check in a Long Island House Before We Arrive

Most of the time lost on a Ring job is lost to surprises we could have seen in a photo: a chime that turns out to be a wireless unit with no wires, a transformer hidden behind a finished basement ceiling, a router in an upstairs bedroom on the far side of a hi-ranch. Ten minutes with your phone camera before the visit saves most of it. Please do not open the transformer or the chime wiring yourself; just look and send us what you see.

Stand where the thief would stand

The best camera position is decided from the outside. Walk to the end of your driveway and look back at the house. Where would someone stop to look at your cars? Where would a delivery driver leave a box? Which side door is out of sight of the street? The News 12 clips from Wantagh and Massapequa Park both show what a doorbell sees well: the person on the step and the package at their feet. What a doorbell does not see well is the driveway beside the house, the side gate or the back door. Take a photo from each of those places and send them with the request, and we will suggest which device belongs where.

Look at where the light comes from

Many Long Island front doors face a bright street or a low winter sun, and a camera pointed into either sees a dark silhouette instead of a face. A porch with a deep overhang can help, or it can put the doorbell in shadow while the street behind blazes. Note which way your front door faces and when the sun hits it. If you do not know, a photo taken at about the time packages usually arrive tells us enough. For driveways, notice whether a streetlight sits across the road; it can wash out a camera at night or, aimed well, light the cars for free.

Tell us what you already own

Many people book after buying a Ring kit on sale, or after a previous owner left one behind. List every Ring device you have, even old ones in a drawer, and tell us whose Ring account the existing devices are registered to. A doorbell still tied to a previous owner's account has to be released by that owner or reset following Ring's process before it can join yours. Knowing that in advance means we do not stand at your front door waiting on a stranger's email.

Ring Camera Installation for Long Island's Architecture

Long Island's houses look alike from the road and differ a great deal at the doorbell. The type of house changes three things about a Ring job: where the power comes from, how the WiFi reaches the cameras, and what the cameras are fixed to. These are the types the public record describes across Nassau and Suffolk, and how we handle each.

Levittown Capes and Slab Ranches

Keri Murray Architecture describes Levittown's ranches as built on concrete slabs with radiant heating pipes and an expandable attic but no garage. No basement means wires travel through the attic, and we never drill into a heated slab.

Split-Levels and Hi-Ranches

Common in 1950s and 1960s Nassau, including near Hofstra. Several half-levels stand between the router and the front door, so WiFi is planned level by level and wires follow the stair walls.

Postwar Houses on Block Foundations

Capes and ranches with finished basements added later. Transformers and wires vanish behind finished ceilings, and getting a cable outside means a sealed hole through block.

Colonials and Newer Two-Stories

Typical of Suffolk's 1970 median. Usually a working chime circuit and an attached garage; the job is mostly placement, angles and long-driveway coverage.

South Shore Shingle and Waterfront Houses

Wood-frame and cedar-shingle homes near the bays and barrier beaches. Mounts go on blocks, not straight onto curved shingles, with stainless fixings and sealed penetrations against salt and wind.

North Shore Estates

Gold Coast-era houses and large modern properties along the old 25A corridor. Long gate drives, stone and stucco walls, outbuildings, and WiFi that has to be engineered rather than hoped for.

On slab ranches. A ranch built on a slab with heating pipes in it is the one house on Long Island where we never drill down. Keri Murray Architecture's account of Levittown is clear that the ranches were heated with hot-water radiant pipes, and while many have since been converted to other heat, there is no way to know from the surface what is left in the concrete. So every wire in a slab ranch goes up, not down: from the transformer location into the attic, along the joists and down inside the wall to the doorbell or out through the soffit to a floodlight. Attics in these houses can be shallow and, in summer, very hot, so we schedule attic work for the cooler part of the day where we can, and we look for any insulation or old materials we should not disturb before we crawl through.

On split-levels and hi-ranches. These are the houses where Ring doorbells most often show "offline" a week after a do-it-yourself install. The router usually sits in the family room or den on the lowest level, where the cable company brought the line in, and the front door is a half-flight up and on the far side of a stair wall and a kitchen. A doorbell is a small radio with a small antenna, and it is trying to send video through all of that. Before we mount anything, we stand at the door with a signal meter. If the reading is weak, the fix is one of three things: move the router to a more central level, add an access point on the upper level fed by a cable from the router, or, for the rare door that still cannot hear the network, a wired connection to a model built for it. The chosen fix goes on the quote with its line from the price list.

On block foundations and finished basements. A restoration contractor working in Uniondale describes many local capes and ranches as sitting on concrete-block foundations, with finished basements added in the 1960s and 1970s. That same contractor warns that postwar houses frequently contain asbestos materials. We treat that warning seriously. Before we drill through an old basement ceiling tile, a pipe wrap or a patch of old flooring to chase a doorbell wire, we look at what it is made of, and if it could be a suspect material we stop and route another way. When a cable has to leave the house through the foundation, we drill the block, not the mortar joint, sleeve the hole and seal it so water and insects stay out.

On shingle and waterfront houses. One Long Island installer, Rolo Electronics, says the island's salt air, humidity and storms demand marine-grade hardware and sealed equipment. Whatever brand you use, we agree with the basic point: near the bays and the Sound, the mount is the weak link. A Ring camera screwed straight onto curved cedar shingles leans, lets water run behind the base and works loose in wind. We fit a flat mounting block first, fix it into the framing with stainless screws, seal every penetration and keep cable entries pointing down so water drips away rather than into the wall. When Ring is the right tool for a business, and when it is not

Ring works well for a business that wants a handful of cameras, alerts on the owner's phone and a doorbell at a back or service door. It is less suited to a business that needs many cameras recording all the time to a recorder on site, footage kept for a set number of days for an insurer or a landlord, or several staff watching different screens. Ring saves video to the cloud on a paid plan, and each camera relies on WiFi and the internet connection. A deli on Hempstead Turnpike with three cameras and an owner who checks the phone fits that model. A warehouse on Route 110 with a dozen loading doors usually fits a wired system better. We will tell you which side of that line your business sits on before you buy hardware, and we install both.

Rules and practical limits for business cameras on Long Island

  • State licensing. New York requires a security and fire alarm installer license, under 19 NYCRR 195.2, for installing or servicing CCTV used to detect or monitor intrusion, theft or movement, as a Kirschenbaum law firm article explains. The state's licensing guidance exempts passive, non-detecting devices. Our work is done under NYS license #12000287431 either way.
  • County home improvement licensing. Nassau County requires a home improvement license to work on residential property, and its own FAQ says anyone working only on commercial buildings does not need one. Suffolk's licensing rules, in Chapter 563 of the county code, do not apply in towns or villages that license trades themselves. We hold what each job requires and will show it on request.
  • Signage and obvious cameras. New York Penal Law §250.65, as Ambient.ai summarizes it, protects systems where a written notice that video surveillance is in use is conspicuously posted, or where the cameras are clearly and immediately obvious. A small sign at the door of a store or office costs little and removes doubt.
  • No cameras where people expect privacy. Civil Rights Law §52-a makes video surveillance unlawful in places where people have a reasonable expectation of privacy. Restrooms, changing areas and similar rooms are off limits, and we will not aim a camera into them.
  • Audio is a separate decision. Justia Answers advises disabling audio unless a consenting participant will be part of any recorded conversation. For a counter or a waiting room where customers talk to each other, we usually set Ring cameras to video only and leave the owner to decide in writing.
  • Facial recognition. TechCrunch reported a June 2026 class action claiming Ring's Familiar Faces feature stores images of passersby without consent. Familiar Faces is off by default, according to Smart Home Perfected. We leave it off on business installs unless the owner has taken advice and asks for it.
  • New power is electrician work. A camera on a pole or wall with no outlet nearby needs a line-voltage circuit from a licensed electrician. Our quote says so rather than running an extension cord.
Field Notes

Field Notes: The Meter Comes Out Before the Doorbell Does

We do not publish stories about a named customer's house unless we can document them, so this is not a tale about one address. It is the routine our technicians follow at the front door of the kind of house Long Island is full of: a 1950s cape or ranch, a doorbell button that has been painted over twice, and a new Ring doorbell still in its box on the hall table.

We leave the Ring in the box. First we press the old button and listen to the chime, because a weak, buzzy ring already tells us something about the transformer. Then we find the transformer, often near the boiler or the electrical panel, sometimes in the attic, and put a meter on it. We open the chime and look at how it is wired and what kind it is. We take the old button off and look at the two wires behind it: their color, whether they are brittle, whether the copper has gone green near the weather. We stand at the step with the phone and read the WiFi signal, then do the same at the driveway. Only when power, chime, wires and signal all check out does the Ring come out of its box. If one of them fails, we show you the reading and fix that first. That order is what separates a doorbell that works for years from one that drops off the app every cold night.

Your Questions Answered

Ring Camera Installation in Long Island: Questions Answered

Power and the old doorbell circuit

Our house was built in the 1950s. What happens if the original doorbell transformer is too weak for a Ring doorbell?

The doorbell will usually still turn on, which is what makes the problem confusing. An underpowered Ring doorbell tends to reboot, lag on live view, lose WiFi or drain its backup battery even though it is "hardwired." We test the transformer under load before installing. If it falls short, we replace it with a properly rated one, including the transformer Ring sells for its own doorbells where that suits the house, and we mount it where the next person can find it. A 1955 Nassau house may still have its original unit, or one swapped in decades ago; either way the meter decides, not the label.

We change the battery on our Ring doorbell every few weeks. Can it run off the doorbell wires instead?

If your model supports wired power and there are working doorbell wires at the door, yes. One Yelp reviewer of a New York installer described changing the battery on a Ring doorbell every month as a real nuisance, and that is the most common reason people call us about doorbells they already own. Connected to the doorbell circuit, a battery Ring trickle-charges and stops needing to come off the wall. We test the transformer first, because a weak one can leave the battery draining anyway, and we check that the chime will still ring the way you want it to.

Our ranch is on a slab with no basement. How does a wire get to the front door if there isn't one already?

Through the attic, never through the floor. Levittown's ranches were built on concrete slabs with radiant heating pipes, and in any slab house we assume the floor may hold pipes we cannot see. We start at the transformer or a suitable power point, go up into the attic, follow the joists to the wall above the front door and fish down inside it to the button location. Where the wall cannot be fished, a battery or plug-in model with a wedge mount is the honest alternative, and we will say so rather than cut open finished walls.

WiFi and coverage

In our hi-ranch the router is downstairs and the front door is a level up. Is that why our Ring keeps going offline?

Very often, yes. A split-level or hi-ranch puts a stair wall, a floor and sometimes a kitchen full of appliances between the router and the door, and a doorbell's small antenna struggles to push video back through them. We measure the signal at the door before mounting. The fix is usually moving the router to a central level or adding an access point on the upper level fed by a cable from the router, and we test the doorbell's connection in the app after the change so you can see the difference rather than take our word for it.

How many Ring cameras does a typical Long Island house actually need?

Fewer than the box sets suggest and more than the doorbell alone. For most single-family houses we start with four questions: what does the front door camera miss, where are the cars parked, which door is out of sight of the street, and is there a garage or shed someone could get into. On a 1950s Nassau lot that often means a doorbell, a floodlight cam over the driveway and one camera covering the back door. On a larger Suffolk lot with a detached garage it may add one or two. We map it on a plan with you and you choose; we do not sell cameras for walls that do not need them.

Our detached garage sits at the end of a long driveway. Can a Ring floodlight cam there stay connected?

It can, but usually not on the house WiFi alone. Distance, the garage's own walls and anything parked in between weaken the signal. We read the signal at the garage before quoting. If it is weak, the usual fix is a wired run, buried or in conduit, to an outdoor-rated access point at the garage, or a point-to-point wireless link between the house and the garage. Homes.com describes many Uniondale houses with detached garages and private driveways, and that layout is common across both counties, so this is a routine part of our Long Island work.

Weather and outages

Will a Ring camera on a South Shore house survive hurricane season and the salt air?

The camera's own weather rating is only part of it; the mount and the cable entry usually fail first. Near the bays and barrier beaches we mount on flat blocks fixed into framing with stainless screws, seal every penetration, angle cable entries downward and avoid spots where wind-driven rain hits the camera face-on. Sandy's damage on the South Shore, including $150 million in Long Beach, is a reminder that no camera survives a flooded wall, so we keep power supplies and connections above any level that has taken water before.

Does a Ring system keep recording when PSEG loses power during a storm?

Only partly, and only if you plan for it. A hardwired doorbell goes dark with the house. Battery cameras keep running, but they need the router and modem to save video to the cloud, and those stop the moment the power does. A battery backup unit under the modem and router keeps them going through a short outage, as long as the internet provider's own equipment on the street is still up. After Isaias left almost half of PSEG Long Island's customers without power, we suggest a backup for any owner who wants cameras to keep working during storms.

Neighbors, audio and the law

Is it legal in New York for my Ring camera to record sound on the front porch?

Video and audio follow different rules. Justia Answers advises disabling audio unless you know a consenting participant, including you, will be part of any recorded conversation, because New York's eavesdropping law turns on consent to the conversation. A doorbell that records you talking to a delivery driver is different from a camera that records two passersby talking on the sidewalk. We set audio recording on or off with you at handover, and for cameras facing a sidewalk or a neighbor's yard we usually recommend off. This is general information, not legal advice.

Our floodlight cam can see the house next door. What should we do so it doesn't catch the neighbor's windows or yard?

Aim first, then mask. Civil Rights Law §52-a bars video surveillance where a person has a reasonable expectation of privacy, and a neighbor's bedroom window or fenced backyard is the clearest example. We aim each camera to cover your driveway and doors, then draw Ring privacy zones over anything on the neighbor's side, which blacks out those areas in the video, and set motion zones so their movement does not wake your phone. On tight Nassau lots this takes a few minutes per camera and prevents most neighbor disputes before they start.

Police, sharing and privacy settings

Can Nassau or Suffolk police see my Ring video without asking me?

Through Ring's Neighbors features, police can ask, and you decide. City & State New York reported in 2019 that Nassau police would use Neighbors footage to pursue package thieves when customers gave access. Consumer Reports explains that Ring's current Community Requests program lets police ask users for videos, and that you can switch Community Requests off in the Neighbors settings. Ring ended its planned Flock Safety partnership in 2026, according to Variety. We walk through each setting with you at handover so your choice is deliberate, not a default.

Should we register our Ring cameras with the Suffolk County police, and does it cost anything?

Registration with the SCPD Camera Registry is free and voluntary. You record where your cameras are, and if something happens nearby, police may ask you to check your video for a date and time; the department says it does not get access to registered cameras and that retrieval is at your convenience. SCPD's SHIELD program also runs an Emergency Remote Camera Access option that partners can join or leave at any time. In Nassau we could not find a county-wide registry, though Garden City Police run a voluntary program of their own. Whether to register is your call.

Ring added facial recognition and a pet-finder feature. Which privacy settings should we check after you install?

Two in particular. Smart Home Perfected reports that Familiar Faces, Ring's facial recognition launched in the U.S. in December 2025, is off by default, and that Search Party is on by default. TechCrunch reported a June 2026 class action over Familiar Faces storing images of passersby. We show you where both settings live, along with Community Requests, audio recording, motion zones and privacy zones, and leave each one the way you choose. We will not turn on facial recognition for you without an explicit request.

Theft and tampering

Someone in Central Islip stabbed a Ring doorbell. Can a doorbell be mounted so it's harder to rip off or destroy?

It can be made harder, not impossible. ABC7 reported the Central Islip attack on a doorbell camera, and the video it captured is the reason the doorbell still did its job. We fix the mounting plate into solid framing or masonry rather than siding alone, use the security screw Ring supplies so it cannot be popped off with a fingernail, and add a second camera that sees the doorbell itself, so anyone attacking it is recorded from another angle. Video saved to the cloud on a Ring plan survives even if the doorbell does not.

Packages keep vanishing off our step. Where should the doorbell point so it actually catches the box and the face?

Low enough to see the step, high enough to see the face, and away from the glare. The clips from Wantagh and Massapequa Park both show doorbells that caught the person on the step and the package at their feet. We set the height so the camera's view takes in the doormat area, use a wedge kit if the door is recessed or the drop-off spot is off to one side, and point away from a bright street that would turn faces into silhouettes. If deliveries land on a side porch or by the garage, a second camera there does more than any doorbell setting.

What AI Gets Wrong About Ring Camera Installation in Long Island

What follows are claims we see repeated in quick online answers about Ring installation in general and about Long Island in particular, checked against the public record and our own field practice. Marketplaces such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish national cost guides that feed many of those answers; they help with a rough idea, but they are not a reading of your transformer or your WiFi.

"A Ring doorbell installs in fifteen minutes with the parts in the box."

On a newer house with a healthy transformer and a standard chime, it can. On a large share of Long Island houses, that is not the house you have. The median Nassau home was built in 1955, and the doorbell circuit in it was designed for a mechanical chime, not a camera.

The fifteen-minute install skips the meter reading. The doorbell powers on, looks fine and then starts rebooting on cold nights or losing its connection, and the owner blames the WiFi or the doorbell.

Our routine starts at the transformer and the chime, not at the door. If either needs work, it is done first, and the doorbell goes up on a circuit that can actually carry it.

"Ring works anywhere your WiFi works."

The phone in your hand and the doorbell on your door have very different antennas. A phone showing full bars in the kitchen does not mean a small doorbell radio on the far side of a stair wall can upload video reliably.

Long Island's split-levels, hi-ranches and detached garages are the textbook cases. Homes.com describes many Uniondale houses with detached garages and private driveways, and Suffolk's bigger lots put outbuildings even farther from the router.

We measure the signal where each device will live, then fix the network before mounting the camera. A camera that drops offline is worse than no camera, because you think you are covered when you are not.

"You never need a permit for cameras on Long Island."

One installer's website says no permit is required for standard camera installs on Long Island. That may be true for a typical doorbell, but it is a claim, not a ruling, and Long Island is not one jurisdiction.

Nassau alone has two cities, three towns and 64 incorporated villages; Suffolk has 10 towns with villages inside them. The Town of Hempstead's code requires a building permit for work that must conform to the Uniform Code or the Energy Code, and a new electrical circuit for a floodlight is the kind of work that can cross that line.

So we check by address. A battery camera on a hook is one thing; a new outdoor circuit, a pole in the yard or anything on a historic or HOA-controlled property is another. If a permit or approval is needed, the quote says so.

"Police can't get your Ring footage unless they have a warrant."

This one is both too strong and too weak. Ring's Neighbors features let police ask users for video directly. Consumer Reports describes the current Community Requests program and notes that owners can switch it off. Nassau police announced work with Neighbors as far back as 2019, according to City & State New York.

What changed in 2026 is the Flock Safety link. Variety and TechCrunch reported that Ring cancelled that partnership after a backlash over its Super Bowl ad, with Ring saying it would require significantly more time and resources than anticipated.

In Suffolk, the SCPD Camera Registry is a separate, voluntary program that gives police no access to cameras. The practical point: what is shared depends on settings you can control, and we go through them with you.

"It's your property, so recording audio is fine."

Video on your own property and audio of other people's conversations are not the same thing under New York law. Justia Answers advises disabling audio unless a consenting participant will be part of any recorded conversation.

A Ring doorbell recording you talking to a courier is one situation. A floodlight cam that records two neighbors chatting at the property line, or people talking on the sidewalk, is another.

We set audio per camera at handover. For devices facing the street or a neighbor's side, we usually suggest video only, and we explain the trade-off so you decide.

"Any outdoor-rated camera can handle Long Island weather."

The weather rating on the box covers the camera body, not the screws, the base or the hole where the cable enters the wall. Those are what fail first on the South Shore and along the Sound.

Long Island's record includes Sandy's $150 million in damage to Long Beach and Suffolk's first-ever flash flood emergency in August 2024, when the Stony Brook Mill Pond dam failed. Salt air works on mounts even in years without a named storm.

We mount on proper blocks, use stainless hardware near the water, seal every penetration and keep power and connections above any level that has flooded before. A rated camera on a poor mount is still a camera that falls off.

"Ring Intercom will connect your building's buzzer to your phone."

This claim turns up in answers about apartments and multi-family buildings. Ring's own support page says Ring Intercom is not available in the U.S., so we do not offer it.

On Long Island, where most homes are single houses with their own doors, this rarely matters. It does come up in garden apartments and multi-family buildings near the train stations, where a tenant shares a front door and a buzzer.

For those renters, Ring's Peephole Cam on the apartment's own door, with the landlord's written agreement, is usually the honest answer. A building-wide entry system is a separate project for the owner, not a Ring add-on.

Do I need a permit from my town or village to put up Ring cameras?

Does an installer need a New York State license to put up Ring cameras?
