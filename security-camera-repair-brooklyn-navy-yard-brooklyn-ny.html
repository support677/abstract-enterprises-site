The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Navy Yard Properties Need Security Camera Repair

The Brooklyn Navy Yard is not a neighborhood of houses and storefronts. It is a 300-acre industrial park on Wallabout Bay, owned by the City and run by the not-for-profit Brooklyn Navy Yard Development Corporation, with more than 550 businesses and over 13,000 people working inside its gates. Its buildings range from brick Navy storehouses and the concrete bulk of Building 77 to new glass office space at Dock 72 and the manufacturing floors of Building 128. Almost every one of those businesses has something worth watching: tools and materials on a shop floor, finished goods waiting at a loading door, cameras and lenses in a studio cage, inventory in a warehouse, a cash drawer at a food hall counter. And almost every one of them runs its own cameras, because the landlord's security covers the Yard, not the inside of your unit.

That split is the first thing to understand about camera repair here. BNYDC runs a large security operation of its own. Its April 2025 request for proposals for a security assessment describes a Security Department with more than 90 staff, including 71 security officers and 8 sergeants, posted at the Sands Street, Cumberland, Clinton Avenue and Clymer Street gates, a ferry landing booth and a command center. The same document puts the Yard's own "CCTV (closed circuit television/surveillance) System and Digital Video Management" in the scope of the review, along with the "adequacy of existing wiring and infrastructure system to connect individual buildings with the central monitoring station." In plain terms, the landlord has its own cameras, its own recorders and its own monitoring room, and it reviews them every five or six years. None of that is the system on your ceiling. If the camera over your loading door goes dark, BNYDC's security plan does not fix it, and the Tenant Manual says tenants install their own door locks, that BNYDC does not keep keys to tenant spaces, and, in its rules for events, that tenants are responsible for the security within their leased space.

The second thing is how often the footage inside a unit turns out to matter. In 2016 DNAinfo reported that three employees of a B&H Photo Video warehouse at 63 Flushing Avenue, the Yard's own address, were arrested for stealing nearly $4,000 in iPads and an Apple Watch. The theft was found because "a security guard watching surveillance footage after returning to work from a holiday" saw one worker put two iPads in his backpack, and while reviewing the video he spotted two more. In January 2024, according to the Brooklyn Paper's 88th Precinct report, 16 spools of Cat 5E cable worth about $20,000 were taken from a rental company's locked vehicle at Steiner Studios, and the suspect was identified on security cameras. And in December 2024, Williamsburg 365 and Brownstoner reported arrests over copper wire cut from equipment at the Yard's shipyard, including about $10,000 of wire from a crane. Each of those cases turned on video that was recording, kept long enough, and clear enough to identify someone. A camera that quietly failed months earlier gives you none of that.

The third thing is that Yard camera systems fail for reasons that ordinary home systems rarely see. Shop floors are hard on equipment. Sawdust, metal dust, flour, steam and exhaust coat lenses and clog the fans in recorders. Forklifts and pallet jacks clip cameras mounted too low on columns. Big open floors invite cable runs that are longer than a network cable is meant to carry, which works on day one and then turns flaky as connections age. Companies grow, move up a floor, sublet half a unit or take over a space from another business, and the camera system gets split, extended or inherited without anyone writing down what is connected to what. Network changes are constant, too, because tenants switch internet providers and IT contractors, and a new router or firewall is the most common reason cameras disappear from the phone app overnight.

Water and power are the fourth layer. Hurricane Sandy put up to six feet of water across the campus in October 2012, and the Brooklyn Paper reported in January 2025 that FEMA had awarded $28.7 million toward roughly $100 million of damage to motors, electrical systems and building controls. The Local, a New York Times and CUNY neighborhood site, reported at the time that Kings County Distillery lost grain and some electronics when about five feet of water came into its building, and that the ceramic maker IceStone lost manufacturing equipment and spent about three weeks without power. Flooding returned during Ida in 2021 and again in a 2023 storm. For a camera system, that history is a reason to look at where the recorder and its power sit on ground-floor units near the water, and to check that a battery backup is actually holding up the recorder rather than just sitting beside it.

The converted Navy warehouse: long runs along the beams

The older brick buildings in the Yard were built as storehouses, shops and offices for the Navy, and most were later divided into units for fabricators, furniture makers, artists, food businesses and small manufacturers. Camera systems in these spaces tend to grow one camera at a time. The first installer stapled cable along a timber beam or clipped it to a steel joist, the next one added a camera on a different run, and a few years later nobody knows which cable at the recorder goes to which corner of the floor. The ceilings are high, often fifteen feet or more, so a dead camera on a beam means bringing a ladder that fits in the freight car or booking a lift.

Repairs here usually start with an inventory. We trace each cable from the recorder or switch to its camera, label both ends, and test the run. On long open floors the commonest single fault is a connector that has worked loose or corroded where a cable crosses a cold exterior wall, followed by cable crushed against a beam by something stored above it. Where a run turns out to be longer than a network cable is designed to carry, we tell you, because no amount of camera swapping will make that link reliable. The fix is a small switch closer to the cameras or a different route, and it is priced before we start.

The concrete giant: deep floors and many tenants per floor

Building 77 is the Yard's tallest building, sixteen stories and about 952,000 square feet, and its lowest eleven floors were built with walls around 25 inches thick and no windows until the 2017 renovation cut them in. In buildings like these a tenant's camera system is almost always self-contained inside the unit: cameras on the unit's ceiling and walls, cable along the unit's own ceiling, and a recorder on a shelf or in a small rack. What a repair cannot easily do is send cable through the building's structure, so we work with the paths that already exist and ask the property manager first about anything that would mean drilling, coring or using a shared riser.

The new manufacturing building: open floors and shared trays

Building 128, home to the Green Manufacturing Center completed in 2016 and to Newlab, and other renovated industrial buildings give tenants big open floors with exposed ceilings, sprinkler mains, ductwork and cable trays that several companies may use. Camera systems here are usually newer network cameras on PoE, which means the network switch is the heart of the system. The faults we find are switch ports that failed after a power event, switches that ran out of PoE budget when the company added access points and phones, and cameras that were mounted on the nearest steel member rather than where they could see the work.

The glass office building: finished ceilings and changing suites

Dock 72, the 675,000-square-foot office building on the water that topped out in 2017, and other newer office floors in the Yard have finished ceilings, building network infrastructure and lobbies run by the building. The repairs are mostly about the network. A camera that worked for years stops after the company moves to a new internet plan or an IT contractor puts the cameras on a different network segment, and nobody updates the recorder.

The single-story shed: wide spans and walls facing the water

Long single-story buildings in the Yard, like the 1,000-foot Building 293 with its solar roof, and the workshop sheds near the dry docks have wide roll-up doors and walls exposed to wind and spray off Wallabout Bay and the East River. Outdoor cameras on these walls fail the coastal way: connectors that corrode inside their boots, brackets that rust, front windows that haze. Inside, the fault is usually distance, because a camera at the far end of a long building may sit on the very edge of what its cable can carry.

Roof use in the Yard is prohibited except in emergencies unless your lease grants roof rights, so cameras and cables that a previous tenant put on a roof edge are worth a second look. If one has failed, we discuss moving it to a wall rather than repairing it where it is.

The stage and studio complex: quiet, dark and full of expensive gear

Steiner Studios, which opened in 2004, and the production companies around it keep cameras on equipment cages, loading doors and corridors, and the camera system has to work in a place that is often dark, often full of lighting and grip gear on carts, and sometimes recording sound. Equipment theft is the reason these systems matter. Field Notes

Field Notes: Checking a Camera System That Shares a Rack With the Office Network

These notes set out how our technicians approach a camera system in a Navy Yard tenant space, where the cameras, the office computers and the internet line often meet in the same small rack. They describe our standard checks, not a story from a particular job.

Start at the screen, then find the switch. The technician first notes which channels show video, which are black, which say "no signal" or "offline," and whether playback has gaps. Then the question is where the cameras actually connect. In a Yard unit the answer may be a PoE switch on the recorder's shelf, a switch in the office rack, or a port in a telecom closet down the hall, and finding it is half the diagnosis.

Check the power budget before the cameras. A PoE switch has a total amount of power it can share. When a company adds wireless access points, phones or extra cameras to the same switch over the years, it can run short, and the cameras that drop are the ones that happen to ask for power last. The technician reads the switch's power figures, or measures at the port, before replacing anything.

Ask who changed the network last. Many "dead" cameras in the Yard are alive and simply unreachable after a new router, a new firewall rule or a new IP scheme. The technician asks when the internet or IT setup last changed, checks whether the cameras and recorder are still on the addresses they were set up with, and coordinates with your IT person rather than overwriting their work.

Price before parts, and label before leaving. You hear the cause and get a written price before anything is replaced. When the repair is done, every traced cable is labeled at both ends, a short channel map is left with the company, and the recorder's clock and failure alerts are checked so the next problem announces itself.

Fault by Fault

Dust, Distance, Networks or Impact: How Navy Yard Camera Systems Break

Five causes account for most of the camera failures we find inside Yard tenant spaces. Knowing which one you are dealing with usually tells you what the repair will cost before anyone climbs a ladder.

Dust and residue: the slow fade on production floors

Woodshops, metal fabricators, bakeries, coffee roasters, breweries and distillers all put something in the air. Sawdust and metal fines settle on a camera's front window, flour and sugar dust form a film, and steam and cooking exhaust leave a greasy haze. The picture does not die; it fades over months until faces and plates are a blur, and at night the infrared light bounces off the film and turns the whole frame white. The same dust gets pulled into the recorder's fan and the switch's vents, where it raises the temperature and shortens the life of the hard drive.

The fix is usually cheap: clean the windows with the right cleaner, check the seals, and clean or relocate the recorder so it is not breathing the shop floor. On our Brooklyn list a clean and check for a blurry picture is $80. Where a camera is mounted in the path of exhaust or spray, moving it a few feet out of the plume does more good than any cleaning schedule.

Distance: long floors and the limits of a network cable

A standard network cable is designed to carry data and power about 100 meters, roughly 328 feet, from the switch to the camera. Some Yard buildings are much longer than that, and a camera at the far end of a big floor may be cabled right at the edge or past it. Those cameras often work at first, then start dropping out as connectors age, as more cameras share the switch, or as the cable warms and cools with the seasons. The tell is a camera that goes offline and comes back on its own, sometimes many times a day, while closer cameras on the same switch never miss.

Swapping that camera rarely helps. The repair is to shorten the run, usually with a small PoE switch or extender mounted closer to the far cameras and fed by one cable, and to test every run with a cable analyzer so the weak link is found rather than guessed. It is priced in writing before we start, and it is often less than replacing the cameras someone assumed were bad.

Networks: the IT change that took the cameras with it

This is the single most common reason a Yard tenant calls us. The company switches internet providers, gets a new router or firewall, moves to a new IT contractor, or puts the office on a new address range, and the next morning the phone app says "offline" for every camera. Often the cameras are still recording locally the whole time; the recorder has simply lost its path to the internet, or the cameras have lost their path to the recorder because their addresses no longer fit the network.

Repairs here are mostly configuration, and a configuration fix with no parts usually falls within the $150 service call. The important part is doing it without breaking the office network, so we work with your IT person, keep the cameras on their own network segment where possible, change default passwords if they are still in place, and write down what we set so the next IT change does not repeat the problem.

Impact: forklifts, ladders and moving racks

Cameras on columns and low beams get hit. A forklift mast, a ladder carried through a doorway, a pallet stacked one row too high, or a rolling rack pushed into a corner can crack a dome, snap a bracket or pull a cable out of its connector. The camera may keep working at a crooked angle for weeks before anyone notices it now shows the ceiling. Cameras over loading doors take their own beating from trucks backing in and from the doors themselves.

If the camera body is fine, a new dome or front cover costs $30 to $80 in parts plus labor on our Brooklyn list. If the camera is broken, a same-spec replacement is $250 to $600. Either way we look at whether the camera belongs where it was: a few feet higher, or on the other side of the column, often keeps it out of harm's way for good.

Heat, power and age: recorders in closets

Recorders and switches in the Yard often live in a closet, under a desk, or on a shelf in a mechanical corner, and a lot of them are hot. Heat wears out hard drives, and a drive that is failing usually shows it first as gaps in playback, recordings that stop at random, or a recorder that beeps and shows a disk error. Power interruptions add to it: a recorder without a battery backup takes every flicker as a hard shutdown, and each one is a chance to corrupt the drive or the settings. Older recorders also lose their clock settings, so footage carries the wrong date and time.

The repair depends on what we find. A hard drive swap is $200 to $450 by capacity on our Brooklyn list. A DVR that is not working, including the drive and labor, averages $300 to $700, and an NVR that is not recording runs $400 to $900 depending on the model. If the recorder is past saving, a replacement with a drive and full reconfiguration is $700 to $1,500. In every case we check the clock, set the recorder to warn you when a drive or camera fails, and recommend a battery backup sized for the recorder and switch if one is missing.

Business owners rarely call to say a camera is broken in principle. Internal losses and why retention matters

The 2016 B&H warehouse case is a useful reminder of how losses inside a business usually surface. The guard who found it was reviewing footage after a holiday, and the thefts he saw had happened on different days, weeks apart. Inventory shortfalls are often discovered at a count, a month or more after the fact. A recorder that keeps only a week of video, because its drive is small or because it records every camera at full quality around the clock, cannot help with that. During a repair we check how many days the system actually holds, and if it is short, we show you the options: a bigger drive, recording on motion for quiet cameras, or lower settings on cameras that do not need full detail.

Cameras, employees and New York's monitoring notice law

Many Yard businesses use cameras partly to watch work areas, and New York has rules about monitoring employees. Since May 7, 2022, the state Civil Rights Law has required private employers who monitor employees electronically to give new hires written notice and get their acknowledgment, and to post a notice "in a conspicuous place" where monitored employees can see it. The law firm Kelley Drye's summary notes penalties of up to $500 for a first violation, $1,000 for a second and $3,000 for each one after, enforced by the Attorney General, and points out that the law's text speaks mainly of phone, email and internet monitoring. Whether your cameras fall under it is a question for your own counsel, not for a camera technician, but a repair that adds or moves cameras in work areas is a good moment to check your notices are posted. Cameras in restrooms, locker rooms and changing areas are a different matter altogether, and we do not install or repair them there.

State licensing. The New York Department of State licenses companies that install, service or maintain security systems, and its rules treat a camera system used to detect or monitor intrusion or theft as a security system. These are the situations Navy Yard business owners, office managers and IT contacts bring to us. They are grouped by where the trouble usually starts, and each answer reflects how the Yard's buildings and rules change the repair.

The landlord, the gate and the building

BNYDC has cameras all over the Yard. Can its security team see our cameras, or can we get theirs?

They are separate systems. BNYDC's cameras, recorders and command center belong to the landlord and are part of its own security program, which its 2025 assessment reviewed. Your cameras record to your recorder and are seen by whoever your company gives access to. If an incident outside your unit might be on a Yard camera, ask BNYDC Security directly; that request goes through them, not through us. We do not link a tenant system to the landlord's unless both sides ask for it in writing.

The technician has to get through the gate. Who registers them, and does that slow down an urgent visit?

Your company registers us, the same way you register any visitor. For a single visit the Yard's visitor system issues a one-day QR code, and for longer jobs BNYDC Security issues temporary contractor IDs. When you book, give us the name of the person who will register the technician and the gate you want us to use, and we send the technician's name and vehicle details. Registering the day before keeps an urgent visit from losing its first half-hour at the gate.

The camera that died is on the corridor wall outside our door. Is that ours to fix or the building's?

It depends who put it there, and the cable usually tells the story. If it runs back into your unit and shows up on your recorder, it is your camera and we can repair it, but because it sits in a shared hallway, we let the property manager know before we work there. If it runs to a building closet and is not on your recorder at all, it is probably the landlord's, and the repair is theirs to arrange. We check which it is before you pay for anything.

Internet, IT and the office network

We moved our internet to a different provider and every camera went offline on the app. Is the system broken?

Almost certainly not. When all the cameras drop off the app on the same day as an internet change, the recorder has usually lost its route out through the new router, or its saved network settings no longer match. In most cases it has been recording locally the whole time. We check playback on site to confirm, then restore remote access using the manufacturer's supported method instead of opening ports to the internet. With no parts needed, this usually falls within the $150 service call.

Our IT contractor wants the cameras off the office network. Can that be done without new cable?

Usually, yes. If the cameras already run to their own PoE switch or to the recorder's built-in camera ports, they can be kept on a separate network with only the recorder connected to the office side. If they share the office switch, a managed switch can put them on their own segment over the same cables. We agree the plan with your IT contact before changing anything, and leave them a written list of what was set so the camera network does not surprise them later.

Production floors and warehouses

The camera at the far end of our floor drops out several times a day and comes back by itself. Is it dying?

Usually the camera is fine and the cable is the problem. A camera that comes and goes on its own, while closer cameras on the same switch never miss, is the classic sign of a run near or beyond the roughly 100-meter limit of a network cable, or of a tired connector along the way. We test the run with a cable analyzer. If it is too long, a small switch or extender closer to the far cameras fixes it, and the price is written down before any work starts.

A forklift clipped the camera on a column, and now it shows the ceiling. Do we repair the mount or replace the camera?

Check the picture first. If the image is still clean once the camera is pointed back down, it is a mount and dome repair: a new bracket, and a new dome or cover at $30 to $80 in parts plus labor on our Brooklyn list. If the picture is cracked, dark or blotchy, the camera is replaced, $250 to $600 for a same-spec swap. Either way, we look at moving it higher or to the side of the column away from traffic, so it does not get hit again.

Our bakery's cameras get a white film every few weeks. Is there a camera that copes better with flour and steam?

Partly. A camera with a smooth, sealed housing and a sun hood collects less than an open dome, and a fixed lens behind flat glass is easier to wipe than a curved bubble. But the bigger gain comes from where the camera hangs. Moved out of the line of the ovens, the mixers or the steam vent by a few feet, a camera can go months between cleanings instead of weeks. We look at the airflow before suggesting new hardware, and we price a clean and check at $80 if that is all it needs.

The recorder in our shop's closet beeps and says the disk is abnormal. What does that mean, and is our video safe?

It means the recorder has detected a problem with its hard drive: failing sectors, a drive that dropped offline, or a drive it can no longer write to. Video already on the drive may still be readable, so if you need anything from the last few weeks, tell us before anyone formats or swaps the drive. We export what you need first, then replace the drive, which is $200 to $450 by capacity on our Brooklyn list, and look at why it failed, because a closet full of shop dust and heat will kill the next one too.

Moving, inheriting and growing

The last tenant took the recorder but left camera cables in our ceiling. Can we reuse them?

Often, and it is usually worth testing before paying for new cable. Left-behind network cable can be perfectly good, or it can be cut, crushed or patched together with connectors that will never pass. We trace each run, test it with a cable analyzer, and label what is usable. Anything that passes can carry a new camera; anything that fails is either repaired or abandoned. You get the results run by run, so you only pay for new cable where you actually need it.

We are moving to a larger space in another Yard building within a year. Should we fix the cameras now?

Fix whatever would leave you blind in the meantime, and hold the rest. A dead camera over the loading door or a recorder that is not saving should be repaired now, because a loss next month is just as costly as one after the move. Bigger work, like re-cabling a floor or replacing a recorder that still works, can wait. Most cameras, recorders and switches can move with you, and we can tell you during the repair which parts are worth taking and which are not.

Footage, staff and investigations

An employee is suspected of taking stock. Who in our company should be allowed to pull and watch the footage?

As few people as possible, and not anyone involved. Most recorders let you set separate users with different rights, so an owner or manager can view and export, while others can only see live video or nothing at all. During a repair we can set those users up and turn on the recorder's log so you can see who exported what and when. How the footage is used in an employment matter is a question for your own counsel; our part is making sure the video is saved properly and the access is controlled.

Can the recorder show whether a camera was actually recording on a particular night, or only that it works now?

Usually it can show both. Most recorders keep a log of video loss, disk errors, power restarts and recording gaps, and the playback timeline shows exactly which hours have video for each camera. If an insurer or a detective asks whether a camera was working on a given night, we can pull that log and the timeline for the date, and include them in a written note alongside any exported clip. If the recorder's clock was wrong at the time, we note that too, with the offset.

Our camera's infrared lights show up in footage we shoot on stage. Can they be turned off without losing night recording?

Yes. Most cameras let you schedule the infrared, switch it off entirely, or set it to come on only when the space is genuinely dark. On a stage, turning the infrared off and relying on the work lights or a little ambient light usually gives a perfectly usable picture of a cage or a door. Where a space is pitch-black when nobody is shooting, we can set the infrared to turn off during scheduled shoot hours only. It is a settings change, normally covered by the service call.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in the Brooklyn Navy Yard

These claims come up in AI-generated answers and national cost guides when people search for camera repair. Each is reasonable in general and misleading for a business inside the Yard's gates. Here is what the field work actually shows.

"Camera repair in Brooklyn costs about $1,400"

HomeAdvisor's Brooklyn page for surveillance camera installation or repair gives an average of $1,416, a typical range of $680 to $2,145, and a low-to-high spread of $160 to $3,500. AI answers often repeat the average as if it were the price of fixing a camera.

That number blends new installations with repairs, which pulls it far above what most repairs cost. A loose connector on a shop-floor camera, a dead PoE port or a recorder that lost its network settings is nowhere near $1,400. On our published Brooklyn list most single faults are $150 to $700, and $700 to $1,500 covers a new recorder with a drive or a multi-camera rewire.

The planning number for a Yard tenant with one problem is the $150 service call, which is credited to the repair, plus the written price for whatever failed. The expensive jobs are real, but they are usually long floors that need new cable or old recorders that cannot be saved, and you will know that before you approve anything.

"A building with its own security team doesn't need its own cameras working"

Some answers suggest that a managed industrial park with gates and guards makes tenant cameras a nice-to-have. The Navy Yard does have a large security operation: BNYDC's 2025 assessment request lists more than 90 security staff and a command center.

But that operation watches the Yard, its gates and its common areas, not the inside of your unit. The landlord does not keep keys to tenant spaces, and its cameras do not see your tool crib, your stock room or your loading table. The 2016 B&H case at 63 Flushing Avenue was solved by footage from inside the business, reviewed by its own guard.

"If the camera shows no picture, the camera is dead"

Generic troubleshooting answers often jump to buying a new camera when a tile goes black. It is the most expensive guess you can make.

In Yard spaces, the camera is often not the failed part. A PoE switch that ran out of power, a cable past its distance limit, a connector shaken loose by machinery, or a network address that changed in an IT update will all show a black tile with a perfectly good camera at the far end. Replace the camera and the new one goes black too.

The cheaper route is to test the chain in order: power, cable and connector, switch port, recorder, then the camera. That is what the service call is for, and it is why we write the cause down before the price.

"Wi-Fi cameras are the easy fix for a dead wired camera"

A common AI suggestion is to replace a failed wired camera with a wireless one and skip the cable work entirely.

In buildings like Building 77, with walls around two feet thick on its lower floors, or in steel-and-concrete warehouses full of racking and machinery, Wi-Fi does not travel well. A wireless camera that works on a bench near the access point may stutter or drop out at the far end of the floor, and a busy production floor is full of equipment that can add interference. Battery cameras add a charging chore on top.

Where a wired camera has failed, the existing cable is usually the most reliable path, and testing it costs far less than discovering that a wireless replacement cannot hold a connection. We will still fit a wireless camera where it genuinely makes sense, such as a spot no cable can reach, but we will say so honestly.

"Cloud storage means you never lose footage"

Some answers say that if the footage is in the cloud, recorder failures do not matter.

Most wired camera systems in Yard units record locally to a recorder, and only send clips or alerts to the cloud, if anything. If the drive is failing, those local recordings are at risk whatever the app shows. Even true cloud cameras depend on an internet line that is shared with the office, and a busy upload or a provider outage can leave gaps. A cloud subscription can also lapse when the person whose card or email it was tied to leaves the company.

Cloud backup is a good second copy. But the reliable record is still a healthy recorder, with a drive that is tested, a clock that is right, and an alert that tells you when either fails.

"Hikvision and Dahua cameras are banned, so they all have to come out"

AI answers often say that because of federal rules, Hikvision and Dahua cameras must be ripped out and cannot be serviced. Many Yard systems installed over the past fifteen years use those brands or relabeled versions of them.

The FCC has put both companies on its Covered List, which restricts new equipment authorizations, and in 2026 it opened further rulemaking on Covered List firms. But as the Security Industry Association's Jake Parker told Security Info Watch, Hikvision and Dahua "are not telecom service providers," and a related proceeding on older device authorizations "explicitly would not affect continued use or operation of already-purchased communications equipment." Equipment you already own can generally keep running. Separate rules apply to federal contractors and some government-funded sites.

What changes is the repair path. Replacement parts in those brands can be harder to find, so when one fails we look at whether a compatible camera from another maker will work with your recorder, and if you are a federal contractor, we tell you to check your contract's requirements before choosing. We do not give legal advice on that point; we tell you what will work technically.

"Swapping a camera is a handyman job"

Plenty of answers treat camera repair as something anyone with a ladder can do in an afternoon.

For your own system, in your own space, you are free to try. But in New York the Department of State requires a license for companies that install or service security systems, and camera systems used to watch for theft or intrusion fall under that. In the Yard you also have BNYDC's rules on alterations, electrical work, roofs and shared spaces to think about, and a handyman who drills through a wall or taps a power circuit can put your lease in default.

If you hire someone, ask for their license number and a certificate of insurance, and ask what they will do if the fault turns out to be the network or the cable rather than the camera. Ours is NYS license #12000287431, and our written price tells you exactly what we are fixing.
