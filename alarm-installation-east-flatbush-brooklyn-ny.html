The Neighborhood

Why East Flatbush Properties Need Alarm Installation

East Flatbush is one of the few Brooklyn neighborhoods with a community district all to itself. Wikipedia describes Community District 17 as consisting entirely of East Flatbush and its subsections: Erasmus on the west side, Farragut in the middle and Remsen Village and Rugby to the east. Its main ZIP code is 11203, it counted 135,619 residents at the 2010 census, and it is patrolled by the 67th Precinct from a station house at 2820 Snyder Avenue. The housing is a mix that an alarm installer has to take one building at a time: attached brick row houses, many with a driveway and a deck at the back; detached and semi-detached two-family houses from the 1920s and 1930s; single-family houses with garages; a historic block of limestone and brownstone row houses on East 25th Street; and a handful of very large apartment complexes such as Flatbush Gardens and Rutland Plaza.

The houses explain most of what we do here. Brownstoner’s profile of 649 to 677 East 91st Street, between Avenue A and Avenue B in Remsen Village, describes a row of detached brick two-family houses designed in 1932 by the architect Irving Kirshenblit, each doorway under its own peaked hood because each house holds two apartments, and each house with a two-car garage in back, one garage for each apartment. Listings in the neighborhood describe brick row houses “with deck, driveway” and Jazz Age single-family houses with a fireplace and a garage. Every one of those features is a door: the second front door, the side gate, the deck door, the garage door, the door from the garage into the yard. A kit sold to cover a front door and a living room covers very little of a house like this. Our survey starts at the side path and the back of the lot.

The second thing that shapes an alarm here is who sleeps where. Many East Flatbush houses hold more than one household, and some hold people on every level, including below the first floor. On a morning in March 2026, News 12 reported, a fire tore through the basement of a home on New York Avenue near Avenue D, a basement the report says had allegedly been converted into rooms. A tenant, Lewis Dawkins, woke to the smell of smoke, saw flames coming from a neighbor’s basement room and ran upstairs to warn the others, who called 911. The Department of Buildings told News 12 it had closed complaints about the conversion after its inspectors were twice denied access. A month later an overnight fire in a third-floor apartment on Kingston Avenue killed an 80-year-old woman and her 59-year-old son, and some neighbors on the same floor told News 12 they did not hear any alarms. We are not fire investigators and we do not know what detectors either building had. What these reports tell an installer is that in this neighborhood the alarm plan should include the people asleep upstairs, not only the property, and that a smoke or heat sensor reporting to the panel belongs in the first conversation, not the upsell.

Crime in the 67th Precinct has fallen, and this page says so plainly. The NYPD’s CompStat report for the week ending November 8, 2026 shows 114 burglaries so far this year against 158 at the same point in 2025, a drop of 27.8 percent, with robbery down 15.8 percent and grand larceny down 13.5 percent. CrimeGrade, which grades neighborhoods from police data, gives East Flatbush an A for burglary: about 96 reported a year, or 1.3 per 1,000 residents against 2.4 nationally. The same CompStat sheet also shows burglaries in the most recent 28 days running at 14 against 10 a year earlier, a 40 percent rise over a short window. Averages across a precinct of this size say little about one back door. What a homeowner needs to know is how exposed their own side path, basement and garage are, and whether anybody would find out quickly if one were forced. Those are questions an installer can answer for one house.

The break-ins that do reach the news show what an alarm can and cannot do. In May 2023, News 12 reported, a crew forced the door of an optical shop on Church Avenue; when the shop’s alarm went off they ran through a side alley and into a laundromat on East 18th Street, and police said the group left with around $25,000 worth of goods and the store’s DVR. The alarm worked: it sounded and the burglars left. It did not stop them carrying out what they had already reached, or the recorder that held the video. In a separate case, News 12 reported that three people entered a home near Schenectady Avenue and East New York Avenue, threatened a woman inside with a firearm and carried off a safe holding about $15,000. Both cases point to the same lesson. Where the valuables sit inside the building, and how fast the right person hears about a forced door, matter more than how loud the siren is.

Power is the last local factor. On June 25, 2025, Con Edison asked about 100,000 customers in parts of Brownsville, Crown Heights, East Flatbush, East New York, Flatbush and Ocean Hill to conserve electricity while crews repaired equipment. On January 14, 2026, overhead wires caught fire at East 48th Street and Church Avenue just before 3 a.m., cutting power to 76 Con Edison customers, and residents of one building were evacuated because of high carbon monoxide readings. An alarm that loses power or depends on a home router goes quiet exactly when a block is dark. Every panel we install here reports over a cellular communicator and carries a backup battery, and where there is a fuel-burning boiler or stove, a carbon monoxide sensor on the panel is part of our standard proposal.

Need Repair in East Flatbush?

A Garage Zone That Reads Open, a Dialer That Went Quiet With the Landline, or a Smoke Sensor That Chirps at 3 a.m.: Alarm Repair in East Flatbush

A lot of East Flatbush houses already have an alarm on the wall, often one put in decades ago by an owner who has since passed the house to the next generation. The calls we get about them tend to fall into a few groups. The old system used a dialer that called the monitoring company over a copper phone line, and when the family moved to cable or fiber it stopped reporting without anybody noticing. A contact on a garage door or a basement window has worked loose, so the panel refuses to arm. A smoke sensor or a wireless contact is chirping for a low battery in the middle of the night. A keypad shows a trouble message nobody understands, or the tenant who knew the code has moved out. Or a shop changed hands and the new owner inherited a panel without the installer code. Most of these are sorted out on the first visit, and a hardwired system whose wiring is sound can usually keep that wiring with a new panel and a cellular communicator.

What We Install

Alarm Installation Services in East Flatbush

Detached and semi-detached houses, side path first

A detached house in Farragut or Remsen Village has four sides to approach from, and a semi-detached one has three. We cover the side gate and the rear door first, then the garage, the basement and the ground-floor windows nobody can see from the street, and finish with the front door and a video doorbell. The motion detector goes where anyone coming in from the yard or up from the basement has to pass.

Two-family and three-family partitions

When an owner lives on one floor and rents the other, each household gets its own area on the panel, its own codes and its own app access, with shared doors such as the hall, the basement and the garage assigned to whoever uses them. The tenant’s floor can be armed while the owner is home, and neither household can disarm the other.

Basement and life-safety layer

Where people sleep in the basement or a boiler and gas meters sit below the bedrooms, smoke, heat and carbon monoxide sensors report to the same panel as the burglary sensors, sound on every floor and reach the owner’s phone and, if wanted, the central station. Water sensors near the boiler and the washer can be added on the same visit.

Apartments in the large complexes

In Flatbush Gardens, Rutland Plaza and the other big buildings, a wireless system protects your own apartment door and any reachable windows, with nothing fixed in the hallway. It reports over cellular, so it does not need the building’s wiring, and it comes out cleanly when you move.

Shops on Church, Utica and Nostrand

A storefront here usually has a gate and glass at the front, a back door to a yard or alley, a cellar and sometimes a roof hatch, with apartments overhead. We protect the gate, the glass and the rear first, put interior sensors where the stock and the register are, and add hold-up and duress options for the hours the shop is open.

Medical and professional offices

Offices near Kings County Hospital, SUNY Downstate and Kingsbrook Jewish Medical Center often have a waiting room, exam rooms and a back room with records or sample medications. Each room can be its own zone with its own short list of users, so the cleaning crew can come in without opening the room that matters most.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for East Flatbush’s Architecture

Six kinds of building make up most of our alarm work in East Flatbush. Each one has a different weak side, a different number of households and a different answer to the question of where the panel, the keypads and the life-safety sensors should go.

The detached two-family with a rear garage

Two front doors, a side driveway, a garage at the back with one bay per apartment, and a basement shared by both households. It needs two alarm areas, a plan for who owns the garage and the cellar, and coverage on the side nobody watches.

The attached brick row house with a driveway and deck

Party walls on both sides, a stoop at the front, and at the back a deck door, a driveway or a shared rear lane. The front is watched by the whole block; the back is where we start.

The house with a lived-in basement

Any house where someone sleeps below the first floor, whether a family member, a tenant in a legal unit or a grown child. Smoke, heat and carbon monoxide sensors on the panel matter as much here as the door contacts.

The East 25th Street row house

One of the 56 limestone and brownstone row houses in the historic district, with rounded or angled bays and original details. Wireless sensors and interior keypads keep the facade untouched.

The apartment in a large complex

A unit in a six-story building at Flatbush Gardens or in one of Rutland Plaza’s five buildings. The alarm covers your own door, any windows reachable from a fire escape or the ground, and life-safety sensors inside.

The storefront with apartments above

A shop on Church Avenue, Utica Avenue or Nostrand Avenue, usually with a gate, a back door, a cellar and families living overhead. The alarm has to protect the stock overnight without turning every false trip into a night of noise upstairs.

The detached two-family: two households, one lot, five or six doors

The 1932 houses on East 91st Street that Brownstoner profiled are a clean example of the type: brick, detached, two apartments, a separate entrance for each under its own hood, and a two-car garage behind with one garage for each apartment. Not every two-family in the neighborhood is laid out this way, but where one is, it changes the alarm question from “how many sensors” to “whose sensor is it.” The owner’s apartment and the tenant’s apartment each need an area of their own, so one can be armed while the other is home. The two front doors each get a contact, and each household’s keypad goes just inside its own door. The basement, the side gate and the rear yard door usually belong to the owner’s area, because the owner is the one who goes down to the boiler and takes the bins out. The garage is the part people forget. A garage bay that belongs to the tenant should report to the tenant; a bay that holds the owner’s car and tools should report to the owner. A garage at the back of a lot is a long way from the panel, so we check signal strength out there before promising a wireless contact, and use a repeater when the reading is weak.

The side driveway is the approach that nobody watches. On a detached or semi-detached house it runs past the kitchen and the side windows to the back, and anyone walking down it is out of sight of the street within a few steps. The windows along it are often low enough to reach from the ground. On these houses we put contacts on every side window a person could open from outside, a contact and often a glass-break detector on the rear door, and a motion detector in the room that the side door or the rear door opens into. A floodlight camera over the driveway can be tied into the alarm, so that it lights the drive and records whenever the system trips, and so that the owner can look before anyone calls the police.

The basement of a two-family needs its own decision. If it is used only for the boiler, the meters and storage, it gets a contact on its outside door and any windows at grade, and a motion detector at the foot of the stairs. If either household uses it as living space, the motion detector moves or comes out, and the life-safety sensors become the priority. We talk about that in its own section below.

The attached brick row house: the block watches the front, nobody watches the back

East Flatbush’s attached brick row houses usually have a stoop or a short walk in front, a parlor or living room behind the front windows, and at the back some combination of a kitchen door, a deck, a yard and a driveway or garage. The front of a row house on an owner-occupied block is seen by dozens of people every day. The back faces the yards of the next street and a fence. So our list for a row house begins at the rear door, the deck door and the basement door or windows, then the ground-floor back windows, then a motion detector on the path from the back of the house to the stairs. The front door gets a contact and, often, a video doorbell. Upstairs windows that open onto a deck roof or an extension roof are treated as ground-floor windows, because they are.

Party walls matter for two reasons. First, sound: an outdoor siren on an attached house is heard by the neighbors on both sides as loudly as by the owner, so we place it with that in mind and keep its run time well inside the city’s fifteen-minute limit. Second, signal: thick brick between rooms can weaken wireless sensors at the far end of the house, so the panel goes near the middle of the building and every sensor is tested from its final position before we leave.

The East 25th Street Historic District: keep the facade as it is

The Landmarks Preservation Commission’s 2020 designation describes 56 row houses on both sides of East 25th Street between Clarendon Road and Avenue D, built between 1909 and 1912 by Glucroft & Glucroft for the Henry Meyer Building Company, in the Renaissance Revival style with limestone or brownstone fronts and rounded or angled bays. In houses like these we keep everything inside: small wireless contacts on the inside of the sash and the door frame, keypads in the hall rather than outside, and any siren inside or at the back. Bay windows have several sashes; each one that opens gets its own contact, or one glass-break detector covers the whole bay where that suits the room. If you are planning work on the front of a house in the district, the Commission is the place to ask before anything is fixed to it; an alarm planned this way does not need to touch the front at all.

The apartment in a large complex: your door, your windows, your alarm

Flatbush Gardens, on the site of the old Flatbush Water Works, is 59 six-story brick buildings with 2,469 apartments, and Rutland Plaza on East 93rd Street is five buildings with 428 units. Both have been in the news for building doors that do not lock. In September 2024 News 12 reported that a woman was robbed in her Flatbush Gardens apartment after a man got into the building and kicked her door, and a tenant told the station, “if the basic front door isn’t working, yeah, I feel unsafe.” In July 2025 tenants at Rutland Plaza said front doors there were routinely left unlocked. An alarm inside one apartment cannot fix a lobby door; that is the owner’s legal duty, and HPD treats a building entrance door that will not close and latch as an immediately hazardous violation. What an apartment alarm can do is tell you, the moment your own door or a reachable window opens when it should not, and let the monitoring center send help.

For apartments we use a wireless panel with a cellular communicator, a contact on the apartment door, contacts on windows by a fire escape or on the ground floor, a motion detector in the main room, and smoke and carbon monoxide sensors. Nothing goes in the hallway or on the building’s wiring, and everything comes off cleanly with small screw holes at most when you leave. A keypad can sit inside the door, or the system can be run entirely from key fobs and the phone app for a household that prefers not to remember codes.

The storefront with apartments above: protect the stock, spare the neighbors

Shops on Church Avenue, Utica Avenue and Nostrand Avenue usually sit under one or two floors of apartments, with a roll-down gate and a glass front, a back door to a yard or a narrow passage, and a cellar reached by a trap door or an inside stair. We treat the gate and the glass as one layer, the back door and the cellar as a second, and the interior, where the register, the stock room and any safe are, as a third. The siren is inside the shop, loud enough to drive a burglar out and short enough that the families upstairs are not kept up for long. Separate sections below describe what the Church Avenue burglaries teach about the third layer.

Below the First Floor

Planning an Alarm for a House Where Someone Sleeps Over the Boiler

Most alarm pages treat the basement as a place a burglar might come in. In East Flatbush we also treat it as the place a fire is most likely to start out of sight of the people asleep upstairs. The basement usually holds the boiler, the water heater, the electric panel and the gas meters, and in many houses it also holds a family room, a grown child’s bedroom or a rental. The March 2026 fire on New York Avenue near Avenue D started, tenants told News 12, behind a basement wall, and they believed it was electrical; the FDNY had not confirmed a cause. The person who raised the alarm did so by smell. A smoke or heat sensor on the panel, with sirens on every floor, does not depend on someone waking up.

So in any house where the basement holds equipment or people, our proposal includes three things that a burglary-only kit leaves out. A smoke detector at the top of the basement stairs and, where the ceiling height allows, a heat detector near the boiler and the electric panel, where cooking steam and dust from storage will not trigger it. A carbon monoxide sensor in the basement and one near the bedrooms, because a boiler or water heater that vents badly fills the house from the bottom up. And sounders on the upper floors, so that a detector in the basement is heard in the top-floor bedrooms with the doors shut. All of these report to the same panel and the same app as the door contacts, so the owner hears about smoke in the basement while at work, and the monitoring center can call the fire department if nobody answers.

These sensors work alongside the detectors the law already requires, not instead of them. HPD requires smoke detectors in every dwelling unit of a multiple dwelling, and carbon monoxide detectors in non-owner-occupied one- and two-family homes that have fuel-burning appliances, within fifteen feet of the entrance to each sleeping room. Local Law 157, as SiteCompli summarizes it, adds an approved natural gas detector by January 1, 2027 for multiple dwellings and for non-owner-occupied one- and two-family homes with gas piping, placed in the same room as a gas appliance, three to ten feet from it horizontally, and within twelve inches of the ceiling if it is on a wall. Hardwired gas alarms must be installed by a licensed electrical contractor with permits; battery and plug-in units can be installed by owners or maintenance staff. We do not do electrical work, so we will tell you plainly which devices we can put in and which need your electrician.

There is one thing an alarm installer will not do, and it is worth saying here. We protect the doors, windows and rooms of the house as it is, and we put life-safety sensors wherever people sleep. We do not inspect, certify or approve basement living space; whether a basement unit is legal is a question for the Department of Buildings and the owner’s architect or expediter. If you are buying a house where a previous owner divided the basement into rooms, tell us at the survey, and we will place the detectors so that everyone sleeping in the house is covered while you sort the rest out.

Nostrand Avenue forms the western edge of Flatbush Gardens and has storefronts of its own. A shop that backs onto the yards of houses or small apartment buildings needs its rear door treated as seriously as its gate.

Medical offices and pharmacies near Kings County Hospital and SUNY Downstate need zones for records rooms and medication storage, and user lists that change as staff come and go.

Remsen Avenue and Rutland Road

Toward the eastern end of the neighborhood, near the Sutter Avenue–Rutland Road station at East 98th Street, a small shop with one door, one gate and a cellar is a good fit for a compact system.

Churches, clinics and community rooms

Congregations, day programs and community groups across the district use rooms at odd hours. Separate codes for each group and a clear record of who opened the door last are usually what the trustees ask for first.

What the Church Avenue optical burglary teaches about the inside of a store

The News 12 report on the May 2023 burglary is worth reading closely, because it describes an alarm that did its job and a loss that still ran to about $25,000. The crew forced the door of the optical shop on Church Avenue. The alarm went off. They ran, through a side alley and into a laundromat on East 18th Street, and police said they left with around $25,000 worth of goods and the DVR. In other words, the door contact and the siren did what they are designed to do, which is make noise and send a signal the moment the door opens. The time between that signal and the burglars’ exit was long enough for them to reach the displays and the recorder.

An alarm cannot make that time zero, but the layout inside the store decides how much it is worth to a burglar. We put a glass-break detector and a contact on the front so the signal comes as the glass goes, not after someone is already inside. We put an interior motion detector covering the path to the most valuable stock, so a second signal confirms an intrusion and helps the monitoring center treat it as real. Where the stock lives in a back room or a cabinet, that room or cabinet gets its own contact. And the recorder goes where a burglar is least likely to look, locked away from the sales floor, with its footage also reaching the owner’s phone, so the video does not leave with the DVR.

The side alley in that report matters too. Many storefronts here share a passage or a yard with the building next door, and a burglar who is chased out of one shop can be in the next one’s back door within seconds. Neighboring owners who use the same installer can agree to let each other’s alarm notifications reach a shared contact, or simply exchange numbers so that one owner’s alert prompts a look at the other’s camera. That is a conversation between neighbors, but it is one we are glad to help set up.

Late-closing shops: the hours when the alarm is off

In February 2024, News 12 reported, a man displayed a gun at a deli and grocery on Church Avenue at about 10:40 p.m. and took $600 from the register. The store was open. A burglar alarm is not armed during business hours, so for those hours we add a different set of tools: a hold-up button under the counter that sends a silent signal, a duress code that disarms the panel in front of a robber while quietly calling for help, and an entry chime on the back door so the person at the register knows when it opens. None of these make noise, which is the point. The signal goes to the monitoring center and to the owner, and nobody behind the counter is asked to take a risk.

Back doors, cellars and roof hatches

The back of a storefront is where an overnight burglar has the most time. A steel back door to a yard gets a heavy-duty contact and, on doors that have been pried before, a shock sensor that reacts to forcing before the door opens. The cellar gets its own zone, because a cellar trap door or a sidewalk hatch can be the quietest way in, and a motion detector at the foot of the stairs confirms anyone moving down there. If the building has a roof hatch reachable from neighboring roofs, it gets a contact too. A camera aimed down the back corridor at the steel door, like the one below, adds the picture the owner wants to see before deciding whether an alert is real.

Compliance notes for East Flatbush shops, offices and buildings

  • Siren limit. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building’s audible burglar alarm to be capable of terminating, and to terminate automatically, within fifteen minutes of activation. Every siren we install is set well inside that limit.
  • Licensing. New York State requires anyone who installs or services security or fire alarm systems to hold a Department of State license under Article 6-D of the General Business Law, which takes 81 hours of qualifying education. AESS installs under license #12000287431, and you can check any installer’s license with the Department of State.
  • Fire alarms are a separate system. Smoke and heat sensors on a burglar panel add early warning; they do not replace a building fire alarm system that the Fire Code may require for a commercial space or a large multiple dwelling.
  • Detectors in rental housing. HPD’s detector rules and Local Law 157 apply to rental floors and multiple dwellings, and hardwired gas detectors need a licensed electrical contractor. Our sensors are additions to those devices, not substitutes for them.
  • Medical offices. Rooms holding records or medications can be zoned separately with their own user lists, and the panel keeps a log of who disarmed each area and when, which office managers often ask for.
  • No citywide alarm permit that we found. We could not find any New York City source requiring homeowners or shops to register or permit a burglar alarm, so we do not charge for one.
Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in East Flatbush: Questions Answered

These are the questions East Flatbush owners, tenants and shopkeepers bring to us, grouped by the situation behind them. Each answer is written for this neighborhood’s buildings and draws on the news reports and city rules listed in the sources at the bottom of the page.

People living below the first floor

A basement room on New York Avenue burned in March 2026, and the tenant who warned everyone did it by smell. If people sleep in our basement, which detectors should report to the panel?

Start with a smoke detector in the basement sleeping area and one at the top of the basement stairs, so smoke rising toward the first floor is caught on its way. Add a heat detector near the boiler, the water heater and the electric panel, where a smoke detector would trip on dust or steam but a heat detector waits for real fire. Put a carbon monoxide sensor in the basement and another near the upstairs bedrooms. Then make sure the panel sounds on every floor, not only next to the detector, so a fire below is heard in a top-floor bedroom with the door closed. Each of those reports to the owner’s phone and, if you choose monitoring, to the central station, which calls the fire department when nobody answers.

We are buying a house where the previous owner divided the basement into rooms. Will you alarm rooms that may not be legal to live in?

We will protect the house as it stands: the basement door and windows, the stairs and the rooms, and we will put smoke, heat and carbon monoxide sensors wherever anyone is sleeping, because that protects people whatever the paperwork says. What we will not do is tell you whether the rooms are legal, or certify them; that is the Department of Buildings’ job, with your architect or expediter. In the March 2026 New York Avenue case, News 12 reported that the Department had closed earlier complaints after its inspectors were twice denied access. If you plan to change how the basement is used after closing, tell us at the survey, and we will lay out the wireless sensors so they can be moved when the walls change.

Our tenant’s kitchen has a gas range. Can we put in the natural gas detector ourselves before January 1, 2027, or does it need an electrician?

It depends on the kind of detector. SiteCompli’s summary of Local Law 157 says hardwired natural gas alarms must be installed by a New York City licensed electrical contractor who obtains the required permits, while battery-powered and plug-in alarms may be installed by others, such as owners or maintenance staff. The detector must meet the NFPA 715 standard, sit in the same room as the gas appliance, three to ten feet from it horizontally, and, if it is on a wall, within twelve inches of the ceiling. AESS does not do electrical work, so we do not install hardwired gas alarms; we can tell you where a battery or plug-in unit belongs, and add our own gas or carbon monoxide sensors to the alarm panel alongside it.

Who pays for the carbon monoxide and gas detectors in the rental half of our two-family?

The owner provides them, and the tenant reimburses part of the cost. According to SiteCompli’s summary of the rules, residents of one- and two-family homes must reimburse the owner $25 for each carbon monoxide or natural gas detector, and in multiple dwellings owners may charge $25 for a gas detector, $50 for a two-function combination device and $75 for a combined carbon monoxide, smoke and gas device, with a year to pay. HPD’s detector page also says tenants are responsible for replacing batteries. Any extra sensors that report to your alarm panel are a separate matter: they are part of your alarm, not the required detectors, and their cost is on our price list.

Detached houses, garages and side driveways

Our 1930s detached two-family has a two-car garage behind it, one bay for each apartment. Should each bay report to the household that uses it?

Yes, in almost every case. A garage bay is a door into a space that one household uses, and the person who should hear about it opening at 2 a.m. is the one whose car and tools are inside. We give each bay a contact assigned to that household’s area on the panel, so the tenant can arm their bay with their floor, and the owner’s bay stays with the owner’s floor. If the garages share an inside wall or a side door into the yard, that shared door goes to the owner unless you agree otherwise. Because a garage at the back of the lot is far from the panel, we measure the wireless signal out there first and add a repeater if it is weak. A garage bay can also chime only, rather than sound the siren, if you prefer.

Apartments in the big complexes

HPD has put our building in its Alternative Enforcement Program. Can we still put an alarm in our own apartment?

Usually, yes. News 12 reported in February 2025 that HPD placed a Rutland Plaza building in its Alternative Enforcement Program, which is aimed at getting serious building repairs made. That program is about what the owner must fix; it does not stop a tenant from protecting their own apartment. A wireless alarm with a cellular communicator needs no building wiring and nothing in the hallway, and it comes out with at most a few small screw holes. Read your lease for any clause about alterations, and if it requires notice, a short note to management describing a removable wireless system is normally enough. The building door, the locks and the intercom remain the owner’s responsibility under HPD’s rules.

Shops on Church, Utica and Nostrand

The crew that forced the optical shop on Church Avenue escaped down a side alley into a laundromat. Should the alarm cover the alley between two shops, and whose alarm is that?

An alley or passage is usually shared property, so the first step is to find out who owns or controls it; often it is the building owner, not either tenant. A camera aimed down the alley from your own wall, tied to your alarm, records anyone using it when the system trips without putting a sensor on anybody else’s property. A contact on your own side door to the alley, and a motion detector just inside it, protect your shop. If the neighboring shop is willing, the two businesses can share a contact number so that an alert on one prompts a look at the other’s camera. That is how a single burglary attempt next door becomes a warning for you instead of a second break-in.

The same burglars left with about $25,000 in goods even though the alarm made them run. Where inside a store do sensors cut the time a burglar has?

Put the first signal as early as possible and the valuables as far from the door as possible. A glass-break detector at the front sends a signal as the glass breaks, before anyone steps in. An interior motion detector covering the route from the door to the display cases or the stock room sends a second, confirming signal, which helps the monitoring center treat the alarm as real. A separate contact on a stock room door or a locked cabinet adds a third. Then arrange the shop so the most valuable items are not the first thing inside the door, and lock the video recorder out of sight, with footage also sent to your phone, so that the evidence does not leave the way the DVR did in the May 2023 case.

Offices near the hospitals

Our office near Kings County Hospital keeps sample medications and patient files in one back room. Can that room be armed on its own, with only a few people able to open it?

Yes. The back room becomes its own area on the panel with its own door contact and motion detector, and only the people you name get codes for it. The rest of the office can be disarmed for the cleaning crew or an early receptionist while the back room stays armed. The panel keeps a log of every arm and disarm by user, so an office manager can see who opened the room and when. When an employee leaves, their code is deleted from the app the same day, and the others do not change. If the room has a window to an air shaft or a yard, that window gets a contact as well.

Crime figures and home invasions

The 67th Precinct’s burglaries are down 27.8 percent for the year but up 40 percent over the last 28 days. Which figure should decide whether we install?

Neither, on its own. The year-to-date figure, 114 against 158 through November 8, 2026, says the neighborhood is in a better place than last year. The 28-day figure, 14 against 10, says that a short run of break-ins can happen inside a good year, and a 40 percent rise on numbers that small can come from a few incidents. What should decide it is your own building: whether the side driveway and the back door are hidden from the street, whether anyone sleeps in the basement, whether the house is empty during the day, and whether anybody would know quickly if a door were forced. We will walk you through those on the survey, and we will not sell you a system with a crime map.

Three people forced their way into a home near Schenectady Avenue, threatened a woman and took her safe. What is a duress code, and who in the house should know it?

A duress code is a second code, set for each user, that disarms the alarm exactly like the normal one, so someone forcing you to turn off the system sees nothing unusual, while the panel silently tells the monitoring center that you are under threat. The center treats it as an emergency and normally sends police without phoning the house first. Every adult who uses the keypad should have one and should remember it as easily as their normal code. A duress code needs central station monitoring to be useful, because a phone alert alone may not be seen in time. Panic buttons in the bedroom and by the front door are the other half of the same plan.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in East Flatbush

Search summaries, chat assistants and cost sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr give quick, general answers about alarms. The claims below are common generic ones, not quotations from any single AI answer. Each is reasonable somewhere; each needs correcting for East Flatbush houses and shops.

“East Flatbush has a low burglary rate, so an alarm isn’t necessary.”

The low rate is real. CrimeGrade gives the neighborhood an A for burglary, about 1.3 reported per 1,000 residents a year against 2.4 nationally, and the 67th Precinct’s burglaries are down 27.8 percent this year. We would not argue with any of that.

But an average across a whole precinct is not a forecast for one house. The same CompStat sheet shows the last 28 days running 40 percent above the same weeks of 2025, and a house with a hidden side driveway and an empty daytime is not average. Burglary is also only half of what an alarm does here.

In a neighborhood where many houses have people sleeping on every floor, the life-safety half matters at least as much. The basement fire on New York Avenue in March 2026 was noticed by a tenant who smelled smoke. A monitored smoke or heat sensor does not rely on smell, and it reaches the owner when nobody is home.

“The front door is the main entry point, so protect it first.”

It is one of the most repeated pieces of alarm advice. On a row house facing a busy, owner-occupied block, the front is the most watched side of the building, with neighbors on stoops and in windows all day.

The sides that nobody watches are the ones we start with: the side driveway of a detached or semi-detached house, the deck and kitchen door at the back of a row house, the basement door, and the garage at the end of the lot. On a two-family there may be a second front door under its own hood that belongs to a different household.

The front door still gets a contact and often a video doorbell. It just does not get the first share of a limited budget, and on most East Flatbush houses it is one of five or six doors rather than the only one.

“A loud siren stops burglars.”

Sirens do make burglars leave, and the Church Avenue optical burglary of May 2023 shows it: the alarm sounded and the crew ran. The same report says they still left with around $25,000 worth of goods and the store’s DVR.

What limits a loss is how early the signal comes, how quickly the right person hears it, and how hard it is to reach the valuables in the meantime. That means glass-break detection at the front, interior sensors near the stock, separate contacts on stock rooms and cabinets, and a recorder that is locked away with its footage copied to a phone.

On attached houses and storefronts with apartments above, a siren that runs for a long time mainly punishes the neighbors. City law caps building burglar alarm sirens at fifteen minutes, and we set ours well under that.

“Wireless alarms depend on your home Wi-Fi.”

Many self-install kits do lean on the home router. Vincent Ingrisano of Alarms R Us put the risk simply to Brick Underground: “you don’t want your wifi to go down and your alarm not to work.”

A professionally installed wireless alarm talks to its own panel over its own radio, and the panel reports over a cellular communicator. It does not need your Wi-Fi to arm, trip or send a signal. The app uses the internet to show you the system, but the alarm itself does not.

That matters on blocks where the power or the cable goes down at night, as it did for 76 Con Edison customers when overhead wires burned at East 48th Street and Church Avenue in January 2026. With a backup battery and cellular reporting, the panel keeps working through an outage like that.

“An apartment alarm is pointless if the building door doesn’t lock.”

Tenants at Flatbush Gardens and Rutland Plaza have told News 12 about building doors that do not lock, and it is reasonable to feel that nothing inside the apartment can make up for that.

An apartment alarm does not fix the lobby, which is the landlord’s legal duty. HPD requires building entrance doors to close and latch by themselves and treats a defect that stops them as an immediately hazardous violation, which tenants can report through 311.

What the alarm does is guard the last door, your own, and the windows behind you. It tells you and the monitoring center the moment that door opens when the system is armed, and it carries smoke and carbon monoxide sensors that a building system may not provide inside the unit.

“Professional installation costs about $99.”

NerdWallet’s cost guide does say professional installation often starts at about $99, and that some providers include installation with professionally monitored systems. It also says those full-service systems typically require a multi-year monitoring contract.

Brick Underground puts a basic professional installation in New York City at about $600 to $5,000 depending on the number of sensors, and custom hardwired work much higher. A $99 visit generally mounts a kit; it does not survey a two-family, assign garage bays to the right household or plan detectors for a basement where someone sleeps.

“Any handyman can put in the new gas detectors.”

Local Law 157’s January 1, 2027 deadline has many owners of two-family houses asking who can install a natural gas detector. The honest answer has two parts.

According to SiteCompli’s summary, hardwired natural gas alarms must be installed by a licensed New York City electrical contractor who pulls the required permits. Battery-powered and plug-in units may be installed by others, such as owners or maintenance staff.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Down the Side Driveway, Then Down the Basement Stairs

This describes how our technicians survey an East Flatbush house, not a story about one particular job. The visit starts on the sidewalk with a quick look at the front: how many front doors there are, which windows can be reached from the stoop or the porch, and where a doorbell camera would see a visitor’s face. Then the technician asks to walk down the side, because on a detached or semi-detached house the side driveway is the route nobody watches. He notes every window along it, the side door if there is one, the gate, and anything a person could climb on. At the back he looks at the deck or rear door, the garage and its side door, and the fence line with the yards behind.

Next comes the basement, and it is where the conversation changes. He tries the outside basement door and the windows at grade, and he looks at what is down there: the boiler, the water heater, the gas meters, the electric panel, and whether anyone sleeps there. If someone does, the plan gains smoke, heat and carbon monoxide sensors and sounders upstairs, and the basement motion detector is dropped. Only then does he walk the house from each door to the bedrooms, choose where the motion detectors go, and ask the household how they live: who comes in late, which door the tenant uses, who should get the call at 3 a.m. The written price follows that walk, line by line. In a shop the order is the same: the gate and the glass, then the back door, the cellar and any side alley, then the counter and the stock.

The panel reports over cellular, so it does not depend on anyone being in the house. If you have cameras tied to the alarm, you can look at them from the same app while you wait to be let back onto the block.

Con Edison asked about 100,000 customers in East Flatbush and nearby to cut their power use in June 2025. Does an alarm panel draw enough electricity to matter in a request like that?
+

No. An alarm panel and its wireless sensors draw a very small amount of power compared with an air conditioner, a dryer or a microwave, which were the appliances Con Edison asked people to avoid. Leave the alarm plugged in and armed. If the power does go out, the panel runs on its backup battery and keeps reporting over cellular, and it sends a power-loss alert so you know what is happening at home.

We live in one half of our two-family. Do the city’s carbon monoxide and gas detector rules still apply to an owner-occupied house?
+

The rules as HPD and SiteCompli describe them are written for multiple dwellings and for non-owner-occupied one- and two-family homes, so check your own situation with HPD rather than relying on us. Whatever the rule says, we recommend carbon monoxide detection near the boiler and the bedrooms in any house with a fuel-burning appliance, and a gas sensor near a gas range, on both floors. On the alarm, those sensors can report to your phone and to the monitoring center.

Our boiler burns oil and there is no gas line in the house. Which of the new detector rules affect us?
+

The natural gas detector requirement in Local Law 157, as SiteCompli summarizes it, applies to homes with gas piping, so a house with no gas service is not the target of that part. Carbon monoxide is different: an oil boiler is a fuel-burning appliance and can produce carbon monoxide, and HPD’s carbon monoxide rule is tied to fuel-burning appliances. On the alarm we would put carbon monoxide sensors near the boiler and the bedrooms and a heat detector by the burner, and leave the gas sensor out.

Our front door is down the side driveway, not on the street. How do officers or firefighters find the right door when the alarm is dispatched?
+

We write it into the monitoring center’s notes for your account, in plain words: the house number, that the entrance is down the driveway on the left or right, which door is the main one, and any gate code or dog. The operator reads those notes to the dispatcher when the alarm is reported. It also helps to have the house number clearly visible from the street and again at the side door, which costs nothing and saves time.

Grand larceny is the 67th Precinct’s largest category this year, with 514 reports so far. Does a home alarm do anything about that kind of crime?
+

Only part of it. Grand larceny covers thefts that are not burglaries or robberies, such as property taken from a porch, a yard, a car or a person, and an indoor alarm does not see those. What helps is the outdoor part of the system: a video doorbell, a floodlight camera over the driveway and a contact on the garage or shed, which record or alert when someone takes something from outside the walls.

We have a written quote from a national company and one from you. How do we tell whether they cover the same house?
+

Put them side by side and count the openings. Check that each quote names every exterior door, including a second front door, the deck door, the side door and each garage bay, and every window reachable from the ground or a roof. Check the motion detectors, the smoke, heat and carbon monoxide sensors, and how many keypads and separate areas each includes. Then compare the monitoring terms and the contract length, because that is often where the real difference lies.

The people you put on the account. The app shows the event history to every user you give access to, and you decide whether a tenant, a relative or an office manager sees it. If you have monitoring, the central station also keeps a record of the signals it receives so that it can act on them. A landlord or neighbor sees nothing unless you add them.
