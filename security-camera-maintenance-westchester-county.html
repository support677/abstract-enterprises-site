The Neighborhood

Why Westchester Properties Need Security Camera Maintenance

Westchester is a county of many small police departments rather than one big one — forty-five local police agencies by one count — and several of them have built a quiet habit: asking residents and businesses to tell them where the cameras are. White Plains started its CommunityCam program in July 2016. Tarrytown's police chief announced SecureCam in November 2019 under the heading "Got video?", saying home systems had become sophisticated enough to help with everything from package thefts to major felonies. Harrison runs its own SecureCam, and Yorktown keeps a Community Camera Registry. Registration is free and voluntary in each place, and none of these departments gets live or remote access to your system by signing you up. What they get is a map: when something happens on your street, they know whose door to knock on.

Read the registration forms closely and they look a lot like a maintenance checklist. Harrison and Tarrytown ask for the number of exterior cameras, the number of interior cameras, the make and model of the system, how it stores video, whether it can be viewed remotely, and the number of days video is stored before being overwritten. White Plains asks how many exterior cameras there are, whether storage is a DVR or hard drive, USB drive, cloud, DVD or even video tape, and how many days are kept — and then sends someone to verify the registration in person. Every one of those answers changes over time. A drive starts failing and the recorder quietly stores less. A camera dies and nobody replaces it. A new doorbell goes up with its own cloud account. The registration still says what it said on the day it was filled in.

The request itself also falls on the owner. Yorktown's program says plainly that in most cases the department will email an alert asking you, the camera owner, to review your camera system and tell them whether you have useful footage. Residents in r/Westchester describe the same thing from the other side — one wrote that when police ask, they review their DVR recordings to help identify people breaking into cars. That is a different skill from glancing at a live view on a phone. It means finding a specific window of time, on the right camera, quickly, then saving it in a way someone else can use. The county's Real Time Crime Center in White Plains takes text or video tips by email at tips@wccops.com, and in February 2026 it was gathering information on possible teen "takeover" gatherings at malls in Yonkers, Mount Vernon and New Rochelle. Private footage is part of how all of this works.

Alarm rules add a second set of eyes. The Town of Mamaroneck requires an alarm user permit for every premises with alarm devices; New Rochelle's Chapter 81 requires an alarm subscriber permit; White Plains charges a $50 residential or $100 commercial permit fee and then a rising fee for false alarms, from $25 for the first up to $250 for the twelfth and beyond; Briarcliff Manor charges $80 for a new permit and $50 a year to renew; Bedford's false-alarm fees increase from the third false alarm in a year. A camera that lets you look before an alarm turns into a police response is worth keeping in working order — and a camera that has quietly stopped recording is worth finding before the next alarm, not after. Most owners combine this upkeep with alarm installation and service in Westchester, because the two systems now answer to the same people.

Rules that touch camera upkeep in Westchester

  • State licence for maintenance work. New York requires a security or fire alarm installer licence to install, maintain or service a CCTV system used to detect intrusion, break-in, theft or movement. Ask for the number; ours is #12000287431.
  • Local alarm permits. Mamaroneck, New Rochelle, White Plains, Briarcliff Manor and Bedford all require permits and charge false-alarm fees on their own schedules; check your own town or village.
  • Registry terms. White Plains and Harrison ask registrants not to release video or pictures to the media without first consulting the department, and say registrants are not acting as agents of the police.
  • Staff areas. New York Labor Law §203-c forbids employers to video record workers in restrooms, locker rooms or rooms set aside for changing, absent a court order — so no re-aim on a visit ever brings one of those rooms into view.
  • Microphones. Under Penal Law Article 250, capturing a conversation that no participant agreed to is eavesdropping. Firmware updates sometimes enable audio by default; when we find it on, it goes back off unless you have a lawful reason and consent to use it.

When a Police Department Asks for Video

Yorktown police emailed asking us to check our cameras for a two-hour window overnight — how do we search that much video in a few minutes instead of watching all of it?

Most recorders made in the last ten years have search tools that almost nobody uses. Instead of pressing play at midnight and watching until two, open playback on the camera that faces the street or the driveway, set the start and end times, and look at the coloured bar under the video. Many systems mark motion or "smart" events — a person, a vehicle — in a different colour, so you can jump straight to the moments when something moved. Some recorders also let you draw a box over part of the picture, such as the end of the driveway, and list only the clips where something moved inside that box. On a two-hour overnight window that usually leaves a handful of events to look at.

The catch is that these tools only work if they were set up and if the person searching knows where they are. Event marking depends on motion detection being enabled on that camera, drawn sensibly and not choked by a swinging branch or a porch light. The box search depends on the recorder having stored motion information along with the video. And the menus are different on every brand: Hikvision, Dahua, Lorex, Uniview and the doorbell apps all hide the same idea in different places.

On a maintenance visit we set this up and then rehearse it with you. We pick a real time window from the last few days, find it the fast way on the camera that matters, save it, and play the saved file on a different computer or phone. Then we write the steps for your brand on one page and leave it with the recorder. When the email arrives — Yorktown says it usually comes as an alert asking the owner to review the system — the reply takes minutes, not an evening.

The White Plains registration asks whether we store on a DVR, a USB drive or the cloud — our doorbell uploads to an app and the other cameras go to a box in the basement, so which one actually holds the evidence?

Both, and that is exactly why the answer needs writing down. A typical Westchester house now has two separate systems that happen to point at the same front door: a wired recorder in the basement or a closet, keeping continuous or motion recordings on its own drive for a number of days, and a doorbell that uploads short clips to a cloud account, kept for however long the subscription allows. White Plains' CommunityCam form offers a choice of DVR or hard drive, USB drive, cloud, DVD or CD and video tape for exactly this reason — it wants to know where to look.

The two keep very different records. The recorder usually has the longer, unbroken view, but only for as many days as the drive holds. The doorbell usually has the sharper face at close range, but only in clips, only when its motion sensor fired, and only for as long as the plan keeps them; if the subscription lapsed, it may keep nothing at all. Some doorbells can also record to the wired recorder, which gives you both in one place, but only if that link was set up and still works after the last app update.

On a visit we list every device that records, where each one stores, how long it keeps video and whose account it belongs to, and we write that on the visit log. If the doorbell can be added to the recorder, we tell you what that takes. And if your registration only lists one of the two, we give you the wording to update it, so that when the police ask the department already knows there may be two recordings of the same moment.

Our doorbell clip and the basement recorder show the same car pulling up, but the times are almost two minutes apart — which one is right, and does the difference matter?

Probably neither is exactly right, and yes, it can matter. Each device keeps its own clock. A cloud doorbell usually takes its time from the internet automatically. A recorder may take its time from the internet too, or it may still be running on whatever time somebody typed in when it was installed, drifting a few seconds a week. After a power cut, a recorder without a working time server can come back hours or even days off. Two minutes is a typical gap on a system nobody has looked at in a few years.

When footage from your house is being lined up with footage from a neighbour's camera, a store down the road or a police camera, a two-minute gap makes the sequence harder to follow and invites questions about which clip came first. It also makes searching harder: an officer asks for 3:12 a.m., you look at 3:12 on the recorder, and the car has not arrived yet because your recorder is running slow.

The fix is simple and we do it on every visit. Every recorder, camera and doorbell is pointed at the same reliable network time source and set to the Eastern time zone with daylight saving switched on correctly. We then compare the on-screen time against a reference clock and write the difference in the log. If a device cannot hold accurate time on its own, we note that, and the log tells anyone reviewing a clip how far off that device was at the last check.

A neighbour wants to post our clip of the break-in to a town Facebook group while the police are still investigating — is there any reason to wait?

The two Westchester registries whose terms we read both say something about it. White Plains' CommunityCam terms and Harrison's SecureCam terms each ask registrants not to release video or pictures to the media without first consulting the police department. Whether a community Facebook group counts as "the media" is not spelled out, but the reason behind the request is easy to understand: a clip posted early can warn the people in it, invite guesses that point at the wrong person, or put details out that investigators wanted to keep back.

There is also a practical risk on the maintenance side. A clip that has been downloaded to a phone, trimmed, screen-recorded and re-uploaded loses its original quality and its embedded time information. If the police later need the original, it has to come from the recorder or the cloud account — and if the recorder has overwritten that day in the meantime, the trimmed phone copy may be all that is left.

So our advice is to secure the original first and ask the department before sharing anything. On a visit we show you how to lock or protect a recording on your brand of recorder so it is not overwritten, and how to export a full-quality copy to a USB drive. That way the original stays intact whatever you decide to share, and you can answer the department's request without losing anything. This is practical guidance, not legal advice; the department handling the case is the right place to ask what it wants.

When the Paperwork No Longer Matches the System

We signed up for Harrison's SecureCam when the system went in, and since then we have replaced the recorder and added three cameras — what on our registration has gone stale, and how do we fix it?

Probably most of it. Harrison's form asks for the number of exterior cameras, the number of interior cameras, the make and model of the system, the storage and connectivity, the number of days video is stored before being overwritten, and whether the system can be viewed remotely. A new recorder changes the make and model and nearly always changes the number of days. Three more cameras change the count and, because they share the same drive, shorten the days again. If any of the new cameras record to a cloud account, the storage answer has changed as well.

None of this is a problem on its own; the registration is voluntary and the department will still call. The problem is what happens when they rely on it. An officer reading "14 days" may not ask about an incident from ten days ago if your new system actually keeps seven, or may not ask you at all about a side street that your new camera now covers. The registry is only as useful as the facts in it.

On a visit we read every one of those answers from the system itself: the camera count by location, the recorder model, where each device stores, the oldest recording on the drive that day and whether remote viewing works. We put them on a single page in the same order as the form. Harrison's page says registrations can be cancelled by email to the address it lists; for changes, the simplest route is to ask the department how it prefers to receive them and send the new page.

Does signing up for a police camera registry let officers log into our cameras or watch them live?

Not under the four Westchester programs we read. White Plains says its police will not have remote, live or any other access to your system without your express consent. Yorktown says that at no time will its police have direct access to your surveillance system. Harrison and Tarrytown describe their SecureCam programs as a way for police to know where cameras are and to contact you after an incident nearby, then request to view and copy the video. Registration gives them your location and contact details, not a login.

Where people get confused is with separate programs — in some places police invite businesses to share live feeds through a dedicated platform — and with old installer accounts. It is common to find a system with five or six logins on it: the owner, a former tenant, the original installer, a family member who has moved away, a "guest" account created for a babysitter. None of those belongs to the police, but each is a door into the system that nobody is watching.

On a maintenance visit we list every account on the recorder and in the app, confirm with you who each one belongs to, and remove or disable the ones that should not be there. We also check that remote access goes through the manufacturer's current app rather than through an old open port on the router, and we record the result in the log. If you ever decide to share a camera live with anyone, that is a deliberate choice you make with your own login, not a side effect of registering.

Our village charges for repeat false alarms — can the cameras let us see what set the alarm off before anyone is sent?

Often, yes, and in Westchester it can save real money. White Plains charges $25 for a first false alarm, $50 for the second and third, $75 for the fourth and fifth, $100 for the sixth through eleventh and $250 for the twelfth and beyond; Bedford's fees step up from the third false alarm in a year; Briarcliff Manor, New Rochelle and the Town of Mamaroneck all run permit systems of their own. A camera that shows you the dog, the balloon or the cleaner who forgot the code within seconds of the alert lets you call the monitoring company before a car is dispatched.

That only works if three things are true. The camera covering each alarm zone has to exist and point at the right place — many systems have a camera on the front walk but none on the back door that trips the alarm. Your phone has to reach the recorder quickly from wherever you are, which means remote access that works over a cellular connection, not just on the home Wi-Fi. And whoever gets the alarm call needs to know which camera to open, which is easier when cameras are named "Back door" rather than "Camera 6".

On a visit we walk the alarm zones with you, match each one to the camera that sees it, rename the cameras accordingly and test remote viewing from a phone on cellular data. Where the alarm system and the recorder can be linked so that an alarm event bookmarks or pops up the matching camera, we check the link still works. Whether your monitoring company will act on what you see is up to its own procedures and your town's rules, so ask them; our part is making sure the picture is there when you look.

When the Power Comes Back

After the July 2026 storms our power came back and the doorbell reconnected, but the app still shows the wired cameras as offline — what changed?

Very often the router changed, not the cameras. When a house loses power, everything restarts at once when it returns, and devices come back in a race. If the router finishes last, or hands out addresses differently than before, a recorder or camera that was relying on a particular address can end up unreachable even though it is running perfectly. The doorbell, which talks straight to the cloud, reconnects on its own. The recorder, which may depend on a port setting or a fixed address that no longer matches, does not.

The July 4, 2026 storms are a good example of how many Westchester systems go through this at once. Con Edison said that Yonkers, Mount Vernon, Rye and New Rochelle had the greatest number of storm-related outages in the county, and late on July 5 about 2,600 Westchester customers were still waiting for power. Every one of those households and businesses restarted its network when the lights came back. Some systems also lose their time setting in the process, which is why we check the clock at the same time.

The lasting fix is to make the system restart cleanly on its own. On a visit we give the recorder and any IP cameras addresses that cannot be handed to something else, make sure remote access uses the manufacturer's cloud connection rather than a fragile router rule, and put the router, the recorder and the PoE switch on the same battery backup where possible, so a short blink never restarts them at all. Then we prove it: power everything down, bring it back up in the wrong order, and confirm that every camera returns without anyone touching it.

Our internet provider swapped the modem and now the recorder can only be viewed at home — is that a maintenance job or theirs?

Usually ours, although the trigger was theirs. A new modem or gateway often arrives with a new network name, new password, a different address range and its own firewall settings. Devices that join the new network automatically, like phones and laptops, keep working. A recorder set up years ago for the old network may join it but lose the remote connection, or keep working on the home Wi-Fi only because the phone and recorder are side by side. The provider's job ends when the internet works; it does not include your camera system.

The symptom matters in Westchester because remote viewing is one of the questions on the Harrison and Tarrytown registration forms, and because it is what lets you check an alarm from a train platform or an office. A system that works at home but not away from home looks fine every evening and fails exactly when you need it.

On a visit we test remote viewing from a phone on cellular data, not on your Wi-Fi, and fix whatever is blocking it — the recorder's network settings, the manufacturer's cloud service setting, the app version on your phone. We never open the router to the internet in a way that exposes the recorder directly. And we write down which modem or gateway the system was tested with, so the next swap comes with a note of what to check.

Street Cameras, Night Glare and Busy Days

The camera facing our street is the one police would want, but at night every passing car turns the picture white — can that be adjusted, or is it the wrong camera?

It can usually be adjusted. A camera that looks along a street at night is dealing with two extremes at once: a dark road and bright headlights. Out of the box, many cameras set their exposure for the dark, so a pair of headlights floods the picture just as the car passes the one spot where its shape or plate would be visible. Infrared adds to it if the camera's own night lights bounce off a nearby wall, a mailbox or a hedge in the foreground.

Several settings help, and which ones exist depends on the model: wide dynamic range or backlight compensation, a faster shutter at night, a lower infrared level or an infrared "smart" mode that dims close to the lens, and a slight change of angle so headlights cross the frame rather than shine into it. Sometimes the honest answer is that a wide-angle camera will never read a plate at forty feet, and the view is best used for movement and timing while a narrower camera covers the driveway mouth.

We test street cameras after dark with real traffic passing, adjust them, and then play back the recording rather than judging the live view, because the recorded picture is what an investigator will see. Before-and-after stills go into the log. If a camera needs replacing to do the job you want, we say so and price it separately.

Our store at Cross County Center was on the list of possible "takeover" sites in February 2026 — how do we make sure the cameras keep full quality when the place is packed?

By testing what happens when every camera sees movement at once. Many recorders lower quality, drop frames or skip channels when the total recording load climbs past what the recorder or its drive can write. On a quiet Tuesday nothing triggers it. On a crowded Saturday afternoon, or during the kind of sudden crowd the county's Real Time Crime Center was watching for in February 2026, every camera in a store records motion at once, and an overloaded recorder can quietly fall behind just when the footage matters most.

The signs are in the recorder's logs and settings: channels set to a higher bitrate than the recorder's total capacity can sustain, a drive that is old or not rated for surveillance work, a network switch that drops packets when every camera is busy, or "event" recording that bumps quality up for every channel at once. You will not see any of that on a monitor in the back office, because the live view and the recording are separate streams.

On a business visit we add up the recording load against the recorder's rated limit, check the drive's health and type, look at the switch, and then test with as many cameras as possible seeing motion at the same time — staff walking every aisle, for example. We play the recording back and look for dropped frames or blurred motion. If the system is at its limit, we suggest the cheapest fix first, usually adjusting bitrates on the less important cameras.

Our office building has a guard watching the camera monitors all day — doesn't that mean someone would already notice if a camera stopped working?

Not necessarily. A guard watching a wall of live tiles sees what the cameras show right now. The guard does not see whether the recorder is writing those pictures to a drive, how many days the drive holds, whether one drive in a set of four has failed, whether the time stamp is right, or whether a camera has slowly drifted off its view a few degrees at a time. A camera that freezes on its last picture can look live for hours. A camera that records only in low quality because its settings were reset looks identical on the monitor.

Guards also see the same tiles every day, which makes slow changes hard to spot: a lens slowly dimming with dust, a tree growing into a parking-lot view, an infrared light that has failed on one side. Those are exactly the things a maintenance visit compares against the last visit's reference pictures.

So we treat the guard as an early warning and maintenance as the check on the recording. On a visit we read drive health and retention, play back recordings from every camera, compare each view with its reference image, and give the security desk a short list of things they can check daily — a frozen clock on a tile, a black tile, a camera that suddenly shows a wall — and who to call when they see one.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Maintenance in Westchester County

We could not capture Google's AI Overview for this topic from the environment we research in, so nothing here quotes it. Instead, these are seven statements that turn up again and again in product pages, forum replies and general guides — the material such summaries are built from — each checked against what Westchester's own police and town rules actually say.

Claim 1: "Registering your cameras with the police lets them watch you"

This worry comes up whenever a department launches a camera program, and in r/Westchester threads about police plate-reader cameras it is easy to see why people are wary of surveillance in general. But the registries in Westchester that we read describe something much narrower.

White Plains says its police will not have remote, live or any other access to a registered system without the owner's express consent. Yorktown says that at no time will its police have direct access. Harrison and Tarrytown describe contacting the owner after an incident nearby and requesting to view and copy footage. What police get is your address and contact details.

The real access risk sits elsewhere: forgotten installer accounts, a shared password used by half the family, a router rule opened years ago. Those are maintenance problems, and they exist whether or not you register.

Claim 2: "If something happens, the police will pull the footage themselves"

Sometimes detectives do visit and copy video, and White Plains' program describes a verification visit when you first register. But Yorktown's program is explicit that in most cases the department will email an alert asking the camera owner to review the system and say whether there is useful footage.

That puts the first search on you. If the only person who knows how to use the recorder is the installer who set it up, or a family member who has moved out, the answer to the email may be "we couldn't find anything" when the video was sitting on the drive all along.

So part of maintenance in Westchester is training, not hardware: a rehearsed search, a saved export, and a written page of steps for your own brand, kept where the next person will find it.

Claim 3: "Higher resolution always makes footage more useful"

More pixels help, up to a point. But on the same drive, a higher resolution or bitrate means fewer days stored. The Harrison and Tarrytown forms ask how many days your video is kept before being overwritten precisely because a sharp recording that no longer exists helps no one.

A 4K camera set to maximum quality on a small drive may keep a week; the same camera at a sensible bitrate may keep three. For a request that arrives ten days after an incident, the second setting is the useful one.

We set each camera's quality to its job — full detail on the doorway and the driveway mouth, lighter settings on the back yard — and then read the oldest day on the drive to prove the balance works.

Claim 4: "Cameras put an end to false-alarm fines"

Cameras can help you avoid a dispatch, if you look before the monitoring company calls the police and your company accepts your word that it was a false trip. They do not change your town's permit rules or its fee schedule, and they do not stop the sensor that caused the alarm from doing it again.

White Plains' fees climb from $25 for a first false alarm to $250 for the twelfth and beyond; Bedford's rise from the third false alarm in a year. A camera only helps if it covers the zone that tripped and you can open it fast from a phone away from home.

Maintenance makes that possible: zones matched to cameras, cameras named for what they see, remote access tested on cellular data. The sensor itself is your alarm company's job.

Claim 5: "Infrared night vision lets cameras see in the dark as well as by day"

Infrared lets a camera produce a picture in darkness, in black and white, over a limited range. It does not handle bright light in the same scene well. A street camera at night is dark road and passing headlights at once, and many cameras out of the box wash out exactly when a car is in frame.

Infrared also reflects. A camera close to a wall, a gutter or a dome bubble can glare in its own light, and a spider web in front of the lens glows like fog. All of that is invisible during a daytime visit.

That is why our visits include an after-dark check of street-facing and driveway cameras, with playback of the recording rather than a glance at the live view.

Claim 6: "A wired system comes back by itself after a power outage"

The cameras usually do power back up. Whether they reconnect to the recorder, the recorder reconnects to the internet, and the clock comes back right is another matter. After the July 4, 2026 storms, thousands of Westchester households restarted their networks at once.

Routers that hand out new addresses, recorders that lose their time server, PoE switches that come up after the recorder has already given up looking for cameras — each of those leaves a system that looks fine on a wall but is not recording everything, or cannot be reached from a phone.

A controlled restart test on each visit, fixed addresses, and a shared battery backup for router, recorder and switch turn "usually comes back" into "has been tested coming back".

Claim 7: "Recording your monitor with your phone is good enough to send the police"

It is better than nothing, and the county's Real Time Crime Center does take video tips by email. But a phone recording of a screen loses resolution, adds glare and reflections, and loses the time and camera information embedded in the original file.

If an investigator later needs the original, it has to come from the recorder or the cloud account — and if that day has been overwritten in the meantime, the phone copy is all that is left.

Protect the original first, export a full-quality copy to a USB drive, then send whatever is asked for. We set up and rehearse those steps on every maintenance visit.

Field Notes: Ask the System the Question Before Anyone Else Does

On a Westchester visit I start by pretending to be the police. I pick a time from the last few days — something the owner remembers, a delivery or a car leaving — and ask them to find it on the camera that faces the street. Then I watch. How long it takes, which menu they open, whether they know the recorder's password, whether the clip they find has the right time on it. That five minutes tells me more about the system than an hour of cleaning.
