The Neighborhood

Why Nassau County Properties Need DVR Upgrades

Nassau is owner-occupied suburbia on a scale few counties match. The Census Bureau's QuickFacts put the owner-occupied rate at 81.9 percent for 2020–2024, across roughly 458,000 households and a median home value of $684,700. Those owners bought camera kits and hired installers in waves — the analogue boxes of the 2000s, the 1080p coax kits of the early 2010s — and a large share of those systems are still running on the original recorder. The cameras outside usually still work. What fails is the box in the basement, the attic or the garage: a hard drive that has been writing around the clock for eight or ten years, a power supply that has ridden through every summer brownout, firmware that no longer talks to a current phone app. Replacing that one box is the cheapest way to make the whole system useful again, and it is the job this page is about.

The shape of a Nassau lot changes the job more than anything else. A typical property here is not one wall facing a sidewalk. It is a front walk, a driveway that runs back past the house, a garage that may be attached or may stand on its own at the rear of the lot, a fenced yard, often a pool, and a side passage. Each of those has a camera on the older systems we take over, and each camera has a coax run back to the recorder — some through the house, some buried across the lawn to the garage, some stapled along a fence. Those runs are the expensive part to replace and, in most houses, the part that is still sound. A hybrid recorder that speaks the same high-definition-over-coax signals the cameras already use lets the runs stay where they are.

The reason owners call is usually something that happened on the driveway. Nassau County Police told reporters in January 2024, when a multi-agency burglary and stolen-car task force was launched in Mineola, that burglars are attracted by cars in people's driveways, and that the share of break-ins happening while residents were at home had gone up even as the early-year count fell from 34 to 25. A county legislator's 2022 update passed on an NCPD alert that car thieves were checking whether side mirrors were folded — a sign the key fob had been left inside — and asked residents to keep driveways lit and to note licence plates, which police called "extremely valuable". In July 2026 the department's Major Case Squad announced an arrest for residential burglaries across the county stretching from July 2025 to May 2026, in which jewellery was taken from inside the homes. When something like that happens on a block, every neighbour opens the camera app, and that is when a failing recorder is discovered: the night in question is missing, the plate is a smear of pixels, or the app simply will not connect.

The other recurring trigger is weather. LIPA's own 30-day review of Tropical Storm Isaias in August 2020 counted roughly 645,000 customer outages and eight days to full restoration; during Superstorm Sandy in 2012 the outage count across Long Island passed 900,000. An old DVR with no battery behind it simply stops when the power goes, and some come back with a corrupted disk or the clock reset to a default date. Our Nassau upgrades therefore treat the recorder as more than a box: it goes somewhere dry and ventilated, on a battery backup alongside the modem, with its clock set from the internet so that every recording carries the right time. Owners who are replacing the recorder anyway often add a monitored alarm for the house at the same visit, so a door contact can tell the cameras when to pay attention. The wider question of which recorder technology to choose across Nassau and Suffolk is covered on our Long Island DVR upgrade hub.

How each building type changes the swap

Slab-on-grade houses. A Levitt house was built on a concrete slab with radiant heating pipes in it, so there is no basement to hide cable in and nothing gets drilled into that floor. On these houses the coax almost always runs through the attic and down an interior wall, or along the outside under the soffit. When the old recorder lived in a now-finished attic, we usually move the new one down to a ventilated closet on the main floor, feeding the existing runs to it through one new chase rather than re-pulling every camera. A recorder baking in an attic in August is one of the most common reasons these boxes fail early.

Houses in the old surge zone. South of Sunrise Highway, the basement or the ground floor is the wrong home for electronics. We put the new recorder, the battery and the modem on a shelf at least one floor above the lowest level, and bring the coax up to it through a sealed sleeve. If a camera on the dock or bulkhead side has been through salt water, we test its run before trusting it on the new box.

Large North Shore lots. High-definition analogue signals travel a long way on good coax — Hikvision rates its third-generation Turbo HD for up to 800 metres of 1080p over coaxial cable — so distance to a gatehouse or a pool house is rarely the limit. The limit is the condition of a run that has been underground for fifteen years. We test the far cameras first, because they decide whether the upgrade is a box swap or a box swap plus one new link.

Split-levels with attached garages. The garage ceiling is usually where the cable bundle crosses, and it is also where an automatic door opener, a freezer and a workshop compressor share the circuit. We keep the new recorder off that circuit and check every garage-side camera for interference once the door motor runs.

Two-families. Each household gets its own app login limited to the cameras that concern it: the shared driveway and front door for both, the private yard only for whoever uses it. The recorder lives in the owner's unit or a locked utility room, not in a common hallway.

Storefronts. The shop recorder moves from the floor under the counter to a locked, ventilated shelf in the back office. Exterior cameras on the landlord's fascia stay where the lease allows; anything new on the roof or the parapet is cleared with the landlord in writing first.

Rules and paperwork for Nassau recorder work

  • County licensing. Nassau's Department of Consumer Affairs licenses home improvement businesses — one of the largest licence categories it oversees. We carry the paperwork that the county and your village ask for, and we show it before work starts.
  • Village and town permits. Whether a permit is needed depends on the municipality: the Town of Hempstead, the Town of North Hempstead, the Town of Oyster Bay and each of the 64 villages run their own building departments. A like-for-like recorder swap on existing low-voltage wiring rarely needs one; a new outlet, a trench across a lawn or a pole in a front yard may.   • State licence and insurance. AESS works under New York State low-voltage licence #12000287431 and is insured on every Nassau job; landlords and co-op boards get a certificate in the wording they ask for.

  • Line voltage. If a new camera position or the recorder's new home needs a new outlet, that part is done by a licensed electrician; our work stays on the low-voltage side.
  • Privacy. We install video without audio by default, aim cameras at your own property, and mask any part of a view that falls on a neighbour's windows or yard.
Your Questions Answered

DVR Upgrades in Nassau County: Questions Answered

These are the questions Nassau owners ask about garages at the back of the lot, cars on the driveway, the pool, the next storm, village rules and the shop on the turnpike. They are grouped by the problem behind them, and each answer is the one we would give on the phone.

Detached Garages and the Back of the Lot

Should the new recorder live in the detached garage, where the old one was, or move into the house?

Into the house, in almost every case. A detached garage is unheated in January, hot in July, damp in between, and it is the building most likely to be broken into — which means the recorder is sitting in the place the burglar is standing. Hard drives are rated for an operating temperature range that an uninsulated garage leaves in both directions. The usual fix is not to re-pull every camera: we leave the garage cameras on their existing coax, run one new protected link from the garage to a closet or utility room in the house, and land everything on the new recorder there. If the link has to cross the yard, it goes in conduit below the frost line or, where trenching would mean tearing up a patio, over a pair of point-to-point wireless radios. The garage then holds only cameras and perhaps a small switch, and losing it no longer loses the evidence.

The garage camera shows rolling lines whenever the door opener runs. Will a new recorder cure that?

Probably not on its own, because the cause is usually outside the recorder. Lines that crawl across the picture when a motor starts are classic interference: either a ground loop, where the camera's power and the recorder's power sit on different electrical grounds and a current flows along the coax shield, or noise picked up where the coax runs alongside the opener's wiring or a fluorescent fixture in the garage ceiling. We trace the run, separate it from the line-voltage wiring, power the camera from the same supply as the recorder where possible, and fit a video ground-loop isolator if the two buildings simply cannot share a ground. Once the picture is clean on the old box, it will be clean on the new one, and the new one's higher resolution makes the improvement much more obvious.

How do we get a camera onto a detached garage that has never had any cable run to it?

There are four practical ways, and the yard usually decides. A trench with conduit from the house to the garage is the most reliable and lasts as long as the house; it needs an 811 mark-out, a path that avoids the pool equipment and sprinkler lines, and restoration of the lawn. An aerial run along an existing fence or between eaves is quicker but looks it, and some villages and associations dislike it. A pair of point-to-point wireless radios, one on each building, carries video across fifty to a few hundred feet with no digging at all, as long as each can see the other. And for a single camera, a coax or network cable can sometimes ride inside an existing conduit that already feeds the garage's power, if it was sized to allow it and the code permits low-voltage in that conduit — which needs checking before anything is pulled.

Our side door on the garage only ever records the top of someone's head. Can the upgrade fix that angle?

Yes, and it is one of the most common complaints on older systems. Installers in the 2000s mounted cameras as high as the ladder allowed, which keeps them out of reach but turns every face into a hat brim. The answer is a second, lower camera at the door, set at roughly head height on the frame or the wall beside it, in a vandal-resistant housing, aimed back along the path a person walks toward the door. The old high camera stays for the wide view of the yard. On a hybrid recorder the new camera can be an IP model, giving a sharper face at a short distance, while the older coax cameras keep their existing runs.

Driveways, Cars and the Plate You Actually Need

What does it take to capture a readable licence plate at the end of a long driveway?

A dedicated camera, aimed for that one job. A wide driveway camera that also shows the lawn and the street spreads its pixels across all of it, so a plate thirty or forty feet away is a handful of blurred pixels, especially at night when headlights flare. A plate camera uses a narrower lens zoomed onto the spot where every car has to slow — usually the apron at the kerb or the turn into the garage — with a fast shutter so motion does not smear the characters, and infrared tuned so the reflective plate does not white out. On an upgrade we usually keep the existing wide camera for context and add the plate camera beside it as an IP model on the hybrid recorder. NCPD has told residents that plate numbers are "extremely valuable" to an investigation; this is the camera that captures them.

Will person and vehicle alerts work on the older coax cameras along our driveway, or only on new ones?

On many current hybrid recorders, smart detection runs inside the recorder rather than the camera, which means it can classify people and vehicles on analogue HD channels too. The usual limit is the number of channels it can analyse at once; a recorder may support person and vehicle detection on four or eight channels, not all sixteen. We assign those channels to the cameras that matter at night — the driveway, the side passage, the back door — and leave the rest recording continuously. The quality of the old camera matters as well: a soft, low-resolution picture gives the classifier less to work with, so a camera that produces constant misses or false alarms on the new box is a candidate for replacement, not the whole system.

Can the cameras tell me when someone tries the car doors in the driveway at three in the morning?

They can tell you someone is there, which is what matters. We draw an intrusion zone around the parked cars or a line across the driveway on the recorder, limit it to people, and schedule it for the hours when nobody should be out there — so the phone stays quiet when you take the bins out at eight but pings when a figure walks up the drive at three. Pairing that with the floodlight or the porch light on a motion sensor helps twice over: the person is lit for the camera and is more likely to leave. No camera stops a car being stolen when the fob is inside it; the police advice to lock the car and take the fob indoors still applies.

Pools, Decks and the Back Yard

Can one camera cover the whole pool, or does the deck need two?

On most Nassau back yards it takes two, placed at opposite corners. A single camera on the back of the house sees the near side of the pool well and the far side poorly, and it looks straight into the low sun on summer evenings and the glare off the water. The usual pair is one wide camera on the house covering the deck, the back door and the near edge, and a second on the garage, the pool house or a fence post looking back toward the house, which catches the gate and anyone approaching from the rear fence. Between them, nobody can stand anywhere on the deck without being seen from the front. Where the old system had only the house camera, the upgrade is a good moment to add the second on a spare recorder channel, and we draw a privacy mask on either camera if its frame reaches over a neighbour's fence.

One back-yard camera washes out white at night while the others look normal. Is that the old recorder?

Almost never; it is something in front of the camera. Infrared light from the camera bounces back off anything close to the lens — a soffit edge, a gutter, a downspout, a branch, a spider web across the dome — and floods the sensor, so the picture goes milky or bright white while every other camera looks fine. Dome cameras with a scratched or dirty bubble do the same. We clean the cover, move or re-angle the camera so nothing sits within a foot or two of the lens, swap a dome for a turret where the bubble is the problem, and check the night picture before we leave. A new recorder makes a good picture sharper; it cannot fix a picture blinded at the camera.

Can the recorder flag only real activity around the pool gate, not leaves and the pool cleaner?

Yes, with a line drawn across the gate rather than motion detection over the whole yard. Old DVRs alerted on pixel change, so water, wind in the hedges, shadows and the automatic pool cleaner all triggered recordings and pushed notifications. A tripwire on the gate set to people only, scheduled for the hours you care about, ignores everything inside the pool area and wakes up when someone comes through the gate. For families with young children, that alert is a useful second pair of eyes, but it is not a pool alarm and does not replace a self-latching gate or the barriers the code requires.

Storms, Outages and the Recorder's Clock

How long will a battery backup keep the recorder going in a long PSEG outage like Isaias?

Hours, not days, and it should be sized for that honestly. A home recorder with its drive, a modem and a small switch might draw well under a hundred watts together; a desktop battery backup of the kind we fit runs that load for something like an hour or two, depending on the battery and the camera count. That is enough to ride out the short blinks that crash old DVRs and to keep recording through the first, most important part of a storm. For a multi-day outage like Isaias, where LIPA counted eight days to full restoration, the answer is the house generator: we connect the recorder to a circuit the generator or transfer switch carries, and make sure the battery backup keeps it running through the seconds it takes the generator to start. The recorder is then set to start recording again on its own when power returns, so nobody has to remember to restart it.

After a storm the old recorder came back showing the wrong date. Does that matter for footage the police want?

It matters a great deal. A recorder that has lost its clock stamps everything with the wrong date and time, so the clip of the person on your driveway at 2:14 a.m. on Tuesday may be filed as something in 2000. Detectives can still use it, but somebody has to explain the offset, and an incorrect timestamp is an easy thing to challenge. Older DVRs keep time with a small coin battery on the board, which fails with age, and many were never connected to an internet time source. On the upgrade we set the new recorder to synchronise its clock from the internet, confirm the time zone and daylight-saving rule, and check after a deliberate power cut that it comes back with the correct time.

Lightning struck near the house and two channels went black. Was it the recorder or the cameras?

It can be either, and often it is both ends of the same run. A nearby strike induces a surge along long outdoor cables — the buried coax to a garage, a run along a fence — and it does its damage where the cable ends: the camera's video board at one end, the recorder's input at the other. We test it methodically: swap the dead camera's cable onto a channel that works, and plug a known-good camera onto the dead run. If the camera shows on another input, the recorder's input is damaged; if a good camera shows nothing on that run, the camera or the cable is. A recorder with one dead input is often still worth replacing at that point because the rest of the board may have been stressed too. On the new installation we add surge protection on the long outdoor runs and put the recorder on a battery backup with surge suppression, so the next strike costs a camera rather than the whole system.

Village Rules, Police and Paperwork

Does our village need a permit for a recorder swap or one extra camera?

Usually not for the swap, sometimes for the extras. Replacing a recorder on existing low-voltage cabling is not the kind of work most building departments permit. Adding a camera on an existing building with low-voltage cable generally is not either. Where permits do come in is the work around it: a new electrical outlet, a trench across a front lawn, a pole or post in a front yard, or anything on a building in a historic district. Nassau has three towns and 64 villages, each with its own building department, so we confirm the rule for your address before we quote any of those items. If your house is in a homeowners' association or a co-op, its rules on exterior fixtures apply on top.

Garden City police run a camera registry. Does signing up give them access to our recorder?

No. The Garden City Police Department describes its Private Camera Registration Program as voluntary, and states that it does not give the department access to privately owned camera systems. Registering simply puts your address on a list so that, after a crime nearby, detectives know which homes and businesses may have video instead of knocking on every door. If something happens, a detective may ask to look at footage from a specific, limited window. You decide what to share, and the export routine we set up on the new recorder makes it quick to hand over just that clip. Other departments in the county may run similar programmes; ask your village or precinct.

Can the video doorbell we already own show up on the new recorder?

Sometimes, and it depends on the doorbell rather than the recorder. Doorbells that speak the open standards recorders use for IP cameras can be added to a hybrid or network recorder as a channel, so the front step records alongside everything else and is kept for the same number of days. Many consumer doorbells sold for use with their own cloud app do not offer that, and they keep working in their own app beside the recorder instead. We check the model before the visit and tell you which it is; if your doorbell cannot join the recorder, a small camera above the door on its own channel gives the recorder a view of the step anyway.

Strip-Mall Storefronts

Our landlord owns the facade and the rear lot. Who decides where the shop's cameras go?

Read the lease first; it usually answers this. Most retail leases let the tenant install security equipment inside the unit freely and need the landlord's written consent for anything fixed to the exterior, the roof or the parapet. Cameras already on the facade from a previous tenant may belong to the landlord. On an upgrade we keep every interior camera, move the recorder to a locked back-office shelf, and prepare a one-page mark-up of any new exterior positions for the landlord to sign before we drill.

Our shop shares a rear lane with the next tenant. Can our camera watch our back door without filming theirs?

Yes, with careful aim and a mask. The camera goes on your side of the shared wall, angled along your stretch of the lane toward your door, dumpster and parking spaces rather than straight across. Where its frame still reaches the neighbour's door, the recorder blacks that part out with a privacy mask so it is never recorded. A person-detection line drawn across your back door, active only after closing, then tells you about someone at your door without alerting every time the neighbouring shop takes a delivery. If the lane is the landlord's, the mark-up we prepare for the landlord shows exactly what the camera sees.

Capes, Ranches and Systems That Came With the House

In a Levitt-built house with no basement, where does the cable from the new recorder go?

Wherever the old cable went, which on a slab house is almost always up and over. Levitt houses were built on concrete slabs with heating pipes cast into them, so nothing runs under the floor and nobody should drill into it. The existing coax normally rises from each camera into the attic and drops down one interior wall or chase to wherever the recorder sits. On the upgrade we keep those runs, move the recorder out of a hot attic if that is where it lives, and bring the bundle down to a ventilated closet through one tidy new chase. If the attic has since been finished into a room, the runs are often hidden behind the knee walls, and we trace them with a tone generator before deciding anything.

We just bought a house with cameras already up. What should we check before deciding what to keep?

Five things, in this order. First, what each camera actually shows — previous owners often aimed cameras at things that mattered to them, such as a boat or a side gate you do not use. Second, which cameras still give a clean picture day and night. Third, what kind of signal each camera uses and which recorder brand runs them, because that decides whether a hybrid recorder can take them all. Fourth, where the recorder is, what it is plugged into, and whether it is open to the internet through the router. Fifth, whether anything from the old owners is still connected to it: their phone app, their email for alerts, their cloud account. We go through all five on one visit and give you a short list of what to keep, re-aim, replace or remove before you spend anything on new hardware.

How we size the drive for a Nassau property

Retention is the number of days back you can go before the recorder overwrites the oldest footage, and on a suburban house it is driven by three things: how many cameras record continuously, at what resolution and frame rate, and how busy each scene is. A street-facing camera on a through road with traffic all night fills the drive faster than a camera on a quiet back fence, because modern compression saves less when the picture keeps changing. We do not guess. After the new recorder has run for a day, we read the bit rate it reports for every channel, multiply out to the drive size installed, and tell you the real number of days. If it is short of what you asked for, we lower the quality on the cameras that do not need it — the side passage, the empty garage interior — before we suggest a bigger drive.

Two settings help most on Nassau lots. Recording the driveway, the doors and the register continuously while recording quieter cameras only on motion stretches retention without losing the views that matter. And using the recorder's smart detection to mark events lets you jump straight to the people and cars on a busy night rather than scrubbing hours of tree shadows across the pool. Whatever the setting, we leave the drive-health screen and the retention figure where you can find them.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About DVR Upgrades in Nassau County

Our build environment could not load a live Google AI Overview, so instead we took the claims that turn up in cost guides, product pages and forum replies for DVR-upgrade searches — the material AI answers are assembled from — and set each against what Nassau houses and shops actually show us.

“A new DVR costs $100 to $350”

HomeGuide's camera-installation cost guide says a new DVR or NVR costs $100 to $350 when an existing unit cannot support more cameras, and puts DVR storage at $100 to $300. Those are honest box prices, and an AI answer that repeats them is not wrong about the box.

The installed job is different. A Nassau upgrade includes a surveillance-rated drive large enough for the retention you want, testing every coax run (including the one buried to the garage), new connectors where the old ones have corroded, a battery backup, the phone app, smart-detection zones and a working export routine.

That is why this page has always published roughly $500 to $1,400 installed for most Nassau homes, with the channel count and the drive size moving the figure most. If an online answer quotes a box price, compare it with the installed total, not the shelf tag.

“Just buy a new system”

One of the most common replies on camera forums to anyone with an old recorder is some version of "your best bet is to just buy a new system", and AI summaries tend to inherit that shortcut. For a small apartment with two cameras, it is reasonable.

For a Nassau house with eight or twelve cameras on coax that runs through a slab house's attic, along a fence and under a lawn to the garage, a new system means new cable to every position. That is where the money and the mess are, and it is the part that is usually still sound.

Installers in the same threads say the opposite from experience: on inherited systems, the cheapest path is often keeping the cameras, swapping the recorder and upgrading only the cameras that justify it. That is the path we price first, and we say so in writing when a camera is not worth keeping.

“Any DVR will work with your old cameras”

Summaries often treat "analogue camera" as one thing. In practice the coax cameras of the last fifteen years speak several different high-definition languages — HD-TVI, HD-CVI and AHD — plus old standard-definition analogue, and a recorder that does not support a camera's signal shows a black or rolling picture.

Mixed systems are common here because many houses were added to over the years: four cameras from the original kit, two from a later kit from a different brand, one added by a handyman. Each channel has to be identified before a recorder is chosen.

Current hybrid recorders auto-detect most of these signals, which is why they are our default. We still read each camera's label or test its output before ordering, because a single unsupported camera discovered on the day is the most avoidable delay in this work.

“Any hard drive will do”

Generic answers sometimes suggest dropping a spare desktop drive into the recorder. It will work, for a while.

A recorder writes every second of every day. Western Digital rates its WD Purple surveillance drives for workloads of up to 180 TB a year — about three times its desktop drives — with firmware intended to reduce dropped video frames. A desktop drive in that duty is the reason many old Nassau DVRs we open have a disk full of bad sectors and a beeping alarm.

We fit surveillance-rated drives only, sized for at least the retention you ask for, and we show you where the recorder reports drive health so a failing disk is noticed before it costs you a month of footage.

“Put it all in the cloud and you won't need a recorder”

Cloud cameras suit some homes, and AI answers often present them as the modern replacement for a DVR. Nassau is well connected — the Census Bureau counts 94.8 percent of households with a broadband subscription — so connection is rarely the obstacle.

The obstacles are elsewhere. Cloud recording needs every camera to upload continuously, usually with a per-camera subscription, and it stops when the modem loses power. In the eight days it took to restore everyone after Isaias, a cloud-only system would have recorded nothing once the modem's battery, if any, ran down.

A local recorder on a battery and the house generator keeps recording regardless, and the app can still push alert clips to your phone when the internet is up. For most Nassau families, local recording with remote viewing is the sturdier arrangement.

“Hikvision and Dahua are banned, so the recorder has to go”

This is a common overstatement. In November 2022 the FCC adopted rules prohibiting new equipment authorizations for equipment on its Covered List; for Hikvision and Dahua, the Congressional Research Service explains, video surveillance equipment is covered when it is used for public safety, government facilities, critical infrastructure or other national security purposes.

The order is about authorising new equipment, not about removing a recorder from a family room in Seaford. A homeowner with a working system from one of those makers is not breaking a rule by keeping it.

There are still good reasons to choose carefully: supply of new models, firmware support, and your own comfort. We install several brands and tell you plainly what each choice means for your house, rather than replacing equipment on the strength of a headline.

“An old DVR is safe as long as it has a password”

A password helps, but many older recorders were exposed to the internet through port forwarding set up years ago, and run firmware that will never be updated.

Attackers scan for exactly those boxes. Fortinet's FortiGuard Labs reported widespread exploitation of CVE-2024-3721, an unauthenticated command-injection flaw in a family of DVRs, with the compromised recorders pulled into a botnet used for denial-of-service attacks — no password needed.

On every upgrade we close the old port forwards on your router, connect the new recorder through the maker's app service instead, update its firmware, set a strong administrator password and create separate, limited logins for the rest of the household.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Test the Garage Run First

On a Nassau upgrade I do not start at the recorder. I start at the camera furthest from it, which is nearly always the one on the garage at the back of the lot. That run has been underground or along a fence the longest, it has the most connectors, and if anything on the property is going to fail a test, it is that one. If the garage camera gives me a clean picture on a test monitor at the house end, the job is a box swap and the owner can stop worrying about trenches. If it does not, I would rather know before I unbox anything, because it changes the plan and the price.

The second thing I check is what the recorder is plugged into. More often than not it is a power strip on the floor of a basement or attic, behind the boiler or beside the dehumidifier, with nothing between it and the next outage. The new one goes up off the floor, onto a battery backup it shares with the modem, and I write down which circuit it is on.

The last thing, before I leave, is to switch it off at the battery and back on, then look at the clock and the record light. If it does not come back by itself with the right time, the job is not finished. — Anwar Timothy, NYS licence #12000287431

What does a recorder swap usually cost for an eight-camera Nassau house with a detached garage?
+

If every camera works and the coax to the garage tests good, the published ranges are $650 to $1,000 for an eight-channel recorder swap and $800 to $1,200 for an eight-channel hybrid that can take IP cameras later, which is the one most Nassau owners choose. Is a Nassau County home improvement licence needed for someone to swap a recorder?
+

Nassau's Department of Consumer Affairs licenses home improvement businesses, and it is reasonable to ask anyone working in your house for their licence and insurance. AESS works under New York State low-voltage licence 12000287431, carries insurance on every Nassau job and shows the paperwork your village, co-op or landlord asks for before work starts. Can the new recorder send a snapshot when the garage door or a gate opens?
+

Yes. Most current recorders have alarm inputs, so a simple contact on the garage door or the pool gate can tell the recorder to start recording that camera at full quality and push a snapshot to your phone. Where you already have an alarm panel with a contact on that door, its output can often be wired to the recorder instead. It is a small addition that turns a generic motion alert into a specific one you will actually look at.

Do freezing January nights affect a camera or a recorder in an unheated garage?
+

Cameras are generally built for outdoor winters and cope with a Long Island January. Recorders and their hard drives are indoor equipment with a narrower operating range, and a recorder left in an unheated garage or shed sees both cold winters and hot summers, plus condensation when the temperature swings. That is why we move the recorder into the house on an upgrade and leave only cameras, and if needed a small outdoor-rated switch, in the garage. If a camera's picture fogs on cold mornings, a fresh seal or desiccant usually cures it.

If our recorder died overnight, can someone come out today?
+

Often, yes. We work two-hour arrival windows seven days a week up to 8 PM, and a recorder failure is booked into the next open window. Bring a photo of the recorder's back panel to the call so we can bring a matching hybrid recorder and drive. Most systems are recording again within one to two hours of arrival. Until then, do not keep powering a recorder that is clicking or beeping about its drive.

Can a camera cover the boat or trailer stored beside the house over the winter?
+

Yes. A camera on the side of the house or the garage can watch a boat, trailer or second car parked in the side yard, with a line drawn around it on the recorder so a person approaching it after dark raises an alert. If it sits out of sight of every existing camera, the upgrade is a good moment to add one on its own channel, aimed so the hitch and the registration on the trailer are in frame.

Will replacing the DVR interrupt the alarm company's monitoring?
+

No. The burglar alarm and its central-station monitoring run on their own panel and communicator, separate from the camera recorder, and a recorder swap does not touch them. If the recorder is wired to an alarm output, or the alarm company also provides video, we coordinate with them so the connection is restored and tested before we leave.

Can the new recorder switch on the yard floodlights when someone walks up the drive?
+

Often, yes. Many current recorders have alarm outputs, small relay contacts that the recorder can close when a person-detection zone is triggered, and an electrician can wire such an output to switch a floodlight circuit through a suitable relay. A simpler route is a camera with its own built-in deterrence light, or a floodlight camera on its own channel. Either way, a light that comes on when someone is detected lights the person for the camera and tends to send them away.

Is there a monthly fee after the upgrade?
+

Not from us. The recorder stores footage on its own drive in your house and the manufacturer's app connects to it without a subscription for live view, playback and alerts. Optional cloud storage plans from some manufacturers exist, and professional alarm monitoring is a separate service, but neither is needed for the system to record and reach your phone.

Do you take the old DVR away, and what happens to the footage on its drive?
+

That is your choice. Many owners keep the old recorder for a few weeks in case they need older footage from it; we show you how to power it up on its own to play back. If you want it removed, we can take it, and the drive can be wiped or physically destroyed first so that video of your family and home does not leave with it in readable form.
