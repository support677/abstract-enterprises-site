Element 4 · Why Staten Island Jobsites Get Hit

Framed Houses on Dark Blocks, and Nobody Around to See a Thing

Staten Island builds the way no other borough does — detached and semi-attached houses in rows, whole tracts framing at once, additions rising over occupied kitchens, and now West Shore warehouse boxes and a North Shore waterfront pipeline. That building pattern creates a theft pattern the rest of the city doesn't have: value spread across quiet blocks with no doormen, no foot traffic, and often no real fence — just orange mesh, a porta-john, and a framed structure holding tens of thousands in lumber, Romex, and boxed appliances that anyone driving past can inventory through the studs.

The national numbers set the floor — $300 million to $1 billion a year in stolen equipment and materials per NER/NICB reporting, tool recovery under 7% — and the Island's rhythm adds its own lines. Framing packages that vanish before the crew starts. Condensers unbolted a week from CO. Rough-in pulled back out of open walls. Model homes relieved of their staging TVs. And the timing is dependable: long weekends, when a block goes three days without a delivery truck, an inspector, or a witness.

And to be straight about the rules: none of this is a code issue. One- and two-family work here rarely touches the city's heavier site-safety tiers, and nothing anywhere in the code requires a camera on any of it. What a monitored system is on this Island is the witness the block doesn't have — detection where there's no fence, light where there's no streetlamp, and a live response at 3 AM on a cul-de-sac where the nearest awake human is otherwise a mile away. Presence, priced monthly.

Element 5.5 · Terminology & Name Variants

Pre-CO, Rough-In, the Leapfrog — the Island Vocabulary

Pre-CO window — the stretch between substantial completion and the certificate of occupancy, when a house is finished, furnished with mechanicals and appliances, and empty. Per square foot, the most-robbed phase in residential construction.

Rough-in — the wiring, plumbing, and HVAC installed in open framing before insulation and drywall. On the Island's house builds it sits visible through the studs for weeks — copper, Romex, and PEX on open display to every passing vehicle.

The leapfrog — our tract method: a small set of units relocated lot to lot as each house enters its exposure window, so three cameras protect nine houses across a buildout instead of nine cameras standing guard over foundations.

Exposure window — the phase when a given house holds its peak stealable value: lumber drop, rough-in, and pre-CO. Coverage scheduling on a tract is just mapping these windows across the lots.

Condenser pad — the exterior slab where the AC unit lands late in the build, usually at the rear, usually dark, and removable by two people with straps in under ten minutes. The single most targeted object on a finishing house.

Model home — the furnished sales asset on a tract: staged, advertised, and predictably empty every night. It gets a permanent-style package because it's a retail store that closes at five.

Element 9 · 14 Real Questions From Staten Island Builders, Developers & Trades

What the Island Asks After the Lumber Walks

Collected on site walks from the ferry to the Outerbridge — answered the way we answer them standing on the subfloor.

I'm building nine semi-attached houses on one tract in my Eltingville buildout, and a whole framing package disappeared off lot four over a weekend. Do I really need cameras on every lot?
The tract math

No — you need coverage that reads the tract the way the thief does. A buildout concentrates its risk in whichever houses are in an exposure window — lumber drop, rough-in, pre-CO finish — and that's rarely more than two or three lots at once. The working setup: one unit on the street approach logging every vehicle that enters, plus two leapfrogging to the houses currently holding value, relocated as phases advance, included. Three cameras, nine houses, full coverage of the actual risk — for less per house than the plywood you'd spend boarding windows, and unlike plywood, it identifies the truck.

Somebody pulled the Romex back out of my framed walls the week before insulation — twice now, two different houses. How does anyone even steal wire that's already installed?
The rough-in strip

Quickly, unfortunately — installed rough-in is just staged copper to someone with side cutters, and a framed house displays it through every stud bay. The two-house pattern says you're being watched: someone knows your inspection rhythm and hits the window between sign-off and drywall. Close it deliberately — an interior unit on the main floor, armed the minute the crew leaves, plus a frontage head logging the vehicle that keeps slowing down. Rewiring a stripped house costs multiples of the copper's scrap value in schedule, re-inspection, and callbacks, which is why this theft justifies coverage faster than almost any other.

Condensers got unbolted off the pad at two of my finished houses the week before closing. Fifteen minutes of work for them, five grand and a delayed CO for me. What's the counter?
The condenser pad

Watch the pad like it's a safe, because economically it is. Condensers vanish pre-CO for the reasons you've discovered: outside, at the rear, fast to unbolt, on a house nobody visits daily. The counter is a monitored head on the pad and rear approach — person detection, strobe, talk-down — turning the fifteen-minute job into a lit, announced thirty seconds, plus the habit of setting condensers as late as the trades allow. Cages slow a theft; a talk-down cancels one. And the same head covers the appliance staging inside, next on the same shopping list.

Our model home got broken into for the staging TVs and the kitchen appliances. It's furnished on purpose — we can't exactly empty it every night. What protects a house that's dressed to be looked at?
The model problem

Treat it as what it is: a retail store that closes at five. A model is advertised, photographed, furnished, and predictably empty every night — a burglary target designed by a marketing department. The fix is small and permanent-style: entry heads, an interior head over the main living space, arming that follows sales-office hours so the staff never touches a keypad, and monitored response overnight. Signage does half the deterrence, since the whole point of a model is that people look at it. When the tract sells out, the system comes off or converts into the final buyer's home cameras — more than once, the closing incentive that sold the model itself.

My lot has orange mesh, a porta-john, and stakes. No fence, no gate, no power. Everyone tells me cameras need infrastructure I don't have.
The bare lot

Everyone's describing wired systems and calling it a law of nature. Battery-cellular units carry their own power and signal — high-capacity batteries, solar assist where the sun allows, LTE backhaul — and mount to a pole, a tree line, or the structure the day it exists. No fence changes the philosophy, not the feasibility: on an unfenced lot the property line is enforced by detection instead of chain link, analytics drawing the boundary and a talk-down announcing it. Half the Island's house builds run exactly this way, stakes to CO, without a wire on the lot. The infrastructure you're missing is the infrastructure the units bring.

There isn't a working streetlight within 300 feet of my site. Pitch black by nine. Won't cameras just record darkness?
The dark block

Consumer cameras will. Site-grade ones treat your dark block as home-field advantage: infrared sees in zero ambient light, low-light sensors pull usable color out of near-nothing, thermal reads body heat with no light at all — and the strobe inverts the situation entirely, converting pitch black to stadium lighting the instant a person crosses the zone. The part worth internalizing: darkness was only ever the thief's ally because nobody could see. Once the site can see and the block can't, the darkness switches sides — the unlit cul-de-sac becomes the easiest place on the Island to get caught on camera.

I'm fitting out a warehouse on my West Shore project — laydown the size of a football field, racking and dock equipment arriving for months. This isn't a house-lot problem. What's the big-site version?
The big box

The logistics stack, scaled to acreage. Elevated auto-tracking PTZ sweeps the laydown and truck court — one high head doing the work of six fixed ones. Plate capture at the gates logs every tractor and box truck against the delivery schedule. Once the interior phase starts — racking, dock levelers, the equipment that arrives by the trailer and disappears by the pallet — compact heads move inside to receiving and staging, with monitored escalation on the docks. Same monitored-response philosophy as a house lot with an extra zero on the footprint, engineered from day one to convert into the facility's permanent system at turnover.

We're putting a second story on an occupied house — the family lives downstairs while we build up top. Tools and material stay on site, but it's also somebody's home. How does security work in that arrangement?
The occupied addition

With more precision and more courtesy than a vacant site needs. Coverage targets the construction zones only — exterior staging, scaffold access, the upstairs work area — armed for the hours the crew is gone and the family is asleep, exactly when staged material on an addition gets shopped. Aiming discipline matters doubly: heads point at your scaffold and material, privacy masking blacks out the neighbors' yards and the family's own windows, and the homeowners get feed access — which they invariably love. Done right, the system protects your tools, their home, and the relationship. We do it right.

Lumber pricing made my framing drop worth more than my truck, and it sits on the lot for two weeks before the framers start. Can you cover a pile?
The pile weeks

Yes — and the pile weeks deserve dedicated coverage, because banded framing packages are the most liquid asset in residential construction: untraceable, universally needed, pre-loaded onto skids for the thief's convenience. A monitored unit with detection, strobe, and talk-down covers the drop; a frontage head logging plates covers the reconnaissance that precedes it. Two schedule habits multiply the protection: take delivery close to framing start, and stage the package deep on the lot — every foot from the curb adds carry time, and carry time is what talk-downs interrupt. The unit that guards the pile then rides the build to CO.

Every hit we've ever taken happened on a holiday weekend — Memorial Day, July 4th, Labor Day. Is that a real pattern or just my luck?
The long-weekend harvest

It's the realest pattern in residential site theft. A holiday weekend hands the thief three straight nights with no deliveries, no inspectors, no crews — and on a residential block, no witnesses either, since the neighbors are at the shore too. Sites get scouted Thursday, harvested Saturday, discovered Tuesday. We build for the calendar: extended arming across the long stretch, monitored response prioritized when the block goes quiet, and a pre-holiday health check confirming every head is aimed, charged, and reporting. Your luck isn't bad — your exposure was scheduled. So is the coverage.

The house is done and we're just waiting on the CO — fixtures in, appliances in, nobody on site for days at a stretch. It feels like the riskiest moment of the whole build. Is it?
The pre-CO dead zone

It is, and your instinct is better than most builders' spreadsheets. The pre-CO stretch combines maximum contents with minimum presence: the house holds its full appliance package, fixtures, condensers, and finish material while daily traffic drops to nothing. Every incentive that protected the site — crews at seven, deliveries at ten — has evaporated, and the only thing between the contents and a box truck is a builder's lockset. The fix is cheap relative to the exposure: keep one monitored unit through closing, on the rear access and interior staging, armed continuously. It's the same rental that watched the framing — it just stays six more weeks, through the only phase where the house is both full and abandoned.

Vandals keep breaking windows in my nearly finished houses — not stealing anything, just smashing. Insurance covers glass but the delays are killing my closings. Does security even work on people who don't want anything?
The window breakers

It works better on them, actually. Theft crews weigh risk against payoff; vandals — usually local, young, and on foot — run on zero payoff, so any risk at all changes the math. A strobe that turns the yard to daylight and a live voice announcing they're recorded ends the average window-breaking career on the spot, because the activity depends on being consequence-free. When it doesn't end immediately, the footage identifies repeat players — faces, routes, the same three kids — and gives the precinct something concrete. Glass is insurable; blown closings aren't. The deterrence package exists for exactly this species of loss.

A national camera company quoted me a full solar tower — trailer, mast, the works — for a quarter-acre house lot. That can't be right, can it?
The tower on the lawn

It's right for their inventory and wrong for your lot. The tower exists for highway projects and hundred-acre laydowns, where a twenty-foot mast earns its freight bill. On a quarter-acre residential lot it's a monument — overkill in coverage, oversized in cost, conspicuous on a block of two-family homes. The right-sized answer is one or two compact battery-cellular heads on the structure or a pole: same detection, same monitoring, same talk-down, at a fraction of the monthly. We stock both scales and quote what the site needs, which on a house lot is essentially never the tower. When the footprint grows into warehouse acreage, we'll talk masts.

I've got five active jobs scattered across the Island, fifteen minutes apart. Managing five separate camera setups sounds worse than the theft.
The scattered portfolio

Then don't manage five setups — run one account with five addresses. Every lot gets the units its phase calls for, all in a single app: one login, per-site arming, alerts labeled by address, footage searchable across the portfolio, one monitoring protocol, one call list. The fleet flexes as the portfolio does — when the Annadale house closes, its units shift to the new foundation without a new contract. Scattered-lot builders are half our residential book precisely because the alternative really is worse than the theft. One screen, whole operation.

Element 10 · People Also Ask

Fast Answers to the First-Search Questions

How do I stop framing lumber theft?

Time deliveries close to framing start, stage the package deep on the lot, and cover it with a monitored unit — person and vehicle detection, strobe, and talk-down turn a quiet load-out into an interrupted one, and a frontage head logs the scouting that precedes it.

How do I protect AC condensers on new construction?

Set them as late in the schedule as possible, and watch the pad with a monitored head covering the rear approach — detection plus talk-down cancels the fifteen-minute unbolt job that cages only slow down.

How do you secure a new house before move-in?

Keep one monitored unit through the pre-CO stretch — the weeks when the house is full of appliances and fixtures but empty of people — covering the rear access and interior staging, armed continuously until closing.

How much do construction site cameras cost on Staten Island?

Monitored rentals start at $349 a month for one unit, $649 for two, and about $999 for full multi-unit monitored coverage; wired systems run $500–$2,500 per camera installed.

Do construction sites without fences need cameras?

They need them more — on an unfenced lot, detection is the perimeter. Battery-cellular heads with analytics zones enforce the property line that chain link never got built, with a talk-down announcing it.

How do you secure a model home?

Entry and interior heads, arming synced to sales-office hours, and monitored overnight response — a furnished, advertised, predictably empty house is a retail store after closing, and it gets protected like one.

What's the best security setup for home builders?

A small monitored unit set that rides each build from lumber drop through CO, relocations included, plus a portfolio app when the lots multiply — coverage that follows the exposure window instead of the address.

When do construction sites get robbed most?

Long holiday weekends and the pre-CO stretch — the calendar's quietest windows, when sites go days without deliveries, inspectors, or witnesses. Coverage should be built around both.

Element 10.5 · AI Overview Reality Check

What the AI Overviews Get Wrong About Island Jobsites

Machine-written answers average every jobsite in America into one paragraph. The Island's sites don't average. Where the summaries break here:

1. "Suburban-style sites are safer than city jobsites."

They're quieter, which is not the same thing. A dense-borough site has all-night foot traffic, lit corners, and a hundred accidental witnesses; a residential block at 2 AM has none of those — just a framed house displaying its contents through the studs.

Low witness density is the thief's favorite site condition, and the Island's building pattern supplies it by the mile.

Quiet blocks don't reduce the risk. They privatize it.

2. "A tarp over the lumber keeps it out of sight, out of mind."

A tarp advertises that something under it was worth covering. Framing crews, thieves, and everyone else on the Island reads a blue rectangle on a lot the same way: banded package, pre-skidded, ready to load.

Concealment isn't a strategy when the object is the size of a car and the block is empty.

Detection with response is the strategy. The tarp can stay for the rain.

3. "Condensers are too heavy to steal."

Two people, two straps, a hand truck, ten minutes — the weight argument dies at every stripped pad on the Island. Resale and scrap value make the effort a wage, and the rear-yard placement makes it private.

The same logic once claimed catalytic converters were safe under parked trucks.

Heavy means slower, not safe. Slower is exactly what monitored detection is built to exploit.

4. "The neighbors will notice anything suspicious."

At 3 AM on a residential block, the neighbors are the least reliable security system ever proposed — asleep, away for the long weekend, or reasonably assuming the truck at the framed house is the builder's.

A pickup loading material looks identical whether it's authorized or not. Neighbors can't tell; a plate log can.

The site needs its own witness. That's the entire product.

5. "Wait until the house is worth protecting."

Per square foot, the framed shell with rough-in is the value peak — lumber at market prices, copper and Romex on open display, and everything at its most liquid and least traceable.

Waiting for drywall means insuring the vault after the cash moved out.

Coverage should start when the value arrives — the lumber drop — not when the house looks finished.

6. "GPS trackers in the tools make cameras unnecessary."

Trackers are a recovery bet on a sub-7% recovery game — they tell you where the tools went after the loss already happened, assuming the tag survives the first parking-garage sweep.

Cameras with monitored response play the only winning position: interruption, before the truck loads.

Track the tools if you like. Watch the site either way.

7. "One hardware-store floodlight cam covers a whole build."

Porch logic on a construction problem. A consumer floodlight cam watches one approach, needs house Wi-Fi that doesn't exist yet, and alerts a phone that's on Do Not Disturb — while the build has four approaches, no network, and no one awake.

Site coverage is zones, self-powered units, analytics, and a monitoring desk that answers.

The floodlight cam is a fine housewarming gift for the buyer. It's not site security.

Element 21 · Field Notes

What I Check on an Island Walk Before We Mount Anything

Installer's notes — the house-lot checklist:

1. Map the exposure windows across the lots. Which house gets lumber this month, which hits rough-in, which is waiting on a CO — the leapfrog schedule writes itself off the phase map.

5. Read the calendar before the layout. Closings, holiday weekends, delivery dates — half of Island security is scheduling. The arming plan gets built against the calendar, not just the floor plan.

My lot only has orange mesh and stakes — do cameras work without a real fence?

Yes — detection replaces the perimeter a suburban lot never gets. Battery-cellular heads on poles or the structure itself watch the approaches with person and vehicle analytics, so the property line is enforced by alerts and talk-down instead of chain link. Half the Island's builds run exactly this way.

How do I protect condensers and appliances in a house waiting on its CO?

Cover the pre-CO window deliberately — it's the most-robbed stretch of any house build. A monitored unit watching the condenser pad and rear access, an interior head where the appliances stage, and armed after-hours zones turn the finished-but-empty weeks from an open invitation into a recorded, answered space.

I'm building multiple houses on one tract — does every lot need its own cameras?

No — coverage follows the phase, not the lot count. Two or three units typically hold a tract: one on the street approach and gate, the rest leapfrogging to whichever houses are in their exposure window — framing, rough-in, or pre-CO. Relocations between lots are included, so the same hardware protects the whole buildout.

Can you secure a furnished model home?

Yes — model homes get a small permanent-style package: entry and interior heads, arming that follows sales-office hours automatically, and monitored response overnight. The staging furniture, TVs, and appliances that make the model sell are exactly what walks out of an unwatched one.

I build on scattered lots across the Island — one system or five?

One account, every site. Each lot gets the units its phase needs, and everything lands in a single app with per-site arming, alerts, and footage. When a house closes, its units shift to your next lot — the portfolio stays covered continuously and you manage it from one screen.

Our sites only ever get hit on long weekends. Can coverage flex for that?

Holiday weekends are harvest season — three quiet days, no inspectors, no deliveries — so we build for them: extended arming windows, monitored response prioritized on the long stretches, and pre-holiday checks that every head is aimed and healthy before the site goes silent.

My street has no lighting at all — can cameras see anything out there?

Darkness is a solved problem: infrared and low-light sensors see in zero ambient light, thermal heads detect body heat where optics can't, and the strobe converts pitch black into daylight the moment a person crosses the zone. On an unlit block, the system is the only witness — and it's the one that works best at night.

Can you handle a warehouse-scale project — big laydown, dock equipment, racking?

Yes — logistics builds get the big-footprint stack: elevated PTZ over the laydown and truck court, plate capture at the gates, and interior heads once racking, dock levelers, and equipment start landing. Same monitored response, scaled to a site measured in acres instead of lots.

The lot is raw — no power, no internet, nothing. What runs the cameras?

The units bring their own everything: high-capacity batteries, solar assist where the exposure allows, and LTE cellular for signal. Nothing on the lot is required — coverage starts the day the survey stakes go in and runs until the house has walls, power, and a wired option if you want one.

Do you take the cameras down when we sell the houses?

Yes — removal at closeout is included, timed to your closing calendar so coverage runs through the final walk-through. Builders who want a selling point instead can have us convert the setup into the buyer's permanent home camera system — a clean handoff either way.
