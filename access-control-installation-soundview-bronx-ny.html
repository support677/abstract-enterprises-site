One Campus, Several Owners · Gates That Keep Their Hours · Bid-Ready Scopes · Free Exit Always

Soundview was built in large pieces. Between the 1940s and the 1970s the city and the state filled it with superblocks: nine NYCHA developments and a ring of Mitchell-Lama complexes such as Lafayette-Boynton, Lafayette-Morrison and Stevenson Commons, most of them towers turned inward around private courtyards and parking lots. Now new buildings are going up on those lots and lawns, often with a different owner from the towers beside them. When one fenced campus becomes several buildings, public paths and shared driveways, the question is no longer how to lock the campus but which door, gate and amenity belongs to whom, and on what hours. That is the work we plan here.

The Neighborhood

Why Soundview Properties Need Access Control

Soundview sits on the Clason Point peninsula in the southeast Bronx. Wikipedia gives its generally recognized boundaries as the Cross Bronx Expressway to the north, White Plains Road to the east, Lacombe Avenue to the south and the Bronx River to the west, with the Bruckner Expressway cutting through the middle. It is part of Community District 9, the 43rd Precinct patrols it from 900 Fteley Avenue, and its public-housing developments are covered by Police Service Area 8. The ZIP code is 10472 north of the Bruckner and 10473 south of it. Community Board 9 itself still treats Bronx River, Clason Point, Harding Park and Soundview/Bruckner as separate communities, and the name “Sound View” for the whole area was chosen by Borough President Herman Badillo in 1967; on this page Soundview means the blocks between the Bronx River and White Plains Road, with Clason Point and Harding Park covered on their own pages.

The place was farmland and estates until the IRT Pelham Line opened along Westchester Avenue in 1920 and one- and two-family houses followed. What changed it most came later. In 1941 Clason Point Gardens became the first NYCHA development in the Bronx; in 1947 the city put up 947 temporary apartments in 473 Quonset huts near Bruckner Boulevard and Boynton Avenue; and through the 1950s and 1960s more public housing went up across the peninsula. Wikipedia lists nine NYCHA developments in Soundview today, from the 45 two-story buildings of Clason Point Gardens to the nine 14-story towers of Bronx River Houses and the twenty-eight 7-story buildings of Sotomayor Houses, formerly Bronxdale.

Then came the middle-income towers. Through the 1960s and early 1970s the state’s Mitchell-Lama program built Lafayette-Morrison, Carol Gardens, River Plaza, Lafayette-Boynton and Stevenson Commons. They are big: The Real Deal described Lafayette-Boynton in 2011 as a four-building, million-square-foot complex with 972 apartments, and City Planning’s environmental review describes Stevenson Commons as nine buildings and 948 apartments completed in the mid-1970s on a superblock of about 15.6 acres bounded by Lafayette Avenue, White Plains Road, Seward Avenue and Thieriot Avenue. Both complexes, like most of their generation, were laid out around private open space rather than along the street. WXY, the architects of the current Stevenson Square plan, put it simply: the original buildings face a closed central courtyard, and much of the land around them was surface parking or unused.

That land is now being built on. In 2019 two buildings with 223 and 212 apartments opened at 1520 and 1530 Story Avenue, on the northern end of the Lafayette-Boynton campus, after its developers reconfigured the parking and landscaping. In 2020 the city financed Soundview Homes, 72 co-ops in ten four-story townhouses on NYCHA’s Soundview Houses campus. In June 2024 Casa Celina, a 16-story senior building with 204 apartments, opened on what NYCHA called underused land at Sotomayor Houses. And on October 14, 2026 the Governor announced the completion of Stevenson Square Phase I: two four-story co-op buildings with 58 homes and a senior residence, the first three of six new buildings planned for the Stevenson Commons superblock.

Every one of those projects changes doors that used to be simple. A fence that once enclosed one owner’s towers now has a second owner’s front door inside it, or a new public walk running through it. A parking lot with one gate becomes a construction site, then a building, then a set of paths. A private driveway that served one garage starts serving three. City Planning’s review of Stevenson Commons spells it out: about 1.94 acres of the redeveloped block would be open to the public, but the playground and tennis court facilities would be locked during the evening hours to ensure security. Somebody has to decide which gates lock when, who holds the keys and codes, and what happens to the people inside at closing time. Most of the access control work we are asked about in Soundview is some version of that question.

The other strand is the stock that was here already. Rows of attached and semi-detached multi-unit houses, small walk-ups along the side streets, and shops along Westchester Avenue under the 6 train, White Plains Road and Soundview Avenue all need ordinary, well-installed doors. Crime in the 43rd Precinct is far lower than it was: the precinct’s figures, as summarized on Wikipedia, show the seven major felonies falling from 9,500 in 1990 to 3,143 in 2025, with burglaries down by more than 85 percent. What remains is mostly the everyday problem of buildings with many doors and many people, which is exactly what access control is for.

Repairs

Lobby Door Stuck or a Gate Reader Dead in Soundview?

If a tower’s lobby door is standing open, a garage door will not close, or a gate reader has died with residents waiting outside, call the Soundview line at (646) 490-0629 and tell us first whether anyone is locked in or out. A gate around a playground or a court that will not unlock in the morning, or will not lock at night, counts as urgent too, because on a shared campus somebody else’s schedule depends on it.

The faults we find most often on the campuses are physical, not electronic. A lobby door closer has worn out and the door no longer pulls itself shut against the strike. A gate leaf has dropped on its hinges and the lock no longer lines up with the keeper. A reader post beside a path has been hit by a plow or a mower. A cable run across a courtyard was cut during landscaping for a new building. And in buildings that changed managers, the system works but nobody has the administrator login.

We work on systems other installers fitted, including equipment another owner on the same campus put in. Where a door belongs to a different owner from the one who called us, we make it safe and lockable, tell both sides what we found, and do the rest only with that owner’s authorization.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Soundview’s Architecture

Soundview has more kinds of buildings than most Bronx neighborhoods, because each decade added its own. The types below are the ones we plan for. We describe them in general; every building is surveyed before anything is chosen, and we never assume that two buildings in the same complex were built or wired the same way.

Mitchell-Lama towers of the 1960s and 1970s

High-rise slabs and towers set back from the street across lawns and parking, with lobbies facing a courtyard or a drive. Long walks from the sidewalk to the door, garages or surface lots, compactor and service doors off long corridors, and lobby doors that have been replaced more than once.

Public housing campuses

Seven-story and taller buildings in groups, owned by NYCHA. We work there only when the owner or a PACT manager hires us; residents’ apartment doors belong to the housing authority.

Co-ops converted from Mitchell-Lama

Lafayette-Morrison was turned into affordable co-ops for its existing tenants after a 2006 sale, as The Real Deal reported; a 2006 New York Times article, which we could read only as an excerpt, said the converted complex would be renamed Lafayette Estates. Boards there make the decisions, and many shareholders have lived in the buildings for decades.

New walk-up co-ops

Low-rise buildings such as Stevenson Square’s, where WXY describes each stair leading directly to two apartments per floor with no corridor, and eight projecting stair volumes serving 58 homes on Seward Avenue. That means several street doors per building instead of one lobby.

New senior buildings

Casa Celina on the Sotomayor campus and the senior residence at Stevenson Square: one lobby, on-site services, residents who may use walkers or wheelchairs, and staff who need access to the people they look after.

Older houses and walk-ups

Wikipedia notes row houses, semi-detached and detached homes and apartment buildings throughout, most with several units. These need the same doors as anywhere else in the Bronx: a good street door, a working intercom and codes that end when tenants leave.

How we adapt the hardware to these buildings

  • Block-and-plank construction. Habitat for Humanity describes the new Stevenson co-ops as block and plank: concrete masonry bearing walls with precast concrete plank floors. There is no hollow wall or ceiling to fish cable through, so conduit and boxes for readers have to be placed before the block goes up, or surface raceway planned afterwards.
  • Old aluminum lobby doors. Many towers still have heavy storefront-type aluminum and glass doors. The narrow stiles limit which locks fit, so we check the frame and the door before choosing between an electric strike, an electrified exit device or a magnetic lock with code-required releases.
  • Long underground runs. On a campus the controller may be in one building and the gate it runs in the middle of a lawn. We use the conduit that exists where we can prove it is clear, and otherwise plan the trench or the wireless link with the owner’s landscape plan in hand.
  • Many owners, many systems. Two owners on one campus rarely want to share one controller. We keep each owner’s system separate and connect them only where a shared gate needs to accept both sets of credentials.
  • Fire-rated doors. Stair, corridor and garage doors in towers are usually fire-rated, so hardware must be listed for the door and the door must still close and latch on its own.

Campus Infill

One Campus, Several Owners: Doors When the Superblock Is Built On

The postwar campus was designed around a simple idea of security: one owner, one fence, a few gates, and towers set well back inside. Residents came in through a gate and crossed private ground to their lobby. That idea is being taken apart in Soundview one parking lot at a time. The new buildings are deliberately different. WXY writes that, unlike the original Stevenson Commons buildings, the new ones face the street, and its plan places shared spaces along a new pedestrian walk. Casa Celina rose on a parking lot inside a public housing campus. The Story Avenue buildings were built by reconfiguring the parking and landscaping of an existing complex, and their developer lists greenways, a playground and a fitness area built for both new and existing residents.

For doors, that means the old fence stops being the line between inside and outside. Some of the ground inside it is now another owner’s front yard; some of it is a walk the public may use by day; some is a driveway several garages open onto. The useful line becomes the door of each building, and the gates that remain have to be thought of as shared equipment with hours, not as a wall.

We approach a campus in this order. First, ownership: which parcel each door, gate, path and drive sits on, taken from the owners’ own documents rather than from what the fence suggests. Second, use: who walks or drives through each opening, at which hours, and whether the approved site plan or a shared-use agreement already says it must be open. Third, the building doors themselves, which are where homes are protected and where readers and panels do the most good. Fourth, the shared gates, which get the simplest equipment that will keep to its schedule and let people out. Only then do we talk about brands.

The hardest moment is the middle of construction. For a year or more a campus has a building site in the middle of it, a fence that moves every few months, contractors who need a way in, and residents whose usual route to the bus or the store has disappeared. City Planning’s review of Stevenson Commons laid the work out in three phases running from 2021 to an estimated early 2028, with surface lots and driveway access changing between phases. A gate that was the right answer in phase one can be in the wrong place by phase two, so we plan temporary arrangements that can be moved and reused rather than permanent hardware on a fence that is about to come down.

Open Space on a Schedule

Public by Day, Locked by Night: Gates That Keep Their Hours

The old Soundview campuses kept their open space private. City Planning’s review notes that at Stevenson Commons access to the fields was restricted and secured with fencing. The redevelopment plan reverses that for part of the block: about 1.94 acres of publicly accessible open space, with paths through it, while the playground and tennis court facilities would be locked during the evening hours. A future plan for the block also mentions new tennis courts, a walking path and a sensory playground, according to Habitat for Humanity’s project page.

A gate that has to be open to the public at set hours and locked at others is a scheduling problem first and a lock problem second. If a person has to remember to walk out at dusk and lock it, it will sometimes be forgotten and sometimes be locked early, and either failure is visible to everyone. A powered lock on a schedule solves most of that, but it brings its own questions: what time counts as evening in December and in June, who can change the schedule for a community event, and what happens to a parent and child still on the court when the lock engages.

Our answer to the last question is always the same: the gate locks against coming in, never against going out. We fit hardware that opens from the inside with a push bar or a lever at any time, set the outside to lock on schedule, and where the space is big enough that someone might not notice closing time, add a short warning such as a light or a chime a few minutes before. If an owner wants the outside to stay unlocked during the day but still know when the gate was used, a door contact can count openings without stopping anyone.

Schedules also need an owner. On a campus with several buildings, one of them operates the gate; the others need a simple way to ask for a change. We set up holiday and event exceptions in advance, give the operating owner’s staff the power to change them, and keep a short written schedule posted at the gate so residents and the public know the hours. When the approved plan sets the hours, the schedule follows the plan, not the convenience of whoever runs the controller.

Mitchell-Lama Housing Companies

Buying Access Control Under the Mitchell-Lama Rules

Habitat for Humanity describes Stevenson Commons as a 948-unit HPD Mitchell-Lama complex, and at the October 2026 announcement Council Member Amanda Farías spoke of the 948 existing Mitchell-Lama apartments having their affordability protections extended for another 40 years. For a housing company supervised by the city’s Department of Housing Preservation and Development, buying an access control system is not only a building decision. The city’s Mitchell-Lama rules set out how contracts are let.

Under 28 RCNY § 3-07, contracts for building services, repairs, replacements and improvements are to be let on the basis of lowest cost compatible with quality. Any contract over $100,000 must be let on the basis of at least three competitive bids and submitted for HPD’s written approval, together with the bids and a contract signed by the winning bidder; HPD may also require any individual housing company to submit contracts over $5,000. The rule requires two pieces of contract language: that material, equipment and workmanship are subject to HPD inspection before final payment, and, for contracts that need approval, that no work shall commence until HPD approves the agreement. Managing agents must also notify HPD when payments to one vendor reach $100,000 in a fiscal year, after which further charges need HPD’s prior written approval.

We are not lawyers and the housing company’s managing agent and attorney are the right people to apply the rule. What we can do is make our part easy to compare and approve. We write the scope door by door so other bidders can price exactly the same work, keep alternates (such as phone credentials or a second garage reader) as separate lines rather than hiding them in a total, state the model numbers we propose and the equivalents we would accept, and include the inspection and approval language the rule requires. We also say plainly in writing that we will not start work until the approval the contract calls for has been given.

Not every Soundview tower is still in the program. The Real Deal reported in 2011 that Lafayette-Morrison had been converted into affordable co-ops for its existing tenants after a 2006 sale, and that the buyer of Lafayette-Boynton had agreed to keep it middle-income for 40 years in exchange for a tax abatement. Buildings that left the program, or are regulated under a different agreement, follow whatever their own documents say. We ask at the first meeting which rules apply rather than assume.

Occupied Renovations

Renovating With Residents at Home: PACT, Temporary Moves and Fobs

Soundview’s public housing is also being rebuilt from the inside. The Housing Development Corporation announced in 2024 the financial closing of a $275 million PACT renovation at Sack Wern Houses, covering more than 400 apartments in seven buildings for nearly 800 residents, with the buildings converted to Project-Based Section 8 while NYCHA keeps ownership of the land and buildings. The partners’ own site lists what changes, including enhanced security and site-wide cameras and access control, and a new property manager. Its meeting list includes two sessions in late 2023 on temporary on-site moves. Wikipedia also records a $93 million modernization of apartments at Bronx River Addition.

We are not involved in those projects and mention them because they show what an occupied renovation does to doors. While apartments are gutted, families move for weeks into a vacant apartment elsewhere on the campus and then move back. Contractors need access to the floors they are working on and nowhere else. Old locks and intercoms stay in service until the new ones are ready, then everyone changes over at once. Each of those is an access task, and a system that is set up for it makes the renovation calmer for the people living through it.

The practices we use apply to any occupied renovation in Soundview, whether under PACT, a Mitchell-Lama capital program or a private owner’s upgrade. Credentials follow people, not apartments: a family moving to a temporary unit keeps its own fobs, which are given access to the temporary building and floor for the dates of the move and lose it on the return date. Contractor credentials are tied to a phase and a floor. And the changeover from the old system to the new one is done building by building, with enrollment sessions held in the lobby or community room at hours residents can make, in the languages the building’s residents use.

Older Residents on the Campus

Senior Buildings and Long-Time Shareholders

Two kinds of older residents shape door work here. The first live in the new senior buildings. Casa Celina, opened in June 2024, has 204 apartments for older adults: according to the NYCHA Journal, 62 are reserved for seniors who were formerly homeless, 142 are for households at or below 50 percent of area median income, and one is for a live-in superintendent, with JASA managing the building and providing on-site services. At Stevenson Square, WXY describes a senior residence of 116 studios with supportive services and laundry near the lobby and common rooms facing the pedestrian path; the Governor’s announcement gives the senior component as 117 apartments in one place and 116 in another, so we quote both.

The second kind have lived in the Mitchell-Lama towers for decades. City Council budget documents for fiscal 2021 list funding for a Lafayette Estates NORC, a naturally occurring retirement community program, for senior activities and supportive services. In a building like that, the people who need to get in are not only residents and visitors but nurses, caseworkers, home-care aides and program staff, and the residents themselves may be slower to reach the door or less comfortable with phones.

For both, the principles are the same. A resident must always be able to let their own visitor in, from a phone or an indoor station, without passing through an office. The service office can be a first stop for visitors who do not know an apartment number, but not a gate. Program staff get credentials in their own group with the hours their program runs, so a resident’s door is opened by the resident, not by a master key carried by whoever is on shift. And lobby doors are timed for people with walkers and wheelchairs: the lock releases first, any power operator opens the door second, and the door relocks only after it has closed.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control in Soundview

Soundview’s shops are on a few long corridors and in shopping centers. Wikipedia names White Plains Road, Soundview Avenue and Westchester Avenue as the main commercial corridors, and the Shops at Bruckner on Story Avenue, built in 1966, and Bruckner Commons across White Plains Road as the big centers, now under one owner and being renovated. The campuses have their own retail too: the Governor’s announcement mentions a supermarket and the Stevenson Family Health Center at Stevenson Commons. In June 2020, the Bronx Times reported, a group smashed the glass window of a cell phone store on Westchester Avenue at about 2:40 a.m., broke through its front gate and took about $50,000 in cash and property.

White Plains Road

Story Avenue and the shopping centers

Soundview Avenue

A long north–south corridor with local shops, offices and community services, from the Bruckner toward Clason Point.

Campus storefronts and clinics

Supermarkets, health centers, day-care and community spaces on the ground floors of campus buildings, each needing its own street door and a controlled door into residential space.

The Bronx River edge

Storage, warehousing and auto repair along the western edge, with yard gates, roll-up doors and offices that need staff codes and after-hours records.

Rules that shape business doors here

  • Getting out: under the city Building Code a door on an exit route has to open from the inside with no key, code or special knowledge, so a store’s reader always sits on the street or corridor side and staff leave by an ordinary lever or panic bar.
  • The landlord’s say: a store tenant rarely owns its door frame, and shopping centers guard their service corridors closely, so we prepare a one-page drawing and equipment list the tenant can send for written approval before anything is drilled.
  • Sound on cameras: recording a conversation nobody in it agreed to can be a crime under New York’s eavesdropping statute, so door stations at a store are set to talk live but not to save audio, unless the owner’s lawyer says otherwise.
  • Employee codes: giving every worker a personal code shows who unlocked the back door each morning; only the owner and the manager see that history, and a departing worker’s code is removed before their final shift ends.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Soundview: Questions Answered

These are the questions Soundview boards, managing agents, owners and store tenants ask us about doors on campuses that are changing, in Mitchell-Lama companies, in occupied renovations and in shopping centers. Each answer is how we would handle it; where a rule or code is involved, we say which and suggest when to check with the right professional.

When a New Building Goes Up on the Campus

The construction fence for the new building on our old parking lot now blocks the walk residents took to the bus on White Plains Road. How do we keep a temporary route controlled while the fence is up?

Agree the temporary route with the general contractor before the fence closes, not after residents complain. Usually it is a fenced walk along the edge of the site with a gate at each end. The campus-side gate takes residents’ existing fobs and stays on the same schedule as the gate it replaces; the street-side gate opens freely from inside. The construction gate is a separate opening on its own contractor credentials.

We fit temporary locks and readers to the contractor’s fence posts or to freestanding posts so they can be unbolted and moved when the fence moves at the next phase, and we wire them back to the building’s system with a short temporary run or a wireless link. Lighting along the temporary walk matters as much as the lock, and we tell the owner where it is missing.

When the new building on our campus opens, its residents will cross our grounds to reach their own entrance. Must our perimeter gate come out, or can two owners share one gate?

First check what the owners agreed: many infill projects come with an easement or shared-use agreement saying who may cross which ground and whether gates may be closed. If the agreement allows the gate, it can stay and serve both buildings. One owner operates the gate controller, and the other owner’s residents are either enrolled on it or accepted through a link to their own system.

What should not happen is for one owner to hand the other a single shared code, or a box of its own fobs. Codes get passed on and fobs never come back. A shared gate should know which building a credential belongs to, so each owner can switch off its own people without asking the other.

Our approved site plan makes the new open space public by day, with the playground and tennis courts locked in the evening. Can those gates lock on their own, and what happens to someone still inside at closing time?

Yes. A powered lock on a schedule locks the outside of the gate at the set time and unlocks it in the morning, with holiday and event exceptions loaded in advance. Anyone still inside walks out with a push bar or lever at any hour; the lock only stops people coming in. For a large court or playground we add a light or chime a few minutes before closing.

The hours should come from the approved plan or the owners’ agreement, and be posted at the gate. A door contact then confirms each night that the gate actually shut, and sends a message to the operating owner’s staff if it was propped or jammed.

The new co-op beside us has a separate street door for every stair, each serving two apartments a floor. Does each stair door need its own call panel?

Usually yes, and a small one. With no lobby, a visitor goes straight to the stair door of the apartment they want, so the panel at that door only needs the few apartments on that stair: a single-button or short-directory station with a reader. Putting one large panel at one door would send visitors to the wrong stair.

All the stair doors can still run on one controller and one administrator login, so the board manages every door, fob and schedule in one place. The bike room, the rear yard gate and the meter room are added to the same system on their own groups.

Mitchell-Lama Contracts and Approvals

Our access control job will probably cost more than $100,000. Could the board split it into a lobby contract and a garage contract so each stays under HPD’s three-bid threshold?

We would advise against it, and so should your managing agent. The rule’s purpose is competitive bidding and HPD review on large work; HPD can require approval of contracts over $5,000 anyway, and the rule separately requires the managing agent to tell HPD when payments to one vendor reach $100,000 in a fiscal year, after which further charges need HPD’s prior written approval. Splitting one project into pieces with the same contractor tends to arrive at the same place later and with less goodwill.

What does make sense is phasing a project for genuine reasons, such as funding or the order buildings are renovated, and bidding it honestly as a whole with each phase priced separately. Ask the housing company’s attorney how the rule applies to your plan before anything is signed.

HPD has to approve our contract before any work starts. What can safely be done about a failing lobby door while we wait?

Keep the existing system working and the door secure as maintenance, not as the new project. Adjusting or replacing a worn closer, realigning a strike, replacing a failed power supply or repairing a cut wire keeps the building safe and is ordinary upkeep, priced on its own and kept small. Whether even that needs separate approval is for the managing agent to confirm under the company’s own arrangements with HPD.

What should wait is anything that belongs to the approved scope: new readers, panels, controllers or cabling. Starting those early runs straight into the contract clause that says no work shall begin until HPD approves the agreement.

Garages, Driveways and Old Towers

Our tower’s below-grade garage opens off a private driveway the new buildings will also use. Can our garage door open only for our residents while the driveway stays open to everyone?

Yes. Control moves from the driveway entrance to the garage door itself. A reader on a post at the top of the ramp, or a long-range vehicle tag reader, opens the garage for your residents only, while the driveway stays open for the other buildings’ garages, deliveries and emergency vehicles. City Planning’s Stevenson Commons review describes exactly this kind of layout, with garage entrances reached from a private driveway off Seward or Lafayette Avenue.

Two details matter. The reader post must sit where a driver can reach it without blocking the driveway for the next car, and the garage door’s safety devices must stop it closing on a car or a person. If the driveway ever needs a gate of its own, that is a shared gate and the owners decide together.

Our tower still has its original 1970s aluminum-and-glass lobby doors. Can a reader and an electric lock go on them, or do the doors have to be replaced first?

Often they can stay. Narrow-stile aluminum doors take electric strikes designed for them, electrified exit devices, or electrified locks fitted in the stile, provided the frame is sound and the door hangs true. We check the pivots or hinges, the closer and the frame anchors first, because a door that drags or flexes will defeat any lock.

Replacement makes sense when the frame is corroded, the glass is not safety glazing, or the door has been patched so often that nothing lines up. If replacement is coming within a year or two, we fit the reader and panel so they can move onto the new door.

Residents, Staff and Service Providers

During our development’s renovation, some families will move to a vacant apartment in another building for a few weeks. How does a resident’s fob follow them there and back?

Keep the family’s own fobs and change what they open. Management enters the move dates, and the fobs gain the temporary building’s entrance and floor until the move-back date, when that access ends by itself. The family keeps access to its home building throughout, so they can check on belongings and collect mail.

Issuing new fobs for a few weeks creates a pile of loose credentials nobody collects. Package room access and courier instructions should follow the family too, which is easier when every household has its own credentials and the system knows where each one is staying.

Our co-op runs a NORC program whose nurse and caseworkers visit older shareholders at home. Can program staff get credentials for the floors where their clients live without a master key?

Yes. Program staff go into their own group, with the building entrance and the floors where clients live, during the hours the program runs, and nothing else. Each worker has a personal credential, so when someone leaves the program their access ends that day and the log shows who came in.

The apartment door is still the shareholder’s. Staff knock or call, and the shareholder opens. If a shareholder wants a worker to be able to let themselves in, for example after a fall, that is a separate, written arrangement between the shareholder, the program and the board, not a building-wide master key.

Our store sits in a shopping center where the landlord controls the rear service corridor. Can our back door get its own reader on the landlord’s corridor?

Usually, with the landlord’s written approval. The reader goes on the corridor side of your door and is wired from inside your space, so nothing crosses the landlord’s ceiling or walls without permission. Your staff use their own codes or fobs, and the door still opens freely from inside for fire exit.

Some centers already run their own corridor access system and prefer tenants to join it. In that case we connect your door to theirs, or fit a stand-alone reader that the center agrees to, and we make sure you can still switch off your own employees without waiting for the landlord.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Soundview

Ask a chatbot or a search summary about securing a housing complex and the answer is written for a generic American apartment block. Below are seven ideas from those answers that Soundview boards and managers bring to us, and why the campuses here call for something different. We paraphrase the ideas rather than quote any tool.

“Fence the whole property and put a reader on the gate”

That was the postwar campus model, and it is the one being taken apart in Soundview. Once a second owner’s building sits inside the fence, or the approved plan opens part of the land to the public by day, a single perimeter gate either locks out people with every right to be there or stops protecting anyone.

The doors that protect homes are the building entrances. Perimeter gates still have a job, but it is a scheduled, shared job: open when the plan says, locked at night, free exit always.

When an owner asks us for “a gate on the fence”, we ask whose land the gate stands on and who else uses it before we talk about hardware.

“One system for the whole complex is always cheaper”

For one owner with several buildings, a single system usually is simpler. For several owners on one site, it often is not: one owner ends up running the others’ doors, every change needs two sets of approvals, and nobody is sure who pays for repairs.

Separate systems per owner, linked only at the gates they share, cost a little more at the start and a lot less in arguments. Each owner can add and remove its own people without asking.

Where a single platform genuinely suits everyone, we put the cost split and the support arrangement in writing before installation.

“Just get three quotes and take the lowest”

For an HPD-supervised Mitchell-Lama company, three bids are required for contracts over $100,000, but the rule says lowest cost compatible with quality, and three quotes only help if they price the same thing. Three installers given a vague request will quote three different systems.

A door-by-door scope with alternates kept separate is what makes bids comparable, and it is what a supervising agency can review quickly.

We write our scopes so the next two bidders can use them, because a board that can compare honestly is more likely to choose well.

“Expect to pay the national average”

Cost sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish national ranges for access control, built from projects across the country. They mix single offices with suburban homes and know nothing about a campus with shared gates, block-and-plank walls or a supervising agency’s paperwork.

The table further down this page is the real list, identical to Brooklyn’s: one opening with a strike and a reader or keypad runs $1,500 to $2,500 fitted, and fitting out an entire building starts around $15,000 and can pass $40,000.

What moves a Soundview price is the number of openings, the distance from controller to gate, how many owners must be coordinated and how much door repair comes first.

“Fobs and cameras will stop break-ins like the Westchester Avenue looting”

In the 2020 incident the Bronx Times reported, the store’s window was smashed and its front gate broken at 2:40 a.m. No reader stops someone breaking glass. What a door system can do is make sure the gate and back door report when they are forced, and keep a record of who opened up each day.

Real protection for a store front is physical: the gate, the glazing, the lighting and an alarm that someone responds to. Access control is about staff and keys, not about stopping a crowd at night.

We tell store owners which part of their problem each product solves, so they do not spend on the wrong one.

“A smart lock app is enough for a senior building”

Many older residents do not want to manage an app, and some do not carry smartphones. Apps also tempt operators to route every visitor through an office.

In senior buildings we keep fobs and indoor stations available, let residents admit their own visitors from a simple phone call, and give program staff their own credentials rather than master keys.

We watch residents use the system at handover, with their usual bag and walker, before we leave.

“Soundview is too rough for anything but steel and guards”

That picture is decades out of date. The 43rd Precinct’s seven major felonies fell from 9,500 in 1990 to 3,143 in 2025, according to the precinct figures summarized on Wikipedia, and burglaries fell by more than 85 percent. New co-ops, senior buildings and renovated towers are being built and opened across the neighborhood.

Most of what owners ask us for is ordinary building management: lobbies that close properly, gates that keep their hours, staff who can be switched off on their last day, and couriers who can reach a package room without reaching the stairs.

We would rather fit fewer, well-chosen doors than sell more hardware than a building needs.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Walking a Campus With the Site Plan

This is a note about how we work, not a story about a particular property. On a campus, the first walk takes longer than people expect, because we walk it twice. The first time we follow the site plan: every gate, path, door and ramp on paper. The second time we follow the people: where residents actually cut across a lawn, which gate is always propped because it is the short way to the bus, which door the couriers use because the lobby is too far from the street.

The two walks never match exactly, and the differences are where the design comes from. A gate on the plan that nobody uses can be locked on a schedule and left alone. A worn track across the grass to a side door tells us that door needs a reader, or the path needs to be made official. A propped gate is usually a gate in the wrong place, and no lock will fix that.

Then we sit down with every owner who has a stake in what we saw, and agree in writing who runs what before we price a single reader.

FAQ

Soundview Access Control FAQ

How do two owners on one campus share the cost of a gate or reader they both use?
+

The way the owners agreed to share the land usually decides it, so start with the shared-use or easement agreement. We price a shared gate as its own line, separate from either building, and can split it into two invoices in whatever proportion the owners agree. Each owner then pays for its own doors in full.

Can a NYCHA resident hire AESS to put a keypad on their own apartment door?
+

No. Apartment doors and locks in NYCHA developments belong to the housing authority, and under PACT the new property manager looks after them. A resident who wants a different lock should ask the management office; we only work on a development when its owner or manager hires us.

Do you work as a subcontractor to PACT development teams and their property managers?
+

Yes, when we are invited to bid. We can price readers, panels and gate hardware as a subcontract to the general contractor, or directly for the property manager after the renovation. We are not part of any Soundview PACT team today, and we say so to residents who ask.

Our co-op’s first board was elected only months ago, and nobody on it has run a building. What is the least we need to learn to manage the fobs?
+

Three tasks cover most weeks: adding a new shareholder or household member, switching off a lost fob, and changing a door schedule for a holiday or a move. We train two board members and the managing agent on those three in one sitting, leave a one-page guide, and keep the more powerful settings behind a separate login.

Will a reader on a freestanding post beside a campus path get knocked over by snowplows and mowers?
+

It can if it stands at the edge of the path. We set gate readers on a post set back from the plowed edge, protected by a bollard or the gate pier itself, and ask the grounds staff which way their machines turn. A reader built into the gate pier is the safest place where the pier allows it.

What records should we keep so HPD’s inspector can check the work before final payment?
+

Keep the approved contract, the as-built drawing showing every reader, lock and cable route, the equipment list with model numbers, the test sheet for each door including free exit, and the credential register handed to management. The HPD rule says workmanship is subject to HPD inspection before final payment, so we assemble that packet with the closeout invoice.

Does adding access control raise carrying charges in a Mitchell-Lama co-op?
+

We do not set carrying charges and cannot promise either way. How a capital project is paid for, and whether it affects charges, is decided by the board and managing agent with the supervising agency. What we can do is give a price broken down by building and door, so the board can plan the project in stages if it needs to.

Our superblock has entrances from four streets plus a private driveway. Which of them really need readers?
+

Start with the doors into buildings, because those protect homes. Then the gates people actually use at night, then the ones used mainly by day. A gate that is only a shortcut can often be closed after dark on a schedule rather than fitted with a reader. We map the entrances on the site plan and number them in order of priority.

Can the panel at a senior building’s entrance ring the on-site service office first and the resident second?
+

Yes. A call can go to the service or management office during its hours and straight to the resident at other times, or ring both at once. Residents should always be able to let their own visitors in; the office is a first stop for visitors who do not know the apartment number, not a gate the resident has to pass through.

Can visitors to the health center on our campus be kept out of the residential courtyard?
+

Yes. Patients use the street entrance to the clinic, and the door from the clinic side into the courtyard is set for staff credentials only, or exit-only if the fire exit plan allows it. Where the courtyard is public by day under the site plan, we schedule it to match those hours instead.
