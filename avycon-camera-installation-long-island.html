The Neighborhood

Why Long Island Properties Need AVYCON Camera Installation

Long Island is two counties of mostly detached houses, and a camera system here has to cover a driveway, a back door and a yard rather than a lobby and a hallway. The Census Bureau's 2023 American Community Survey counts 477,860 housing units in Nassau County, of which 364,529 are one-unit detached homes, and 579,790 units in Suffolk County, of which 463,095 are detached. That is roughly three homes in four in Nassau and four in five in Suffolk standing on their own lot. In the city, a building camera system usually watches shared doors. Out here, most systems watch a single family's property line, a driveway with cars parked in it, a side gate, a detached garage, and often a pool, a shed or a dock. That shapes everything about the install: how many cameras, which lenses, how far the cables run, and where the recorder lives.

The age of those homes shapes the wiring. The same Census tables give a median year built of 1955 for Nassau housing and 1970 for Suffolk. Newsday, in an analysis of nearly 12,000 home sales in 2026, found that more than 5,000 of the houses sold were built in the 1950s and 1960s. The best-known of them are in Levittown, which Encyclopedia.com says began in 1947 on 1,300 acres of potato fields and by 1951 had about 17,500 single-family homes. EBSCO's history puts the final number at 17,447. The first houses were Cape Cods, offered as rentals; in 1949 Levitt and Sons switched to ranches, all sold. Many of those houses were built on a concrete slab with no basement, and one Levittown history notes that copper coils carrying hot water from the furnace were set into the slab to heat the house. For a camera installer, that single fact rules out the trick that works in a colonial with a basement: dropping cables down through the floor and running them across the ceiling below. In a slab house, cable goes through the attic, along the garage, or outside under the eaves.

Then there is the reason people call. Long Island's recent police news is full of cameras doing their job and cameras being defeated. In May 2026, Daily Voice reported that a burglary crew in North Valley Stream repositioned a family's cameras and covered the lenses with tape while the family was away. The same month, a Roslyn Heights homeowner watched two men on his camera system trying to break into a storage unit, and police tracked their car and arrested them. In June 2026 the LI Herald reported four men sentenced for a key-fob burglary spree through Woodmere, Lawrence, Hewlett Harbor and Franklin Square, with the district attorney urging residents to consider cameras and alarms. ABC7 reported an East Hills case that month in which three people walked up a driveway at 3:45 a.m. and searched a car before at least two of them went into the house. In 2024, ABC7 described a burglary task force of Nassau and Suffolk police, district attorneys, the NYPD, State Police and the FBI, with police saying the burglars "are attracted by cars in people's driveways." Those stories describe exactly what a Long Island camera system has to see: the end of the driveway, the car door, the side of the house, and its own lenses.

Package theft is the other daily concern. CBS New York reported in November 2025 that the Suffolk district attorney charged 14 men in a porch-theft ring that worked 31 communities, including Brentwood, Central Islip, Shirley and Deer Park, from October 2023 to February 2025, taking more than 200 phones and tablets. News 12 Long Island has shown porch thefts on Walters Avenue in Wantagh and on Atlantic Avenue in Massapequa Park. Most of those clips come from doorbell cameras. A doorbell sees the person on the step. It usually does not see the car at the curb, the plate on that car, or the direction it drove off. That gap between a doorbell clip and usable evidence is one of the main reasons owners move up to a wired recorder with cameras placed for faces and plates.

Why AVYCON in particular? Three things come up on Long Island. First, AVYCON's NDAA statement says its compliant cameras, encoders, NVRs and DVRs avoid critical components and systems-on-chip from vendors the NDAA prohibits, naming Huawei, ZTE, Hytera, Hikvision and Dahua, and that they are made and substantially transformed in the United States, South Korea and/or Vietnam. Schools, government contractors and businesses with insurance or contract requirements ask for that by name. Second, AVYCON's support FAQ says its NVRs accept third-party cameras that speak ONVIF, so a property with a few decent cameras from another brand does not have to throw them away. Third, the AVYCON ONE app adds people and vehicle detection, facial recognition and license plate reading on the same phone screen where you watch the live view. The app is also where the trouble is: it was rated 2.9 out of 5 from 52 ratings on the App Store listing we checked, and one reviewer wrote that it "seems to hang about 50% of the time." That is why the last step of our install is always the app on your phone, tested before we leave.

We are plain about what our research did not find. AVYCON is based at 16682 Millikan Avenue in Irvine, California, with a second office in Korea, and we found no AVYCON office or certified dealer on Long Island. We found no Reddit threads, Patch stories or Yelp reviews about AVYCON installation in Nassau or Suffolk. We also did not find any official rule that settles whether a camera install needs a local permit everywhere on the island; sources disagree, and towns and villages set their own requirements. Where this page talks about what we expect inside a Levittown ranch or a North Shore carriage house, it draws on the building history in the public record and on what houses of that type usually hide, not on a survey of Long Island homes. Nassau: the dense postwar grid

Nassau's median house is from 1955, the decade of Levittown and the many tract developments that followed it. Wikipedia records that before Levittown, William Levitt built 1,080 homes in Westbury and 2,250 in Roslyn, and History.com describes the same sequence. Those houses tend to be modest in size. Wikipedia gives the original Levittown house at about 750 square feet with no basement or porch, on a lot of about a quarter acre. The cameras on a house like that are rarely far from the recorder, so distance is not the problem. Getting the cable from the attic to a camera position without a basement below, without drilling the slab, and without leaving a visible tangle on the siding is the problem. Nassau houses have also been remodeled heavily over seventy years. One Levittown history notes that very few original Capes survive unchanged, which means two houses on the same block can have completely different attics, dormers and wall cavities. We plan each one from its own photos.

Nassau is also where the paperwork is most varied. A homeowner in an unincorporated hamlet like Levittown or Uniondale deals with the Town of Hempstead or the Town of Oyster Bay, while a homeowner a mile away in an incorporated village deals with that village's building department. Some sources say a standard camera install needs no local permit. Low Voltage Nation, by contrast, says the state building code has required permits for low-voltage electrical installations since January 1, 1984, and that virtually all Nassau towns and villages enforce permit rules. We do not settle that argument for you; we check the rule for your address before we book.

Suffolk: bigger lots and longer runs

Suffolk's median house is from 1970, and its land area is more than five times Nassau's. Out there the typical camera job has more distance in it: a house set back from the road, a long drive, a detached garage, a barn or pool house, and in the North and South Fork towns, seasonal homes that sit empty for months. Distance changes the equipment. A camera meant to read a plate at the end of a long drive needs a longer lens or a PTZ, and AVYCON's NDAA product list includes long-zoom PTZ cameras such as 5MP 40X and 8MP 25X models. A garage or outbuilding beyond the practical reach of one copper cable needs either a small PoE switch in that building fed from the house, or another kind of link we plan on site. Empty seasonal homes need the system to keep recording and stay reachable when nobody is there to reset it after a storm.

Suffolk also has a free police camera registry, described below, and a county alarm permit system that covers alarms but not standalone camera recorders. Those two programs come up on almost every Suffolk call, so we explain them in plain terms before you decide what to install.

The city line and the hub areas

Some of the busiest parts of the island are not houses at all. In Nassau, the area around the Coliseum in Uniondale and East Garden City is known as the Nassau Hub; Wikipedia describes it as home to almost half the county's economic activity. In Suffolk, the Ronkonkoma Hub, now called Station Yards, is a 53-acre development around the LIRR station that by March 2020 had converted four vacant industrial buildings into 489 apartments and is planned for up to 1,450 apartments, 195,000 square feet of retail and 360,000 square feet of office space. Hicksville, described by Wikipedia as having the busiest LIRR hub on the island, is adding hundreds of apartments near the station. Those buildings bring the camera questions of the city to the suburbs: landlords, building rules, shared entrances and tenants who cannot drill. We cover them in the building section below.

About AVYCON

What an AVYCON System Is, and What It Is Not, Before It Goes on a Long Island House

Most homeowners who ask us about AVYCON have seen the name on a quote, a school spec sheet or a distributor's catalog rather than in a store. It helps to know what the brand is before deciding it is the right fit for your property, so here is what the public record says, without the marketing.

A professional-channel brand

A reseller's profile says AVYCON was founded in 2012. Its contact page gives a headquarters at 16682 Millikan Avenue in Irvine, California, and an office in Korea. AVYCON's equipment is sold through security distributors; ADI Global Distribution, for example, carries the brand, including NDAA 32-channel NVRs. In practice that means AVYCON is mostly installed by security contractors rather than bought off a shelf by homeowners, which is different from the consumer kits sold in warehouse clubs. The same reseller profile calls AVYCON a global market leader; that is the company's own marketing, and we do not repeat it as fact.

How the pieces fit together

A typical AVYCON system on a Long Island house is an NVR, a network video recorder with hard drives inside, and a set of IP cameras connected by network cable. AVYCON's support FAQ describes the simplest setup: connect the camera directly to a PoE port on the back of the NVR, and the camera appears on the screen after about a minute. PoE, Power over Ethernet, carries power and video on the same cable, so there is no separate power supply at each camera. Where there are more cameras than NVR ports, or cameras far from the recorder, a PoE switch sits in between. AVYCON cameras typically stream over RTSP on port 554, according to iSpy's camera database, which matters when the system has to feed other software. AVYCON also makes cameras that can run standalone; the App Store listing for AVYCON ONE notes that standalone cameras need a microSD card for storage and playback.

The NDAA question

Section 889 of the National Defense Authorization Act is why many institutions now ask for "NDAA-compliant" cameras. AVYCON's NDAA statement says specified AVYCON cameras, encoders, NVRs and DVRs do not use critical components or systems-on-chip from prohibited vendors, which it names as Huawei, ZTE, Hytera, Hikvision and Dahua, and that its compliant products are manufactured and substantially transformed in the United States, South Korea and/or Vietnam. Two cautions apply. Not every AVYCON product is on the compliant list, so a job that needs compliance needs each model checked against AVYCON's current list. And compliance is about the equipment, not the install; a compliant camera on a badly planned network is still a weak system. We check the list for every model on a compliance job and put the model numbers on the written quote.

The app and the AI features

The AVYCON ONE app sorts alerts into Event, AI and Abnormal categories and supports facial recognition, people and vehicle detection and license plate reading, according to its App Store listing. Those features are useful on Long Island, where the questions after an incident are usually "was it a person or a raccoon" and "what car was it." They are also where most frustration comes from. The listing showed 2.9 stars from 52 ratings when we checked. One reviewer said the app hangs about half the time when viewing cameras and that their installer also struggled to get the app to save the device. Another asked for a way to add cameras back to the app after installing a new base unit. Our reading is that the app rewards careful setup and punishes a rushed one. We treat app setup as part of the install, not a favor at the end, and we test it on your phone on cellular data, away from your home Wi-Fi, before we leave.

Keeping cameras you already own

AVYCON's FAQ says third-party cameras can be added to its NVRs using the ONVIF protocol, if the camera supports it. That matters on Long Island because many houses already have a few cameras from an earlier system. ONVIF support is not a promise that every feature carries over; AI detection, for instance, may only work on AVYCON's own cameras. We test each existing camera on the new recorder and tell you which features you will and will not get before you decide to keep it.

Decide what you need to recognize, not just what you want to see

"I want to see the driveway" and "I want to read the plate of a car stopped at the end of the driveway at 3:45 in the morning" are two very different camera jobs. The first is a wide lens on the corner of the garage. The second is a narrower lens, a carefully chosen angle, and a camera placed so the headlights do not wash out the picture. A face at the front door needs a camera near head height looking slightly down, close enough that the face fills a good part of the frame. Write down what you would want to hand to a detective after a break-in, and we will plan the cameras around that list rather than around how many cameras a kit happens to contain.

Think about where the recorder hides

The North Valley Stream case showed that some burglars go after the cameras. Others go after the recorder, because taking it takes the footage too. We usually suggest a closet, a basement corner or a utility area away from the front door, with the NVR on a shelf, a battery backup beside it, and its network cable running back to the router. If you have a spot in mind, photograph it. If you do not, we will suggest two or three during the survey and explain the trade-offs: a hidden spot may be warmer, further from the router or harder to reach for future drives.

Look at the outside walls from the street

Stand on the sidewalk and look at your house the way a stranger would. Where would you walk up unseen? Which windows are hidden by a hedge? Is there a side gate or a back fence that opens onto a path or a park? Long Island's lots often have a side yard that nobody looks at, and that is usually where we find the gap in an existing system. Photos from the street and from each corner of the yard let us mark camera positions on paper before the visit, so the survey is about confirming, not guessing.

AVYCON Camera Installation for Long Island's Architecture

Installers who work on Long Island tend to describe the same set of homes: the colonials, split-levels, ranches and Capes of places like Levittown, Hicksville and Massapequa; the Gold Coast estates of the North Shore; the wood-frame and cedar-shingle houses of the South Shore; and waterfront and seasonal homes out to the East End. Each changes three things about an AVYCON job: how cable reaches each camera, where the recorder can live, and who has to agree before we drill.

Slab Ranches and Capes

Levittown-style houses on concrete slabs with no basement, some with heating coils in the slab. Cable goes through the attic to the eaves or along the garage; the floor is never drilled.

Split-Levels and Hi-Ranches

Multi-level 1950s through 1970s houses, common in both counties. Cable has to climb between half-levels, often through closets stacked above one another or along the garage wall.

Colonials With Basements

More common in Suffolk's later housing. A basement or crawlspace lets cable run below the floors and up the outside walls, which keeps the attic quieter and the eaves cleaner.

North Shore Estates

Large houses on long drives, often with gatehouses, garages and outbuildings. Distance, zoom lenses and switches in secondary buildings drive the design.

South Shore and Waterfront Homes

Wood-frame and shingle houses near the bays and canals, with docks and bulkheads to watch. Weather, salt air and flood levels decide where the recorder and switches sit.

Apartments and Condos Near the Stations

New rental buildings at Station Yards in Ronkonkoma and near the Hicksville LIRR station. Landlord and building rules come first; most hard-wired work needs written permission.

Slab houses: the attic is the road

A Levittown-style ranch gives cable three possible paths: up into the attic, along the garage, or outside. The attic is usually best. From the hatch we crawl to the eaves above each camera position and drop the cable down through the soffit or the top of the wall, so the camera mounts on a short, clean run. Older attics bring their own care. Insulation may be thin or packed in, and in houses of this age it is not always the modern kind. A restoration contractor working in Uniondale warns that postwar Capes and ranches there frequently contain asbestos materials. We do not disturb material we cannot identify, and if anything in the attic or a wall looks like it could be asbestos, we stop and route around it or recommend testing before we continue.

The slab itself is never drilled. Even if a heating history is right that only about five percent of Levittown houses still run their original radiant coils, nobody can tell from the surface which houses those are, and a drill bit through a live hot-water line under the floor is a disaster. Where a camera has to go on a wall with no attic above it, such as a garage that was converted to a room, we run cable along the outside in a paintable raceway or under a soffit return, and seal every hole through the siding.

Split-levels: climbing the half-floors

Split-levels and hi-ranches, which a Hempstead real-estate source describes around Hofstra and the Coliseum, put three or four floor levels in a small footprint. The upper bedrooms sit over the garage, the living room sits a half-flight up from the entry, and the family room sits a half-flight down. Cable from a recorder on the lower level to a camera on the upper eaves has to cross those offsets. We look for stacked closets, a chase beside the chimney or the space beside the garage stair. The garage ceiling is often the easiest horizontal route, because it is usually unfinished or easy to open and patch. We plan these houses carefully because a split-level wall that looks simple from the outside can hide a floor change halfway across.

What a business system has to do that a house system does not

A homeowner looks at a camera clip when something happens. Warehouses and flex buildings along Route 110 and in Hauppauge usually need the longest runs and the hardest mounts: block walls, steel beams, high ceilings and yards with trucks moving at night. Rules and practical limits for business cameras on Long Island

  • The state installer license applies. The NYS Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers. As Kirschenbaum & Kirschenbaum explains, 19 NYCRR 195.2 covers a closed-circuit television system used full-time or part-time to detect or monitor intrusion, break-in, theft, movement, sound or fire. A business camera system for theft is squarely inside that.
  • County licensing differs. Nassau's Office of Consumer Affairs licenses home improvement contractors, and the county's FAQ says those who work only on commercial buildings do not need that license. Suffolk's county code sets up licensing through its Office of Consumer Affairs for regulated trades, with exceptions for towns and villages that license their own.
  • Audio is a separate legal question. New York requires consent for audio recording, and installer and legal sources advise turning audio off unless a consenting person is part of the conversation. On business systems we leave camera audio off by default.
  • Face and plate features need a policy. AVYCON ONE offers facial recognition and plate reading. A Long Island criminal-law blog notes New York has no law governing how long plate data is kept. Businesses should decide who can see that data and how long it stays before switching those features on.
  • Exits and fire equipment stay clear. Cameras and cable never cover exit signs, sprinkler heads or fire alarm devices, and nothing is fixed to fire-rated assemblies without the right method.
  • Insurance papers and hours. Landlords, managing agents and school districts usually ask for a certificate of insurance and set work hours. Send the requirements with the booking and we schedule inside them.
Field Notes

Field Notes: The Attic Walk Before Any Camera Goes Up

We do not publish stories about a particular customer's house unless we can document them, so this is not a tale about one Long Island address. It is the routine our technicians follow on the first visit to a slab ranch or a Cape, which is the kind of house Nassau is full of.

We start outside, not inside. We walk the lot with the owner and stand at each corner of the house, asking the same question every time: what do you need to see from here? We look at the eaves above each position and note whether there is a soffit we can feed through or a solid fascia. Then we go up the attic hatch with a light and look before we touch anything: the depth and type of insulation, where the bath fans and the old wiring run, whether the eaves are reachable or pinched down to nothing at the edges. We look at the garage ceiling and the wall shared with the house. We find the router and the electrical panel. We pick a recorder spot with the owner, away from the front door, with an outlet and a short path to the router. We hold a phone at each camera position and look at the screen, so the owner sees roughly what the camera will see. Only then do we write down the cable routes. In houses of this kind, that walk is what keeps the cable out of the slab, the insulation undisturbed and every camera aimed at something that matters.

Fitting AVYCON to what you already have

We have four cameras from another brand on our Massapequa house. Will an AVYCON NVR record them, or do they all have to go?

Often they can stay. AVYCON's support FAQ says its NVRs can add third-party cameras that support the ONVIF protocol. Most network cameras from established brands support it, but not all, and older analog cameras on coaxial cable do not work with an NVR at all without an encoder. We connect each existing camera to the AVYCON recorder during the survey or at the start of the install and check three things: that it streams, that it records reliably, and which features work. Basic motion recording usually carries over. AVYCON's AI detection for people and vehicles, facial recognition and plate reading are generally tied to AVYCON's own cameras, so a kept camera may record but not sort its alerts as cleverly. We tell you that plainly, camera by camera, and you decide which to keep and which to replace.

Our security integrator needs the RTSP address of the AVYCON cameras for other software. Is that something you set up?

Yes. iSpy's camera database notes that AVYCON cameras typically stream over RTSP on port 554, which is the standard way to hand a live stream to video management software, a monitoring service or a display system. We confirm the stream path on your specific model, create a separate user with view-only rights for that connection so the main admin password is never shared, and document it for whoever manages the other software. On a business network we also check that the port is only reachable inside the network or through a secure connection, not opened to the whole internet.

NDAA and institutions

By model number, on paper. Not every AVYCON product is on that list. For a school, daycare, campus or any client with a compliance requirement, we choose each camera, recorder and encoder from AVYCON's current compliant list, put the exact model numbers on the written quote, and give the administration the list we checked against. If the board's requirement goes beyond NDAA, for example a specific country of manufacture, we raise it before ordering, because AVYCON says its compliant products are made and substantially transformed in the United States, South Korea and/or Vietnam, not in one place.

Licenses and paperwork

Which license should a camera installer working in Nassau or Suffolk actually hold?

There are two layers. At the state level, the Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers, and 19 NYCRR 195.2 covers closed-circuit television used to detect or monitor intrusion, theft and movement, which is what most Long Island camera systems do. The Department of Labor's licensing summary lists the requirements for the license: at least 18 years old, at least 81 hours of qualifying education, an installer exam and fingerprints. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431. If the village has nothing to file for a job like yours, we tell you that too.

Slab houses, split-levels and long runs

Our Levittown ranch has no basement. How do the cables get from the cameras to the recorder without drilling the slab?

Through the attic, the garage or the outside walls, never the floor. In most slab ranches and Capes, the attic runs over the whole house and reaches the eaves, so we drop each camera's cable down through the soffit or the top of the wall right above the camera. The recorder usually sits in a closet whose ceiling opens into the attic, so the cables come down a single path into that closet. Where a camera sits under a room with no attic above it, such as a converted garage or a dormer, we run the cable along the outside in a paintable raceway or tucked under the eave, sealed where it passes through the siding. The slab stays untouched because some Levittown houses still carry heating coils in the concrete and there is no way to know from above which ones.

Our detached garage is at the back of a long Suffolk lot. Can one AVYCON system cover it as well as the house?

Yes, but the garage usually needs its own small network point. A single copper network cable has a practical length limit, and a detached garage at the back of a big lot can be near or past it once the cable goes up the house, across the attic and down again. The common answer is a cable from the house to a PoE switch inside the garage, which powers the garage cameras and sends their video back on one line. How that line crosses the yard depends on the ground, the driveway and what is already buried; we plan it on site and put the method on the quote. If the garage already has power and a network line, the job is simpler and we test what is there first.

Privacy and neighbors

The house next door is ten feet from our side yard. How do you aim the cameras so we are not recording into their windows?

With angles first and privacy masks second. New York's Civil Rights Law section 52-a makes it unlawful to install or use a video surveillance device where a person has a reasonable expectation of privacy, and installer guidance recommends digital privacy masking on exterior cameras to avoid capturing a neighbor's yard or windows. On a tight Long Island side yard, we aim cameras along your own wall and down toward your own path, rather than across at the house next door. Where some of the neighbor's property still falls in the frame, we draw a privacy mask over it in the AVYCON recorder, which blacks out that part of the picture permanently. In Shirley, CBS New York reported police saying a backyard visible to the public is fair game, but a window is a different matter, and we keep cameras away from them.

Our AVYCON cameras have microphones. Should we turn the audio on?

Usually not on outdoor cameras. New York requires consent for audio recording, and legal guidance on home cameras advises disabling audio unless you know a consenting participant, such as you, will be part of any recorded conversation. A camera on your front door picking up two strangers talking on the sidewalk is the kind of recording that causes trouble. We leave audio off by default and turn it on only where you have a clear reason and understand the rule, for example on a doorbell where you are the one talking to a visitor.

Theft, tampering and the morning after

Burglars in North Valley Stream turned cameras away and taped over lenses. How do you set an AVYCON system up so that does not leave us blind?

Height, overlap and alerts. We mount cameras out of easy reach, usually under the eaves rather than at head height, so nobody can turn or cover them without a ladder. We place cameras so that each important camera is seen by another one; a person reaching up to tape a lens is then recorded by the camera across the yard. And we turn on the recorder's abnormal-event alerts, which in AVYCON ONE are a separate alert category, so that a camera that goes offline or loses its picture sends a notification to your phone. On a house that sits empty for long stretches, those alerts are what tell you something is wrong before you get home.

Our cars sit in the driveway overnight. Which camera positions catch someone trying the doors or reading the key fob signal?

Two kinds: a wide view of the whole driveway from the house, and a narrower view of the driveway mouth for faces and plates. ABC7 reported police saying Long Island burglars are attracted by cars in driveways, and the East Hills case in June 2026 began with three people searching a car at 3:45 a.m. A wide camera on the garage corner shows what people do around the cars. A second camera aimed at the point where the driveway meets the street, mounted where headlights do not blind it, is what gives you a usable picture of a face or a plate as people arrive and leave. We set the recorder to treat people and vehicles in the driveway as alerts while ignoring cars driving past on the street.

Power and storms

During Isaias half of Long Island lost power. What happens to an AVYCON system in an outage like that?

Without a battery backup, it stops, along with the router that sends alerts to your phone. ABC7 reported that at the peak of Tropical Storm Isaias in August 2020, almost half of PSEG's 1.1 million Long Island customers were in the dark. We recommend a battery backup for the recorder, the PoE switch and the router together, sized to ride through a short outage and shut the recorder down cleanly in a long one, so the drives are not damaged. A battery will not keep a system running for days. If you have a whole-house generator, we connect the recorder to a circuit it covers, and we set the recorder to restart and resume recording on its own when power returns.

What AI Gets Wrong About AVYCON Camera Installation in Long Island

What follows are claims we see repeated in quick online answers about AVYCON, NDAA cameras and camera installation on Long Island, set against the public record and our field practice. National marketplaces such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish cost guides that feed many of those answers; they help with a rough sense of scale, but they are not a quote for a slab ranch in Levittown or an estate in Old Westbury.

"An AVYCON recorder only works with AVYCON cameras."

Some consumer kits are locked to their own recorders, and quick answers often assume every brand works that way. A Slickdeals thread about one warehouse-club kit, for example, has a buyer saying those cameras would only work on that brand's recorder.

AVYCON's own support FAQ says the opposite for its NVRs: third-party cameras can be added if they support ONVIF. That is useful on Long Island, where many houses already have a few cameras from an earlier system.

The fair version is that ONVIF gets the video onto the recorder, but not always the smart features. We test each existing camera on the new recorder and tell you what you will and will not get from it before you decide to keep it.

"NDAA-compliant means made in America."

The phrase gets shortened that way, and it is not what AVYCON says. Its NDAA statement describes compliant products as manufactured and substantially transformed in the United States, South Korea and/or Vietnam, without critical components or chips from the named prohibited vendors.

For most Long Island homeowners, the difference does not matter. For a school, a government contractor or a business with a written supply-chain requirement, it can matter a great deal, and the requirement should be read exactly as written.

"You don't need a license to install cameras in New York."

"Camera installs on Long Island never need a permit."

An installer's page says standard camera installations in Nassau and Suffolk typically do not require local permits, and AI answers repeat that as a flat rule. Another source says the state code has required permits for low-voltage electrical installations since January 1, 1984, and that virtually all Nassau towns and villages enforce permit rules.

With 64 incorporated villages in Nassau alone, plus towns, a blanket answer cannot be right for every address. What applies in one village may not apply in the hamlet next to it.

Our approach is boring and reliable: we look up the rule for your address before booking and tell you what, if anything, has to be filed.

"Run the cables through the basement."

Generic install guides assume a basement. Nassau's median house was built in 1955, and the most famous houses of that era, in Levittown, were built on slabs with no basement at all, some with heating coils cast into the concrete.

Following a basement-first guide in a slab house leads people to drill the floor or to staple cable along the baseboards. Neither is a good result, and drilling the slab can hit a live heating line.

In slab ranches and Capes, the attic is the route, with the garage and sealed exterior runs as backups. In split-levels, it is stacked closets and the garage ceiling. In Suffolk's later colonials, the basement guide often does apply.

"Turn on facial recognition and plate reading; it's all legal."

AVYCON ONE does offer facial recognition, people and vehicle detection and plate reading, and answers about those features tend to stop at "how to enable." Plate reading has its own debate on the island. For a private house, a camera that reads plates on your own driveway is a sensible tool. For a business, decide who can see that data and how long you keep it before you switch it on.

"PoE is plug and play, so the install is the easy part."

AVYCON's FAQ is right that a PoE camera plugged into the NVR appears on the screen in about a minute. That minute is the end of the job, not the job. Do you do AVYCON systems for businesses as well as homes on the island?

Can I put AVYCON cameras up myself at my own Nassau shop?
+

A permit guide says a homeowner installing on their own home does not need a state installer license, but a shop is a different situation, and paid installers need the NYS security installer license. If you do it yourself, check your village or town rules, keep audio off, keep cameras out of private spaces such as restrooms, and make sure exits and fire equipment stay clear. We are happy to review a self-installed system afterwards.

What should I ask a camera installer on Long Island before I sign a contract?
+

Ask for the NYS security installer license number and the county license that applies to your address, a certificate of insurance, the exact camera and recorder models, who keeps the admin password, how each cable will reach each camera, and what is excluded. A good quote names all of it in writing. Ours carries NYS license #12000287431.

I rent at Station Yards in Ronkonkoma. Can I have an AVYCON camera installed in my apartment?
+

Usually only inside your own unit and with the landlord's written permission for anything that drills or wires. A standalone AVYCON camera recording to a microSD card, aimed at your own door from inside, is often the practical option. We do not mount on hallways, entrances or exterior walls of a managed building without management's approval.

Is it legal to put cameras on the outside of my house in New York?
+

Yes, with limits. Civil Rights Law section 52-a prohibits video surveillance where people have a reasonable expectation of privacy, and New York requires consent for audio recording. Aim cameras at your own property, use privacy masks where a neighbor's yard or window falls into the frame, and leave outdoor audio off unless you have a clear reason and understand the rule.

How many AVYCON cameras does a typical Long Island house need?
+

Enough to cover every way onto the property and the places you would want evidence from: front door, driveway and driveway mouth, back door, side yards, and any detached garage. A compact slab ranch on a quarter-acre lot needs fewer cameras than a split-level with a detached garage or an estate with outbuildings. We count them from a site walk, not from a kit size.

Should I register my cameras with Suffolk or Garden City police after the install?
+

It is free and voluntary. The Suffolk County Police SHIELD camera registry records where cameras are so detectives can ask you to check footage for a date and time, and the department says it does not have access to the cameras. Garden City's police run a similar private camera registry. Suffolk SHIELD also has an emergency remote access program you can opt into or out of at any time.

Does an AVYCON camera system need a county alarm permit?
+

A camera recorder on its own is not an alarm. Suffolk requires a permit for alarm systems in its police district, and Nassau registers alarms that send signals for police response, with false-alarm fees in both counties. If you add a monitored alarm to the cameras, the permit rules for that alarm apply, and we explain them at booking.

Can an AVYCON system keep an eye on a seasonal house in the Hamptons over the winter?
+

Yes, with a few extra steps. We add a battery backup, set the recorder to restart and resume recording on its own after a power cut, turn on abnormal-event alerts so a camera that goes offline notifies you, and make sure the app works from wherever you spend the winter. The East Hampton Star has reported police using video from a camera inside a burglarized Sammy's Beach Road house.
