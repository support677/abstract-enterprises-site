The Neighborhood

Why Park Slope Properties Need Security Camera Repair

Park Slope runs downhill from Prospect Park West to Fourth Avenue, and from Flatbush Avenue and Grand Army Plaza in the north to the Prospect Expressway in the south. Most of it is brownstone and limestone row houses from the late nineteenth century, many split into flats or small condos, with six- to eight-story pre-war apartment houses along Prospect Park West and the park blocks, newer rental buildings along Fourth Avenue since the avenue was rezoned in 2003, and storefront strips on Fifth and Seventh Avenues. Much of the neighborhood sits inside the Park Slope Historic District, designated in 1973 and extended in 2012 and 2016. That background matters to a camera repair in three practical ways: where the recorder ends up, how the cables reach the street, and what the front of the building will tolerate when a camera is replaced.

The first is concealment. Park Slope owners spend real money keeping their facades original, so the camera systems here were usually installed to be seen as little as possible. Cables were fished through the walls during a gut renovation, run behind baseboards, dropped down old chases to the cellar or brought out through a mortar joint beside the stoop. That makes the systems tidy and hard to fix. When a camera fails ten years later, nobody remembers which wall its cable is in, and the contractor who knew has moved on. A big part of a Park Slope repair is tracing a run by measurement from both ends before anything is opened, so that a dead stoop camera is fixed at the connector instead of with a new hole in the brownstone.

The second is the slope itself. Water runs downhill, and the bottom of the hill is Fourth Avenue. Streetsblog's 2023 history of the avenue traces documented flooding at Fourth Avenue and Carroll Street back to 1922 and notes that "the avenue sits at the bottom of the hill of Park Slope, so a lot of rainwater pools on the thoroughfare during storms." Tropical Storm Ophelia lifted parked cars there in September 2023, and on October 30, 2025 a sudden downpour flooded basements in Brooklyn and turned Fourth Avenue near Carroll Street, on the Park Slope and Gowanus border, into a river. The city's sewer upgrade for the corridor is planned in phases through 2033. In the meantime, the cellars on the lower blocks between Fourth and Fifth Avenues are where many recorders, power boxes and PoE switches sit, and they are the first part of a camera system to take water. Residents on r/parkslope describe waking at 4:30 in the morning to "water rising at a very fast rate" in the basement. A recorder on the floor of a cellar like that is a repair waiting to happen.

The third is power. On a Friday night at the end of January 2026, after a snowstorm and a hard freeze, Park Slope and Boerum Hill began losing power in pieces. ABC7 reported "flickering lights and appliances that cut out unexpectedly" before many blocks went fully dark for the weekend; by Monday nearly 2,000 Con Edison customers were still out, and the utility blamed melting snow and salt for blowing out underground equipment. A bodega owner told the station there was "no lotto machine working, no EBT, no credit card, nothing is working." Camera systems went down with everything else, and many came back wrong: a switch that never powered its cameras again, a recorder that rebooted on its setup screen, a battery backup that had been dead for years, a drive that came up with errors after a night of flickering voltage. Those are the calls that filled our Park Slope schedule for weeks afterward.

And then there is the reason anyone cares. Park Slope's avenues have had their share of incidents where a store's cameras were the record. In January 2023, ABC7 reported that "surveillance video captured three armed suspects" at Facets, a jewelry store in Park Slope, smashing display cases and taking about 100 engagement rings worth roughly $2 million; the owner had buzzed one man in and two more followed, and "the smash and grab was caught on the store's many cameras." In September 2026, video from the 8th Street Mini Market's cameras of the store's kitten being taken spread across Reddit and local news. In each case the footage only helped because the cameras were recording, the clip could be found and someone knew how to copy it out cleanly. Those three things are what a repair has to restore.

Many Park Slope owners pair the camera repair with a check of the intercom or door buzzer on the same stoop, because the same front door carries both systems and the same cellar often powers both.

The one- or two-family brownstone: the stoop, the garden and a cellar full of history

The typical Park Slope camera system on a brownstone has four to eight cameras: one above or beside the stoop door, one under the stoop at the garden-level entrance, one or two on the rear wall looking over the garden, sometimes one in the stair hall, and indoor cameras added later. The recorder is in the cellar or a closet on the garden floor, beside the boiler or the internet equipment, because that is where the installer found power and the cables from front and back could meet. The cables almost always run inside the walls. The front camera's cable typically comes through a mortar joint in the brownstone and drops down inside the front wall or alongside the stoop to the cellar, and the rear cameras' cables come in through the back wall at the parlor or garden level.

The faults follow from that layout. The under-stoop camera sits where salt and slush drip from the steps all winter, so its connector corrodes. The rear cameras sit behind vines and trees, and a gardener cutting back wisteria or ivy can pull a cable loose. The stair-hall camera fights the light from the skylight at the top of the stairs. And the recorder in the cellar breathes damp air, sits on a shelf near a washing machine or a water heater, and draws power from whichever outlet was closest when it went in. We trace each run, fix the failed part where it failed, and move the recorder somewhere dry and on its own protected power if it needs it.

The brownstone in flats or a small condo: one system, several households

Many Park Slope row houses were divided into apartments decades ago, and many have since become two- to six-unit condos. The camera system in a house like that covers shared spaces: the stoop, the front door, the hall, the garden that belongs to one unit but is seen from all of them, and the cellar. The questions on these repairs are about people as much as hardware. Who owns the recorder? Who pays? Who should see which cameras? On r/parkslope, residents have described landlords who would never put in a camera and supers who use building cameras to keep tabs on tenants, which one commenter called "borderline harassment." Both problems get worse when nobody has thought about access.

So before we fix anything we write down who approves the work, who holds the admin login and who sees what. Then we repair the failed parts, put the admin login in the owner's or the condo's name, and give each household its own viewing login limited to the cameras it should see. A camera a tenant put up on their own is left alone unless they ask us to look at it.

Limestone and brick row houses: a bracket on plain stone, not on the carving

Not every Park Slope front is brownstone. Many side streets have limestone and brick row houses with carved bands, bay windows, stone cornices and areaway ironwork. On these fronts a camera's position was usually chosen to stay off the decorative stone, and a repair should keep it there. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's guidebook directs new equipment to mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork and away from decorative surfaces and cast iron, and limits visible conduit to about ten inches. On a repair that means reusing the existing anchors whenever they are sound, choosing a replacement camera that fits the old bracket, and keeping any new cable inside the wall rather than adding exterior conduit. When an old anchor has loosened in soft stone, we reset it in the same joint instead of drilling a new one in the face of the stone.

Pre-war co-ops near the park: lobby, service door and a board

The elevator buildings on Prospect Park West and the blocks near the park usually have a lobby camera or two, an elevator camera, cameras at the service entrance and the basement corridors, and a recorder in a locked room off the basement or the lobby. Many of these systems went in fifteen or twenty years ago as coax cameras and have been patched since, so a single recorder may have old analog cameras, a few newer HD-over-coax models and one or two network cameras on an adapter. The service entrance camera fails most, because deliveries, hand trucks, hose-downs and trash all pass under it.

Fourth Avenue and newer rentals: network systems and inherited logins

The buildings along Fourth Avenue, and newer ones such as the 180-apartment Paseo on Fifth at Baltic Street and the 144-apartment building rising on the former Grand Prospect Hall site on Prospect Avenue, have network camera systems: dozens of cameras on PoE switches in a telecom room, a recorder or a cloud service, cameras in the garage, the package room, the bike room and the lobby. Their faults are rarely the cameras. They are switches that lost power when a breaker was reset, cameras that stopped recording after a firmware update, a drive set to keep too few days, and a management office that cannot log in because the builder's contractor kept the passwords.

We map the telecom room and the settings before we order any part. Where the builder's contractor still holds a service agreement, we check it first, so the owner is not paying twice or voiding something they rely on.

The walk-up over an avenue shop: two owners, one cellar

On Fifth and Seventh Avenues many buildings have a store at street level and three or four floors of apartments above. The store's cameras watch the counter, the door and the stockroom; the building's cameras, if there are any, watch the residents' door beside the shop and the stairs. Both systems usually end up in the same cellar, sometimes on the same recorder and the same power strip, because whoever installed the second system found the first one already there. When the store changes its internet or the landlord has the cellar rewired, the other system goes dark.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Finding a Hidden Fault in a Park Slope Brownstone Without Opening the Wall

These notes describe the order our technicians work in on a typical Park Slope brownstone whose cameras were concealed during a renovation and whose installer is no longer around. It is our working method, not a story about one address.

Save the footage, then read the log. Before anything is restarted, the technician exports whatever the owner needs and notes the recorder's clock against the real time. Then the recorder's own log shows when each camera dropped, whether the drive reported errors and whether the power failed, which often points straight at the fault: a camera that vanished the night of the outage, or one that has dropped every time it rains.

Measure before opening anything. A cable tester tells the technician whether the run is broken and roughly where. In a brownstone that distance usually lands on a predictable spot: the junction box behind the cornice or above the door, the point where the cable passes through the front wall, the cellar ceiling where it was stapled to a joist, or a splice an electrician hid behind a cover plate during the renovation.

Fix at the spot, not the wall. Most faults turn out to be a connector, a splice, a power adapter or a junction box, all reachable without touching plaster or stone. Only when a run is truly damaged inside a finished wall do we price an alternative, and you choose between a new concealed run, a short exterior run on the rear wall where the rules allow, or a wireless link for that one camera.

Check after dark, then hand over a map. Every camera is checked by day and under the block's real night light, alerts for video loss and drive errors are sent to the owner's phone and tested, and the owner gets a written map of which cable goes where. The next repair, whoever does it, starts with that map.

Fault by Fault

Water at the Bottom of the Hill, Days Without Power, Winter Salt and Changing Light: Why Park Slope Camera Systems Fail

Four causes account for many of the camera failures we find on Park Slope houses, buildings and storefronts. Some are true of every Brooklyn neighborhood; several are particular to a neighborhood built on a hill, wired out of sight and mostly landmarked. Knowing which one you have is the difference between a $150 visit and a new system.

Water at the bottom of the hill: the cellar recorder on the low blocks

Every Park Slope cellar is damp; the ones near Fourth Avenue also flood. When the storm sewers on the avenue back up, water can come in through floor drains, under-stoop doors and the joints of old foundation walls in minutes. The Root Hill Cafe's owner told Streetsblog that during Ophelia the bubbling drain gave them "two to three minutes, tops." A recorder on the floor, a power box on a low shelf or a PoE switch plugged into a power strip lying on the slab does not survive that. Even a recorder that stays dry can suffer when its power supply, sitting lower, shorts and takes the cameras with it.

After a flood we test before we replace. A recorder that was never under water often only needs its drive checked and its fan cleaned; a power box that sat in water is replaced, along with any connector that turned green; cables that were submerged are tested for water inside the jacket before we decide to keep them. Then everything moves up: the recorder onto a wall bracket or into a closet on the floor above, the switch and power box beside it, and the cables rerouted so no connector sits below the high-water line. Your insurer will want photos and a written description of what failed, and we provide both.

Days without power: what a long outage does that a short one does not

Most camera systems shrug off a short power cut. The February 2026 outage was not short, and it started with hours of flickering before the lights went out completely. That pattern is hard on electronics: a recorder that reboots over and over in an unstable supply can corrupt its settings or mark its drive as faulty, and a PoE switch can lose ports. Then the system sits cold and unpowered for two or three days in a freezing cellar, and comes back all at once when the grid returns.

What we find afterward is consistent. A battery backup whose batteries had been dead for years, so the recorder never had any protection. A switch that powers up but no longer powers its cameras. A recorder that boots to its setup wizard. A drive the recorder now calls "abnormal." No battery backup can run a camera system for three days, and none should be expected to; its job is to ride through flickers and give the recorder a clean shutdown in a long outage. We size the backup to do that, set the recorder to shut down gracefully when the battery runs low and start up on its own when power returns, and check that the system comes back without anyone touching it.

Winter salt: the stoop, the areaway and the under-stoop door

Con Edison blamed melting snow and salt for the underground failures behind the February outage, and the same salt water works its way into camera connectors at street level. The camera under a brownstone's stoop sits directly below steps that are salted all winter, and every thaw drips brine onto its bracket, its cable entry and the junction box behind it. The camera on the areaway gate or beside the garden-level door gets splashed by every shovel. By spring the connector is green, the picture flickers or rolls, and eventually the camera drops off.

The repair is a cleaned or replaced connector inside a sealed junction box, a drip loop so water runs off the cable before it reaches the camera, and dielectric grease on the connections. Where a camera hangs directly under the steps' drip line, moving it a few inches on the same mortar joint often keeps it dry for good.

Light that changed: skylights, street trees and the neighbor's floodlight

A camera is set up once, under that season's light. Park Slope light changes a great deal. Brownstone stair halls are lit from a skylight at the top, so the top-floor landing camera alternates between glare at noon and darkness at night. The street trees along the side streets fill a stoop camera's view in summer and open it in winter. A neighbor's new motion floodlight over the shared garden wall blinds a rear camera every time a cat walks past. A shop's new LED sign throws glare onto the door camera.

Most of these are fixed with exposure, backlight and day-night settings, a small change of angle, a lens set narrower, or a privacy mask over the source of glare. Occasionally the right answer is a camera better suited to mixed light, and if so we name it on the written price before anything is ordered.

Rules that touch a commercial camera repair

State licensing. The New York Department of State licenses companies that install or service security systems, and its rules extend to closed-circuit television used to watch for intrusion, theft or movement. An owner can work on their own system, or have an employee do it, without a license; a company working on someone else's property needs one. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431.

Cameras that watch staff. New York Labor Law §203-c requires employers who monitor employees electronically, including by video, to give written notice and get acknowledgment. Footage requests. Detectives from the 78th Precinct may ask for a copy of a clip; a copy with the original timestamps usually answers that without the recorder leaving the store. Landmarked fronts. Many Fifth and Seventh Avenue buildings, and most of the side streets between them, sit in the Park Slope Historic District or its extensions. Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Repair in Park Slope: Questions Answered

These are situations Park Slope homeowners, condo boards, landlords, co-op managers and shop owners describe when they call. Each answer is written for the houses, buildings and storefronts here.

Brownstones and row houses

Every winter the camera under our stoop, the one that watches the garden-level door, starts flickering and then drops out after a thaw. Is the salt from the steps above getting into it?

Very likely. The under-stoop camera sits right below steps that are salted all winter, and every thaw drips brine onto its bracket and into the connector or junction box behind it, where it corrodes the contacts. We open the connection, clean or replace the corroded connector, put it inside a sealed box with a drip loop on the cable and grease on the contacts, and if the camera hangs directly in the drip line, move it a few inches along the same joint so the water misses it.

Our brownstone's facade is being resurfaced next spring, and the masons will patch the stone around the stoop camera's bracket. Can the camera go back into the patched stone afterward?

Usually not into the soft new patching material itself, because it rarely holds an anchor as well as sound stone or a mortar joint. Before the work, have the camera taken down, labeled and stored, and ask the masons to leave the cable end coiled and protected. When they finish, we remount it on a mortar joint or sound stone as close as possible to the old position, using the existing cable, and test it day and night. If your building is landmarked, keeping it in the same general spot keeps the repair an ordinary replacement.

Our gardener cut back the wisteria on the rear wall, and since then the camera looking over the garden has been dead. Did they cut the cable?

Quite possibly cut or pulled. Rear cameras on Park Slope brownstones often have their cable clipped along the back wall under the vines, or running through them to an entry point at the parlor floor. We test the cable from the recorder end to find the break, splice or replace the damaged section, and re-run it in a protected channel tight to the wall or inside, so the next pruning does not take it out again.

The camera on our top-floor landing shows nothing but a white glare at midday, when the sun comes through the skylight, and is almost black at night. Is it the wrong camera?

Not necessarily. It is pointed at the brightest thing in the building by day and sits in the darkest spot by night. Turning on wide dynamic range, tilting it so the skylight is out of the frame, and masking any remaining patch of sky usually fixes the daytime picture, and setting its night mode properly, or adding a small light on the landing, fixes the night. If the model cannot handle both, we name a better-suited camera on the written price before ordering.

Shops and services on Fifth and Seventh Avenues

We want to post our store's video of a theft online, the way the 8th Street Mini Market's footage went around the neighborhood. Should anything be blurred first, and can the recorder do that?

It is your decision whether to post, and it is wise to tell the detective on the case before you do. Blurring customers, staff and anyone else who is not part of the incident is good practice, and some recorders can export with privacy masks or blurring, but most cannot do it automatically. We export a clean, full-quality copy with the original timestamp for the police first, then a second copy for posting that you can blur in an ordinary video editor, and we show you how to make both next time.

Our boutique has a two-door vestibule: we buzz customers through the street door, then the inner door. Which door should the camera that records faces watch?

The inside of the vestibule, looking at people as they stand at the inner door. That is where a visitor stops, faces the glass and waits, which gives the camera a still, frontal, well-lit face, and it is also the last point before someone reaches the counter. A second, wider camera can watch the street door. We set the face camera to its best recording quality and confirm the picture on the screen next to your buzzer.

Our recorder sits in the cellar we share with the restaurant next door, right under their kitchen exhaust duct, and its case has a sticky brown film on it. Is that hurting it?

Yes, over time. The recorder's fan pulls that greasy air through its case, and the film builds on the fan, the vents and the circuit board until it overheats, and the drive suffers first. We clean the recorder, replace the fan if it is clogged, read the drive's health, and move it into a closed cabinet or another spot in the cellar away from the duct, with its own battery backup.

We run a preschool on the garden and parlor floors of a brownstone, and parents ask to watch the classroom cameras from work. Can that be set up safely after the repair?

It can, but think about it before it is switched on. Every parent who can watch a classroom also sees other people's children, the teachers, and anything on the walls, and a parent's login can be shared. If you allow it, we set up a separate, view-only login limited to the classroom cameras, with no playback or export, kept away from entrance and office cameras, and which you can switch off at any time. We schedule the repair itself around naps and pickups.

Co-ops, rentals and Fourth Avenue buildings

The camera over the service entrance of our Prospect Park West co-op fails far more often than the lobby cameras. Why does the delivery door camera keep dying?

Because it lives the hardest life in the building. Hand trucks and trash bins knock it, the super hoses down the area under it, delivery workers prop the door with whatever is nearby, and in winter the salt and slush come through that door first. We check the camera, the cable and the junction box for knocks and water, mount it higher and more firmly, seal its connections, and if it hangs where carts hit it, move it to a protected corner that still sees the door.

A tenant in our walk-up wants to know who can watch the hallway cameras and whether the super uses them to keep tabs on visitors. What should the owner be able to tell her?

Exactly who has a login, what each login can do, and whether playback is recorded in the system's log. On a repair we set that up so it can be answered: the owner or agent holds the admin login, the super gets live view only, nobody else has access, and the recorder's log shows who played back or exported what. A clear, written answer protects the owner as much as the tenant.

Cameras with round BNC connectors, from old analog to HD models, still common on brownstones and co-ops wired fifteen or more years ago. Typical faults: salt-corroded connectors at the stoop, failed power boxes in the cellar, and infrared that has dimmed with age.

The box that holds the footage, in a cellar, a parlor closet, a co-op's recorder room or under a counter. Typical faults: worn drives, full disks, fans clogged with dust or kitchen grease, and settings lost after a long outage.

Varifocal domes and bullets

PoE switches and extenders

The switches that power network cameras, often in a cellar or a telecom room. Typical faults: dead ports after the outage, overloaded power budgets when a camera is added, and switches plugged into the floor of a cellar that floods.

Power supplies and battery backups

Central power boxes, plug-in adapters and UPS units. Typical faults: blown channel fuses, adapters that sat in water, and backups whose batteries died long before anyone needed them.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Park Slope

These claims turn up in AI-generated answers, help forums and national repair guides. Each is true somewhere, and each sends Park Slope owners toward the wrong repair, a lost clip or a bill they did not need.

"A battery backup keeps your cameras running through an outage"

Quick answers often recommend a UPS as if it made a camera system outage-proof.

A typical backup runs a recorder, a switch and a few cameras for minutes to an hour, not days. The February 2026 outage kept parts of Park Slope dark into a third day. And many backups we find in cellars have batteries that died years ago, so they protected nothing even in the first minute.

A backup's real job is to ride through flickers and dips and let the recorder shut down cleanly in a long outage. We test its batteries, size it to that job, and set the recorder to restart on its own when power returns.

"Just run new cable on the outside of the house"

When a hidden cable fails, some answers suggest running a new one along the exterior and being done with it.

On a landmarked brownstone or limestone front that is often the wrong move. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's guidebook limits visible conduit to roughly ten inches and directs equipment away from decorative surfaces, and a cable stapled across the face of a front is exactly what the rules try to prevent. Most hidden runs fail at a reachable spot anyway.

We locate the fault by measurement and fix it where it failed. If a run really is lost inside a finished wall, the options are priced before anything is opened, and an exterior run is offered only where it is allowed, usually on the rear wall.

"If the cameras look fine, the system is fine"

Many answers treat a live picture as proof that the system works.

The live picture says nothing about recording. A recorder can show every camera perfectly while its drive has stopped saving, its disk is full and set to stop, or its schedule was switched off by an update. That is the most common fault we find, and owners discover it the morning they need a clip.

Check playback, not live view: pick a camera, go back a day, and watch a minute. On every repair we turn on alerts for disk errors, a full disk and video loss, and test that they reach your phone.

"Switch to Wi-Fi cameras and skip the wiring"

Answers about failed cables often suggest replacing wired cameras with wireless ones.

A Wi-Fi camera still needs power from a cable, an outlet or a battery that drains faster in the cold. And the signal has to get through brownstone and brick, floor joists and plaster. In a four-story house with the router on the parlor floor and a camera on the rear wall of the garden level, it often does not arrive reliably, and a mesh system's automatic update can knock every wireless camera off at once.

Wireless is a good answer for one camera a cable cannot reach. For the rest, repairing the existing cable is usually cheaper and much more dependable.

"After a flood, replace everything that got wet"

Some answers advise throwing out every part of a camera system that was near water.

In Park Slope cellars after a storm, the parts that sat in water and the parts that only got damp are different repairs. A power box or a switch that was submerged should go. A recorder that stayed on a shelf may only need its drive checked and its fan cleaned. Cables can often be tested and kept.

Do not power anything that was under water until it has been checked, and photograph everything for your insurer first. We test before we replace, and we write down what failed so the claim has a clear record.

"Your landlord or co-op is required to keep the cameras working"

Some answers lump cameras in with heat, locks and intercoms as things a tenant can get the city to order fixed.

Brick Underground has reported, citing a New York real estate attorney, that city buildings are not required to have functioning cameras, and that landlords generally do not have to show tenants footage outside a legal process. Intercoms and door locks are a different matter. A dark camera in a Park Slope walk-up is usually the owner's decision, not a city order.

For tenants that means asking the owner or managing agent in writing. For owners and boards it means the camera system is theirs to maintain, and a dark one helps nobody when a tenant, an insurer or a detective asks for video.

"Camera repair costs about $100 to $300"

Cost answers often quote a low national range taken from single consumer cameras.

HomeAdvisor's Brooklyn page puts the average surveillance camera install or repair at $1,416, with a typical range of $680 to $2,145 and jobs from $160 to $3,500. That page blends new systems with small fixes, which shows why one number tells you very little.

On our Brooklyn list most single repairs land between $150 and $700, a brownstone with awkward cable access can add $100 to $200, and you get a written price for your fault after the diagnosis, before any part is replaced.

FAQ

Park Slope Security Camera Repair FAQ

Can the baby monitor camera in our nursery be added to the same app as the house cameras after the repair?
+

Sometimes. Many baby monitors are closed systems that only work with their own app or screen, while others can send a standard video stream that a recorder can show. If yours can, we can add it as a view-only camera and keep it out of recording if you prefer. If it cannot, it stays on its own app, and we make sure it is not slowing down the Wi-Fi the house cameras depend on.

After our mesh Wi-Fi system updated itself overnight, every wireless camera in the brownstone dropped off the app. Do the cameras need replacing?
+

Almost never. Mesh updates can change settings the cameras rely on, such as the radio band, the security mode or how devices are handed between units, and older cameras simply stop connecting. We reconnect each camera, set the mesh so it keeps them on a band they support, and give the cameras fixed addresses so the recorder and the app find them again after the next update.

We keep an e-bike charging in the cellar. Can the repaired cellar camera warn us if there is smoke or a fire?
+

A camera is not a smoke detector and should never be relied on as one. Some cameras can send an alert when a scene changes suddenly, and that can be a useful extra, but it can miss smoke in the dark and it is not built or tested for fire. Put a working smoke alarm in the cellar, follow FDNY guidance on charging lithium-ion batteries, and use the camera for what it is good at, which is showing you what happened.

The detective from the 78th Precinct asked for our stoop video. Can we send it electronically instead of handing over a USB stick?
+

Ask the detective how they want it, because practice varies by case. Either way, the safe order is the same: export the clip from the recorder with its original timestamp, keep an untouched copy for yourself, and send or hand over a copy. If the file is too large to email, the detective may give you a way to upload it. We can export the clip and write down the recorder's clock difference so the time on the video is clear.

We have a baby who naps on the parlor floor. Can the noisy parts of the repair be scheduled around nap times?
+

Yes. Most of a camera repair is quiet testing and settings work, and the noisy parts, such as ladder work against the front, drilling an anchor or working in the cellar below the nursery, can usually be grouped into one window. Tell us the nap times when you book, and we choose the arrival window and the order of the work around them.

Our parlor floor has its original pressed-tin ceiling. Can a camera cable that runs above it be repaired without damaging the tin?
+

In most cases, yes. We locate the fault by testing the cable from both ends, and the break is usually at a junction box, a splice or where the cable enters or leaves the ceiling, not in the middle of the tin. If the run above the tin really has to be replaced, we look for a route from the floor above or through an existing opening, and you get the options and prices before anything is touched.

Our recorder is in the same closet as the air handler for the new heat pump system. Does the heat or the vibration matter?
+

It can. Air handlers move warm or cold air and vibrate while they run, and recorders like steady temperatures and no shaking, especially their hard drives. A recorder on a shelf bolted to the same wall as the unit, or in a closet that heats up when the system runs, tends to have shorter drive life. We check the temperature inside the recorder, and if it runs hot or the shelf vibrates, we move it to a cooler, steadier spot nearby.

Can the repaired camera in our vestibule send an alert when a package or a stroller is moved, rather than every time someone walks through?
+

Some cameras have an object-removal or object-left setting that does exactly that, though it works best in a steady, well-lit space with few people passing. If your camera has it, we set the zone around the spot where packages or the stroller sit and test it. If it does not, we narrow the motion zone to that spot, which cuts most of the alerts from people walking through.

Can we see the stoop and garden cameras on a smartwatch once the system is working again?
+

Often, yes, depending on the brand's app. Many camera apps can send motion alerts with a snapshot to a watch, and some can show a short live view. We set up the phone app first, turn on the alerts you want, and then check what your watch can show with that app.

Can the stoop camera make a chime inside the house when someone walks up the steps, even though we have no video doorbell?
+

Usually. Many recorders and cameras can play a sound through a small speaker or a phone, or trigger a chime box, when someone enters a zone you draw on the stoop.
