The Row · The Odd House · The Bell Pair · The Transformer · Brooklyn List Prices

New Lots went up a row at a time. On block after block south of Dumont Avenue the houses stand shoulder to shoulder, the same width, the same age, built to the same plan: sixteen-foot one-family houses on Schenck Avenue and Hendrix Street, eighteen-foot houses on Hinsdale Street and Alabama Avenue, two-family houses on Barbey Street and Georgia Avenue, a run of twenty-one identical six-family walk-ups on Miller Avenue. When every house on a block face was wired the same way, the house next door is the best drawing you will ever get of your own bell circuit. And when one house in the row has been split into more apartments, given a new door or had its cellar rebuilt, that difference is usually where the fault lives. This page is written around that idea: read the row first, then the house.

The Neighborhood

Why New Lots Properties Need Door Buzzer Repair

New Lots is the old name for this part of Brooklyn, older than East New York itself. Wikipedia's article on East New York treats New Lots as one of its sub-sections and explains the name: the "new lots" east of the Town of Flatbush were laid out in the eighteenth century as an eastward extension of Flatbush. The area broke away from Flatbush in 1852 as the Town of New Lots, kept a town hall on Bradford Street, and was annexed to the City of Brooklyn in 1886 as its 26th Ward. East New York began as a real-estate venture on the part of the town north of New Lots Avenue. Today the two names overlap, and Wikipedia gives New Lots no street boundaries of its own.

So this page uses the city's definition, and says so. The Department of City Planning divides Community District 5 into neighborhood tabulation areas, and one of them is called East New York-New Lots. It runs roughly from Dumont and Blake Avenues south to Linden Boulevard, Stanley Avenue and Flatlands Avenue, and from Van Sinderen Avenue east to Fountain Avenue, with New Lots Avenue, Hegeman Avenue and Livonia Avenue crossing it. Every figure below covers the tax lots in Community District 5 inside the fourteen census tracts that make up that area. People who live just south of Flatlands Avenue or north of Dumont Avenue may call their block New Lots too; the numbers simply do not include them.

Inside those lines, New Lots is a neighborhood of houses. Of the 4,854 lots that hold homes, 4,054 are houses with one, two or three households, and most of them are attached to the house on one or both sides. The apartments are concentrated in a few places. About 3,200 homes sit on six tax lots that PLUTO lists under the names of the partnerships now running Boulevard Houses, Linden Houses and the Pennsylvania Avenue-Wortman Avenue Houses, public housing that Wikipedia says moved to private management under the city's PACT program by the end of 2021. Another 2,800 or so are in other buildings of fifty homes or more, among them newer subsidized buildings on Livonia Avenue, Stanley Avenue and the side streets. And about 2,750 are in buildings of six to forty-nine homes, most of them three- and four-story walk-ups that PLUTO dates to around 1930, lined up on Miller, Pennsylvania and Hegeman Avenues and Vermont Street.

That mix explains what a door buzzer repair means here. In a walk-up, a buzzer is a lobby panel, a pair of wires for every apartment and a strike on the street door, and the city's housing inspectors treat it as part of the building's legal equipment. In a two- or three-family house it is a short bell circuit, a transformer in the cellar and a release button upstairs, and the owner often lives in the house. In a one-family row house it is a doorbell. What all three have in common in New Lots is that the building next door was very probably built by the same hands in the same year, which turns out to be the most useful fact a repair technician can have.

  • 5,517 tax lots in our study area, 4,854 of them holding homes, 18,418 homes in all (MapPLUTO, Community District 5 lots inside the East New York-New Lots tabulation area).

Read the Row

Why a New Lots Buzzer Repair Starts Next Door

Most of Brooklyn's buzzer advice sorts buildings by age or by height. In New Lots the more useful question is a different one: was this house built alone, or as one of a row? Developers here put up houses in long runs, one plan repeated down a block face, and the city's tax map still shows it. We grouped every house in the study area with its neighbors on the same block face whenever they shared the year PLUTO gives for construction and had the same street frontage to within a foot. Of the 4,054 houses with one to three households, 2,487, or 61 percent, stand in a run of at least three. Nearly half stand in a run of five or more, and a quarter in a run of ten or more.

Three block faces on Schenck Avenue and Hendrix Street each hold 27 one-family houses, every one sixteen feet wide and dated 1960. Two block faces of Barbey Street hold 24 two-family houses each, eighteen feet wide, from the same year. On both sides of Livonia Avenue at the western end of the area, on Hinsdale Street and on Williams, Alabama, Georgia and Snediker Avenues, runs of 18 to 23 one-family houses, all eighteen feet wide, carry years from 1997 to 2002; the Nehemiah housing organization says 700 of its homes were built in the New Lots section of East New York beginning in 1996, and these blocks fit that description. On Miller Avenue, 21 three-story walk-ups with six apartments each stand in a single unbroken run, each 29 feet wide, all dated 1930.

Why should any of this matter to someone whose buzzer has stopped working? Because a row is a set of copies. When a builder put up twenty houses to one plan, the bell button went in the same place on every front, the wire took the same path from the button to the cellar, the transformer was mounted on the same joist, and the release button for the upstairs apartment was put in the same spot on the same wall. Nobody in New Lots has wiring drawings for a 1920s two-family house. But the house next door, if it has not been changed, is a full-size drawing of yours.

On the map each square is a tax block, placed at the average position of its lots, with street names set at the average position of the lots that face them. The long red runs south of Linden Boulevard are the 1960 rows; the dense red blocks in the west, on both sides of Livonia Avenue, are mostly the late-1990s rows.

What the row tells a technician before the panel comes off

Reading the row is not guesswork; it is a set of checks. On arrival we look at the fronts of the houses on either side and, where the owners are happy to say, ask how their bells behave. If three houses in a row of twenty all have their bell buttons on the brick to the right of the door, and yours has a hole there and a new button on the door frame, someone moved your button, and the old wire is probably still in the wall behind the hole. If every house in the row has two buttons and yours has three, a third household was added, and the third button was wired by whoever made the change, not by the builder. If the neighbors' transformers hum on the cellar wall just inside the front foundation and yours is not there, it was moved, and the search starts by finding where.

The same logic works in the other direction. When a row-mate's bell works and yours does not, and the two houses still match from the sidewalk to the cellar, the fault is far more likely to be wear in a part than a design problem, because the design is proven next door. That narrows the work to the button, the transformer, the chime or buzzer, the release button and the strike, tested one by one. When the houses no longer match, the difference comes first, because changes made decades later by different hands are where splices, buried junctions and undersized wire tend to sit.

None of this needs a neighbor's permission to enter their house. Almost everything we compare is visible from the street or described by its owner. We never touch another owner's circuit without their say-so.

Rows of every age

Rows are not a feature of one period in New Lots, which is why this page does not sort houses by their year. The tax map shows runs of two-family houses dated 1899 to 1901 on Georgia Avenue, two-story four-family rows dated 1923 to 1935 on Pennsylvania, Sheffield and Williams Avenues and on Bradford and Wyona Streets, the long 1960 runs on Schenck Avenue, Hendrix Street and Barbey Street, a 1975 run of 21 two-family houses on Van Siclen Avenue, and the late-1990s runs on either side of Livonia Avenue. The city's construction years for older houses are often estimates, so two neighbors may share a year because a clerk rounded it; matching frontage on the same block face makes the grouping much more reliable, and the photos of a block usually settle it.

What changes from one kind of row to the next is the circuit the builder chose. The one-family runs were built with a single doorbell. The two-family and three-family runs have a button per household and, usually, a release button upstairs that frees the street door. The four-family houses and the six-family walk-ups have a small panel and, often, a vestibule with an inner door. The method does not change: find a matching neighbor, list the differences, test the parts that are the same.

The Odd House Out

When One House No Longer Matches Its Row

A row stays a row from the street long after its houses have gone their separate ways inside. The tax map shows how often that happens. Of the 237 runs of five or more houses in the study area, 93 contain at least one house recorded with a different number of homes from the rest of its row: a three-family house in a row of two-families, a two-family house in a row built as one-families, or the reverse. Across those 93 rows there are 200 such odd houses, and 122 of them have more homes than their neighbors.

More homes almost always means someone added an apartment, and an added apartment needs a way to be called. In our experience that is where a New Lots bell circuit is most likely to have been improvised. A third button gets mounted beside the original two and spliced into one of their wires; a basement unit gets a battery doorbell stuck to the side door; a top-floor apartment gets a release button on the same pair as the second floor, so that one button now opens the door for two households and rings in the wrong place. None of that is in the builder's plan, and none of it is visible from the house next door, which is exactly why the comparison is so useful: whatever your house has that its unaltered twin does not, look there first.

Barbey Street shows the pattern in a single block face. The 24 houses on block 4421 are all eighteen feet wide and all dated 1960, and seventeen of them are recorded today as two-family houses. Seven are recorded as three-family. If the builder put two buttons and two release buttons into each house, the seven three-family houses each carry a circuit that someone extended later, and the people who extended them were not working from the same plan as each other.

What usually changes, and what it does to the bells

  • A household added upstairs or in the cellar. A new button and a new chime or buzzer, often spliced onto an existing pair. Symptoms: two apartments ring together, one button rings the wrong floor, or the new bell works only when the old one is pressed too.
  • A new front door. A heavier steel or steel-and-glass door replacing the original, sometimes with a different latch. Symptoms: the release buzzes but the door stays shut, because the strike was never matched to the new latch or frame.
  • A vestibule enclosed or opened up. Many row houses had an outer and an inner door; owners have removed one or added one. Symptoms: the release opens a door that is no longer the one visitors use.
  • A cellar finished as living space. Transformers and junctions that used to be on an open joist end up above a new ceiling. Symptoms: a dead circuit and nobody knows where the transformer went.
  • A video doorbell fitted. The original transformer may not supply what the doorbell needs, and the mechanical chime may have been bypassed. Symptoms: the doorbell restarts, or the chime inside stops sounding.

The sidewalk shows buttons, doors and vestibules; the owner tells us about apartments and cellars; the meter does the rest.

The cellar-apartment pilot and the extra bell

Community District 5 was chosen by the city for a pilot program to legalize basement and cellar apartments. HPD's page for the Basement Apartment Conversion Pilot Program says it offers eligible owners of one- to three-family homes in East New York and Cypress Hills low- or no-interest, possibly forgivable, loans to turn a basement or cellar into a safe, legal apartment, under building-code changes passed in February 2019. City & State reported in January 2023 that the pilot had drawn only five participants. Whatever the count, the pattern is familiar from our side: a ground-level or below-grade unit added to a house that was built for one or two households, with a door of its own and a bell that was not part of the original row.

If your house is one of them, the bell for that unit is a separate circuit from the street door's and should be treated as one. It needs its own button, its own sounder and a clear label at the transformer, and it must not share a pair with an upstairs release. Whether a unit is legal is a question for the Department of Buildings and HPD, not for a buzzer technician; we repair and label what is there, and we will say so if the wiring we find needs an electrician rather than us.

The Public Record

What the City's Files Say About New Lots Buzzers

Two city records touch New Lots's front doors. HPD, the housing agency, writes a violation when its inspector finds a broken bell, buzzer or intercom in a building it inspects, citing section 27-2005 of the Housing Maintenance Code. 311 keeps the complaints that lead to those inspections, under a category for bells, buzzers and intercoms. We matched both to the tax lots in the study area on October 22, 2026.

HPD's file holds 482 intercom violations at 225 buildings, the oldest from October 1987 and the newest from October 20, 2026. 403 are Class B, 78 Class A and one Class C, and 125 were still open when we looked. Almost every one, 438 of them, names the apartment where the defect was found, in wording such as "at building entrance to intercom system … in the foyer located at apt 5". The yearly count has moved around, with a high of 58 in 2024.

The records sit mostly where the apartments are. Buildings with six to nineteen homes, the walk-ups, carry 202 of the 482 violations, about eleven for every hundred homes they hold. Four- and five-family houses carry 92. One-, two- and three-family houses together carry 59, under one per hundred homes, because the city's intercom rules are written for multiple dwellings, and an owner who lives in the house is unlikely to report its bell to the city. The 311 file tells the same story from the other end: 408 complaints from 157 addresses since January 2020, 126 of them from walk-ups and 98 from the largest buildings.

Why the row is a repair tool, not a risk score

We checked whether the odd houses in a row draw more complaints or violations than the houses that still match their neighbors. They do not, at least not in any way the numbers can show: the counts at houses are too small, and an owner living downstairs fixes or ignores a bell without involving the city. Among buildings with five to forty-nine homes, those standing in identical runs and those standing alone were cited at almost exactly the same rate, 59 of 152 against 59 of 162. On the 21-building Miller Avenue run, 9 buildings have been cited, 13 times in all, and the other 12 never.

So the row does not predict which house will fail. What it does is shorten the search once a house has failed. The records tell us where the city has been; the row tells us what the building looked like before anyone changed it.

Of the 408 complaints, 94 ended with a violation and 165 with an inspection that found none. In 135 cases the inspector could not get in or could not finish. That last number matters to tenants in two- and three-family houses: a complaint closed for no access leaves the bell exactly as it was, and the 311 record asks the caller to file again.

Door Buzzer Repair Services in New Lots

Every card below is repair work on a bell, buzzer or intercom that is already there. A house or building that wants a new system instead is quoted under intercom installation in New Lots. 🏘️ Row-comparison diagnosis

We start on the sidewalk: your front against your neighbors', buttons, doors and vestibules, then the cellar. Whatever your house has that its unaltered twin lacks is checked first, before a single cover comes off.

🔔 Bells, chimes and buzzers

Dead buttons, chimes that hum instead of ringing, buzzers that sound in the wrong apartment. We test each button to its sounder and replace what has failed, matching the voltage the house actually supplies.

🔌 Transformers

The small transformer that powers a house's bells, usually on a cellar joist or beside the electrical panel. We test it under load, replace it if it has failed, and label which circuit it feeds.

🚪 Release buttons and strikes

The upstairs button that frees the street door and the strike in the frame that it drives. We check the strike against the latch of the door that is hanging there now, which is often not the door the strike was bought for.

🧵 Untangling added circuits

Where a third household, a cellar unit or a rear apartment was spliced onto an existing pair, we trace the splice, give each household its own path where the wiring allows, and label every pair at the transformer.

📞 Walk-up panels and handsets

Lobby panels, apartment handsets, riser pairs and the supply in three- and four-story walk-ups on Miller, Pennsylvania and Hegeman Avenues, including the apartments named in an HPD violation.

🚪 Vestibule doors and closers

Inner and outer doors that no longer close and latch by themselves. A door that drifts open defeats the release; we adjust or replace the closer and the latch alongside the electrical repair, quoted first.

📹 Video doorbells on old bell wire

A wired video doorbell asks more of a transformer than a button did. We check what the transformer can supply, whether the old chime still needs to sound, and whether a battery doorbell has simply been stuck beside a dead circuit.

Building Specialists

Door Buzzer Repair for New Lots's Architecture

New Lots's homes fall into eight kinds, and for six of them the row they stand in matters more than anything else about them. These descriptions are the ones we work from on the phone; the counts come from MapPLUTO for the study area.

Sixteen-foot one-family rows

The 1960 runs on Schenck Avenue and Hendrix Street: three block faces of 27 houses each, all sixteen feet wide. One household, one doorbell, one transformer. On fronts this narrow, later owners often moved the button to make room for a light or a camera.

Eighteen-foot one-family rows

The late-1990s runs on both sides of Livonia Avenue, on Hinsdale Street and on Williams, Alabama, Georgia and Snediker Avenues, which the Nehemiah organization's description of 700 homes built in New Lots from 1996 fits. Each house has an ordinary doorbell, and the job we expect most here is a wired video doorbell that the original transformer cannot keep running.

Two-family rows

The largest group: 114 runs of five or more, from the 1899 and 1901 rows on Georgia Avenue to the 1960 rows on Barbey Street and a 1975 row of 21 on Van Siclen Avenue. Two buttons, two sounders and a release button upstairs that frees the street door. Where a couplet shares a front step, two houses' buttons can sit a few feet apart.

Three-family houses

696 lots. Some stand in rows of their own, 21 runs of five or more, and 96 stand as the odd house in a row built with fewer homes per house. With three households the house is a multiple dwelling in the law's eyes, and the entrance door rules in the next section begin to apply. The third circuit is the one we expect to find improvised.

Four-family houses

507 lots with four or five homes, most of them two-story houses that PLUTO dates between 1923 and 1935, in rows on Pennsylvania, Sheffield and Williams Avenues and on Bradford, Wyona and Vermont Streets. A small button plate, often a vestibule, four lines to the cellar, and 92 of the area's HPD intercom violations.

Three- and four-story walk-ups

232 buildings with six to nineteen homes, almost all dated around 1930, with Miller Avenue's run of 21 six-family buildings the longest. A lobby panel, a pair per apartment, handsets, a supply in the cellar and a strike on the street door. They hold 202 of the 482 HPD intercom violations.

Newer subsidized buildings

Elevator buildings put up since 2011 on Livonia Avenue, Stanley Avenue, Sheffield Avenue and nearby, many owned by housing development fund corporations. Networked door stations, fob readers and magnetic locks are common; the first question is who holds the administrator account.

PACT-managed public housing

Boulevard Houses, Linden Houses and the Pennsylvania Avenue-Wortman Avenue Houses, now run by private managers under the city's PACT program. Residents report entry problems to the management office; we do not repair those systems at a resident's request, but we can work for a manager that engages us.

Couplets, shared steps and buttons a few feet apart

A 2022 Columbia University preservation thesis by Jesse Kling on postwar row houses in Brooklyn and Queens describes two-family brick rows with "a pair of entrances, each accessible by its own porch, stoop, and walkway" at the center of each couplet. Its examples come from Canarsie, Flatlands and Queens rather than New Lots, but the arrangement is easy to spot on any New Lots block where two doors meet in the middle of a pair of houses. Two houses' buttons then sit within arm's reach of each other, sometimes on the same strip of brick. Couriers press the wrong one, and so, occasionally, does whoever replaced a button. When a call comes in about a bell that rings next door, the first check is whether the right button is wired to the right house.

Commercial Door Buzzer Repair

Business Door Buzzer Repair in New Lots

New Lots has one real shopping street and a handful of avenues where stores sit among residential rows. A shop's own door usually has a commercial lock and, sometimes, a buzzer for deliveries. The apartments upstairs have their own door and their own bells. We repair both, and ask which door is failing and whose cellar holds the transformer, because in a store building the two circuits often share a cellar and sometimes a transformer.

New Lots Avenue

The neighborhood's main shopping street, sharing its old name: 104 of the area's 209 store-and-apartment lots are on it. The 3 train's southern terminal, New Lots Avenue station, is a few blocks north at Ashford Street and Livonia Avenue. The New Lots Reformed Church stands at 630 New Lots Avenue and the New Lots branch library at 665. Most store buildings here have one or two apartments above and a residents' door beside the shop.

Livonia Avenue

Runs under the elevated New Lots subway line, with stations at Van Siclen Avenue and Pennsylvania Avenue. Fifteen store-and-apartment lots, and several newer subsidized elevator buildings put up between 2016 and 2018, the kind of building where networked entry is the norm.

Pennsylvania Avenue

One of the three primary thoroughfares Wikipedia names for East New York. In New Lots it is lined with walk-ups and four-family houses, among them a 1930 run of five sixteen-apartment buildings, and it has more HPD intercom violations, 53, than any other street in the study area.

Hegeman Avenue

A long east-west residential street with twenty store-and-apartment lots scattered between rows of houses and walk-ups. HPD has written 40 intercom violations on it.

Linden Boulevard

The wide boulevard through the middle of the study area, another of East New York's primary thoroughfares. On the New Lots stretch its frontage is mostly houses, apartment buildings and the PACT campuses.

Miller Avenue

Not a shopping street but the densest apartment street in New Lots: 48 buildings with six to forty-nine homes, most in the 21-building run of 1930 walk-ups, plus seven store buildings. HPD has written 50 intercom violations here.

Rules that apply at a New Lots entrance

  • When a house becomes a multiple dwelling. The New York State Attorney General's tenants' rights guide says multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968 must have automatic self-closing and self-locking doors at all entrances, and those with eight or more apartments a two-way voice intercom that releases the door; older buildings must provide them if a majority of apartments ask (Multiple Dwelling Law § 50-a). A three-family house is a multiple dwelling, so a two-family row house turned into three apartments after 1968 has its entrance door rules changed by the conversion, even though its neighbors' do not.
  • Self-closing doors. HPD treats a defective self-closing door in a multiple dwelling as a Class C immediately hazardous violation, to be corrected within 14 days of the notice (Housing Maintenance Code § 27-2041.1). A buzzer repair that leaves a walk-up's street door unable to close and latch on its own is not complete.
  • Intercom defects. HPD writes intercom violations under Housing Maintenance Code § 27-2005, and in New Lots 438 of 482 name an apartment. The repair has to reach that apartment's station and line, not just the lobby.
  • Line-voltage work. The Department of Buildings requires a licensed electrician for line-voltage electrical work. A transformer that must be moved to a new outlet or circuit, or wired into a panel, needs one; we do the low-voltage side and work with the electrician.
  • Egress and reach. Magnetically locked doors must still let people out under building-code egress conditions, and the 2010 ADA Standards put the highest forward reach at 48 inches.
  • Public housing under PACT. Entry systems at Boulevard Houses, Linden Houses and the Pennsylvania Avenue-Wortman Avenue Houses belong to their managers. Residents should report problems to the management office.

For a store, card or fob entry and an intrusion alarm are separate services we can quote alongside the door.

Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Repair in New Lots: Questions Answered

These are questions we expect from New Lots owners, tenants and managers, grouped by the situation they come from. Each is answered here once and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Reading the Row

Our neighbor on Hendrix Street has offered to let your technician look at their cellar, since our two houses were built as twins. Does a look at the house next door actually save time, and do you need it?

It can save a good deal of time, and no, we do not need it. In a row of identical one-family houses the transformer, the bell wire and the chime were put in the same places in every house by the same crew. If the twin next door still has its original arrangement, two minutes in its cellar tells us which joist to look along in yours, where the wire comes through the front foundation, and whether a junction box sits behind the vestibule ceiling. That replaces the slowest part of a house call: finding where things were hidden.

We treat the offer as a courtesy, not a requirement: our technician looks only, with the neighbor present, and touches nothing on their circuit. In most rows the comparison happens on the sidewalk anyway. If the neighbor's house has itself been altered, we say so and stop using it as a reference.

On every other house in our row the bell button is on the brick to the right of the door, but ours is on the door frame. Does a moved button usually mean trouble behind the wall?

Often it does, though not always. When a button is moved, the wire that used to end at the old position has to be extended to the new one, and the extension is usually made inside the wall or behind the trim with whatever connector was in the installer's pocket. Years later that joint is a common failure point: corroded, pulled loose or simply taped.

The repair is to find the old position, which is usually easy because the neighbors show us exactly where it was, open the joint, and either remake it properly in a box or run the wire cleanly to the new position. If you would rather have the button back where the builder put it, so your front matches the row, that is often the simpler job, because the original hole and wire path are still there.

In our row of identical houses, ours is the only one whose bell has failed twice in a year, even after a new button. What makes one house in a matching row keep failing?

When identical houses behave differently, the cause is almost always something that happened to one house and not to the others. We look for three things. First, water: a downspout, a cracked sill or a step that slopes toward your door rather than away from it puts water on your button and your wire, and a new button behind the same leak fails the same way. Second, a load that the builder never planned for: a lighted button, a video doorbell or a porch fixture tied into the bell transformer can overheat it or starve the chime. Third, an old joint somewhere along the run that comes and goes with temperature.

Replacing the button again does not touch any of those. The useful test is to measure the transformer while the bell is being rung, check what else is connected to it, and follow the wire from the button to the cellar looking for the joint. Whatever your house has that a long-working neighbor's does not is the first suspect.

When the House No Longer Matches

Our row was built as one-family houses, but ours now has a separate apartment downstairs with its own side entrance. When that apartment's bell stopped working, the main doorbell started ringing weakly too. Can one problem cause both?

Yes, and in a house like yours it is the most likely explanation. A one-family house was built with one transformer sized for one doorbell. When a second apartment was added, its bell was very often connected to the same transformer, sometimes to the same pair of wires. A short in the new circuit, a sounder that has failed in a way that draws too much current, or a cheap connector that has started to leak current can drag the voltage down for both bells at once.

We separate the two circuits at the transformer and test each on its own. If the original bell recovers the moment the downstairs circuit is disconnected, the fault is downstairs. The lasting repair is usually to give the apartment its own button and sounder on a properly sized transformer, or on its own transformer, with both circuits labelled. Your neighbors in the row will still have one doorbell each; your house now has two households, and its wiring should say so.

The transformer in our neighbors' cellars is on the front wall, just inside the foundation, but there is nothing there in ours, and our bell still works. What is powering it?

Something is, and it is worth knowing what. The possibilities we find most often in altered row houses are a transformer moved to the electrical panel or beside the boiler when the cellar was rewired; a transformer mounted inside a chime box upstairs; a plug-in transformer hidden behind furniture; or no transformer at all, because the original bell was abandoned and a battery doorbell now does the job while the old button sits dead beside it.

We find it by measuring at the button and the chime and following the wire, not by opening walls. The answer matters for two reasons. A transformer buried behind a finished ceiling or wired into a panel by a non-electrician may need an electrician's attention, and the next person to work on the bell will need to know where it is. We note its location and rating on the paper we leave.

Narrow Fronts and Doorbells

Our sixteen-foot house has the electric meter, a light, the mailbox and the bell all within a couple of feet of the door. Where can a video doorbell go without crowding the others?

On fronts this narrow the answer is usually the strip of trim or brick between the door frame and the nearest window, mounted with a slim plate and, where the doorbell needs it, a small wedge so the camera faces the step rather than the wall. Narrow models made for door frames fit better than square ones. The bell wire is normally already at the old button position, so the doorbell goes there or very close to it, which keeps the wiring simple.

Two cautions. The meter and its conduit are the utility's and must not be disturbed, so we keep the doorbell clear of them. And a light fixture right above a camera can wash out its picture at night, so we look at where the light falls before we decide the height.

Our late-1990s house on Hinsdale Street still has its original doorbell, and the chime has finally given out. If we replace the chime, should the button and the transformer be replaced at the same time?

Not automatically. We test the button and the transformer first, and replace only what is failing. A transformer that still supplies its rated voltage under load can go on for many more years, and a button that makes clean contact does not need changing.

That said, there are two situations where doing all three makes sense. If you are planning a wired video doorbell, the transformer may need to be larger anyway, and the old button is coming off regardless. And if the houses on either side of yours have recently needed transformers, the same model of a similar age may be near the end in your cellar too; we would measure it carefully and tell you what we see, then leave the decision to you.

Walk-Ups and Four-Families in a Run

Our six-family on Miller Avenue is one of a long run of identical buildings with different owners. The building next door just had its lobby panel replaced. Can we choose the same panel, and will it work on our old wiring?

You can, and the run makes it more likely to work, because the buildings were probably wired with the same number of pairs to each apartment in the same way. But probably is not certainly. Over ninety years some buildings in a run will have had risers or handsets changed. Before recommending a panel, we count the working pairs at your supply, test what each apartment's line can carry, and check what your handsets are. If your wiring matches what the panel needs, the choice next door is a reasonable guide.

Two neighboring buildings on the same panel can also share spare parts and a super's know-how. Replacing a panel is installation rather than repair, priced separately, but we will tell you on a repair visit whether your wiring would support it.

HPD cited the intercom in our four-family, and the violation names the apartment by its position, something like "2nd story, 2nd apartment from south at west." How do we match that to our apartment numbers?

HPD's inspectors describe a location by floor and by counting apartments from one side of the building, then name the side of the building the apartment is on. In the New Lots records we looked at, wording like "located at apt 5, 2nd story, 2nd apartment from south at west" is typical, and 438 of the 482 intercom violations name an apartment. To translate it, stand in the public hall on the floor named, find which direction is south, count apartments from that end, and note whether the apartment is on the west side of the hall.

If the apartment number HPD wrote matches the door, go by the number. If it does not, or your building numbers apartments differently from the way the inspector counted, the position wording is the safer guide, and the repair should cover that apartment's station and line, not just the lobby panel. We read the open violations for your building before the visit so the quote names the apartments they cite.

FAQ

New Lots Door Buzzer Repair FAQ

Our house is the corner house of its row, with the front door on the side street. Is a corner house usually wired differently from the rest of the row?
+

Often, yes. Builders frequently treated the end of a row differently: a wider lot, a door turned to face the side street, sometimes a store on the ground floor or an extra apartment. That means the corner house is the one house in the row its neighbors cannot fully explain. We still use the row for the parts that match, such as the cellar layout and the type of transformer, but we expect the button, the door and the wire route to be the corner house's own and we trace them from the button rather than assuming.

We are bringing a house in a row back into use after it sat empty for years. What should be checked on the bells before anyone moves in?
+

Everything from the button to the strike, in that order, before the tenants arrive rather than after the first complaint. Empty houses lose bell circuits in predictable ways: buttons corrode, chimes and buzzers seize, transformers are disconnected when the power is shut off, and wires get cut during cleanup. We test each household's button to its own sounder, measure the transformer under load, try the release from every floor and make sure the street door closes and latches by itself. If the house now holds more households than it did, each one should have its own labelled circuit from the start.

Should the bell transformer have its own circuit breaker?
+

It does not have to, but it should be on a circuit that someone can identify. In most row houses the transformer is fed from a general circuit, and when that breaker trips or is switched off for other work, the bells go quiet and nobody connects the two. The line-voltage side is an electrician's work in New York City, so if you want the transformer on its own breaker or moved to a better spot, an electrician does that part. What we do is note on our paperwork which breaker feeds the transformer, so the next time the bells stop you can check the panel first.

Is a doorbell repair in a one-family row house charged from the same price list as an intercom repair in a walk-up?
+

Yes. Both start with the $250 service call and use the same Brooklyn list lines. What differs is which lines apply: a one-family house usually needs at most a transformer or a button, while a walk-up may need stations, a panel or a riser pair. A one-family doorbell repair is often finished within the service call or with one more list line, so the total tends to be at the low end of what this page shows.

A contractor is about to replace our front door. What should we tell them so the bell and the upstairs release keep working?
+

Tell them three things before the old door comes out: that the frame holds an electric strike wired to a release button upstairs, that the bell button and its wire are on or beside the frame, and that both must be reconnected and tested before they leave. Ask whether the new door's latch will line up with the existing strike; if it will not, the strike has to be changed to suit the new door. If you let us know the date, we can come after the door is hung to fit or adjust the strike, which is far easier than undoing a door that was installed without one.

There are two small transformers on the wall of our cellar. How do we know which one runs the bell?
+

Look first, without touching the wiring. Transformers for doorbells and for heating controls often look alike, and many cellars have both: one for the bells and one for a thermostat or a boiler control. The label usually gives the output voltage; doorbell transformers are commonly 10, 16 or 24 volts, and heating ones are usually 24. The wire also helps: the bell pair heads toward the front of the house, while a thermostat wire heads up to a room or to the boiler. If you cannot tell, leave both alone and mention it when you call; we identify them with a meter and label each one.
