The Neighborhood

Why Gravesend Properties Need Alarm Installation

Gravesend is older than almost any place in Brooklyn and newer-looking than most. Wikipedia's article on the neighborhood records that Willem Kieft, director of New Netherland, granted a land patent to Lady Deborah Moody and a group of English settlers in the 1640s, that it was the only English-chartered town among the six original towns of Kings County, and that its first plan was a 16-acre square inside a wooden palisade, cut into four quarters by two roads: Gravesend Road, now McDonald Avenue, running north and south, and Gravesend Neck Road running east and west. The four blocks bounded by Village Road South, Village Road East, Village Road North and Van Sicklen Street still mark that old center.

Where the neighborhood begins and ends depends on who is drawing the map. The same Wikipedia article gives two sets of limits: the Belt Parkway, Bay Parkway, Avenue P and Ocean Parkway in its opening lines, and East 12th Street or Coney Island Avenue, Stillwell Avenue, Avenue P and Coney Island Creek and Shore Parkway in its geography section. The ZIP code is 11223, the 2020 census counted 30,587 residents, and the area is split among Community Boards 11, 13 and 15 and among the NYPD's 60th, 61st and 62nd Precincts. We use the wider definition on this page and serve the whole of it at the same Brooklyn prices.

What matters for an alarm is the way the houses and the cars sit on the lots. Wikipedia records that many one-family houses went up here in the 1920s and were converted into two-family houses during the Depression, that the northeast section was redeveloped from the 1990s with larger single-family houses, and that some two-family houses have since been turned back into one. The result is a mix of attached and semi-detached brick houses with a driveway down one side or a garage set under the front, two-family houses with two doors and sometimes two old alarm systems, and, between Ocean Parkway and McDonald Avenue around Avenues S, T and U, very large new houses that 6sqft described in 2015 as covering most of their lots and often hidden behind fences, tall trees or hedges. Almost every one of those properties has a car on it, and often two or three.

The recent burglary record reads like a list of the openings those houses have. Besides the East Fourth Street key theft, Brooklyn Paper's 61st Precinct blotter has described a burglar who got into a house on East 23rd Street near Avenue U through the kitchen window overnight while the owner was out from 7 p.m. to 7 a.m., taking $1,900, two passports and four credit cards; a February 2020 attempt at a house on East First Street at Avenue W in which part of a basement window was removed, though nothing was reported missing; and, the same week, the side door of a car parked at McDonald Avenue and Avenue U broken into in the afternoon and $5,880 in cash and bank checks taken. Another Brooklyn Paper item describes two burglars forcing the rear windows of both apartments in a two-family house on West 16th Street between Bay 50th Street and Avenue Z at 12:30 in the afternoon on January 20 and leaving with $13,000. The 62nd Precinct blotter reported a resident who came home around 9:30 on a November night to find his front door pried open and was beaten by the two men still inside.

Cars and car parts come up again and again. A Brooklyn Paper report on a 61st Precinct community council meeting quoted the commanding officer warning about a crew stealing tires and rims from vehicles in Sheepshead Bay, Gravesend, Midwood and Manhattan Beach, and telling residents, "your car is not a safe"; the precinct had made 19 car break-in arrests that year. Elsewhere in the city the NYPD's 20th Precinct warned in March 2026 that thieves were "walking into homes, grabbing key fobs, and driving off with the vehicles," and urged owners to keep fobs away from front doors and windows. An alarm designed only around jewelry boxes and back windows misses what many burglars here are after. A camera on the driveway is often part of the same plan, which we cover on our Gravesend security camera installation page, and many families add a code lock on the side door through access control in Gravesend.

A Garage Zone That Will Not Clear, a Keypad Beeping Since the Last Power Cut, or a Break-In Last Night: Same-Day Alarm Repair in Gravesend

Three repair calls come up in Gravesend more than any others. The first is the garage: an overhead door contact knocked out of line by the car bumper or by years of the door slamming down, so the panel refuses to arm or reports the garage open all night. The second is an old system that beeps and shows a trouble light because its backup battery is dead or its phone dialer lost the copper line it depended on. The third is the morning after a break-in.

For the first two, a photo of the keypad display sent to (347) 934-8335 usually tells us which part has failed before we drive over. For the third, we can put contacts on the doors and a motion sensor in the hall the same day or the next, then come back to finish the windows and the garage once the full system is agreed. An exterior bell that will not stop is also a same-day job: New York City's Administrative Code, § 24-238, requires a building burglar alarm to be able to shut itself off, and to do so, within fifteen minutes of going off.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Gravesend’s Architecture

Gravesend's houses fall into a handful of types. Each one puts the car, the keys and the easy windows in a different place, and that is what decides the sensor list.

Attached brick row houses with a garage below

Two-story houses in a continuous row, many with the garage set into the front at basement level and a short steep driveway. The garage leads into the basement, the basement leads up to the hall, and the front and back are the only exposed walls.

Semi-detached houses with a side driveway

Pairs of houses sharing one wall, with a driveway down the open side to a garage at the back or under the house. Every window on the driveway side is reachable from a parked car, and the side door is the one the family uses.

1920s houses converted to two-family

Houses built for one family and divided during the Depression, according to Wikipedia, with an upstairs apartment reached by its own door or a shared hall, and sometimes now being converted back.

Large new houses north of Avenue U

Big houses with terra-cotta roofs and Spanish or Mediterranean details, built on lots where smaller houses once stood and screened by hedges, fences and trees, with long runs of glass at the back and garages for several cars.

Older wood houses near the old town square

A few surviving frame houses around Gravesend Neck Road and the Village Roads, including the landmarked Lady Moody–Van Sicklen House at 27 Gravesend Neck Road, with deep cellars, old sash and lots laid out on the colonial plan.

Apartment buildings and co-ops

Marlboro Houses' seven- and sixteen-story NYCHA buildings, two Mitchell-Lama co-ops noted by Wikipedia, and walk-ups along the avenues, where the alarm stays inside one apartment.

Method notes by building type

Brick walls and finished basements. Most Gravesend houses are brick on the outside with plaster or drywall inside, and many basements have been finished as dens, playrooms or a grandparent's apartment. That rules out the easy route through open joists that older frame houses offer. We wire where a ceiling or a closet gives us a path and use wireless contacts everywhere else, which keeps a recently renovated house intact.

Garages that are part of the house. A garage built into the front of a house is not an outbuilding: once its overhead door is open, the next barrier is usually a light interior door to the basement. We treat that interior door as a perimeter door, often an instant one at night, and put the overhead door on a separate zone so the family can open the garage without disarming the whole house.

The Keys Are in the House

Why a Gravesend Alarm Starts With Where the Car Keys Sleep

Most modern cars cannot be driven away without their key or fob, and a thief who wants one has three options: find a car left unlocked with the fob inside, capture the fob's radio signal from outside the house, or go into the house and pick the keys up. Police statements in 2023 and 2026 describe all three in New York City. On Staten Island in July 2023, ABC7 showed a car taken from a driveway in under fifteen seconds; the local precinct commander said that in most such thefts the car had been left unlocked, likely with the fob inside. On the Upper West Side in March 2026, the 20th Precinct warned of thieves entering homes through unlocked or weak doors and windows to grab fobs, and also described "relay" attacks in which a device near the house picks up a fob's signal and replays it at the car. And in Gravesend the same month, the people who entered the East Fourth Street house at 3:40 a.m. left with two car keys and nothing else that News 12 reported.

An alarm cannot do much about a car left unlocked with a fob on the seat, and it cannot stop a radio signal from leaving the house. What it can do is make the third route, walking in for the keys, noisy and short. That depends almost entirely on how the system is set up for the hours when the family is home and asleep, which is exactly when the East Fourth Street entry happened. Many home alarms are armed only when everyone leaves, or are put in a "stay" mode at night that watches the doors and windows but switches off every interior sensor. A burglar who gets past a door that was left unlocked, or a window that was never on the alarm, then has the whole ground floor to himself.

We design Gravesend systems the other way round. First we ask where the keys end up. In most houses it is a hook or a bowl within a few steps of the door the family uses, which in driveway houses is often the side door or the door from the garage, not the front door. Then we make that spot and the path to it part of the night mode. A motion sensor in the hall that is ignored during the day becomes an instant zone at night, with no entry delay, so anyone crossing it sets off the sirens immediately. The bedrooms, the upstairs hall and the bathroom stay out of the night mode, so nobody has to remember the alarm on the way to a glass of water. Better still, the keys move to a drawer or cabinet in a room off the hall, away from the front door and windows.

The second part of the plan is the door the burglar is most likely to use: in attached houses the light interior door from the garage, in semi-detached houses the side door off the driveway, used twenty times a day and locked only some of them. Both get their own zones, instant at night.

The third part is the driveway itself. When the East Fourth Street burglars were in the house, it was a car alarm, not a house alarm, that sounded, and according to Shmira's director his patrol arrived "in literally seconds because we were already on patrol." Most households do not have a patrol around the corner at 3 a.m. An outdoor sensor or a beam across the driveway that sounds a chime in the bedroom, sends a phone alert and turns on a light gives the family the same warning a car alarm gave that night, but before anyone has tried a car door, and before anyone is inside. We set driveway detection to alert, not to call a central station, because animals and passers-by will cross it now and then.

The Car Side of the Lot

Driveways Between Brick Houses, Garages Under the Front, and Cars Parked Two Deep

Walk the residential blocks between Avenue P and Avenue X and the curb cuts tell the story. On many streets of attached houses, each house has a short driveway dropping to a garage under the front, and the cars that do not fit in the garage park on the slope. On the semi-detached blocks, a driveway the width of one car runs along the open side of each pair of houses, often with two or three cars parked one behind the other. On the larger lots north of Avenue U, gates and hedges hide the driveway entirely. Each layout gives a burglar a different approach, and each one changes what the alarm should watch.

The sunken garage under an attached house is the most common arrangement we see and the most misunderstood. From the street it looks like part of the car's world, separate from the house. In fact the garage is the house's basement: once the overhead door is up, the person inside is out of sight of the street and one interior door away from the stairs. Many families leave that interior door unlocked because they use it constantly, and many older alarms either skipped the garage altogether or put the overhead door and the interior door on one zone, so that opening the garage to take the car out meant disarming everything. We split them. The overhead door is its own zone with its own setting; the interior door is a perimeter door, armed whenever the house is armed.

Electric openers bring one more problem: remotes. A garage remote clipped to a sun visor in a car parked outside is a way into the house for anyone who breaks into the car. With an overhead door contact and an interior door contact on separate zones, the app records every opening of the garage, and the night mode treats the inner door as instant, so a garage opened with a stolen remote at 3 a.m. still meets a siren at the next door.

Side driveways have their own pattern. The windows along the driveway side of a semi-detached house are the easiest windows on the lot to reach: a car parked under them is a step stool, and the driveway itself is screened from the street by the cars parked in it. The kitchen is often on that side too, which matters because a 61st Precinct burglary on East 23rd Street near Avenue U, reported by Brooklyn Paper, came through a kitchen window overnight while the owner was away. We count every window along a driveway as reachable, whatever its height, and put contacts on all of them, with glass-break detection in a kitchen or den with several windows in a row.

North of Avenue U

The Northeast Blocks: Large Houses That Nobody Can See From the Street

The corner of Gravesend bounded by McDonald Avenue, Kings Highway, Ocean Parkway and Avenue U looks nothing like the rest of the neighborhood. Wikipedia records that affluent Sephardi Jews, mostly from Syrian families, moved into that area in the 1970s and built large Spanish Colonial-style houses. A 2015 article on 6sqft, citing The Wall Street Journal, called the community the largest Sephardic Jewish community in the United States, with about 30,000 people, and described a building boom from the 1990s in which families tore down modest houses close to the synagogues and built large single-family homes in their place. The most sought-after houses, it said, cluster on a few blocks around Avenues S, T and U between the synagogues on Ocean Parkway and a prominent yeshiva on McDonald Avenue, and there are only a few hundred of them. A Brooklyn Eagle piece placed the cluster of large houses within East 1st Street, Ocean Parkway, Kings Highway and Avenue U.

For an alarm, the defining feature of these houses is in the same 6sqft description: they cover most of their lots and are often hidden behind fences, tall trees or hedges. Privacy is the point of the planting, and it works both ways. A person standing at a side window or a rear door of one of these houses cannot be seen from the sidewalk, from the house across the street, or often from the house next door. The hedge that keeps the street out also keeps a burglar in shadow for as long as he likes.

That changes the order of priorities. On a large hidden house we put more weight on detecting someone outside before they reach the glass: a beam or an outdoor sensor at the gate and along the side paths, a light that comes on with it, and a chime indoors. The perimeter itself is usually large, with long walls of sliding doors at the back, several garage doors, a separate entrance to a finished basement and often a second kitchen. Those houses need glass-break detection in the big rooms, interior motion that covers the routes between floors, a keypad on every main level, sounders on the bedroom floor, and enough zones that an alert tells the owner exactly which side of the house it came from.

These houses also keep changing; the Brooklyn Eagle piece mentioned a one-story extension being added to a house on Avenue T according to Buildings Department filings. We fit panels with plenty of spare zones, so a new wing later means a few new sensors, not a new system.

The Old Town and the Two-Family Houses

Around Gravesend Neck Road: 1920s Houses, Two Households and the Odd Lots of a 1640s Plan

The colonial center of Gravesend, where Gravesend Neck Road meets McDonald Avenue, is now mostly ordinary residential blocks, but the old plan shows through. Wikipedia describes the original town as four quarters, each divided into ten plots, and says the grid can still be seen on maps and aerial photographs. The square of streets formed by Village Road North, Village Road East, Village Road South and Van Sicklen Street holds the Old Gravesend Cemetery and the Lady Moody–Van Sicklen House, and the lots around them meet at angles the later Brooklyn grid never quite straightened out. Back yards in this part of the neighborhood often border four or five neighbors' yards instead of one, which means more unseen approaches to the back of a house than on a regular block.

Much of the housing nearby belongs to the generation Wikipedia describes: one-family houses built in the 1920s and converted into two-family houses during the Great Depression. That history leaves traces an alarm installer runs into all the time. A house that was split may have two front doors or one front door and a shared hall; it may have two electric meters and two basements separated by a partition; and it may have two alarm systems, one put in by each household decades apart, neither of which knows about the other. When the house becomes one family again, as Wikipedia notes some have, those two systems are usually the first thing we are asked about.

Our approach depends on how the house is used now. If two households still live there, we design two areas on one panel, so the owner and the tenant each arm their own rooms with their own codes, the shared doors protect both, and the alert for an upstairs window goes to the upstairs household. If the house is becoming a single home again, we test every old cable run from both systems, keep the ones that read clean as feeds into one new panel, take out the dead keypads, dialers and bells, and patch the holes.

The few older wood houses near the town square need their own care. The Lady Moody–Van Sicklen House at 27 Gravesend Neck Road, which the 6tocelebrate landmark record dates to about 1760–1810, was designated a New York City landmark on April 12, 2016, and is described as the only known eighteenth-century stone farmhouse in Brooklyn. It is a private home and we have never worked there; we mention it because houses of that age, and the old frame houses around it, have deep cellars, irregular openings and original sash that call for contacts mounted without cutting into old wood, and because any exterior work on a designated landmark goes through the Landmarks Preservation Commission. An interior-only alarm normally keeps an owner clear of that process altogether.

South of Avenue X

Marlboro Houses, the Low Blocks by Coney Island Creek, and Where the Panel Should Live

The southern part of Gravesend is different again. Marlboro Houses, the NYCHA development completed in February 1958, fills 33.54 acres between Avenue V and Avenue X, with three sixteen-story buildings and twenty-five seven-story ones, according to Wikipedia. The neighborhood's library branch is close by at 303 Avenue X near West 2nd Street, opened in 1962 and renovated in 2001. South of the Belt Parkway lies White Sands, now two residential blocks and a Home Depot built by 2002, according to Wikipedia.

In a Marlboro Houses apartment the alarm is a tenant's own system inside one unit, and the rules come from NYCHA, not from us. We install removable wireless equipment on the apartment door and windows, a motion sensor in the main room and a keypad inside the apartment, never in the public hallway, and we do it only after the tenant has the written consent NYCHA requires for any fixture. Nothing touches the building's intercom, the hallway or the roof. When the tenant moves, the system comes down and goes with them. The same approach suits the two Mitchell-Lama co-ops Wikipedia lists in the neighborhood, where a co-op board's alteration rules take the place of NYCHA's.

The low blocks near the creek and the Shore Parkway raise a different question: water. Describing Hurricane Sandy in 2012, a National Weather Service coastal-flood impact document reported that inundation "extended inland to Avenue X into Bensonhurst, Gravesend and Sheepshead Bay, shutting down the Belt Parkway." Wikipedia's Gravesend article includes a photograph of the neighborhood in Sandy's aftermath. We do not have block-by-block flood records for Gravesend and do not claim any. What we do on houses south of Avenue X, as a matter of practice, is keep the alarm panel, its battery and its cellular communicator on the main floor rather than in the basement, so a flooded basement does not take the alarm with it, and offer water sensors at the lowest points: beside the boiler, the water heater, the floor drain and any basement door that faces the street.

Commercial Alarm Installation

Business Alarm Installation in Gravesend

Gravesend's shops line a few long avenues rather than one main street, and each has its own mix of businesses, hours and building types. These are the corridors we work on most, with what we see on each.

Avenue U

A commercial thoroughfare from Stillwell Avenue east through Homecrest, per Wikipedia, with F and N stations and the Avenue U Chinatown east of Coney Island Avenue. Shops sit under apartments, with deliveries at the back.

Kings Highway

The northern edge of the Sephardic blocks, with F and N stations. Stores have glass fronts, roll-down gates and staff on rotating shifts.

McDonald Avenue under the F

The old Gravesend Road, renamed in 1933 over the Gravesend Chamber of Commerce's objection, according to Wikipedia, and covered since 1919 by the elevated Culver Line. Wikipedia calls it a commercial corridor; the businesses under the structure often have overhead doors, side yards and a steady rumble of trains overhead.

86th Street and Stillwell Avenue

The western edge of the neighborhood, with the N at 86th Street and the Art Deco Verizon building at Meucci Triangle, 86th Street and Avenue U, which Wikipedia pictures.

Coney Island Avenue

The eastern edge in the wider definition of the neighborhood, home to the 61st Precinct station house at 2575 Coney Island Avenue, with businesses set back behind parking.

Cropsey Avenue and the Shore Parkway

The big-box area around the Home Depot built at White Sands and the stores along Cropsey Avenue near Coney Island Creek, with flat roofs, loading doors and big lots.

Rules a Gravesend business alarm has to respect

  • Fifteen-minute siren limit. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 says a building's audible burglar alarm must be capable of shutting off, and must shut off, within fifteen minutes of activation. We program every siren to stop well inside that limit, which also spares the tenants who usually live above the store.
  • Licensed installers. New York State's Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers; its licensing page describes 81 hours of qualifying education and an exam. Ask anyone who offers to wire your store for the license number. Ours is #12000287431.
  • Fire alarms are a separate system. Where a building has a fire alarm panel, our burglar alarm does not touch it, and changes to fire systems go through Gravesend fire alarm installation under the fire code's own rules.
  • Smoke and carbon monoxide in rentals. HPD's detector rules require smoke detectors in multiple dwellings and carbon monoxide and gas detectors in one- and two-family houses that are not owner-occupied and have fuel-burning appliances or gas piping. Many store owners here also rent out the apartment upstairs, and we check those detectors while we are in the building.
  • No permit claimed. We looked for a New York City permit or registration rule for burglar alarms and did not find one; the alarm-permit ordinances our search turned up belonged to towns outside the city. If that changes, we will say so here.
Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Gravesend: Questions Answered

Car keys, fobs and the driveway

In March 2026 two people walked into a house on East Fourth Street at 3:40 a.m. and left with two car keys while the owner was home. What should be armed while we sleep so that cannot happen quietly?

Everything on the ground floor that a person has to cross to reach the keys, and nothing on the floor where you sleep. That means the outside doors, the door from the garage and the reachable windows armed, plus the hall motion sensor and any sensor covering the spot where keys are kept switched to instant, with no entry delay, in the night mode. The bedrooms and the upstairs hall stay off, so nobody trips it going to the bathroom. With that setup, anyone who gets through an unlocked door sets off the sirens within a step or two of entering, long before reaching a key bowl.

Our key fobs hang on a hook right beside the front door. Where should they live once the alarm goes in, and can a sensor watch that spot?

Away from the front door and away from any window, ideally in a drawer or a small cabinet in a room off the hall, which is also what the NYPD's 20th Precinct advised in its March 2026 warning about thieves entering homes for fobs. We can put a small contact on that drawer or cabinet door and make it its own zone, armed in both the night and away modes, so opening it at the wrong time sets off the alarm even if the person somehow got past everything else. If the hook has to stay, we aim the hall motion sensor so the hook is in the middle of its view.

We have read that thieves can copy a fob's signal from outside the house. Can a burglar alarm do anything against a car stolen that way?

Very little directly, and we would rather say so than sell you something. A relay device works on the radio signal and never needs to open a door or window, so the house alarm has nothing to detect. What helps is a signal-blocking pouch or metal box for the fobs, which the 20th Precinct also recommended, and a driveway sensor or beam that alerts you when someone walks up to the car at night. That alert is the one part of the system that sees a relay theft in progress, because the person holding the device has to stand near your house and the car.

Our car's own alarm went off at 3 a.m. last winter and nobody in the house woke up. Can the house alarm tell us what is happening in the driveway?

Yes. An outdoor sensor or beam on the driveway can sound a chime on the bedroom floor, send a notification to your phones and switch on a driveway light, all without calling anyone. Unlike a car alarm, it reacts when a person walks onto the driveway, not after a car door is tried, so you hear about it earlier. We set it to alert rather than to dispatch, because cats, delivery drivers and neighbors crossing the drive would otherwise create false alarms.

Can arming the alarm for the night also check that the garage door and the driveway gate are actually shut?

Yes, if they have sensors. With a contact on the overhead garage door and one on the gate, the keypad and the app list them as open when you go to arm, and you can close them first or arm with them left out on purpose. If the garage has a smart controller, we can link it so you can close the garage from your phone after the alarm warns you, without walking downstairs.

Windows, doors and the hours burglars pick

A 61st Precinct burglar got into a house on East 23rd Street near Avenue U through the kitchen window overnight while the owner was out. Is a kitchen window at the back a bigger risk than the front windows?

Usually it is. Kitchen windows tend to face a back yard or a driveway, out of sight of the street, they are often left unlocked for air, and in many houses the counter or a chair under them makes it easy to climb in. We put a contact on every kitchen window, and where a kitchen has a run of windows or a glass door to the yard, a glass-break detector too, so a broken pane is caught as quickly as an opened sash.

One Gravesend break-in, at a two-family house near Avenue Z, happened at 12:30 in the afternoon. Is a daytime burglary different from a night one as far as the alarm is concerned?

The equipment is the same; the habits are different. A night burglary usually finds a family home with the alarm in a night mode, while a daytime burglary finds an empty house that someone forgot to arm because they were only going out for an hour. We set up an automatic reminder that notifies you if the house is empty and the alarm is still off, and an app button that arms it from wherever you are, so the midday trip to the store does not leave the house open.

A man in the 62nd Precinct came home to find his front door pried open and was beaten by the two men still inside. Can our alarm warn us before we walk in on a burglar?

Yes, and that is one of the most important things it does. If the alarm trips while you are out, your phone gets the alert first, with the zone that tripped, and if you have a camera or a video doorbell, a clip as well. We tell every family the same thing: if the alert shows a door or interior zone and you did not expect anyone, do not go in; stay outside, call 911 and wait. A siren already sounding when you pull into the driveway is another warning not to walk in.

Big houses and houses that changed

Our house north of Avenue U was built new and sits behind a tall hedge and a fence. Does a house nobody can see from the street need anything a smaller house does not?

It needs more attention outside the walls. The hedge that gives you privacy also hides anyone working at a side window, so we add detection at the gate and along the side paths, a light that comes on with it, and glass-break detection inside the big rooms at the back. Large houses also need more control points: a keypad on each main floor, sounders where the bedrooms are, and zones named by side and room so an alert tells you exactly where to look.

Our house still has two front doors and two old keypads from the years it was a two-family. Can one new panel take over both halves?

Usually yes. We test the wiring from both old systems; every run that reads clean feeds the new panel, so contacts already in the walls keep working, and the dead keypads, dialers and bells come out. If the house is now one family, the second front door becomes an ordinary armed door. If two households still live there, the new panel runs two areas with separate codes instead of two unrelated systems.

Stores on the avenues

Our store takes deliveries all day through a back door onto the lot. Can that door chime inside during business hours and become a full alarm zone at closing?

Yes. The rear door can chime at the counter, or send a phone alert to the manager, every time it opens while the store is open, so a door propped open for a delivery does not stay open unnoticed. The moment the store is armed at closing, the same door becomes an alarm zone with a short or no entry delay. If someone tries to arm with the back door open, the keypad says so before the last person leaves.

Our business keeps customers' car keys in a cabinet overnight. Can that cabinet have its own zone that alerts even when the rest of the alarm is off?

Yes. A contact on the key cabinet or key box can be set as a 24-hour zone that alerts the owner whenever it opens outside the hours you choose, whether or not the rest of the building is armed. During the day it can simply log each opening in the app, so you can see when it was opened and with whose code the building was last disarmed.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Gravesend

Google's AI Overview could not be loaded from the environment this page was written in, so nothing in quotation marks below is copied from it. Each quoted line paraphrases the kind of claim that automated answers, cost guides and contractor listings on sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr commonly repeat about home alarms, and each is checked against what Gravesend's houses and its police reports actually show.

“Burglars want jewelry and electronics, so protect the bedroom and the living room.”

Jewelry and electronics are still taken; the eight Gravesend burglaries News 12 reported in the spring of 2015 involved cash, electronics and jewelry. But a growing share of break-ins are about the car. In March 2026 the people who entered a house on East Fourth Street took two car keys, and News 12 reported no other loss.

The NYPD's 20th Precinct, warning Manhattan residents the same month, described thieves entering homes specifically to pick up key fobs, and advised keeping fobs away from front doors and windows.

So in a neighborhood where almost every house has a car outside, the front hall and the key spot deserve at least as much attention as the jewelry box, and the night mode has to cover them.

“Arm the alarm when you leave; at night, stay mode is enough.”

A typical stay mode watches the doors and windows and turns off every interior sensor. That works if every door is locked and every reachable window is on the system. It fails the moment one of them is not.

The East Fourth Street entry happened at 3:40 a.m. with the owner inside. A night mode that keeps the hall and the key spot instant, while leaving the bedroom floor off, closes exactly the gap a stay mode leaves open.

Most panels we install can run separate stay and night modes; many older systems were never programmed to use them.

“A home alarm protects your car too.”

Only indirectly. A house alarm protects the keys and the house, and a driveway sensor can warn you that someone is near the car. It cannot stop a car left unlocked with the fob inside being driven off, which a Staten Island precinct commander told ABC7 in 2023 was the situation in most of the driveway thefts his officers saw.

It also cannot detect a relay attack, in which a device near the house picks up a fob's signal; the 20th Precinct suggested signal-blocking pouches or metal boxes for that.

An honest plan combines the alarm with locked cars, fobs stored away from the door in a blocking pouch, and a driveway alert.

“Gravesend is a quiet residential area, so a basic kit will do.”

Wikipedia's history of the neighborhood does say crime remained relatively low, citing The New York Times, but in the same sentence it notes high rates of car theft. Quiet streets of houses with cars outside are exactly what car thieves look for.

A basic kit can be fine for an apartment. A semi-detached house with a side driveway, a garage under the front and two cars outside has more openings than a kit's sensor count assumes, plus a garage door, an inner door and a driveway to cover.

Our own Brooklyn list starts at $600 for a basic system, and that is the right answer for some homes here; it is the house, not the neighborhood's reputation, that decides.

“Outdoor motion sensors cause too many false alarms to be worth it.”

Outdoor sensors that call a central station can cause false alarms, which is why we do not set them up that way. On a driveway we set them to chime, notify and switch on a light, and leave dispatch to the door, window and interior zones.

Placement matters as much as settings. Mounted high and aimed down a driveway, or replaced by a beam across the driveway entrance, they see a person and ignore passing cars. Masked properly, they ignore the next driveway too.

The payoff is an early warning before anyone touches a door, which no indoor sensor can give.

“You need a city permit before an alarm can be monitored.”

Some towns outside New York City do require alarm permits or registration, and our search for alarm-permit rules turned up several of them in Westchester, Rockland and the Hudson Valley. We did not find a citywide burglar-alarm permit rule for New York City.

What the city does have is a noise rule: Administrative Code § 24-238 requires an audible building alarm to shut itself off within fifteen minutes. We program every siren to stop well before that.

If that changes we will update this page; for now, a Gravesend homeowner does not need to file anything with the city to have a monitored alarm.

“Anyone handy can install an alarm.”

An owner may install a kit in his own home. A company that installs alarms for other people in New York State needs a license from the Department of State, whose page describes 81 hours of qualifying education, an exam and fingerprinting.

The license matters in practical ways. Insurers ask for it when a monitored alarm supports a claim or a discount, and a licensed installer has to issue identification to the employees who come into your house.

So the first question to put to anyone offering to wire your house or store is what their license number is. Ours, #12000287431, is on each written quote.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Survey the Car Side First, Then Follow the Keys

This describes how we survey a Gravesend house with a driveway in general; it is not an account of any particular job. The technician starts at the curb and walks up the driveway rather than to the front door, because the driveway is where a burglar after a car will start too. He notes how far the driveway runs, whether the garage is under the house or at the back, how its door closes, whether the remote lives in the car, which windows a person could reach from a car roof or a fence, and where light falls at night.

Inside, he asks one question before any other: where do the keys go when you come home? Then he walks that route himself, from the garage door or the side door to the hook or the bowl, and from there to the stairs. Whatever he crosses on that walk is what the night mode has to cover. The rest of the survey, the reachable windows, the basement and any old wiring, fills in around that line. Often the single most useful change is moving the keys.

What does an alarm for a Gravesend semi-detached house with a side driveway and a garage under the house cost on your Brooklyn list?

Do you also work in Marlboro Houses and the Mitchell-Lama co-ops, or only in private houses?
+

We work in both. In a NYCHA apartment the alarm is a removable wireless system that stays inside the unit and goes in only after the tenant has the written consent NYCHA requires. In a co-op we follow the board's alteration rules. The Brooklyn price list is the same in all of them.

Can a driveway camera read the license plates of cars that stop outside our house?
+

Some cameras can, but a plate reader needs a narrow angle, the right height and good light, so a general driveway camera usually cannot. We point plate cameras only at your own driveway or gate, not at neighbors' houses, and we explain what the recordings will and will not show before anything is installed.

Our house was broken into last week. How quickly can you put an alarm in?
+

Usually within a few days of the visit, and sooner for a basic system. We often install a smaller set of devices on the doors and the hall first, so the house is protected the same week, and then return to finish the windows and the garage once the full quote is agreed.

We host big family gatherings with people in and out all evening. Can the alarm stay useful without someone guarding the keypad?
+

Yes. The doors guests use can be left off for the evening while the bedrooms, the garage and any room holding valuables stay armed, and the keypad can chime each time an outside door opens. When the last guest leaves, one button arms the whole house for the night.

Our car already has its own app that warns us. Why pay for a house alarm too?
+

The car app protects the car once someone tries to take it. The house alarm protects what makes that easy: the keys inside the house, the door from the garage and the windows a thief uses to reach them. One warns you after the fact, the other is meant to stop the visit before it starts.

Our new house has a double-height entrance hall with a curved stair. Can one motion sensor cover it?
+

Usually one sensor mounted on the side wall at the usual height covers the floor of the hall and the bottom of the stair, which is the path anyone takes. We avoid aiming it up into the open space, where warm air near the ceiling and a chandelier can cause false alarms, and add a second sensor on the landing only if bedrooms open onto it.

Will snow, rain and blowing leaves set off the driveway sensor in winter?
+

Occasionally, which is one reason we set driveway sensors to chime and notify rather than call anyone. Beams can be set to need a longer interruption, outdoor motion sensors need two separate detections before they react, and both are aimed away from bushes and trees that move in the wind. If one starts alerting in bad weather, we adjust it on the return visit.

Can the alarm turn lamps on and off on a schedule while we are away, so the house looks lived in?
+

Yes. Smart plugs and switches tied to the alarm can follow a schedule or vary the times a little each day, and a lamp near the front window can come on whenever the system is armed after dark. Nothing has to be wired into the walls for this.

Two or three cars are parked one behind another in our driveway every night. Will an outdoor sensor still see someone walking between them?
+

Not reliably if it is mounted low, because the cars block its view. We mount outdoor sensors higher and aim them down the length of the driveway, or place a beam across the entrance from the sidewalk, so a person is seen walking in no matter where the cars are parked.
