Why South Slope Properties Need Security Camera Repair

South Slope is the southern end of Park Slope, usually drawn from about Ninth or Tenth Street down toward the Prospect Expressway, between Fourth Avenue and Prospect Park West. Its streets are mostly two- and three-story brick row houses, a scattering of older frame houses, walk-ups over storefronts on Fifth and Seventh Avenues, a converted clock factory on Seventh Avenue, and the newer condo and rental buildings that went up on Fourth Avenue after the 2005 rezoning. That is the background. For a camera repair the question that matters is simpler: what is in front of each camera at the hours when something actually goes wrong, and is the system still built for that view?

The city's own records answer the first half. We drew an outline around the blocks from 10th to 19th Street between Fourth Avenue and Prospect Park West and pulled the NYPD's complaint records for 2021 through 2025. Inside it there were 295 petit larcenies at apartment houses and houses, the category that takes in most packages lifted from stoops and vestibules, plus 166 grand larcenies at the same kinds of buildings. Of those 295 petit larcenies, 143 had an earliest start time between noon and 6 p.m. and 79 between 6 a.m. and noon. Only 19 began between midnight and 6 a.m. In other words, roughly three of every four thefts from South Slope homes in that file started in daylight, while people were at work, and the camera on the stoop was looking out at a bright street.

That matters because daylight is where many residential cameras are weakest, not night. A camera inside a vestibule, behind the glass of a front door or tucked under the stoop has to look from shade toward sun. Unless it is set up for that contrast, it exposes for the bright sidewalk and leaves the face on the steps in shadow, or exposes for the face and blows out everything behind it. On blocks where the long side of the street gets direct afternoon sun, the problem is worst during exactly the hours the records point to. Many of the "the camera is fine, the footage is useless" calls we get from row houses here are this, and they are fixed with a change of angle, a setting, a lens or a camera better suited to backlight, not a whole new system.

The second half is darkness. NYC 311 holds 449 reports of a single street light out and 18 reports of several lights out inside the same outline between January 2021 and November 2026, with 100 in 2022 alone and 69 so far in 2026. The streets with the most reports were 18th Street with 43, 12th Street with 33, 14th Street with 32 and Prospect Avenue with 22. The same file shows lamps "cycling" on and off, lamps burning through the day, and seven lampposts knocked down this year. A camera whose night picture was tuned under a working street lamp behaves very differently when that lamp dies or starts flickering: it may switch to infrared and lose color, flip back and forth between modes all night, or fill the phone with motion alerts from the change in light. Owners read that as a broken camera. Sometimes it is; often it is the light, and the repair is a setting, an added light of your own, or a report to 311 for the lamp.

Power is the third thread. In early February 2026, melting snow and road salt damaged underground equipment and knocked out power to more than 2,000 Con Edison customers in Park Slope and Boerum Hill; FOX 5 reported more than 1,900 still out in Park Slope on February 1, and ABC7 counted 562 households in the dark on February 3. "Families are unable to make arrangements because the estimated time of restoration keeps moving," Council Member Shahana Hanif told ABC7. The reports name Park Slope rather than particular South Slope blocks, so we do not claim which streets lost power. But when a recorder, a PoE switch and a stack of camera power supplies go down hard and come back on a surge, some of them do not come back right: a drive that will not mount, a clock reset to the factory date, a switch port that no longer powers its camera. Those are repair calls, and they tend to arrive weeks later, the first time someone tries to play something back.

So a camera repair in South Slope is not only "make the dark camera light up again." It is getting each camera to produce a usable picture at the hours the block's own records say matter, getting the recorder to save that picture at enough quality to use, and setting the system to warn you when it stops, before the day you need it. Many owners pair the repair with a check of the door buzzer or video intercom at the same entrance, because on these row houses and walk-ups the same front door carries both systems.

Brick row houses: the vestibule that looks into the sun

The most common camera on a South Slope row house watches the stoop and the front door, and the most common complaint about it is daytime footage. The camera often sits inside the vestibule or just above the inner door, looking out through glass at the steps and the street. In the morning on one side of a street and in the afternoon on the other, the street behind the visitor is many times brighter than the visitor's face. A camera without strong wide dynamic range picks one or the other, and most default to the bright background. The fix depends on what is there. Sometimes it is a menu setting the installer never turned on; sometimes the camera has to move outside the glass, a foot higher or a foot to the side; sometimes the lens is too wide and the face is a handful of pixels. We test with the sun where it actually is during the hours the street's thefts happen, not during a cloudy morning visit, and we show you a test clip before we leave.

Frame houses and two-families: water rides the cable

Brownstoner has written about the wood houses that survive among the brick on the blocks below Fifth Avenue, including a simplified Italianate house at 199 17th Street that was standing by 1880. On houses like these, a camera is usually screwed through clapboard or siding into wood, and its cable goes through a hole beside it. If that hole was not sealed with a drip loop below it, rain follows the cable into the wall and into the connector, and the camera fails months later with green corrosion inside a junction box. When we repair one, we check the wall around the hole as well as the camera, seal what we open, and replace the connector with a weatherproof one rather than taping the old one.

Many of these houses are two-families. The recorder is often in the cellar of the garden unit and the cameras are powered from whichever outlet was closest during the install, which can be the tenant's. When a tenant moves and their electric account closes, or someone flips a breaker in the wrong apartment, half the cameras go dark. Part of a two-family repair is moving the system's power onto the owner's circuit and putting the recorder somewhere the owner can reach without knocking on a tenant's door.

Walk-ups over storefronts: two systems, one doorway

On Fifth and Seventh Avenues the typical building has a shop at street level and two or three floors of apartments above, reached through a narrow street door beside the shop window. The shop usually has its own cameras, bought by the tenant and recorded in the back. The landlord may have a separate camera on the residential door and in the stair hall, recorded on a different box in the cellar. When one fails, nobody is sure whose it is. We start these repairs by sorting out which cameras belong to which recorder and who owns each, label everything, and only then fix what is broken, so the next call does not start from zero.

Fourth Avenue condos and rentals: big systems, scattered logins

After the 2005 rezoning, Fourth Avenue filled with mid-rise buildings such as 554 Fourth Avenue, a 40-residence glass condominium, 575 Fourth Avenue and 599 4th Avenue between 17th and 18th Streets, all listed with video intercom entry. Their camera systems are usually network cameras on a PoE switch, recorded on an NVR in a telecom room, sometimes tied into the intercom so residents see the lobby camera on their apartment screen. The hardware is fine; the problem is that the passwords, the cloud account and the network settings often sit with the original installer, the sponsor or a building manager who has since changed. A repair there begins with getting the board or the managing agent into its own system.

These buildings are also concrete. That makes cable runs stable but means a dead camera in a finished lobby ceiling is reached through an access panel or a fixture opening, not a new hole, and that any work in the garage, roof or riser follows the building's contractor rules: insurance certificate, freight elevator booking, sign-in at the desk.

Converted factory co-ops: long halls, small faces

The Brooklyn Public Library describes the Ansonia Clock Company's Brooklyn plant as filling the block from Seventh Avenue and 12th Street to Eighth Avenue and 13th Street, "said to be the largest clock factory in the world," and now a 71-unit co-op. Buildings like that have corridors far longer than a row house is deep. A single wide camera at the end of a long hall gives the board a neat view of the whole corridor and a face the size of a fingernail at the far end. The repair there is often a lens choice rather than a new system: a varifocal camera zoomed to the stretch that matters, or a second camera at the midpoint. Because the system is the co-op's, we work through the managing agent and the board's chosen contact, and we leave a written note of what was changed.

Landmarked blocks: back in the same joint

On blocks inside the 2012 historic district extension, between about 7th and 15th Streets near Seventh and Eighth Avenues, a camera on the front of a house is a fixture the Landmarks Preservation Commission cares about. Its permit guidebook treats ordinary maintenance and repair of safety equipment as work that does not need a permit, while new installations are expected to be small, well spaced, fixed at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metal, and never drilled through cast iron or decorative stone. In practice that means a replacement goes back in the same position on the same anchors, at about the same size, with any cable kept tight to a joint. If the right repair is to move the camera or add one, we tell you that it is a different kind of job and what the guidebook asks for, before anything is drilled.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Why We Ask South Slope Owners for Two Screenshots

This is how our technicians approach the most common South Slope call, written as our working method rather than a story about any one house. The owner says the stoop camera "doesn't work." Before booking, we ask for two screenshots from the same camera: one from the middle of a sunny afternoon and one from after dark. About half the time the two pictures tell the whole story. If the afternoon frame shows a black figure against a white street and the night frame is fine, the camera is working and is set up for the wrong light; that is usually a setting, an angle or a lens. If the afternoon frame is fine and the night frame is grey grain, we look at the street lamp outside before we look at the camera, because 311 shows these blocks losing lamps often. If both frames are bad, or one is missing, the fault is in the camera, the cable, the power or the recorder, and the visit is planned around testing those in order.

The screenshots also tell us what the recorder is saving, which is not always what the live view shows. We ask for a recorded clip as well as a live frame, because a recorder set to save a low-quality stream can make a good camera look broken. None of this costs anything, and it means the technician arrives with the right part, or tells you before the visit that no part is needed.

Fault by Fault

Afternoon Glare, Dead Street Lamps, Outage Damage, Worn Drives and Saved Quality: How South Slope Camera Systems Go Wrong

Afternoon glare: the picture fails when the records say it matters most

A camera's sensor handles a limited range of brightness at once. Point it from a shaded vestibule toward a sunlit street and it has to choose. Entry-level cameras and many older ones choose the background, so the street looks perfect and the person on the stoop is a shape. Wide dynamic range settings take several exposures and combine them; some cameras do this well, some badly, and many ship with it switched off. Backlight compensation and region-of-interest exposure tell the camera which part of the frame to expose for. A good repair tries those first, then changes the angle so the camera sees the visitor against the brick of the stoop wall rather than the bright street, and only then replaces the camera with one built for backlit doors.

Glare has a second form: the low sun hitting the lens directly, or reflecting off the glass of the door the camera looks through. A camera behind glass also sees itself, its own infrared at night and the room behind it by day. The answers are a short hood, a few degrees of tilt, or moving the camera outside the glass. We test at the time of day the problem happens, which on many South Slope blocks means booking the visit in the afternoon.

Dead and cycling street lamps: when the camera did not change, the block did

At night most cameras either switch to infrared, losing color, or keep color by using whatever light is there. A camera tuned while the street lamp outside was working can fall apart when that lamp goes out: color models turn to noise, infrared models light up only the first few feet and the railing nearest the lens. A lamp that is cycling on and off, a condition 311 records on these blocks every year, is worse, because the camera flips between day and night mode over and over, refocuses, and fires motion alerts each time the light jumps. On the phone that looks like a broken camera. We check the scene before the camera: if the lamp is the cause, we tell you, set the camera to ride through the change, and suggest a small light of your own on a timer or a motion sensor so the picture does not depend on the city's lamp.

Infrared has its own South Slope trap. Stoops come with iron railings, areaway gates and window guards close to the camera. Infrared bouncing off a rail a foot from the lens washes out the whole frame. Moving the camera a few inches or turning down its infrared and adding a separate illuminator fixes it, and neither needs a new camera.

Outage damage: what does not come back after the power does

When power fails and returns, a recorder can come back with its hard drive unmounted, its settings lost or its clock reset. A PoE switch can lose a port, so one camera stays dark while the others work. Small plug-in camera supplies can fail outright. Battery backups, if there were any, may have drained in the first hour of a multi-day event like February 2026's and then cut out on the return. None of this always shows on the live view, so the first sign is often a playback gap discovered weeks later. After an outage we check every part in the chain, set the recorder's time to sync automatically, and size any backup to what it actually has to carry and for how long, rather than adding one that dies in ten minutes.

Worn drives: the part that works every minute of every day

A recorder writing four or eight cameras around the clock wears its drive out. When it starts to fail, the recorder may still show live video while saving nothing, or saving in pieces. Many recorders can warn about it, but only if alerts were set up and go to a phone someone reads. On a cellar shelf in a row house, nobody sees the warning light. A drive swap is one of the most common South Slope repairs and one of the cheapest; the important part is checking what can be copied off the old drive first and setting alerts so the next one does not fail in silence.

Saved quality: sharp live, useless in playback

Many systems show a high-resolution main stream when you watch live but record a lower one to save space, or record only on motion with a sensitivity set so low that the first seconds of a visit are lost. The camera is fine; the recorder was set up to keep the wrong thing. For a block where most thefts are in the afternoon, a reasonable setup keeps full quality on the door cameras around the clock, uses motion-based recording only where it is reliable, and sizes the drive so a week or more is kept even at full quality. We check those settings on every repair, because they are the difference between a clip that identifies someone and one that does not.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Repair in South Slope: Questions Answered

Daytime footage, when most thefts here begin

The police records for our blocks show most thefts from homes starting between noon and 6 p.m., and our afternoon recordings are the worst footage we have. Why would a camera do worse at midday than at night?

Because midday is when the brightness gap between your stoop and the street is biggest, and a camera can only expose for one of them at a time unless it handles wide dynamic range well. At night the scene is evenly dark and the camera lights it itself, so it can look better. The fix is usually to turn on and tune the camera's backlight handling, aim it so the visitor is seen against the stoop or the door rather than the sunlit street, or replace the camera with one designed for backlit entrances. We test at the time of day the problem appears.

A box was taken from our stoop around 2 p.m., and the clip shows the person only from behind, walking away down the steps. Can our camera be changed so it catches people coming up?

Usually, yes. A camera mounted high over the door and aimed straight down the stoop sees the tops of heads on the way up and backs on the way down. Moving it lower and to the side, so it looks across the top step at the height of a face, or adding a second small camera at the door looking outward, catches people as they arrive. On a brick stoop that is often a short cable extension along a mortar joint. We would also check that the recorder is saving the full-quality stream from that camera, because a face at the top step is only a small part of the frame.

Night pictures and street lighting

The street lamp in front of our house on 18th Street has been out for weeks, and our camera's night picture went from color to grainy black and white. Is the camera failing, and should we report the lamp?

Most likely the camera is doing exactly what it was set to do: it lost the light it used for color and switched to infrared, which covers a shorter distance and shows no color. Report the lamp to 311; 18th Street had more street-light-out reports than any other street in our outline between 2021 and 2026, so you will not be the first. In the meantime, and for the next time it happens, a small fixture of your own over the stoop on a timer or motion sensor makes the night picture independent of the city's lamp. If the picture is still poor with the lamp working, then we look at the camera.

The lamp on our corner keeps blinking on and off all night. Our camera flips between color and black and white, and our phones get an alert every few minutes. Can the camera ignore it?

Yes. Each time the lamp drops out, the camera decides it is dark, switches mode, refocuses and sees the change as motion. We can set the camera to hold its night mode on a schedule instead of switching on light level, slow down how quickly it changes modes, and use smart detection that triggers on a person rather than any change in brightness. Report the cycling lamp to 311 as well; it is a known complaint type, and the city logged several on these blocks every year from 2021 on.

Power, recorders and the February outage

After the February 2026 outage our cameras came back, except one. The switch in the cellar shows that camera's port lit amber instead of green. Did the outage damage the switch?

Possibly, but an amber light on a PoE switch can also mean the port is linked at a lower speed, or the camera on that run is drawing more power than the port will give after a restart. We test the port with a known-good camera and a cable tester, and test the camera on a different port. If the port is bad, the switch is replaced; if the camera or its connector failed, that is what we replace. Switches and small supplies are common casualties of hard power returns, so we check the rest of the chain at the same time.

Our recorder is in the cellar under the stoop, and it only showed us it was not recording when we went to find something from three weeks ago. How do we make sure we find out in days, not weeks?

Turn on the recorder's own health alerts, which most models have: video loss on any channel, drive error, drive full, and recording stopped. Send them to an app or email you check, not to an address an installer set up years ago. We set those up on every repair, then trigger each one in front of you so you see what the alert looks like. A monthly two-minute check, picking a random hour from last week and playing it back, catches anything the alerts miss.

Row houses, condos and co-ops

Our house is on 13th Street just outside the historic district line, but the houses across the street are inside it. Which rules apply to replacing our front camera?

The district boundary is drawn by lot, so what matters is whether your own building is inside it, not the block. You can check your address on the Landmarks Preservation Commission's online map. If your house is outside the district, a camera replacement is governed by ordinary city rules and your own judgment. If it is inside, a replacement in kind in the same position is ordinary repair under the LPC guidebook. Either way, we put the new camera into a mortar joint rather than the stone, because that is good practice on any old facade.

In our Fourth Avenue condo, each apartment's intercom screen shows the lobby camera. Since a contractor worked in the building, our unit's screen shows a blank square while our neighbors' screens are fine. Is that the camera or our apartment?

If other apartments still see the lobby camera, the camera itself is working, so the fault is somewhere between the building's network and your screen: a cable or connector in your unit's wall, a port on the building switch, or your screen's settings. We test the feed at the switch, at your apartment's jack and at the screen. Because the screen and the wiring inside your walls may be yours while the switch is the building's, we confirm with the managing agent who covers which part before we replace anything.

Our co-op is in a converted factory, and the cameras at the ends of our long corridors show faces at the far end too small to recognize. Is that a repair or a whole new system?

Usually a repair. A wide-angle camera at the end of a long corridor spreads its pixels over the whole hall, so a face forty feet away is tiny. Swapping to a varifocal camera and zooming it to the stretch where people actually pass, usually the stair doors and the elevator, or adding a camera at the midpoint, solves it without touching the recorder. We check the recorder's free channels and licenses first, so the board knows whether a midpoint camera fits on the current system.

Shops and the street

Our camera watches the bike we lock in the front areaway. The bike was taken, and the clip shows the lock being cut but only a pair of hands. Can the camera be set up to see who did it?

Yes, with a change of angle rather than a new camera in most cases. A camera aimed straight down at the bike gets hands and the tops of heads. Re-aiming it to look across the areaway toward the gate, at about the height of someone bending over, shows faces as they come in and leave. A second, wider camera covering the gate and the sidewalk gives context. NYPD records for our outline show 242 petit larcenies on the street over five years, so a view that reaches the gate is worth more than one that only sees the bike.

A new shelving unit in our Fifth Avenue grocery now blocks half the view from our aisle camera, and that is the aisle where things go missing during the day. Do we move the camera or replace it?

Usually move it. In a drop ceiling, shifting a dome two or three tiles, or replacing it with a varifocal dome aimed down the aisle from the end, restores the view without new cable beyond a short extension. If the aisle is long, a narrower lens at the far end does more than a wide one in the middle. Because shoplifting at chain stores, groceries and drug stores is the most common theft in the NYPD records for these blocks, we also check how many days the recorder keeps, so a loss found at inventory can still be looked up.

Types of Security Cameras and Recorders We Repair

We repair systems from any manufacturer and any installer. These are the kinds of equipment we meet most on South Slope row houses, condos and avenue shops, and what usually goes wrong with each.

Coax and HD-over-coax cameras

Older systems in many row houses, wired with coaxial cable to a DVR in the cellar. Connectors corrode at the facade, and power often comes from a separate multi-camera supply that fails first. Often upgraded in place to higher resolution on the same cable.

Network (IP) cameras

Standard in Fourth Avenue condos and newer shop systems, powered and connected by one cable to a PoE switch or NVR. Faults are usually a switch port, a connector, a password or an address change after a network update.

DVRs, NVRs and hybrid recorders

The box that keeps the video. Drives wear out, settings get lost in outages, firmware updates break phone viewing. Hybrid recorders let a row house keep its coax cameras while adding network ones.

Varifocal domes and bullets

Cameras whose lens can be zoomed after mounting. The answer for long co-op corridors, deep store aisles and stoops where a face must fill more of the frame.

Wide dynamic range door cameras

Cameras built to see a face in shade against a bright street. The usual replacement for vestibule and stoop cameras that fail in South Slope's afternoon light.

Pan-tilt and 360-degree cameras

Motorized or fisheye cameras on corners, roofs and large store floors. Motors, presets and dewy domes are what we fix most often.

Wi-Fi, battery and cube cameras

Consumer cameras on stoops, in apartments and in back yards. Signal through brick, batteries in winter, and app accounts tied to someone who moved away are the usual problems.

Video door stations

Intercom stations with built-in cameras at condo lobbies and row-house doors, sometimes recorded by the main system, sometimes not. We check which before promising footage from them.

PoE switches, supplies and battery backups

The parts that power everything and fail when the power misbehaves. Checked on every outage-related call and sized properly when replaced.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in South Slope

Search summaries and home-service sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr give general advice about camera repair. Some of it is right everywhere. Some of it misses how cameras fail on these particular blocks. Here are the claims we hear repeated most often, and what we find on South Slope repairs.

"Night vision is the hardest test for a security camera"

For a camera in an open yard, maybe. For a South Slope stoop or vestibule camera, daylight is often harder. The NYPD's records for our outline show 222 of 295 thefts at homes starting between 6 a.m. and 6 p.m., most of them in the afternoon, when a camera looking from shade toward a bright street struggles most.

A camera that looks excellent at night can still fail the test that matters on these blocks: showing the face of someone standing on a stoop with the sun behind them. Reviews and spec sheets that focus on night range do not tell you that.

So on every repair we check the day picture at the hour it is worst, not only the night picture. Often the fix is a setting or an angle the installer never revisited after the first sunny afternoon.

"If the night picture goes bad, the infrared LEDs are failing"

Sometimes they are. But on a block where 311 records street lamps going out every year, with 69 reports of a single lamp out in our outline in 2026 so far, a sudden change in the night picture is often the scene, not the camera. A color camera that relied on the lamp outside will turn grainy the night the lamp dies.

The quick test is to look at the camera's picture on a night when the lamp is known to be working, or to light the scene with a flashlight from a distance. If the picture recovers, the LEDs are fine.

Replacing a camera because a street lamp died costs money and does not fix anything when the next lamp goes out. A small light of your own on a sensor, or a camera set for the darkness the block actually gets, does.

"A battery backup keeps your cameras running through an outage"

A small backup keeps a recorder alive through a flicker or a few minutes. It does not carry a recorder, a PoE switch and eight cameras through an outage that lasts days, as parts of Park Slope saw in February 2026, when hundreds of households were still without power on the third day.

The honest goal for most homes is to ride through short drops without a restart, shut down cleanly on a long one so the drive is not damaged, and restart properly when power returns. That needs a backup sized to the load and set to tell the recorder to shut down.

After a long outage, the repair is checking what did not come back: a drive, a switch port, a supply, the clock. We check them all rather than assuming the live view means everything is fine.

"Most camera problems are fixed by rebooting the recorder"

A restart does clear some software faults. But on a recorder with a failing drive, or one where you need last week's footage, a restart can make things worse: some recorders come back asking to format a drive they can no longer read, and the logs that show when recording stopped can be lost.

If you need footage, leave the recorder running and call. If you do not, a single restart is fine to try, but write down what the screen said before you do it.

If the same fault returns after a restart, that is the recorder telling you something specific, usually about its drive or its power. That is the moment to have it looked at.

"Any handyman or electrician can fix a security camera system"

In New York, companies that install or service security and CCTV systems for other people are licensed by the Department of State. An electrician can fix the outlet the recorder plugs into; the cameras, cabling, recorder and network are low-voltage work.

The skills are different, too. Most South Slope camera faults are found with a cable tester, a PoE tester, the recorder's logs and a look at the light at the right time of day, not with a voltage meter on the house wiring.

If a handyman has already been in, tell us what was changed. It saves time, and it is common for a well-meant fix to have moved the problem rather than solved it.

"Landmark rules make any camera work on a historic block a permit job"

Not for a repair. The LPC's permit guidebook treats ordinary maintenance and repair of health, safety and utility equipment as work that does not need a permit. Replacing a dead camera in the same place is that kind of work.

What the guidebook governs is new or moved equipment: cameras well spaced, no more than two per 25 feet of frontage on a small building, fixed into mortar joints or plain surfaces, never through cast iron or decorative stone, with very little visible conduit.

So a repair on a historic block is usually simple. A change of position is a different job, and we tell you that before anything is drilled.

"Camera repair costs about $100 to $300"

That range describes a single consumer camera somewhere quiet. HomeAdvisor's Brooklyn page puts the average surveillance camera install or repair at $1,416, with a typical range of $680 to $2,145 across all job sizes. Other NYC companies publish $150 and $250 diagnostic calls before any parts.

On our Brooklyn list, a setting or connector fix can be covered within the $150 service call or run $150 to $350, a single camera replacement $250 to $600, and a recorder replacement $700 to $1,500. The fault decides the price, and you see it in writing first.

The expensive repairs are the ones done twice: a camera replaced when the drive was the problem, or a drive replaced when the power supply was killing it. Testing in order is how we avoid that.

Can the recorder keep the afternoon hours at full quality and save space overnight, instead of recording everything the same way?
+

Yes. Most recorders let you set different recording rules by camera and by time of day. On a South Slope house we usually keep the stoop and door cameras at full quality around the clock, because that is cheap insurance, and use motion-based recording or a lower stream for cameras that watch a quiet yard or a hallway overnight. The goal is to keep at least a week of usable video at the quality that identifies a face, and we size the drive and the schedule together to get there.

The dome over our stoop camera has turned yellow and cloudy from years of sun. Can just the dome be replaced?
+

Often, yes. On many dome cameras the clear cover is a separate part, and on our Brooklyn list a dome or front cover replacement is $30 to $80 for the part plus labor. If that model's cover is no longer sold, or the sun has also damaged the seal behind it, replacing the camera can be the better value, and we will tell you which on the visit. On a south- or west-facing facade, a camera with a hood or a shaded position lasts longer than one sitting in full sun.

Can you put a small screen inside our vestibule or shop entrance that shows people they are on camera?
+

Yes. A public-view monitor shows the entrance camera's live picture to anyone walking in, which many shop owners and some building owners use as a deterrent. It connects to the recorder or directly to the camera, and on a working system it is usually a short job. We place it where it is visible but out of easy reach, and set it to show only the entrance, never cameras covering private spaces.

We are tenants paying for the repair ourselves. Can you explain the problem to our landlord or managing agent on the phone?
+

Yes, with your permission. Once the fault is found, we can describe it to the owner or agent and send them the written price, so they can decide whether to pay for it or approve the work. Before the visit, do we need to clear the hallway or the cellar under the cameras?

It helps.
