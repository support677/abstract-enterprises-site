Six-story pre-war buildings: one basement, one super, one recorder for everyone

Kensington's six-story elevator buildings were built when nobody imagined cameras. When a landlord added them, usually in the last twenty years, the installer chose the shortest path: a recorder in the super's office or a basement room near the boiler, coax or network cable run up through an old pipe chase or along the elevator shaft, and cameras in the lobby, the vestibule, the mailroom, the basement corridor, the back door to the trash area and sometimes the roof door. Each camera was powered from a single box in the basement or from plug-in adapters on whatever outlet was nearby. The result is a system that works until one thing changes, and then several cameras fail at once for one reason.

What changes is usually the basement. A boiler room runs hot all winter. A super's office gets a second refrigerator or a space heater on the same outlet as the recorder. The landlord replaces the boiler, and the plumber moves a cable to get the new one in. The building's electrical service is upgraded and the recorder outlet is moved to a different circuit, sometimes one that shuts off with the lights. We trace these failures from the outlet up: power at the recorder, power at the camera power box, the health of the recorder's drive and its temperature, and then each camera along its run. When six cameras drop together, the cause is almost always shared, a power box or a switch, and fixing it costs far less than replacing six cameras.

The facades bring their own repairs. Some of these buildings have stone or terra-cotta bands, cornices and ornament around the entrance. Cameras were often fixed into the stonework or hung on long steel arms to clear a cornice. Over time water gets into connectors behind the ornament, arms loosen, and a camera tilts until it shows the sidewalk at its feet. A repair here often means re-sealing the connection, tightening or replacing the arm, and setting the camera to show the steps and the door rather than the sky. Where a camera must come off a decorative surface, we put the new one on plain masonry or a mortar joint so the facade is not drilled again.

Post-war towers: long runs, many floors, many people with logins

The taller buildings along Ocean Parkway, many built in the early 1960s and many since converted to co-ops, usually have cameras in the lobby, at the garage entrance and ramp, in the laundry room, at the back door, on the roof and sometimes on every floor at the compactor room. That means cable runs of a hundred feet or more through risers and ceiling spaces, and a recorder in a basement office far from the cameras at the top. Network cable has a practical length limit, and some systems were stretched past it with extenders or small switches hidden in a closet on a middle floor. When the upper-floor cameras start dropping in and out, the fault is often one of those hidden switches, overheating in a closet or plugged into an outlet that someone unplugs to run a vacuum.

Duplex-porch two-families: two households, one set of cameras

The stacked porches that Forgotten New York found on Turner Place give each household its own front door and its own porch, one above the other. Cameras on these houses usually hang from the underside of the upper porch, aimed at the lower door and the steps, with another at the side path and one at the back. The recorder sits in the basement, the cellar or a closet in one of the two apartments. Two things go wrong. Water runs through the boards of the upper porch onto the camera and its connector every time it rains, and over a few years it gets inside. And the household that does not have the recorder has no way to know when it stops working, or to reach it when they need a clip.

A repair here means sealing or moving the connector out of the drip line, fitting a camera rated for the wet, and, where both households agree, setting up separate app access so each family sees the cameras it shares and not the ones that are private. If the recorder is in the tenant's space, we can usually move it to a shared closet or the owner's floor so the owner can reach it without knocking on a door. The owner of the house approves the work; the tenant does not have to pay for repairs to the landlord's system, and we confirm who is paying before we book.

Foursquares and Victorians: porches, attics and old wood

Kensington's Foursquares, the square two-story houses with hipped roofs and wide porches on the numbered streets off Beverley Road and Ditmas Avenue, and its older detached Victorians, take cameras on porch ceilings, porch columns, eaves and the side of a driveway. Cables run through attics and down inside porch columns or along siding to the basement. Old wood moves with the seasons, so camera mounts loosen and tilt, and screws pull out of soft trim. Squirrels chew cable along gutters and in attics. Paint crews paint over domes and lenses. Vinyl siding installers take cameras down and put them back on without sealing the hole. Each of these is a small repair if it is caught early and a bigger one once water has gone down the cable into the house.

Row houses on the lanes and mixed-use buildings on the avenues

On Kermit Place, Friel Place and the other short lanes Forgotten New York walked, houses are narrow and attached, and a front camera sits over the door or the stoop with little room to aim. Back yards are small and overlooked by neighbors on three sides, which makes a back camera's view a matter of courtesy as well as security. On the avenues, a store at street level shares a building with apartments, and the store's recorder is often in the same cellar as the landlord's. We have found store cameras plugged into the landlord's power box, landlord cameras recorded on the store's recorder, and a single recorder that nobody would admit to owning. Before any repair on a mixed-use building we confirm whose equipment is whose, so the right person approves and pays, and so nobody loses a camera they thought was theirs.

Most Kensington owners who call about cameras on a mixed-use or apartment building end up pairing the camera repair with access control at the front door or a working intercom, because the door, the buzzer and the camera that watches it are one system as far as the tenants are concerned.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Repairing a Kensington Camera System From the Basement Up

These notes describe the order our technicians work in when several cameras are out at a Kensington apartment building, two-family, house of worship or store and nobody is sure why. It is our working method, not a story about one address.

Save the night first. If anyone needs footage from a recent date, the technician exports it before restarting or unplugging anything, and notes the recorder's clock against a phone set to network time. On a building, this is also when we ask who requested the clip, because a co-op board, a managing agent and a detective may each want a copy and each has a different right to one.

Start at the power. The technician checks the outlet, the power strip, the battery backup if there is one, the camera power box or PoE switch, and the recorder's own power and temperature. After a brownout like the one in July 2026, this is where the damage usually is: a power box with a failed output, a switch that powers some ports and not others, a battery backup whose battery died years ago and now does nothing, or a recorder that restarted mid-write and marked part of its drive as bad.

Read the recorder's own log. Most recorders keep a record of video loss, drive errors and restarts with times. That log often tells us which camera dropped first, at what hour, and whether it was the night of an outage, a storm or the day the roofers were up top. It also tells us whether the drive has been recording at all.

Then walk the cables to the cameras. Every dark camera is tested at its end: power arriving, data arriving, connector condition, lens and housing. Water behind a cornice, under a porch, or at the top of a coax run on an outside wall is the most common Kensington finding. Cables cut by facade, roof and window crews come next.

Why Systems Fail Here

Brownouts, Basements and Porches: Why Kensington Camera Systems Stop Recording

Camera systems everywhere fail for the same handful of reasons. What changes from neighborhood to neighborhood is which of those reasons comes up most. In Kensington, three come up again and again, and shared ownership and lost logins, covered in the questions below, make each of them harder to spot.

Brownouts: when the power sags instead of going out

A power cut is easy to understand: the system goes off and comes back on. A brownout is harder on equipment. When Con Edison lowered voltage by 8 percent across Kensington and the neighborhoods around it on July 3, 2026, lights dimmed and air conditioners struggled, but most people did not think about the recorder in the basement. Recorders, PoE switches and camera power boxes are built to run on a steady supply. Under low voltage, a cheap power box can drop its outputs one by one, a switch can stop powering its furthest cameras first, and a recorder can restart in a loop. Each restart while the drive is writing risks damage to the drive or the file it was writing.

The fix depends on what took the hit. A failed power box or switch port is replaced. A drive with bad sectors is replaced and the footage that matters is copied off first. A recorder that will not hold its settings may need a firmware reload or a replacement. Then comes prevention: a battery backup sized for the recorder and the switch, with a battery that is actually good, on an outlet that is not shared with an air conditioner or a refrigerator. Not every battery backup protects against low voltage; the kind we recommend for recorders corrects the voltage as well as covering short cuts, and we explain the difference before you buy one.

Basements and closets: heat, damp and shared outlets

Recorders in Kensington live in some hard places. A boiler room in January runs hot and dry. A cellar under a two-family in August runs warm and damp. A hall closet in an apartment has no air moving at all. A stock room behind a store on Church Avenue has the recorder on a shelf above a refrigerator compressor. Hard drives in recorders are built for constant writing, but heat shortens their life, and a forum moderator on IPCamTalk put it plainly when a user reported losing a drive every few months: "If you have frequent failures in a system, you should look at temperature and vibration." Industry guides put the life of a DVR or NVR at five to ten years depending on care and environment, and in a hot closet it is the short end.

On a repair visit we read the drive's health, check the recorder's temperature, clean the fan and vents, and look at what else shares the outlet. Sometimes the right repair is not a part at all but moving the recorder a few feet, onto a shelf with air around it, off the floor of a cellar that takes water, or away from the boiler. If a recorder has to move, the cables have to reach it, and we price that before we start.

Porches, cornices and facades: water finds the connector

Kensington's housing has a lot of places where water runs down onto a camera: the underside of an upper porch on a duplex-porch two-family, the edge of a Foursquare's porch roof, the cornice of a pre-war apartment house, the canopy over a store. Cameras are sealed, but the connection where the camera's pigtail meets the building cable often is not, or was taped by an installer years ago and the tape has failed. Water gets into the connector, corrodes it, and the picture goes from fine to flickering to black over a season or two. Sometimes the water travels down the cable into the wall.

The repair is to cut back to clean cable, re-terminate, seal the connection properly in a box or with the right fittings, and move it out of the drip line if we can. If the camera itself has taken water, it is replaced. On the published Brooklyn list, a connector reterminate is $150 to $350 and a single camera replacement on a working system is $250 to $600, so catching it at the connector stage matters.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Repair in Kensington: Questions Answered

These are the questions Kensington owners, boards, congregations and store owners bring to us most, grouped by the kind of building they come from.

Houses of worship and avenue stores

Someone walking in our front door at night could reach the donation boxes, the office and the back exit, the way the man in the January 2025 Kensington synagogue theft moved through that building. Our cameras cover the door and the office but not the hallway between them. Can that gap be closed with the cameras we already have?

Often, yes. Most institutional systems we see in Kensington were laid out one camera at a time, each pointed at something someone worried about, so the path between those things is where the gaps are. On a repair visit we walk the route a person would take from each door, look at what every existing camera records along it after dark, and see whether re-aiming or refocusing one or two of them, especially a wide-angle camera near the door or a varifocal one at the end of a corridor, can cover the hallway without losing what they watch now. If a gap cannot be closed that way, we tell you which single added camera would close it and what it costs on the Brooklyn list. Either way, the cameras along that route should send an alert to more than one person if they stop recording, because a building that is empty overnight is exactly where nobody notices.

Our restaurant in Little Bangladesh keeps its recorder in the basement under the kitchen. It switches itself off on busy evenings when the exhaust fan and the walk-in cooler are both running. Is the recorder dying?

Probably not, though it will be if this keeps happening. A recorder that shuts off only when heavy kitchen equipment runs is usually sharing a circuit or an overloaded power strip with that equipment, or sitting on an outlet whose voltage dips every time a big motor starts. Each sudden shutdown while the drive is writing risks damaging the drive. We check the outlet and circuit, test the recorder and drive, and then give it its own clean power, typically a battery backup that also steadies the voltage, on an outlet that does not share a load with the cooler or exhaust. If the basement is hot from the kitchen above, we also look at where the recorder sits and whether it needs space and air. The fix is often the battery backup and a short move, not a new recorder.

Apartment buildings and co-ops on Ocean Parkway and the avenues

The cameras on the upper floors of our Ocean Parkway co-op drop in and out, but the lobby cameras never do. A board member found a small box with blinking lights in a closet on a middle floor. Could that box be the problem?

Very likely. In taller buildings, installers often put a small network switch or an extender in a closet partway up, because a single network cable can only run so far from the recorder in the basement. That hidden box powers and connects every camera above it. If it overheats in an unventilated closet, runs on a plug-in adapter that is failing, or shares an outlet that someone unplugs, every camera above it drops while the lobby cameras, wired straight to the basement, carry on. We test that switch, its power and its ports, replace it if needed with one sized for the cameras it powers, put it on a protected outlet and label it, so the next super knows what it is and does not unplug it to run a floor polisher.

After a package went missing from our lobby, the clip showed only a dark outline of a person against the bright glass door. Is the lobby camera bad?

Usually not. A camera facing a glass door in daylight is looking straight into a bright street, so it exposes for the brightness and leaves anyone in front of the door as a silhouette. The fix is a camera setting called wide dynamic range or backlight compensation, which most lobby cameras have but which is often switched off, and sometimes a change of angle so the camera looks across the door rather than straight at it. If the camera is too old to handle that contrast, we replace it with one that can. In Kensington's long pre-war lobbies, a second camera on the inside wall that sees faces as people come in is sometimes the better answer, and we tell you which before you spend anything.

Our board wants to know whether the cameras kept recording on July 3, 2026, when Con Edison cut the voltage across Kensington. How can we tell if anything was lost that day?

The recorder can usually tell you. Most recorders keep a log of restarts, video loss on each camera and drive errors, with times, and playback shows a timeline for each camera with gaps where nothing was saved. If the drive still holds that date, we read the log and the timeline for July 3 and the days after it, note any gaps camera by camera, and write it up for the board. If the drive has since overwritten that week, the log may still show restarts and errors from that day. The same check tells us whether anything was damaged, a drive with new bad sectors or a power box that lost an output, and whether the system needs a battery backup that corrects low voltage before next summer.

Our super left, and the new super cannot get into the recorder. The managing agent says the old super still has the app on his phone. What is the right way to sort this out?

First, the owner or managing agent should confirm in writing who now administers the system. Then we copy any footage that matters, sign in using the manufacturer's recovery process for that model with proof the building owns it, set a new administrator password held by the owner or agent, and delete every old account, including the former super's. The new super gets his own login with the access his job needs, and nobody shares one password again. Changing the password alone is not enough, because some apps keep a device linked through the manufacturer's cloud account. We remove that link too, so the old phone cannot see the cameras. The whole job is usually a single visit within the service call or a small charge on the Brooklyn list.

Two-families, Foursquares and the lanes

Water drips through the boards of our upper porch onto the camera over the downstairs door every time it rains, and the picture has started rolling and flickering. Is the camera finished?

Not necessarily. Rolling and flicker after rain usually means water in the connection between the camera's short cable and the building cable, not in the camera itself. We open the connection, cut back to clean cable, re-terminate it and seal it in a weatherproof box, and move it out of the drip line under the porch boards if we can. If the camera housing has taken water, the lens fogs or the picture stays bad when dry, and then the camera is replaced. On a duplex-porch two-family, we also look at the upper household's porch: if a gutter or a gap between boards is pouring water onto that spot, moving the camera a foot sideways can save the next one.

The cable to our Foursquare's porch camera runs down inside a hollow porch column. A painter re-caulked and repainted the column, and now the camera is dead. Can the cable be replaced without taking the column apart?

Usually. The first job is finding where it broke, because a painter may have only knocked the camera loose or painted over its connector at the top of the column. If the cable inside the column is damaged, we try to pull a new one through using the old one as a guide. If the old cable is stapled or stuck inside, we run the new one along a less visible route, behind a column edge, under the porch ceiling or through the porch floor, and paint the cover to match. We tell you which route and price before cutting anything, and we do not drill new holes in old trim when an existing one can be reused.

Our front camera faces Albemarle Road, but anyone on the far sidewalk is only a few pixels tall. How far away can a camera actually identify a person?

It depends on the camera's resolution and lens, not on how much of the street it shows. A wide camera that covers the whole block makes everyone small; the same camera zoomed in on the gate and the near sidewalk can show a face. For recognizing a person, a camera has to put enough pixels on their face, which with a typical wide lens means a few yards, not across a street. That is why police releases, like the September 2025 surveillance video from near McDonald Avenue and Albemarle Road, are often clips of someone walking close past a camera. On a repair visit we can adjust a varifocal camera to cover the approach to your door at a useful size, and if you want the far sidewalk too, we say whether a second, zoomed camera is worth it.

Equipment

Types of Security Cameras and Recorders We Repair

We repair whatever is on the building, whoever installed it. These are the kinds of equipment we find on Kensington houses, apartment buildings, houses of worship and stores, and the faults each tends to develop here.

Coax and HD-over-coax cameras

Cameras with round BNC connectors, from old analog to HD models, still common in six-story buildings and two-families wired fifteen or more years ago. Typical faults: water in connectors under porches and cornices, failed adapters and worn infrared.

Network (IP) cameras

Cameras powered and connected through a single data cable, standard in newer co-op and store systems. Typical faults: a failed switch port, a damaged plug at the camera, an address conflict after a router change, and cable runs stretched too far.

DVRs, NVRs and hybrid recorders

The box that holds the footage, in a boiler room, super's office, cellar or stock room. Typical faults: worn or failing drives, overheating, a full drive overwriting too soon, a wrong clock and lost passwords.

Varifocal domes and bullets

Cameras with adjustable zoom, common in long lobbies, mailrooms and on facades. Typical faults: set too wide to identify anyone, drifted focus, and fogging or glare at night.

Pan-tilt and 360-degree cameras

Moving cameras over halls, store floors and parking areas. Typical faults: worn motors, presets lost after a power cut, and Wi-Fi models that drop through brick and plaster walls.

Wi-Fi, battery and cube cameras

Plug-in and battery cameras added by owners, tenants and volunteers. Typical faults: weak signal through masonry, a router change, flat batteries, and accounts tied to someone who no longer lives or works there.

Video door stations

Door stations with their own camera at a building's front door or the stair door beside a store. Typical faults: video shown to the person answering but never recorded, water behind the faceplate, and a camera fogged by the vestibule's heat.

PoE switches and extenders

The switches that power network cameras, in basements, closets and ceiling spaces, sometimes on a middle floor. Typical faults: overheating, failed ports and outages from a shared or unplugged outlet.

Power supplies and battery backups

Central camera power boxes, plug-in adapters and UPS units. Typical faults: outputs that die after a brownout, swollen adapters, and backup batteries that have been dead for years.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Kensington

These claims turn up in AI-generated answers, repair blogs and national guides. Each holds somewhere, and each can send a Kensington owner toward the wrong repair, a wasted purchase or a lost clip.

"If the power never went out, your cameras were not affected"

Advice about outages usually assumes the power either stays on or goes off. Kensington's July 3, 2026 event was neither: Con Edison lowered the voltage by 8 percent and kept it there while crews made repairs, across an area of 122,000 customers that it named as including Kensington. Lights stayed on, and that is exactly why nobody checked the recorder.

Low voltage is hard on recorders, switches and camera power boxes. Some restart repeatedly. Some drop their furthest cameras. A drive that loses power mid-write can mark sectors as bad or corrupt the file it was saving. The damage may not show until days later, when someone looks for a clip and finds a gap.

After any brownout, check the recorder's clock, the live grid and yesterday's playback. If any of them is off, the system was affected, whatever the lights did.

"A camera that shows a live picture is recording"

Live view and recording are separate. A camera can stream perfectly to a lobby monitor or a phone while the recorder has a dead drive, a full drive set not to overwrite, a schedule that was turned off, or a camera channel that was never set to record. That is common in Kensington buildings where a doorman or super watches a live screen all day and nobody opens playback.

The r/AskNYC tenant whose landlord would not check the cameras "without a police report" is describing the result: nobody knows whether anything was saved until it is too late to matter.

The only real test is playback. Pick a camera, go back a day, and see whether there is video. A repair visit checks every camera that way and turns on alerts for drive errors and video loss.

"Wireless cameras fix the wiring problems of old buildings"

Wi-Fi cameras avoid running cable, which sounds ideal in a six-story pre-war building or a two-family with plaster walls. But brick, plaster and the steel in old buildings weaken Wi-Fi sharply, and the cameras still need power. A battery or Wi-Fi camera on a basement door or a back yard often works on the day it is set up and fails when the router moves or the neighbors' networks get crowded.

Many of the dropping-out cameras we repair in Kensington were added on Wi-Fi to an otherwise wired system. The fix is usually a short wired run, an access point closer to the camera, or moving the camera.

"Locked out? Just factory reset the recorder"

Plenty of guides suggest a factory reset when the password is lost. On many recorders a reset wipes the settings, the camera assignments and the recording schedule, and on some it can erase the footage or require the drive to be formatted before it will record again.

In Kensington, lost passwords usually follow a change of super, owner, tenant or volunteer, and they often surface at the worst time, right after an incident, when the footage is what everyone needs. A reset at that moment can destroy the very clip you were trying to reach.

Save the footage first, then recover access through the manufacturer's process for that model with proof of ownership. We do that on repair visits routinely.

"Old cameras should all be replaced together"

Some advice says an old system is not worth fixing. Sometimes that is true: if several cameras have already failed, the recorder is obsolete and the cable is damaged, a replacement may cost less over time. But many older Kensington systems fail at one point, a power box, a switch, a connector or a drive, and the rest is fine.

Industry guides put recorders at five to ten years depending on care and environment. A recorder in a cool, dry room can outlast that; one in a hot boiler room may not reach it. The cameras on the walls often outlive the recorder.

We test before recommending. If a repair costs more than it is worth, we say so, and we show you what replacing just the recorder or just the failing cameras would cost.

"The police have cameras, so private ones add little"

City cameras watch some intersections and public spaces. They do not watch your building's mailroom, your store's register, your porch or the hallway of a house of worship. The 2025 Kensington cases on the record, the synagogue theft and the shove near McDonald Avenue and Albemarle Road, were described in the news through surveillance video, and the synagogue footage came from inside the building.

Private cameras are often what detectives ask for first after an incident on a block. They are only useful if the recorder kept the clip and the clock was right.

A repair that fixes the clock, extends how long the drive keeps footage and confirms the camera at the door actually records is what makes a private camera count.

"Camera repair is a handyman job"

Swapping a camera on a bracket looks simple, and an owner can do it on their own property. Repairing a system for someone else is regulated: New York State requires companies that install or service security systems, including CCTV used to detect intrusion or theft, to be licensed by the Department of State.

The job also involves more than the camera: power boxes, switches, drives, network settings, logins, and footage that may be evidence. Getting any of those wrong can lose a clip or open the system to strangers.

AESS repairs under NYS license #12000287431. Ask anyone who offers to fix a building's cameras whether they hold one.

FAQ

Kensington Security Camera Repair FAQ

One of our wired cameras drops off for a few seconds several times a day and then comes back on its own. What usually causes that?
+

On a wired camera that recovers by itself, the usual causes are a loose or corroded connector, a power supply or adapter that sags under load, a switch port that is failing, or an address conflict on the network. Weak Wi-Fi causes the same symptom on wireless cameras. The recorder's log usually shows the exact times of each drop, and matching those times to what else happens in the building, such as an elevator, a boiler or a kitchen starting up, often points straight at the cause.

Our recorder is about eight years old and still works. Is it likely to fail soon just because of its age?
+

Age alone does not decide it. Industry guides put recorders at five to ten years depending on care and environment, so eight years is within the normal range but toward the end of it. What matters more is how hot and dusty its room is, how its drive is doing and whether it still gets firmware updates. A repair visit reads the drive's health and checks the recorder's temperature and fan, and then we tell you honestly whether it has years left or should be planned for replacement.

How long should the hard drive inside a camera recorder last before it needs replacing?
+

A surveillance-rated drive is built for constant writing and often lasts several years, but heat and vibration shorten that, and a drive in a hot closet or boiler room can fail much sooner. Ordinary desktop drives put in recorders to save money tend to fail first. Most recorders can report a drive's health, and replacing a drive that is starting to show errors, before it dies, is far cheaper than losing the footage on it.

Most of our cameras are old analog models. Should we replace the cameras or only the recorder?
+

It depends on what is failing and what you want to see. If the cameras still give a usable picture and only the recorder is dying, a new recorder that accepts the existing cameras and coax can be the cheapest fix, and you can upgrade cameras one at a time later. If several cameras are already failing, the picture is too poor to identify anyone, or the cable is damaged, replacing cameras and recorder together usually makes more sense. We test first and price both ways.

Every camera on our screen shows "no signal" since the recorder restarted. Are all the cameras broken?
+

Almost never. When every camera goes at once, the cause is something they share: the camera power box, the PoE switch, the recorder's video inputs or a setting lost in the restart. Check whether the power box or switch has its lights on. If it does and the cameras are still out, the recorder may have reset its channel settings. Either way it is usually one repair, not a dozen new cameras.

I rent an apartment in a Kensington building. Can you put a camera inside my own front door?
+

Yes, within what your lease allows. A camera inside your own apartment, looking at your own door, is usually your business, while anything in the building's halls, lobby or outside the building needs the landlord's permission. Some landlords also restrict doorbell cameras on apartment doors, so check your lease first. We can set up an indoor camera on your own Wi-Fi with no drilling in common areas, and we will not mount anything on building property without the owner's approval.

Is there a charge beyond the service call if the problem turns out to be only a setting?
+

Usually not. Motion, alert, app, login and recording settings with no parts are normally fixed within the $150 Brooklyn service call. You only pay more if a part has to be replaced or a cable repaired, and in that case you get a written price before the work goes ahead.

Can you schedule a repair at our synagogue, mosque or church around prayer times and services?
+
