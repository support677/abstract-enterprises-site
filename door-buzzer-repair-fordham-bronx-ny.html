TERMINOLOGY

Fordham Buzzer Repair Terms, Translated

The words that decide what a repair quote actually covers — because entry buzzer, apartment entry system, visitor entry system, building entrance system, door release system and building access system all describe the same wiring wearing different marketing, and the front vestibule buzzer or lobby entrance buzzer on your building answers to the same parts either way: the door release button, the electric strike behind it, the buzzer transformer and buzzer power supply feeding them, the entry system wiring between, and on newer platforms a remote door release for phones.

Entry panel. The street-side unit with the buttons or directory. In Fordham it has usually outlived several paint jobs — often repairable long after it looks done.

Door strike. The electric latch in the frame that the buzz releases. The most common “intercom’s broken” call that is actually a $200 strike, not a $2,000 panel.

Transformer. The small basement unit converting house power to buzzer voltage. When the whole system dies at once, this is suspect number one.

Riser. The wire bundle climbing the building from panel to apartments. One damaged pair in the riser explains most “half the building is dead” calls.

4-wire / 5-wire intercom. The classic audio systems in most prewar stock — talk, listen, ring and release on dedicated conductors. Simple, durable, and almost always repairable.

Handset / station. The in-apartment unit. Individual stations fail individually — replacing one does not mean touching the other forty.

Telephone entry. The directory systems that call a phone instead of a wired handset. Failures here are usually programming and call-routing, not hardware.

Buzz-in / release. The moment the strike unlocks. If tenants hear the visitor but the door never gives, the release side — strike, wire or button contact — is the fault line.

Amplifier. The shared audio brain in many older systems. Crackle, hum or one-way voice across many apartments points here, not at forty individual handsets.

Video door station. The camera unit at the entrance on modern systems. Blank screens upstairs are as often a station or wiring fault as a monitor fault — we test before we replace.

Mobile release. Newer systems’ phone-app buzz-in. On upgrades we add it where owners want it; on repairs we keep whatever the building already relies on working.

Repair vs replace. Our rule in Fordham: if the wiring tests clean and parts exist, repair wins. Replacement earns its cost only when the platform itself — not one component — has failed.

The buzzer rings the apartments but the front door never unlocks — big job?

Usually small. When audio works and the release does not, the fault is almost always at the door itself: a worn electric strike, a broken release conductor at the frame, or a button contact that no longer completes the circuit. It becomes a bigger conversation only when the release wiring fails somewhere inside a wall — and even then we trace before anyone talks about replacement.

Only some apartments in my building get the buzzer — why?

Because the system is fine and one pathway is not. Shared components — panel, transformer, amplifier — serve everyone, so when units 2, 5 and 6 are dead while the rest work, the fault sits in the riser pair or the individual stations for those lines. We trace the run, repair the break or swap the dead stations, and the rest of the building never gets touched. It is the most common Fordham call there is, and it is a repair, not a rewire.

Our panel was painted over years ago and half the buttons stick — save or replace?

Painted panels are a Fordham institution, and more of them are savable than owners expect. Stuck buttons get freed or replaced, contacts cleaned, and the panel keeps working under its paint. Replacement earns the money when the board behind the buttons is corroded, parts no longer exist, or the building wants a directory and video anyway — in which case the new panel reuses the existing riser wherever it tests clean, which keeps the upgrade honest.

A tenant says the buzzer is the landlord's problem — is that right?

As a general rule in NYC multiple dwellings, yes: the entrance door lock and the buzzer or intercom that serves it are building systems, and keeping them working sits with the owner. Tenants can report dead systems to 311, and long-dead buzzers are the sort of thing that shows up in HPD complaints. We work for the owner or manager directly, or for a tenant with the owner’s approval — and we are happy to send the owner the estimate. That is general information, not legal advice; specific disputes belong with the city or an attorney.

The whole system died after a power blink — what happened?

Almost certainly the transformer or a surge-hit board, and both are among the fastest fixes we do. Basement transformers here run for decades and then quit in a blink, taking the entire vestibule with them; telephone-entry boards sometimes lose their programming instead. We test at the power side first on any all-dead call, carry replacement transformers on the truck, and reprogram directory boards on site. Yes, and on the Fordham Road corridor we do it constantly. Mixed-use buildings often inherit one release serving both the commercial and residential doors, which means the store’s traffic wears the residential hardware and nobody controls who opens what. Replacing with a modern intercom
