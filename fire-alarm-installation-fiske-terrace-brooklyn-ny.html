Fiske Terrace is a small grid of freestanding houses south of Foster Avenue and east of the Brighton line, most of them finished by 1914 by the T.B. Ackerson Company and a handful of other builders, and almost all of them now inside the Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park Historic District. The Neighborhood

Why Fiske Terrace Properties Need Fire Alarm Installation

The blocks hold two very different kinds of housing, and they need very different fire alarm work. When we read the city's PLUTO land-use records for the historic district, it came back as 252 lots holding 319 homes, barely more than one household per lot. Widen the view to the ten tax blocks that contain the district, and the count becomes 384 lots and 1,485 homes. The 290 one-family lots and 37 two-family lots are the freestanding houses most people picture. But 36 apartment buildings on those same blocks hold 1,114 of the 1,485 homes, about three in four. Most sit along Ocean Avenue, including a 108-unit building from 1953, a 96-unit building from 1955 and a 68-unit condominium from 2008, with 1931 walk-ups on East 16th and East 17th Streets at the Foster Avenue edge. Inside the district the record shows build years from 1898 to 1929, with two exceptions: a 27-unit, six-story apartment house from 1936 at 800 East 17th Street and two three-family buildings from 1961. So a fire alarm job here is either a single household spread across four levels of an old wood house, or a building full of households sharing halls, stairs and a cellar under multiple-dwelling rules. We plan the two separately, and we say which one we are quoting.

Six facts that shape fire alarm plans in Fiske Terrace

  • The houses predate household detection by generations. PLUTO shows build years in the district from 1898 to 1929, and the LPC describes about 250 predominantly free-standing single-family houses from the first two decades of the 20th century. Every alarm in them was retrofitted.
  • Three floors plus a cellar is the norm, not the exception. Ackerson's houses were three-story detached homes, and most have a cellar under them and an attic or attic room under the roof. Interconnection between levels is the core of almost every plan.
  • Three in four homes on these blocks are in apartment buildings. Of 1,485 homes on the ten tax blocks around the district, 1,114 sit in 36 apartment buildings, mostly on Ocean Avenue. Those are multiple dwellings, with owner obligations HPD spells out.
  • The district is landmarked, and the exterior is protected. The LPC designated the Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park Historic District on March 18, 2008. Interior alarm work normally stays outside its review, but exterior devices and anything affecting the yards do not.
  • Fireplaces are still in use. Current listings describe working wood-burning fireplaces. Carbon monoxide detection near them, and smoke alarm placement that does not trip every time a fire is lit, are recurring parts of our plans.
  • Battery fires are a citywide concern. Firehouse.com reported in 2026 that the FDNY said battery-caused fires were up about 70 percent that year. In freestanding houses with detached garages, where chargers end up is now a question on our survey sheet.

Need Repair in Fiske Terrace? A Chirp Under a Twelve-Foot Ceiling, Alarms That Don't Talk to Each Other, or a Building Panel in Trouble

Repair calls from Fiske Terrace tend to come in a few familiar shapes. The most common is a chirp nobody can reach: an alarm high on a stairwell ceiling or a tall parlor ceiling, giving a single beep every minute or so, in a house where the family ladder stops well short. That chirp is almost always a low battery or an alarm reaching the end of its working life, and it is quick to fix, but in a three-floor house it is also a moment to check the rest, because alarms installed at the same time tend to fail at the same time. The second is a house where a past renovation added hardwired alarms on one floor and the original battery units on the others never got linked to them, so the alarms sound only where the smoke is. The third is a carbon monoxide alarm sounding near the fireplace or the cellar boiler, which needs to be treated as a real event first and a faulty device second. The fourth comes from the apartment buildings on the edges: a trouble light on a building panel, which can mean a cut wire, a failed panel battery or a device someone removed while painting a hallway.

Building Specialists

Fire Alarm Installation for Fiske Terrace's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for nearly every fire alarm request from these blocks. Four are versions of the freestanding house, and they differ mostly in where the bedrooms sit and how the roof shapes the top floor. Two are the shared buildings at the edges, where the rules change because many households share one stair.

The foursquare under a hipped roof

The boxy three-story house many people picture first. A central or side stair, bedrooms on the second and third floors, and a cellar under the whole footprint.

The temple-house with its gable to the street

A front-facing gable that often holds a top-floor room under sloping ceilings, reached by a narrower upper stair. That room is the hardest one in the house to wake.

The bungalow behind a deep porch

Broad and low, with living space close to the ground and sleeping rooms tucked under the roof in many of them. Distances are shorter, but attic spaces are larger.

The Dutch Colonial Revival under a gambrel

The gambrel roof gives the top floor near-vertical lower walls and a flatter upper slope, which changes where an alarm can sit on the ceiling and still read smoke well.

The house with a rented floor

Thirty-seven two-family lots on the ten blocks, plus single-family houses where a floor is let. A tenant brings HPD's rental rules into a house that otherwise follows owner rules.

The edge apartment buildings

Walk-ups from 1931, the six-story building at 800 East 17th Street from 1936, and the 1950s and 2008 buildings on Ocean Avenue. Shared halls, shared stairs, and building-level obligations.

The foursquare: four levels stacked on one stair

A foursquare is efficient on paper and demanding for fire safety. The rooms stack neatly, the stair runs from the front hall to the top floor, and the cellar sits directly underneath with the boiler, the water heater and whatever has collected over the decades. In a fire, that stair is both the family's way out and the easiest route for smoke to climb. So we plan the stair first. An alarm at the top of each flight, and one in the hall outside each group of bedrooms, gives warning along the route people will use, and the cellar alarm at the foot of the cellar stair catches smoke before it reaches the first floor. Then we look at sleeping rooms. State law asks owner-occupied one- and two-family homes for a smoke alarm with an audible alarm in each sleeping area, and in a house where bedrooms are spread across two floors, we usually place alarms in and just outside them so that a closed door does not leave anyone unprotected.

The temple-house and the bungalow: the room under the roof

Front-gabled temple-houses and porch-fronted bungalows share one problem: the top room. Under a gable or a low roof, the uppermost bedroom is often reached by a narrower stair, sits behind its own door, and has sloping ceilings that make alarm placement less obvious. It is also, in many families, where a teenager or a guest sleeps. We treat that room as the priority on any plan for these houses. An alarm goes in the room itself and another at the top of the stair that serves it, both linked to the rest of the house. On a sloped ceiling we follow the manufacturer's placement guidance, which keeps a device away from the very peak, where trapped air can delay smoke, and away from the lowest part of the slope, where smoke may not reach early.

The Dutch Colonial Revival: gambrel ceilings and attic bedrooms

Period interiors: coffered ceilings, beams, built-ins and crown molding

The interiors in these houses are a big part of why people buy them, and an alarm installation should not leave a mark on them. Our rule is simple: no device is fastened to a decorative beam, a coffer, a medallion or a run of crown molding, and no cable is surface-mounted across a finished room. In a parlor with a coffered ceiling, an alarm goes in the center of a plain panel or, where the ceiling is entirely decorated, in an adjacent hall that still covers the room. Cable runs come from below through the cellar ceiling, up through closets, along existing chases beside plumbing or old gas pipes where those are accessible, or not at all. Where a route would mean opening plaster, we use alarms that link wirelessly to each other, which gives the same whole-house warning without a cable between floors.

The house with a rented floor: two sets of rules in one house

The edge apartment buildings: walk-ups, a 1936 apartment house and Ocean Avenue's larger blocks

The buildings that ring the district are where most of the people on these blocks actually live. They range from 1931 walk-ups on East 16th and East 17th Streets, to the 27-unit, six-story apartment house at 800 East 17th Street from 1936, to Ocean Avenue's postwar buildings with around a hundred units each and a 2008 condominium. Under HPD's notice, a Class A multiple dwelling needs at least one detector within fifteen feet of the primary entrance to each room lawfully used for sleeping, and the building's common areas are the owner's to look after. Where a building already has a fire alarm panel, the rules on that panel matter. Trade summaries of the code explain that replacing detectors, pull stations or control panels with identical parts and no design change counts as a repair, while moving or replacing a control panel needs new plans filed with the FDNY. In these buildings our first visit is usually a test of what is already there, so the board or owner can decide between repair and redesign with the facts in hand.

Field Notes

Field Notes: How a Technician Reads a Fiske Terrace House Before Quoting

This is our standard survey for a freestanding house in the district, written as the order we follow rather than as a story about any one job.

Start at the bottom. The technician goes down the cellar stair and looks at what heats the house and what heats the water, where the gas or oil supply comes in, whether there is a workshop, a laundry or a stored stack of anything, and what is plugged in and charging. He notes existing alarms and their dates, including the ones hanging loose from a ceiling joist. He looks at the cellar ceiling, because it is often the open highway for cable to the first floor.

Then the main floor. He looks at every ceiling for beams, coffers and molding, finds the fireplace and asks whether it is used, checks the kitchen for the range and how much it is used, and notes the closets that stack above one another, since those are the quiet routes upward.

Then up the stair, floor by floor. He counts sleeping rooms, notes who uses them, and checks whether the top-floor room has its own stair or door. He looks at sloped ceilings, knee walls and the attic hatch, and asks whether anyone uses a space heater on cold nights.

Then outside, without touching anything. He walks to the garage if there is one and checks what is stored and charged there. He does not mark the exterior of the house, and he notes anything the owner has asked for on the outside, such as a strobe or a monitoring antenna, as a separate landmark question.

Last, the test. Before he writes a word of the quote, he presses the test button on the existing alarm nearest the cellar and listens from the top floor with the doors shut. That one test tells the owner more about the house than anything on paper.

Compliance notes for Fiske Terrace houses and buildings

  • Owner-occupied houses. A New York State Assembly document notes that owners of owner-occupied one- and two-family homes must install and maintain a smoke detector with an audible alarm in each sleeping area.
  • Rented houses. HPD's notice calls for at least one approved, operational detector per dwelling unit in rental one- and two-family homes, including a carbon monoxide detector within fifteen feet of the primary entrance to each sleeping room.
  • Multiple dwellings. In a Class A multiple dwelling, HPD's notice requires at least one detector within fifteen feet of the primary entrance to each room lawfully used for sleeping. HPD adds that detectors must never be painted over and must have an end-of-life alarm.
  • Filed building systems. The FDNY's Chapter 9 says design and installation documents for fire alarm systems must be submitted to the department for review and approval before installation.
  • Repair or redesign. Identical replacement of detectors, pull stations or panels with no design change is a repair; moving or replacing a control panel needs new plans filed with the FDNY.
  • Tall buildings. One-way voice communication is required in buildings over 125 feet, measured from the lowest level of fire department vehicle access to the highest occupied floor. We measure before assuming.
  • Landmarks. Any project that affects the exterior of a building in a historic district needs an LPC permit even if DOB does not require one. Ordinary repairs and maintenance do not.
Rules and the District

Owner Rules, Rental Rules, Building Rules and the Landmark Line: Which Ones Apply to Your Fiske Terrace Home

Four sets of rules touch fire alarm work in Fiske Terrace, and most homes are governed by only one or two of them. Mixing them up leads owners to buy too much in one place and too little in another. We are not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. What we can do is read the public rules accurately, look at the house or building, and tell you which ones shape the work.

The owner rule. If you own the house and live in it, with no tenant, the baseline comes from state law: a smoke detector with an audible alarm in each sleeping area. That is a floor, not a target. In a three-story house with a cellar, meeting the letter of it can still leave a family that sleeps through a cellar fire, because a bedroom alarm two floors up does not sense smoke that has not yet reached it. Our plans for owner-occupied houses go beyond the minimum on purpose, with alarms on every level, in the stair and near fuel-burning equipment, all linked together. The cheapest version of that, a basic residential alarm system, starts at $600 on our Brooklyn list. Whole-house linked protection is $2,500.

The rental rule. The moment a floor is let, HPD's rules for rental one- and two-family homes come in: at least one approved and operational detector in each dwelling unit, and a carbon monoxide detector within fifteen feet of the primary entrance to each sleeping room. HPD's detector page also says carbon monoxide detectors are required in non-owner-occupied one- and two-family homes with fossil-fuel-burning devices. In a house with a cellar boiler or a gas range, that covers almost every rental here. We plan the rented floor to those points and document it, so the owner has a clear record.

The multiple-dwelling rule. The walk-ups, the 1936 apartment house and the Ocean Avenue buildings are multiple dwellings. In a Class A building HPD's notice calls for at least one detector within fifteen feet of the primary entrance to each room lawfully used for sleeping, along with the never-painted and end-of-life points. Buildings of this kind often also have, or need, a building fire alarm system, and that brings in the FDNY's rules. Chapter 9 says design and installation documents must be submitted for review and approval before installation. When a building's work falls into that category, we say so at the survey, explain that our New York State low-voltage license does not stand in for the licensed roles a filing requires, and coordinate our installation and pre-testing with the party who files. Filed systems are priced per building after the design, because the building and the filing set the cost.

The landmark line. The fourth set of rules is not about fire at all, but it decides how fire alarm work is done on the district's houses. The LPC says you do not need a permit for ordinary repairs or maintenance, but any project that affects the exterior of a building in a historic district needs an LPC permit, even if DOB does not require one. Its Permit Guidebook goes further, covering work on parts of a building not visible from the street and work in front, side and rear yards, and it says interior work needs an LPC permit only if it needs a DOB permit or affects the exterior. In practice, a household alarm system installed entirely inside the house, with no exterior device and no exterior penetration, sits on the interior side of that line. An exterior horn or strobe, exterior conduit, a hole through siding or a roof, or an antenna for a cellular communicator sits on the other side.

How the LPC treats small exterior equipment. The Commission's rule at 63 RCNY §2-20 lets staff approve small equipment, with security cameras, lighting and intercom panels given as examples, when it meets conditions: it is not on or in front of the primary facade unless there is no feasible alternative, it is attached at mortar joints or plain material, its finish matches, conduit is not visible or is kept to roughly ten inches and painted, and the device is not visible or minimally visible. The rule does not name fire alarm horns or strobes, and LPC's permit data for the district lists "Lighting fixtures and intercom panels" as a filing category without saying whether alarm devices fall under it. So we do not promise an owner that an exterior strobe is fine. We recommend keeping everything inside wherever possible, and where an exterior device is truly wanted, we treat it as an LPC question to settle before installation. The review is active here: the Historic Districts Council testified at the LPC's August 28, 2026 hearing about a fence application at 1901 Avenue H, a Medieval Revival house from about 1920 to 1922. A resident commenting on Brownstoner in 2008 put the feeling plainly: "I have to get LPC approval to paint my house or re-do my driveway."

What we could not find. We found no HPD violation counts, 311 complaint counts or DOB fire-alarm permit counts for Fiske Terrace itself, and we will not quote a neighborhood figure we do not have. Whether your building has an open violation or a filed system on record is a question about one address, and the city's records for that address answer it.

Wood, Fuel and Distance

Fireplaces, Space Heaters and Empty Rooms: What a Century-Old Freestanding House Asks of Its Alarms

A freestanding wood house is not more dangerous than an attached rowhouse in every way. It has no party wall, so a fire next door does not come through the wall, and a fire inside it does not reach the neighbors' rooms. But that same separation means something else: nobody on the other side of a wall will smell smoke or hear an alarm and knock. In a Fiske Terrace house, the early warning has to come from the house's own devices and reach the house's own sleepers. Everything in this section follows from that.

The fireplace. Listings in the district describe working wood-burning fireplaces, and some families light them through the winter. A fireplace that draws badly, or a damper closed too early, can push smoke and carbon monoxide into the room. Carbon monoxide alarms on the floor of the fireplace room and on the sleeping floors above give warning of a slow buildup that nobody can smell. Smoke alarms need the opposite care: placed too close to the hearth, they sound every time the fire catches, and a family that is woken or startled often enough will eventually take one down. So we keep smoke alarms back from the mantel zone, often in the adjoining hall, and test them with the owner's normal routine in mind.

The space heater. Top floors in old houses can run cold, and some families use a space heater in a third-floor bedroom or an attic room. News 12 Brooklyn's report of an Ocean Avenue fire blamed a space heater left too close to a bed. That report gives no block or date and is not about Fiske Terrace, but it is a common cause of fires in rooms where people sleep. If a space heater is in use, the room it heats needs its own smoke alarm, linked to the rest of the house, and the owner should know that a detector warns about a fire; it does not prevent one. Keeping bedding and curtains away from the heater is the prevention.

The empty room. Large houses have rooms that go unused for days: a third-floor guest room, a sewing room, a storage attic. A fire there can grow unnoticed. Linked alarms make sure the first device that senses smoke sounds everywhere, so an empty room still raises the alarm in the occupied ones.

The empty house. When the family is away, nobody is inside to hear anything, and a freestanding house set back on a quiet block may not show smoke to a passerby for a long time. Some streets here dead-end at the subway cut, and there are few through streets, so even traffic is light. For owners who travel, central station monitoring through our partners, at $19.99 a month, sends an alarm to a monitoring center that can contact the people on the call list.

The garage and the chargers. Many houses here have driveways and garages, some of them freestanding. A garage used as a workshop, or as the place where tools, bicycles or a mower are charged, is a sensible place for a heat detector rather than a smoke alarm, because exhaust and dust confuse smoke alarms. With the FDNY reporting a sharp rise in battery-caused fires citywide in 2026, according to Firehouse.com, we ask where batteries are charged at every survey. Where a garage link is wanted, a wireless-linked heat detector usually reaches the house without any trench across the yard, which matters in a district where yard work can be an LPC question.

The history behind the habit. Fire was on residents' minds from the start. The LPC's designation report records that by 1910 the Midwood Park Property Owners Association had committees that included fire and police services. A century later the tools are different, but the idea is the same: a house that stands alone has to look after its own warning.

Your Questions Answered

Fire Alarm Installation in Fiske Terrace: Questions Answered

These are the questions Fiske Terrace households and building owners actually raise: about fireplaces and cold top floors, about tenants and the law, about the landmark district, and about the larger buildings on the edges. Each is answered in full here and is not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Fire, fuel and heat in the old houses

We light the wood-burning fireplace in the parlor most winter evenings. Where can a smoke alarm go in that room without sounding every time the fire takes a while to draw?

Usually not in the parlor itself, and certainly not on the ceiling above the mantel. A fire that is slow to catch can roll a little smoke into the room for a minute or two, and a smoke alarm within reach of that will sound, which trains a family to silence it. We place the nearest smoke alarm in the hall or the adjoining room, where it still covers the escape route and senses a real fire that spreads beyond the hearth, but is out of the path of the brief puff when the fire is lit. If the room is large or used for sleeping, we may place a device at the far end, away from the fireplace wall. Carbon monoxide detection is the more important device in a fireplace room, because a closed damper or a poor draft can let carbon monoxide build up without any visible smoke. A CO alarm on that floor, and on the sleeping floor above, gives warning of that. We also suggest the chimney be checked by a sweep, because no detector fixes a blocked flue. After installation we ask the owner to light the fire as usual one evening and tell us whether anything sounded.

The third-floor rooms get cold in January and someone uses a space heater up there. Should that change where the detectors go?

Yes. A room with a space heater running, especially overnight, should have its own smoke alarm in the room, linked to every other alarm in the house. Without that, a fire that starts near the heater has to reach the hall before it is sensed, and in a top-floor room behind a closed door that can take long enough to matter. News 12 Brooklyn reported an Ocean Avenue fire blamed on a space heater too close to a bed; the report gives no block, and we do not place it in Fiske Terrace, but it describes a common pattern. If the house has gas appliances or a fuel-burning boiler, a carbon monoxide alarm on that floor is also part of the plan, though an electric space heater itself does not produce carbon monoxide. We also suggest the practical steps no device replaces: keep the heater well clear of bedding, curtains and furniture, plug it directly into a wall outlet rather than an extension cord, and switch it off when the room is empty. Where the cold top floor is a long-term problem, the better fix is in the heating system, but until then the alarm plan should assume the heater is there.

Our attic is unfinished storage under the roof and nobody goes up there for months. Does it need any detection at all?

It does not need a smoke alarm, and a smoke alarm would probably cause trouble there. Unheated attics swing from cold to very hot over a year, collect dust and sometimes condensation, and all of that makes smoke alarms unreliable or prone to false alarms. But an attic full of stored boxes, old furniture and perhaps an old wiring run is a place where a fire can start and grow for a long time before anyone notices, and it sits directly over the bedrooms. A heat detector is the right device. It responds to a fixed temperature or a rapid rise in temperature, it ignores dust and seasonal swings, and when it is linked to the household alarms, it sounds throughout the house. We mount it where heat would collect, away from the coldest corners near the eaves, and we make sure the hatch or stair is not blocked by storage so the device can be tested later. A smoke alarm still belongs at the top of the stair or outside the bedrooms just below the attic, because that is where people need to be warned.

Floors, families and tenants

Our children sleep on the third floor, two flights above our bedroom. Is one alarm per floor really enough to wake them?

Often it is not, and the reason is closed doors and distance rather than the number of devices. In a three-story house, an alarm on the first floor or in the cellar can be faint by the time its sound climbs two flights and passes through a bedroom door, and children in particular can sleep through alarms that adults hear. The answer is twofold. First, put an alarm in or just outside each sleeping room on the third floor, so the warning starts close to them. Second, link every alarm in the house, so that smoke sensed anywhere sets off the devices next to the children. We test this on every job: we trigger the alarm furthest from the third floor and stand in the children's room with the door closed. If the sound is not plainly loud there, we add a device or change the location. It also helps to agree on a family plan, so the adults know they will need to go up rather than wait for the children to come down, and the children know their second way out if the stair is blocked. Some families choose alarms with a recorded voice message, which some research suggests children respond to better, though we would not promise that any device wakes every child.

We have started renting our top floor to a graduate student. Does having a tenant change what the law expects of our alarms?

It can, because the rules for rental housing are stricter than those for an owner living alone. HPD's notice on smoke-detecting devices calls for at least one approved and operational detector in each dwelling unit of a rental one- or two-family home, including a carbon monoxide detector within fifteen feet of the primary entrance to each sleeping room. HPD's detector page adds that carbon monoxide detectors are required in non-owner-occupied one- and two-family homes that have fossil-fuel-burning devices, that detectors must never be painted over and that they must have an end-of-life alarm. Whether a rented floor in your house is a separate dwelling unit depends on how it is set up legally, which is a question for the house's paperwork, not for us. In practice, we protect the tenant's floor to the rental standard either way, with smoke detection near the sleeping room and carbon monoxide detection within fifteen feet of its door, and we link it to the rest of the house so that a fire on any floor warns everyone. We leave the owner a dated record of what was installed and a short card for the tenant explaining the sounds.

We live in the house ourselves and nobody else rents any part of it. What is the legal minimum, and why would we do more?

For an owner-occupied one- or two-family home, New York State law requires the owner to install and maintain a smoke detector with an audible alarm in each sleeping area. That is the minimum, and many Fiske Terrace houses already meet it with battery alarms near the bedrooms. The reason to do more is the shape of the house. A minimum installation protects people near their own bedroom but does not sense a fire in the cellar, the kitchen or an unused room until the smoke has traveled far enough to reach a sleeping area, and in a three-floor house with a cellar, that can take precious minutes. Alarms on every level, in the stair and near fuel-burning equipment, all linked together, mean the first device to sense smoke wakes everyone. Carbon monoxide detection near the boiler, the water heater, gas appliances and any working fireplace adds protection the law's smoke rule does not cover. The cost difference between the minimum and a whole-house plan is set out in our price table further down, and we will tell you plainly which parts of a plan are required and which are our recommendation.

The inspection report on the house we are buying says several smoke alarms are past their date. What should be replaced before we move in, and what can wait?

Anything past its manufacturer's replacement date should be replaced before you sleep there, and in a house with a mix of alarms of different ages, it is usually simpler to replace the whole set at once. Smoke and carbon monoxide alarms have a limited working life, printed on the device, and an expired alarm may not sense smoke properly even if it still beeps when tested. HPD's own guidance points to the end-of-life alarm on modern devices for this reason. Move-in is also the easiest time to do the job right: rooms are empty, floors can be protected without moving furniture, and closets are open for cable routes. We would plan alarms on every level, link them, and add carbon monoxide detection near the boiler and any fireplace, then hand you a list of every device and its date, so you know when the next replacement is due. If closing is close and time is short, we can do a first visit that gets working, linked protection on every sleeping floor, and return later for the rest.

The landmark district

Our house is inside the Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park Historic District. Does a fire alarm installed entirely indoors need anything from the Landmarks Preservation Commission?

Generally not, as long as it really is entirely indoors. The LPC says ordinary repairs and maintenance do not need a permit, and its Permit Guidebook says interior work needs an LPC permit only if it requires a DOB permit or affects the exterior. Household smoke and carbon monoxide alarms installed inside, with cable run through the cellar, closets and interior chases, or with wireless links between floors, do not touch the facade, the roof or the yard. That is how we design almost every district job. Where the line can be crossed by accident is in the details: a cable route that pokes out through siding to reach a garage, a vent or antenna fastened to the outside wall, a box mounted beside the side door. Each of those affects the exterior, and the LPC says any project that affects the exterior of a building in a historic district needs a permit even if DOB does not require one. So our plan for a district house lists, in writing, that nothing will be mounted on or through the exterior. If you later want something outside, that becomes a separate question.

A monitoring company wants to fasten a cellular antenna or a box to the outside of our house. Is that allowed on a landmarked building?

It is an exterior change, so it falls under LPC review, and we would not install it without that settled. The LPC's small-equipment rule at 63 RCNY §2-20 allows staff to approve items such as security cameras, lighting and intercom panels when they meet conditions, including not being on or in front of the primary facade unless there is no feasible alternative, attaching at mortar joints or plain material, matching the finish, keeping visible conduit to about ten inches or none, and being invisible or minimally visible. An antenna is not named in the rule. The better answer is usually to avoid the exterior entirely. Most modern cellular communicators for monitored alarms work from inside the house, and in a wood-frame house with large windows, the signal is often strong enough in the cellar or on the first floor without an outside antenna. We test the signal at the planned location during the survey. If the signal inside is too weak, we look at other interior spots, such as an upper-floor closet, before anyone talks about an outside mount, and if an exterior device is truly the only option, we treat it as an LPC application.

Could we put an outdoor horn or a flashing strobe on the house so neighbors notice a fire while we are away?

We understand the idea, especially in a freestanding house on a quiet street, but we steer owners away from it in this district for two reasons. The first is the landmark rule: an exterior horn or strobe affects the exterior, so it needs LPC review, and the LPC's small-equipment rule does not name alarm horns or strobes, so we cannot promise how it would be treated. The second is that an outdoor sounder relies on a neighbor hearing it, recognizing it as a fire signal rather than a car alarm, and doing something. On a dead-end block by the subway cut, with few passersby at night, that chain is weak. Central station monitoring through our partners, at $19.99 a month, does the same job more reliably: a signal from the house goes to a monitoring center, which contacts the people on the call list and can arrange a response. If an owner still wants an exterior device after that conversation, we help prepare the information the LPC would need and install only once approval is in hand, mounted in the least visible place the house allows.

The apartment buildings at the edges

Our co-op on Ocean Avenue has around a hundred apartments and an elevator. Is that a completely different kind of job from the houses behind it?

Yes, in nearly every way except the goal. A building of that size is a multiple dwelling, and the owner or board is responsible for detection that meets HPD's rules, including at least one detector within fifteen feet of the primary entrance to each room lawfully used for sleeping in a Class A building, plus the common areas. Many buildings like this already have a fire alarm panel of some age, with devices in halls, the cellar and the boiler room. Our first job in such a building is usually to test and document what exists, device by device, so the board can see what works. Then the rules on existing systems guide the choice. Identical replacement of detectors, pull stations or a control panel with no change to the design counts as a repair, while moving or replacing the control panel, or redesigning the system, needs new plans filed with the FDNY, which Chapter 9 says must be approved before installation. A building job also involves a board, a managing agent, access to every apartment and notices to residents, so the schedule and the paperwork take more planning than the devices themselves.

Is our seven-story building tall enough that the fire code would want voice announcements in the halls?

Probably not, but it should be measured rather than guessed. Under the rules summarized by SPARC, one-way voice communication must be installed in buildings over 125 feet, measured from the lowest level of fire department vehicle access to the highest occupied floor. A typical six- or seven-story apartment building of the kind found along Ocean Avenue usually falls well short of that height, but floor heights, a raised first floor or a lower street grade on one side can change the measurement, so we check the building's actual dimensions before saying anything firm. Even where voice communication is not required, a building panel's notification devices, the horns and strobes in the halls, still have to reach every apartment, and that is where older buildings often fall short after decades of renovations. If the building is planning a larger upgrade, the height question is one of the first things we settle, because it changes the design and the filing. We put the measurement and its source in writing for the board.

Are alarms that speak a warning message worth it in a large house?
+

They can help. A voice saying fire or carbon monoxide removes the guesswork about which hazard is present and where, which matters when alarms sound on every floor at once. They cost more than basic models, so we price them as an option on the written plan.

Our top-floor bedroom has the steep and shallow slopes of a gambrel roof. Where does the alarm go?
+

Usually on the flatter upper section of the ceiling, away from the very peak and away from the steep lower slope, following the manufacturer's placement guidance. In narrow dormer rooms we sometimes mount on the wall within the allowed distance from the ceiling.

A family member is hard of hearing. What can be added so they are warned too?
+

Strobe-equipped alarms in their bedroom and the rooms they use most, and, where they want one, a bed shaker or pillow vibrator that responds to the linked alarms. We plan those devices into the same system so they react to a fire anywhere in the house.

How do you protect old floors and stair runners during the job?
+

We cover the working areas and the route from the door with drop cloths, use ladder feet that will not mark wood, keep tools off finished surfaces and vacuum any dust at the end of each area. If something needs to be moved, we ask first.
