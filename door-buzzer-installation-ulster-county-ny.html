The Neighborhood

Why Ulster County Properties Need Door Buzzers Fitted to Converted Houses

The picture of an apartment entry that most buzzer advertising uses — a lobby, a directory of forty names, a super in the basement — describes a small part of Ulster County. The Census Bureau’s 2019–2023 American Community Survey counts 86,178 homes in the county, and 59,136 of them are detached single-family houses. The rest are where entry systems live, and they are mostly small: 6,143 homes in two-unit buildings and 4,985 in buildings of three or four units, against 1,582 in buildings of fifty or more. In the City of Kingston the shape is sharper still. Of 11,049 homes, 2,111 are in two-unit buildings and 1,279 in three- or four-unit buildings, so about 31 percent of the city lives behind a front door shared with one to three other households, and 5,126 of its occupied homes are rented.

Those buildings are old. The same survey puts the median year built for Kingston’s housing at 1939 or earlier, and the city’s own 2020 housing study described roughly 5,000 of its homes as more than 80 years old. A house built for one family and divided later rarely got an entry system designed for the job. What we find is usually a row of separate doorbells, each wired to a different apartment with thin bell wire stapled up the stair, a front door with an ordinary lockset that tenants prop open for deliveries, and sometimes an audio buzzer from the 1970s whose release no longer pulls the latch. The work is less about choosing a panel and more about deciding which door gets controlled, how release reaches each flat, and how to do it without opening up plaster walls.

Ownership and rules shape the job too. Kingston opted into the state Emergency Tenant Protection Act effective August 1, 2022, the first upstate city to do so; it covers buildings of six or more units built before 1974, an estimated 1,300 apartments, and the state agency that runs it lists the entrance and lobby among the building-wide services a tenant can file a complaint about. Every Kingston rental also needs a rental permit and an inspection at least every 24 months, and an owner who lives outside Ulster County must name a managing agent who lives inside it. In the Village of New Paltz, where 1,078 of 2,519 homes are in buildings of five or more units and 1,689 households rent, a rental property must pass inspection within 30 days to count as registered. An entry system that works, latches and can be explained to an inspector is part of running a rental here.

Then there is the setting. Kingston’s Stockade, Rondout–West Strand, Chestnut Street and Fair Street districts put exterior changes visible from the street in front of the Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission; Ulster sits in one of the stormier parts of the state, with 182,760 lightning pulses and 49 thunder days recorded in 2020; and parts of the county along Routes 28 and 212 still have weak or no cell signal. A buzzer plan here has to say where the panel goes on a stone or clapboard facade, what the lock does in an outage, and whether the phone-answering feature an owner wants will actually reach the tenant.

Building Specialists

Door Buzzers for Ulster County’s Architecture

Ulster’s buildings run from seventeenth-century stone houses to 1970s garden apartments and new mixed-use blocks. The entry method follows the wall and the way the building was divided, so these are the six types we plan for most.

Divided Frame Houses

Kingston, Saugerties village and Ellenville have many older houses turned into two or three flats over the decades. The front hall is usually shared, the stair is open, and bell wire already reaches each door.

Dutch Stone Houses

The Stockade, Hurley’s Main Street and Historic Huguenot Street in New Paltz keep colonial houses built of stone. Cable comes through an existing opening or a later wing, never a fresh hole through the face of the wall.

Brick Walk-Ups and Commercial Blocks

Three- and four-storey brick buildings on Broadway, Wall Street, Partition Street and Canal Street with a street door and a stair to flats over shops. The facade belongs to the street; the entry has to keep shop and residents apart.

Post-War Garden Apartments

Low brick buildings in groups, several entrances each, built before 1974 and now often under Kingston’s rent-stabilization rules. Each entrance needs its own panel and release, sometimes sharing one controller.

Mill and Factory Conversions

Former industrial buildings turned into apartments, such as the century-old lace curtain mill on Cornell Street that now holds 55 affordable homes with an artist preference. Long corridors and masonry walls make cable routes the main design question.

Cottages and Accessory Units

A rented cottage behind a house in Woodstock, Stone Ridge or Gardiner, or a second unit over a garage. Usually one door, sometimes a gate, and often no wired path back to the main house.

How the method changes from one building to the next

Frame houses. We start by testing whatever bell or old intercom wire already reaches each flat. Two clean conductors to every apartment are enough for most two-wire audio and video systems, and that turns a week of plaster work into a day. Where a run fails, a new cable usually follows the stair stringer or rises inside a closet stacked above the front hall; the strike cable runs through the door frame on the hinge side, where it is protected.

Stone and brick. On masonry we look for an opening that already exists — the old doorbell hole, a vent, the gap around a pipe or the joint between a stone wall and a later frame addition. When a new penetration cannot be avoided, it goes through a mortar joint rather than a stone or brick face, and is sealed with a material suited to the mortar. Surface panels on masonry are fixed into joints too, so they can be removed later without leaving scars.

Garden apartments and conversions. Here the challenge is distance and repetition: four or six entrances, each needing a station, a strike and a path to a central power supply, sometimes across a courtyard. We look for conduit and basement routes the original builder left, and price each entrance as its own line so an owner can stage the work.

Cottages. A small unit behind a house rarely justifies a trench just for the door. A self-contained video door station with its own power at the cottage, answering on the occupant’s phone, is often the right tool, provided the cottage has dependable internet. Where it does not, an audio station with a wired handset is still the more reliable choice.

Rules and paperwork that come up on Ulster County entry jobs

  • Which state law applies. The intercom requirement New York City owners hear about is Section 50-a of the Multiple Dwelling Law, which applies automatically only to cities of 325,000 people or more. Kingston, New Paltz, Saugerties and every other Ulster city, town and village fall under the Multiple Residence Law instead, which treats a building with three or more families as a multiple dwelling. We do not read either as a reason to skip a working entry; we read them so an owner knows which rules an inspector is applying.
  • Kingston landmark districts. City code requires a certificate of appropriateness from the Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission for a material change in appearance visible from a public street in a landmark district, while ordinary maintenance that changes nothing visible is exempt. The commission says most exterior desk reviews come back within about a week.
  • Egress codes. New York’s 2025 Uniform Code, based on the 2024 International Building Code, took effect on December 31, 2025. Its door rules let electric strikes keep free exit through the lockset, while magnetic locks need specific release devices, a release on loss of power and, where a fire alarm exists, release on alarm.
  • Electrical work. Ulster County’s licensing law defines electrical work broadly, including wiring for signalling and communication. Any new 120-volt outlet or circuit for a power supply is done by a county-licensed master electrician under the permit that requires, and we coordinate with one when a job calls for it.
  • Rental inspections. Kingston inspects rental properties at least every 24 months and the Village of New Paltz requires inspection within 30 days of registration. We leave a one-page description of the entry system — doors controlled, lock type, power supply location — that an owner can show an inspector.
  • Accessibility. Where a commercial entry falls under the ADA, the 2010 Standards limit the high forward reach to 48 inches above the floor, so we place call buttons within that height on public and mixed-use doors.
Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzers in Ulster County: Questions Answered

These are the questions Ulster owners, tenants and managers bring to us, grouped by the situation behind them. They are answered in full here because a two-line FAQ answer does not survive contact with a 1900 house divided three ways.

Houses Divided Into Two, Three or Four Apartments

What a real entry system adds to a Kingston two-family with one shared front door and a doorbell per flat

Three things a row of doorbells cannot do. First, the upstairs tenant can talk to the caller without walking down the stair, which is the moment most front doors get propped open. Second, the tenant can release the door from the flat, so the door can stay locked all day instead of being left on the latch for a delivery. Third, the door closes and latches again by itself after each visitor, because an electric strike only frees the latch for the few seconds the release button is pressed.

In practice the system is a small door station with one call button per apartment, a handset or monitor in each flat, an electric strike in the front door frame, and a low-voltage power supply in the basement or a closet. In many divided houses the existing bell wire to each flat can carry a modern two-wire system, which keeps the job to a day or two.

What it does not fix is a door that does not close. If the closer is weak or the latch does not reach the strike, the best entry system in the world leaves the door open, so we check the door, frame and closer before quoting the electronics.

The upstairs apartment in our converted house has its own outside stair and door. Does it need its own door station?

Usually yes, because the visitor arrives at that door, not the front one. Ulster has a lot of houses where an upper flat was given an exterior wooden stair at the side or back when the house was divided. A call button at the main front door is no help to a courier standing at the top of a side stair.

The simplest arrangement is a separate one-button station at that door, wired to that apartment only, with its own strike if the tenant wants to release it remotely. If the owner prefers one system, most multi-tenant controllers accept a second entrance, and the apartment’s handset then shows which door is calling.

The cable is the practical issue. An exterior stair often has no protected route back into the house, so we run the wire in outdoor-rated conduit tight to the framing, under the treads or along a post, and bring it in through the wall at the landing rather than leaving it exposed where it will be stepped on or painted over.

Putting video on the thin bell wire stapled up a third-floor stair

It often works, if the pair tests clean. Two-wire multi-tenant systems are designed to run audio, video and door release over a single pair, and Aiphone describes its GT Series, for example, as built to take advantage of existing wire. What decides it is the condition of the pair: continuity end to end, no splices hidden behind paint, no shorts to other conductors and a gauge the system’s distance tables accept.

We test each pair before quoting rather than after. Old bell wire stapled along a stair often has a nick where a staple went through the jacket, a splice inside a ceiling where a fixture was moved, or a run that was cut when a kitchen was remodelled. Those faults can be repaired or bypassed one apartment at a time, which still costs far less than rewiring.

If a pair fails badly or the run is too long for video, we replace that one run and reuse the rest. Installers on an access-control forum describe the same approach: keep the working wire, repair what is damaged, and upgrade only the apartments that want more.

Can each apartment in a divided house have a different kind of answering station?

Within one system, yes. Most multi-tenant systems let an owner mix basic audio handsets, video monitors and, where supported, phone answering apartment by apartment. A long-term tenant who wants a simple handset by the door can keep one, while the flat rented to someone who travels can answer on a phone.

The limit is the door station and controller: they must support every type of station you plan to mix. If one tenant later wants video, the station on the door must have a camera from the start, so we usually suggest a video door station even when most flats begin with audio handsets.

Mixing brands is a different matter. Handsets from one manufacturer rarely work on another’s controller, so a building that already has a working system from one brand should usually stay with it for new stations.

Kingston Apartments, Rent Rules and Inspections

Rent-stabilized Kingston buildings: entry failures and the state’s service complaint forms

Tenants in Kingston’s rent-stabilized buildings can file service complaints with New York State Homes and Community Renewal, which administers the Emergency Tenant Protection Act in the city. The city’s ETPA information page describes form RA-84 for building-wide decreased services and lists the entrance and lobby among the shared areas it covers. An owner who receives an order to restore a service has 30 days to comply. Whether a particular entry system counts as a required service in a particular building is a question for HCR or a lawyer; this is not legal advice.

For owners the practical answer is the same either way: keep records. When we repair or replace an entry system in a stabilized building we date the work order, list what failed and what was installed, and note the release test at each apartment. That paper is what an owner needs if a complaint is ever filed.

For tenants, the first step the city’s ETPA page recommends is contacting the owner or the owner’s representative directly. A written description of what fails — no talk, no release, release works but the door does not latch — helps whoever is sent to fix it.

Multiple Dwelling Law or Multiple Residence Law: which one governs a Kingston or New Paltz apartment house

The New York City rule does not apply here automatically. The intercom rule people quote is Section 50-a of the Multiple Dwelling Law: Class A multiple dwellings erected or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments must have an intercom at the self-locking main door, and older buildings must add one if a majority of tenants request it in writing. Section 3 of that law applies it to cities of 325,000 or more; smaller cities, towns and villages can adopt it by local law.

Kingston, New Paltz, Saugerties, Ellenville and the rest of Ulster fall under the Multiple Residence Law, which applies to cities under 325,000 and all towns and villages. Its definition of a multiple dwelling begins at three families, and it carries its own rules on doors and fire safety, such as self-closing doors on public halls. Local codes and lease terms can add more.

So the honest answer is that the NYC section is not the rule here, but that does not mean an owner can let a shared entry fail. Leases, local property maintenance rules and, in stabilized buildings, the service rules above all come into play. Ask your city or village building department which local rules apply to your building.

I own a Saugerties or Kingston building but live in the city. How do I run entry from a distance?

Kingston’s rental code already assumes you will need help: an owner who does not live in Ulster County must name a managing agent who does, responsible for the building’s maintenance and operation. That person, not you, should hold the master settings for the entry system and be the first call when it fails.

The equipment can help. A system that lets an administrator add and remove apartment codes, change a tenant’s answering phone or open the door remotely means the agent does not have to visit for every lease change. If the building has dependable internet, a cloud-managed panel gives both owner and agent a log of who was buzzed in and when.

What we avoid is putting all control on one person’s phone in Brooklyn. We set at least two administrator accounts, write down the installer code and give the agent a printed sheet with the power supply location and how to reset it.

What does a Kingston rental inspector look at on the entry door, and what should we have ready?

The city’s rental code says inspections, at least every 24 months, check compliance with the city code and state laws on safety and maintenance; it does not publish a separate buzzer checklist. In our experience the questions that matter at an entry are the general ones: does the door close and latch by itself, does it open freely from inside without a key, and is any wiring safely fastened and covered.

An electric strike on an ordinary lever lockset passes those tests easily, because the inside lever always opens the door. A magnetic lock raises more questions, since it must release on a push button, on loss of power and, where present, on fire alarm. That is one reason we fit strikes on residential front doors.

Before an inspection we suggest having the one-page system description we leave after installation, the power supply cover closed and labelled, and no extension cords feeding the entry system.

We just bought a Kingston rental and nobody handed over the entry system codes. What now?

It is common. Buildings change hands, the previous owner’s installer is long gone, and the system still works but nobody can add a tenant or change a release time. The first step is identifying the system from the door station and the controller in the basement or closet; most have a model label inside the cover.

Many systems can be reset to factory settings and reprogrammed, which clears the old installer code but also every apartment’s settings, so we record the existing directory first. Cloud-managed systems are different: the account belongs to whoever opened it, and the platform usually has a transfer process that needs proof of ownership.

When the job is done we give the new owner, and the managing agent if there is one, the installer code, the administrator logins and a note of the model numbers, so the next sale does not repeat the problem.

Tenants want to see who called while they were out. Can the entry system keep a picture of each visitor?

Many video monitors can store a still image or short clip of each caller, and cloud-managed systems usually keep a visitor log with images that tenants or the owner can look back at. Audio-only systems cannot, because there is no camera at the door.

Before switching it on, decide who can see the images. In a shared building one tenant’s visitors are not another tenant’s business, so each apartment should see only its own calls, and the owner’s access should be limited to what managing the building needs.

If the aim is a record of everyone who enters the hall rather than who rang, a camera inside the entrance does that job better than the door station.

Student Houses and Village Rentals in New Paltz

A house near SUNY New Paltz is let by the room to five students. Should every bedroom get its own call button?

If each student signs a separate lease, a button per bedroom is usually worth it. Couriers, food deliveries and visitors then reach the right person, and nobody has to answer the door for a housemate. The door station needs as many buttons as rooms, and each room needs an answering station or a phone account.

If the house is let to a group on one lease, one button with phone answering for each housemate often does the job more cheaply: the call rings every phone on the account and whoever is home answers.

Either way, use codes rather than keys for the front door where you can. A code can be deleted in seconds when a student leaves in May; a key that left with a graduate cannot. We set the system so the owner or manager, not a tenant, controls the code list.

Our village rental has to pass inspection within 30 days. Will installing a buzzer delay registration?

It should not, if it is planned. A buzzer installation in an existing building usually takes one to two days on site. The parts that can add time are a new electrical outlet for the power supply, which an electrician installs, and any exterior change in a district that needs review. Neither applies to most village rentals.

The Village of New Paltz publishes its rental registration and inspection forms and its list of registered rentals; the building department can tell you what the inspector expects at the door. We schedule entry work so that the door, closer and latch are finished and tested before the inspection date.

If you are short on time, the order that matters is: door closes and latches, inside release works without a key, then the electronics. A door that latches with a working key and no buzzer yet is safer than a half-installed strike.

Students keep propping the front door. Will an entry system stop it?

It removes the main reason. Doors get propped because someone is waiting for a delivery, a friend or a roommate without a key. When everyone can answer the door from their phone or a station upstairs and release it remotely, there is less reason to leave it open.

The rest is hardware. A properly adjusted closer brings the door back from fully open and latches it, and a door-position switch can tell the owner when the door has been left open for more than a minute or two. Some systems can send that alert to the manager’s phone.

What does not help is a magnetic lock that holds the door shut but is easily defeated by a wedge or tape over the plate. A strike that catches a real latch is harder to cheat.

Landmark Districts, Stone Walls and Old Facades

Kingston’s preservation commission and a new panel on a Stockade or Rondout facade

If the panel changes how the building looks from the street, plan on a review. Kingston’s code requires a certificate of appropriateness from the Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission for a material change in appearance visible from a public street or alley in a landmark district, and it names exterior elements such as light fixtures and signs. Ordinary maintenance that changes nothing visible is exempt.

Replacing an existing panel with one of similar size in the same opening is the kind of work owners often ask about; the city’s HLPC page and staff can say whether a desk review is needed. The commission says exterior desk reviews generally come back within about a week, so it rarely holds a job up if it is asked early.

To make the review easy we supply a photo of the facade, a drawing of the panel at actual size, its finish and the mounting method, and we propose locations that keep the panel off historic trim — often on the door jamb or a side return of the entry rather than the face of the building.

How do you bring cable into a stone house without drilling the stone?

By using an opening that is already there. Stone houses in the Stockade, Hurley and New Paltz almost always have later additions: a frame kitchen wing, a porch, a replaced door frame, a vent or pipe chase. Cable comes through the frame part or alongside an existing service entry, then travels inside to the door.

At the door itself, the strike cable runs through the wooden frame, which is usually much easier to work with than the masonry around it. A small surface raceway painted to match the interior trim covers any short exposed run.

If no opening exists and a new one is unavoidable, it goes through a mortar joint, kept as small as the cable allows, and is sealed with a material compatible with the mortar. We do not fix panels into the faces of old stones.

A north-facing panel on an unheated porch through an Ulster winter

An outdoor-rated station survives it if it is mounted and sealed properly. Manufacturers design multi-tenant door stations for rain and snow, and the ones we use carry a weather rating; the failures we see are almost always installation problems — a top edge that lets water run behind the face plate, an unsealed cable entry, or a station mounted where a roof drips straight onto it.

We mount under the porch roof or a small hood where possible, seal the cable entry from behind, and slope any surface box so water runs off. On wood porches we fix to solid framing, because a station on thin trim moves every time the door slams and eventually breaks its own wires.

Cold mainly affects the strike and latch. Ice and swelling wood can stop a latch reaching the strike, so we check the alignment in a cold month or leave room for adjustment.

Power Cuts, Storms and Locks

When Central Hudson loses power, does our front door lock or unlock?

That depends on the lock you choose. A fail-secure electric strike stays locked when power is removed and needs power only to release, so in an outage the door stays locked from outside while the inside lever still opens it. A fail-safe device does the opposite and unlocks when power is lost. Allegion’s guidance sums it up: fail-secure is more common because of security, and most electric strikes allow free exit either way.

For the front door of a residential building we normally fit fail-secure strikes. Tenants still get out, and the building is not left open for the hours an outage can last; the September 2026 nor’easter cut power to more than 14,000 Ulster customers at its peak.

The cost is that the buzzer cannot release the door during an outage unless the power supply has a battery. A small battery backup keeps release working for a reasonable period, and residents keep their keys or codes for longer outages.

We are on a hill above Rosendale where lightning hits often. How do you protect the system?

Ulster recorded 182,760 lightning pulses and 49 thunder days in 2020, so protection is worth planning for. Most damage arrives through cables that leave the building — to a gate, a detached garage, a second building — and through the power supply’s mains side.

We fit surge protection where any entry cable leaves or enters a building, bonded to that building’s ground, and plug the power supply into a protected outlet or a unit with its own surge protection. Inside a single house the runs are short and rarely the problem.

No protection stops a direct strike. What it does is keep a nearby strike from taking out every station on the system at once, which is the expensive outcome.

Magnetic locks on an apartment-house main entrance: what the code asks for

A magnetic lock can be used there, but it brings code requirements that a strike does not. A magnetic lock holds the door with no latch, so the code requires a way out that does not depend on the occupant knowing the system: release by door hardware with a built-in switch, or a motion sensor plus a clearly signed push-to-exit button within reach of the door, release on loss of power and release on fire alarm where one exists.

That makes a magnetic lock fail-safe by design. Allegion notes that magnetic locks are only available fail-safe, because there is no magnetic hold without power. In an outage the door is unlocked.

For those reasons we use magnetic locks mainly on glass storefront doors or gates where a strike cannot be fitted, and electric strikes on residential front doors. If your building already has a magnetic lock, we check its release devices against the current code before connecting a new buzzer to it.

Internet, Fiber and Weak Cell Signal

Phone answering for buildings in the Route 28 and Route 212 dead zones

Phone answering is not reliable through the cellular network alone in those areas. A 2016 survey of Ulster cell coverage reported gaps along Routes 212 and 28 where mountains block signal, and named ZIP codes around Phoenicia, Shandaken, West Hurley and parts of Saugerties among the worst. Coverage has improved in places since, but you should test your own address.

Phone answering usually depends on the building’s own internet connection at the door station and on the tenant’s phone having data or Wi-Fi. If the building has wired internet and tenants have Wi-Fi indoors, phone answering can work even where cell signal is weak. Cellular call boxes, which use a mobile network instead of internet at the door, are the weakest choice in a dead zone.

In those areas we treat wired handsets or monitors as the main answering method and phone answering as an extra.

Fiber just reached our Kingston street. Should the new entry system run over the internet?

Fiber makes cloud-managed and app-based entry practical where it was not before. Archtop Fiber, headquartered in Kingston, has turned on service in Kingston and Saugerties and has been building in Woodstock; the county’s broadband programme was cleared in 2026 to bring fiber to its last 1,293 unserved locations.

Running entry over the internet brings remote administration, visitor logs and phone answering. It also brings a dependency: if the router is off or the account lapses, the features that need the cloud stop. We set systems so that the door station, the apartment stations and door release keep working locally when the internet is down, and only the remote features pause.

If the system needs a network connection, we give it its own port or network segment, not a spot on a tenant’s home Wi-Fi.

Shops, Short-Term Rentals and Mixed Buildings

How do you separate the store entrance from the apartment entrance on a Main Street building?

By giving residents a door the store does not control. In many Saugerties, Kingston and Ellenville buildings the stair to the upper floors starts at its own street door beside the shop; that door gets its own station and strike, and the shop has none of its codes.

Where the only way upstairs is through the shop, the job is harder. One option is a locked door at the foot of the stair inside the shop, with its own station, so residents can reach it after hours through a separate exterior door if the layout allows. When it does not, we look at an access-controlled path with schedules, which our Ulster access control page covers.

If the shop does need to admit deliveries for residents, a schedule can let staff release the residential door during opening hours only, with each release logged.

Our Rondout building’s basement floods at the highest tides. Where should the power supply go?

Not in the basement. Kingston’s East Strand and North Street waterfront floods often enough that the city won a $1.5 million federal grant in 2024 to raise the streets, and a power supply, battery and controller on a basement wall are the first parts of an entry system to be lost when water comes in.

We mount them at ground-floor level or above: in a hall closet, a utility cupboard by the stair, or a lockable box high on a first-floor wall. The run from there to the strike and door station is short in most Rondout buildings, because the entry is on the ground floor anyway.

If cable has to pass through the basement, it runs high along the joists rather than near the floor, and any junction is kept above the worst water line the owner has seen. After a flood we check the strike and lower door station for corrosion before anything else.

Our tenants are mostly older residents. What makes an entry panel easier for them to use?

Big, well-spaced buttons with clear labels; a handset or speaker loud enough without being turned up all the way; a light over the panel; and a release time long enough for a slower walker to reach the door after pressing release. On many systems that time is adjustable.

Mounting height matters. The 2010 ADA Standards limit the high forward reach to 48 inches above the floor, and we keep call buttons at or below that height on public entrances. For residents with hearing loss, a video monitor with a visual call indicator helps more than a louder chime.

Keep the answering method simple. A wired handset by the apartment door is often easier than an app for residents who do not want a smartphone involved.

Six entrances on one garden-apartment complex, one controller

Each entrance needs its own door station and release, but they do not need separate systems. Many multi-tenant controllers handle several entrances, and Aiphone describes its GT Series as expandable to as many as 5,000 tenant stations. A central controller with a station at each entrance keeps one directory and one set of codes for the whole complex.

The practical questions are cable routes between buildings and power. If the entrances are in separate buildings across a courtyard, we look for existing conduit, basement passages or the original phone or intercom routes before planning anything new.

Owners can stage the work: replace the busiest entrance first on the new controller, then add the others as budget allows. We price each entrance as its own line for that reason.

A local building system versus ADT, Ring and SimpliSafe at a shared Ulster door

National home-security brands sell mainly to one household at a time. Ring’s and SimpliSafe’s video doorbells, and ADT’s doorbell and alarm packages, are built around a single family’s front door and a single account. That is the right tool for a single-family house in Hurley or a cottage in Woodstock, and we install doorbell cameras for those owners on our Ulster doorbell camera page.

At a shared door in a divided house or a walk-up they fall short. A single doorbell rings one account, so the tenant upstairs and the tenant downstairs either share an app login or miss each other’s visitors, and a doorbell does not release an electric strike for whichever apartment the visitor wants. A multi-tenant system gives each apartment its own button, station and release.

A local system also has no required monthly contract: the owner keeps the equipment, the wiring and the codes. AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Door Buzzers in Ulster County

We could not open live Google AI Overviews in our research environment, so this section takes the claims that national cost guides and vendor blogs repeat, and that search summaries tend to echo, and checks them against Ulster buildings. Each claim is quoted or summarised from the source listed at the bottom of this page.

“An intercom system costs about $2,500 installed.”

HomeAdvisor gives an average of $2,500 for installing an intercom system, with a range from about $1,500 to $15,000. Read closely, that guide is about home intercoms: stations in several rooms of one house, a gate intercom, a whole-house system for five rooms.

A multi-tenant buzzer is a different product. The cost is driven by the number of apartments, the number of entrances, the condition of the wire to each flat and the lock work on the door. A two-family Kingston house reusing good bell wire can come in well under that average; a six-entrance garden complex will be far over it.

Our Ulster list starts at $500 for small audio or doorbell-style entry, $900 for video panels and $3,000 for building systems, and every figure becomes a written quote after the walkthrough.

“Wireless intercoms are the easy do-it-yourself option.”

HomeAdvisor says wireless systems are the best option for DIY, and for talking between rooms in one house they can be. They are a poor fit for a shared building entrance for two reasons.

First, the door release. An electric strike needs wired power and a wired release signal, so even a “wireless” entry system has wires at the door. Second, radio and Wi-Fi do not pass easily through stone and brick walls, which many of Ulster’s older buildings have.

Where wire is hard to run, we look first at reusing the bell wire already in the building, which is usually more reliable than a radio link.

“New York law requires an intercom in every apartment building.”

The source of this is Section 50-a of the Multiple Dwelling Law, which requires intercoms in certain buildings of eight or more apartments. That law applies to cities of 325,000 or more, which in practice means New York City, unless a smaller place adopts it by local law.

Ulster’s cities, towns and villages fall under the Multiple Residence Law. It regulates multiple dwellings from three families up but is a different statute with different sections, so blanket statements about “New York’s intercom law” can mislead owners here.

That cuts both ways: no automatic NYC mandate, but local codes, leases and, in Kingston’s stabilized buildings, service rules still apply. Check with your building department.

“Cloud intercoms need no building wiring.”

Some vendors describe their entry panels as requiring no building wiring, meaning no cable to each apartment because tenants answer on their phones. That part can be true.

The panel still needs power, a network connection and a wired connection to the strike or lock, and phones still need data or Wi-Fi to answer. In parts of Ulster where cell signal is weak or a building’s internet is unreliable, a phone-only system can leave visitors standing at a panel that rings nobody.

Cloud systems work well in Kingston buildings with good internet. Elsewhere we combine them with wired stations, so answering does not depend on one connection.

“A magnetic lock is the simplest way to lock the front door.”

Magnetic locks are simple to mount, which is why they appear in so many quick guides. They are not simple to make legal on an exit door: the codes based on the International Building Code require release devices, release on power loss and release on fire alarm where present.

They are also fail-safe by nature. Allegion notes that magnetic locks are only available fail-safe, so the door is unlocked whenever power is out, and Ulster sees long outages.

On a residential front door with a lever lockset, a fail-secure electric strike is simpler, stays locked in an outage and always lets people out.

“Software fees are only a few dollars per apartment.”

Swiftlane’s cost guide puts cloud software at $5 to $15 per unit per month, or $30 to $100 per entry door per month, depending on the platform. For a large complex that may be small next to other costs.

For a four-unit Ulster house, the arithmetic is different: at $10 per unit per month, four units pay $480 a year, every year, which can exceed the hardware cost within a few years.

If the features justify it, fine. If the owner only needs talk and release, a local system with no subscription is often the better value, and we say so.

“Old buzzer wiring always has to be ripped out.”

Many guides assume an upgrade means new cable. Manufacturers of two-wire systems say the opposite, designing them to reuse existing wire, and owners on installer forums describe keeping a working system’s cable and repairing only damaged runs.

What matters is testing. Each pair is measured for continuity, shorts and length before we decide.

In Ulster’s plastered and stone buildings, reusing wire that passes is often the difference between a short job and weeks of wall repair.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Test the Bell Wire Before Anyone Picks a Panel

This is how we start in an old divided house, written as practice rather than as a story about any one building. Before talking about brands, someone opens the existing doorbell or buzzer at the front door, finds the pair that goes to each apartment, and meters it: continuity to the flat, resistance over the length, and whether it is shorted to anything else. It takes perhaps half an hour for a three-family house.

That half hour decides most of the quote. When every pair passes, the job is a door station, a strike, a power supply and a station in each flat, all on the existing wire. When one pair fails, we know which apartment needs a new run and can look for the route up front, rather than discovering it with a drill on the day.

We also look at the door itself: whether the closer pulls it shut from fully open, whether the latch reaches the strike plate, and whether the frame is solid enough to take an electric strike. A surprising share of “broken buzzer” calls in old houses turn out to be doors that no longer latch. We write all of it on the plan, so the owner sees why the quote says what it says.

FAQ

Ulster County Door Buzzer FAQ

Does a new entry system need its own electrical outlet?
+

Usually yes. The power supply for the stations and the electric strike plugs into, or is wired to, a 120-volt source, ideally one that is not shared with a tenant’s apartment so a tenant cannot switch it off. If there is no suitable outlet near the entry, a county-licensed electrician adds one before or during our visit.

Can a tenant add their own smart doorbell at the apartment door upstairs?
+

Usually yes, with the owner’s permission, since it is the tenant’s own door. It does not replace the building entry: the visitor still has to get through the shared front door first. We can make the building system and an apartment doorbell work side by side so they do not ring at the same time.

How long does the front door stay unlocked after someone presses release?
+

Only as long as the system is set to hold the strike, usually a few seconds, which is enough to push the door open. On most systems that time is adjustable, so buildings with older residents or heavy doors can allow longer. Once the door closes, the latch catches again.

Can couriers get into the hallway to leave packages when nobody is home?
+

Some systems allow a separate delivery code or a scheduled release, and cloud systems can let a tenant open the door remotely for a courier. Whether to allow it is the owner’s decision, because it lets someone into the shared hall unattended; many owners prefer a locked package box or a camera on the hall.

What do you need from us before the walkthrough?
+

The address, the number of apartments, a photo of the front door from outside, the location of the basement or utility closet, and whether the building has wired internet. If there is an old buzzer, a photo of its panel helps us find the right parts.

Why does our door still not lock after the buzzer was replaced?
+

Because the door is not latching. A worn closer that stops before the door is shut, a frame that has moved, or a latch that no longer reaches the strike plate all leave the door open no matter what the buzzer does. We adjust or replace the closer and realign the strike as part of the job.

Can one apartment’s calls ring two roommates’ phones at once?
+

On systems with phone answering, one apartment button can usually ring several phones in an account, so whichever roommate is home can answer and release the door. On wired-only systems a second handset can be added inside the same flat.

Do you take out the old panel and patch the opening?
+

Yes. The old station, transformer and dead wiring we no longer need come out, and the opening is covered with the new station, a trim plate or a patch suited to the wall. On masonry we patch with a material compatible with the existing mortar or stone surround.

What does the $300 service call booked online cover?
+

A visit to diagnose an existing buzzer, strike or power supply and make simple repairs. If you go ahead with a larger repair or a new system, the $300 is applied toward that work.

Will the new system work if the building’s internet is off?
+

We set it up so calling, talking and door release between the front door and each apartment keep working locally. Only features that need the internet, such as phone answering and remote administration, pause until the connection returns.
