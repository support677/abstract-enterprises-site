The Neighborhood

Why Flatbush Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Flatbush has spent most of the last decade catching up on the connection at the curb. When Untapped Cities ranked Brooklyn neighborhoods by measured internet speed, using tests recorded between May 2016 and April 2017, Flatbush came in 40th of the 43 rows in its table, with a 90th-percentile average of 74.3 Mbps. Prospect Lefferts Gardens, at its northern edge, sat one place lower at 65.58 Mbps, and East Flatbush was 37th. Those numbers are old, and the article itself called its ZIP-code grouping "not exact." The picture today is different. BestNeighborhood's summary of the FCC's Broadband Data Collection, current to December 31, 2025, lists Verizon fiber as available to 92.73 percent of Flatbush and Ditmas Park, Optimum fiber to 73.50 percent, and smaller providers such as Starry and Astound in parts of the area. For most addresses, a fast plan can now be bought.

That shifts the problem indoors. A fiber terminal mounted on a cellar wall, or a provider's box inside the coat closet of a fifth-floor apartment, delivers the speed to one spot. From there, every device in the building is either on a cable or on Wi-Fi, and in Flatbush buildings Wi-Fi has a hard time. The six-story apartment houses along Ocean Avenue and the side streets off Church Avenue were built in the 1920s and 1930s with masonry walls, plaster on block partitions and long hallways, and an apartment in one of them shares the air with dozens of neighbors' routers. The detached Victorians of Prospect Park South, Ditmas Park and Fiske Terrace have three floors, an attic, and sometimes a carriage house or a garage that someone now uses as a studio. The two- and three-story storefront buildings along Flatbush, Nostrand and Rogers Avenues put a business, its customers' phones, and one or two apartments above it on the same block of masonry. None of these was built with a path for data.

The calls we get from Flatbush fall into a few recognizable patterns. A renter on Ocean Avenue works from the back bedroom and the video call freezes because the provider installed the router at the front door. A family in a Victorian on Westminster Road or Marlborough Road has mesh units on every floor and still loses the signal in the attic office. A landlord with a walk-up on a side street wants cameras in the halls and an entry panel at the door, and needs a network for them that is not any tenant's Wi-Fi. Each of those is a cabling problem before it is a Wi-Fi problem, and each needs a different route and a different person's permission.

Flatbush buildings are also changing hands and being worked on. New rental buildings are going up along the main avenues; New York YIMBY reported in January 2026 that the four-building Bedford Square complex was nearing completion and that foundation work had started at 757 Flatbush Avenue, where a financing package closed in June 2026. In the older stock the opportunity comes with a renovation: a gut job in a co-op apartment, a new kitchen in a Victorian, a storefront fit-out after a vacancy. The city's 2022 assessment counted 1,662 storefronts on the main Flatbush corridors with a vacancy rate of 10.6 percent, and every one of those that turns over is a space where cable is cheapest to install on the day the ceiling is open.

There is a practical reason to plan the cabling rather than leave it to whoever is in the building that day. A Brooklyn homeowner who gut-renovated a house and asked the Brownstoner forum how to wire it for Ethernet heard the same caution from several people: ask hard questions about the cable grade, the testing and the outlets, because plenty of tradespeople can pull a cable but fewer terminate and test it properly. One commenter noted that his own electrician would pull data and coax but would not do the terminations. Another gave advice we still repeat on Flatbush surveys: "pick a good central location like a basement" for the network's hub. The work that makes a network last, the route, the termination, the labeling and the test, is the part that gets skipped when nobody is responsible for it.

Hall Cameras Offline, a Shop Network Dead After a Fit-Out, or a Riser Nobody Can Read? Same-Day Estimates in Flatbush

Repair calls in Flatbush usually follow another trade or another provider. A plumber opens a cellar ceiling in an Ocean Avenue building to reach a leak and pulls the bundle that feeds the hall cameras off its hooks. In a Victorian house the call is more often about one floor that lost its connection after an electrician rewired a room, or a jack that never worked since the previous owner's renovation.

Finding the fault is a sequence, not a guess. A tone generator on the dead jack and a probe at the panel tell us which cable we are chasing. A certifier tells us whether the cable still passes and roughly how far along its length the fault sits, and a look at that spot usually shows the cause: a staple through the jacket, a run crushed under a new floor, a connector that pulled out of its jack, water damage near a cellar pipe. You get a short written list of what can be re-terminated on the spot, what needs a new pull, and what is dead cable that should come out so it stops confusing the next person. If another contractor caused the damage, the findings are written down so you can take it up with them. Call (347) 934-8335 and tell us what stopped working and when. We book the conversation within 2 hours and give a same-day estimate wherever the problem allows one.

One request we get often enough to mention: an owner who has just bought a Flatbush building and found the cellar closet full of wire with no labels and no records. Before any new work, we tone and test what is there and give the owner a list, cable by cable, of what is live, what serves which floor or tenant, and what is abandoned. Structured Cabling for Flatbush's Architecture

Flatbush mixes building types more than most Brooklyn neighborhoods. On a walk from Ocean Avenue to Coney Island Avenue you can pass a 1930s elevator building, a row of detached Victorians with porches and turrets, a block of two-family houses and a two-story storefront building with apartments upstairs. For a cabling crew the useful questions are the same for all of them: where service enters, which way is up, how far each run goes, and who besides the person who called has to agree. The cards below give the short version; the sections after them explain how we work in each.

The four- and five-story walk-up

Brick walk-ups on the side streets off Church and Flatbush Avenues, with a stair, a cellar closet and an owner who controls the common areas.

The detached Victorian

Brick and frame houses holding an owner and a tenant, and attached rows such as those in Lefferts Manor at the northern edge, with one cellar and shared walls.

The prewar elevator apartment house: one old riser, many apartments, one owner

The apartment houses along Ocean Avenue were built to carry telephone lines, not data. Most have a vertical path the original builders left for the phone company, a closet or chase on each floor near the stair or the elevator, or a pipe that carries the old telephone pairs up the building. Over the decades every cable and fiber provider that served the building has added its own lines to that path, often without removing the old ones. When an owner asks us for a building network for cameras, an entry panel or common-area Wi-Fi, the first job is to find out how much room that riser still has and whether anything in it can be removed.

Where the riser is full or unusable, there are other honest routes. A dumbwaiter shaft that has been closed off, a pipe chase beside the bathrooms, the space behind a stair stringer, or an elevator machine room that connects to the roof can each carry a sleeve. Where none of those exists, painted surface raceway up a stair hall is often the right answer, and we say so at the survey instead of promising a hidden route through solid masonry. Every opening we make between floors is sealed with listed firestop as soon as the cable is through, because in a building with dozens of apartments a hole between floors is a path for smoke.

Inside one apartment in a building like this, the job is smaller but has its own limits. The partitions are usually plaster on clay or gypsum block, so a cable cannot be fished down a wall cavity the way it can in a wood-framed house. The provider has left one outlet, often in the living room or the foyer, and the bedrooms are at the end of a long hall. We use door casings, the tops of baseboards, closets and, where the owner or board allows, a shallow channel that a plasterer closes. Nothing goes into the building's hallway or riser without the owner's written agreement.

The four- and five-story walk-up: a stair, a cellar and a landlord

The walk-ups on the side streets between Church Avenue and Caton Avenue, and those on the blocks east of Flatbush Avenue, are smaller versions of the same problem without the elevator. Service arrives in the cellar, often in a small closet by the meters or on the wall beside the boiler. The building's own equipment, a camera recorder, an entry panel, a laundry card system, a superintendent's computer, needs a network that belongs to the building and is not anyone's apartment Wi-Fi. We build that network in the cellar with its own cabinet, its own labels and its own provider connection if the owner wants one.

Getting from the cellar to the upper landings usually means following the stair. In many walk-ups the stair hall has a chase or a closet at each landing, or a pipe space beside the stair that runs the height of the building. Where it does not, a raceway along the ceiling line of the stair hall, painted to match, carries the cables to each floor's camera and to the roof if an access point or a provider's antenna needs it. A small battery backup in the cellar cabinet keeps cameras and the entry panel running through short power interruptions, which matters in a building where the super is not always on site.

The detached Victorian: three floors, an attic and a historic district outside

The freestanding houses of Victorian Flatbush are the closest thing the neighborhood has to a suburban job, with one important difference: many sit inside a historic district. Inside, they are wood-framed, which means interior wall cavities can often be fished from the cellar or from the attic down, and the large footprint means one access point rarely covers a floor. A typical plan puts a small panel in the cellar near the service entry, a cable up to each floor through an interior wall or a closet stack, two access points on the larger floors, and jacks at the desks and TVs. The attic, which many owners use as an office or a teenager's room, gets its own cable rather than a mesh unit relaying through two floors.

What makes these houses slower than they look is everything that has happened to them in a hundred years or more. Walls have been insulated, rooms have been divided and joined, pocket doors and built-ins hide the cavities we would like to use, and original woodwork, stained glass and plaster moldings are things owners rightly do not want disturbed. We test before we promise, with a small inspection opening inside a closet or a look from the cellar under the wall in question. Outside, the rule is simple: no cable on a front facade, and nothing visible added to a house in a historic district without checking the Commission's requirements first. We cover that in the rules section below.

The two-family and the attached row: one cellar, two households

A large share of Flatbush households live in two-family houses or in a converted floor of a larger one. The building usually has one service entry in a cellar the owner controls, and each household may have its own provider account, or the owner may include internet in the rent. Either way, each apartment needs a clean path from the cellar to its own jack or access point location, and shared equipment has to sit where the owner can reach it without going through a tenant's rooms. We plan the cellar as the meeting point: each apartment gets its own labeled runs back to a panel, and each household's equipment is kept separate.

In the attached rows at the northern edge of Flatbush, including the row houses of Lefferts Manor that Wikipedia describes as possibly the neighborhood's earliest, the side walls are shared masonry, so the vertical route is through the interior: a stacked closet, a plumbing chase or the stair wall. We do not drill into a party wall to bury a cable, and we never route through a neighbor's cellar or roof without the neighbor's written consent.

The storefront building with apartments above: a shop's cellar is everyone's cellar

The Cohn Brothers and other builders put up two- and three-story tapestry-brick storefront buildings from 1912 onward, with a store on the ground floor and one or two apartments above, and Brownstoner notes they are common along Nostrand, Flatbush and Rogers Avenues south of Eastern Parkway. In a building like this the shop, the apartments and sometimes the landlord's own equipment all draw service from the same cellar. A cabling job for the shop has to stay inside the shop's space and its share of the cellar, and anything that crosses into the apartments' areas needs the landlord's agreement.

The opposite request comes from the landlord: a network for the building that serves cameras at the storefront and the apartment door, an entry panel, and perhaps a shared connection for the upstairs tenants. We separate those clearly in the cellar, with the shop's equipment in its own lockable cabinet, the building's equipment in another, and each tenant's service on its own labeled cable. When the shop changes hands, as storefronts on these corridors often do, the next tenant inherits a cellar they can understand.

The new rental building: the developer's design and the renter's apartment

New buildings like Bedford Square and the project at 757 Flatbush Avenue are designed with telecom rooms, risers and conduit to each unit, and the building's provider arrangement is set by the owner. A renter in one of them rarely needs, or is allowed, new cable inside concrete. What we are asked for instead is a wired connection from the unit's outlet to a desk in the second bedroom, a better spot for the router than the floor of a closet, or an access point in a long apartment where the provider's router sits at one end. Those are done with surface raceway or a short door-casing route, removable at the end of the lease.

For developers and general contractors, the right moment to bring in a cabling contractor is before the walls close. HPD's residential broadband guidance says in-unit broadband is required in all new construction it finances and common-area broadband in all new construction and some preservation projects, so for an HPD-financed building the cabling scope belongs in the first drawings. We build to the program's design guidelines and coordinate with the electrician, who handles the power for the telecom room.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The First Ten Minutes in a Flatbush Apartment House Cellar

When an owner asks us to put cameras and a building network into a prewar Flatbush apartment house, the survey starts in the same place every time: the cellar closet where the providers' lines come in. We count what is there. In a building that has been served by a telephone company, two or three generations of cable television and at least one fiber provider, there are often several boxes on the wall, some live and some long dead, with no labels. We note which ones carry service today and photograph everything before touching anything.

Then we follow the riser up. On each floor we open the closet or chase door, look at how full the path is and check whether there is room for a sleeve. We look at where the cameras need to be, at the lobby door, each landing and the cellar entrance, and measure the distance back to the riser on each floor. Copper network cable has a length limit for a single run, and in a long building with the riser at one end, a far camera can come close to it. That is something to know before the quote, not on the day.

Last, we ask the superintendent two questions that save trouble later: who else has keys to the closet, and which contractors have been in the building this year. The answers tell us whether the cabinet needs a lock and whether anyone else is likely to move our cables. By the end of the survey we can draw the route floor by floor, mark every opening, and write the quote.

Pathways

From the Cellar Closet to the Sixth-Floor Landing, and From the Front Parlor to the Attic: Routing Cable Through Flatbush Buildings

Two routes account for most of the planning on Flatbush jobs. In apartment buildings it is the vertical one, from the cellar closet to the top floor, with a branch to each landing. In detached houses it is the long diagonal from the cellar at the front of the house to an attic or a third-floor rear room. Neither follows a straight line. A good route goes up somewhere convenient and then across somewhere hidden, and choosing those two places is most of the design work.

Going up in an apartment house, the best routes are, in order: an existing riser with room to spare; a riser that can be cleared by removing dead cable left by earlier providers; a closed dumbwaiter or a pipe chase; the space behind the stair; and, last, surface raceway in the stair hall. Clearing an old riser is often the best value on the job, because the path already reaches every floor. We tone and test what is in it, confirm with the owner which providers are still in service, and remove only what is clearly dead. If there is any doubt about a cable, it stays and gets a label saying so.

Going up in a Victorian, the options are wider because the walls are wood-framed: an interior wall cavity from the cellar ceiling to the second floor, a closet stack, a chase beside the chimney or the plumbing, or the space beside a back stair. The difficulty is the second leg, from the second floor to the third and the attic, where the walls are often smaller and the route has to cross a ceiling. We often go in from the attic and fish down, which avoids opening finished plaster on the floor below.

Going across in an apartment, the cable has to travel from the provider's outlet to the rooms at the far end of the hall. In prewar Flatbush apartments the floors are often concrete or tile over masonry, so there is no joist space below to use. That leaves door casings, baseboards, the space above a closet, and painted raceway at the ceiling line. In a co-op where the board allows it, a shallow channel cut into a partition and closed by a plasterer gives a hidden route; in a rental, raceway is the honest answer and comes out when the tenant leaves.

Openings sealed as the work goes

Every hole between floors or through a fire-rated wall is a path for smoke, air, pests and sound. We seal each one as soon as the cable is through, with listed firestop where it passes between floors or from the cellar into the building, and with a bushing where it passes metal. Where a cable leaves a building, for a garage, a carriage house or a yard camera, it goes through one sealed opening with a drip loop outside and surge protection where it enters. No opening is left open overnight.

Data kept apart from steam, power and the old intercom

Flatbush buildings share their walls and cellars with steam pipes, gas lines, electrical wiring and, in many apartment houses, an old audio intercom whose wires run up the same riser. We keep data cable clear of hot pipes and boiler flues, cross electrical lines at right angles where they must meet, never pull data through the same holes as new power wiring, and route around existing intercom and alarm wires rather than through them. Our cables get labels, so the next person who opens the closet knows which ones are the network.

Service Entry

Verizon, Optimum or the Building's Provider: Where Flatbush Service Arrives and Where the Network Should Start

Before we plan a run, we need to know how service reaches the building, because that decides where the network begins. In most Flatbush houses the provider's line comes in at the front of the cellar, either through the foundation from the street or down from a pole and along the facade. Verizon's fiber service ends at a terminal on the wall; Optimum and other cable-based services end at a splitter and a modem. In an apartment house, the providers' lines arrive in the cellar closet and travel up the riser to each apartment that subscribes, ending at an outlet the installer chose, usually the closest one to the riser.

That is why so many Flatbush apartments have the router on the floor of the foyer closet and the slow room at the far end of the hall. Structured cabling separates the two. The provider's equipment stays where the line arrives, one cable carries the service to a better central spot, and the network spreads from there. In an apartment, the central spot may simply be the hallway ceiling halfway down the apartment, where one access point can reach every room. In a Victorian house it is usually the cellar under the middle of the house, directly below the route that goes up.

Having two fiber providers in much of the neighborhood is an advantage worth planning for. If every room's cable comes back to one panel, changing from one provider to another only changes the first link between the provider's box and the router; nothing inside the walls moves. For a business that cannot be offline, both services can be brought to the same cabinet, with a router that switches to the second line when the first drops. We also recommend leaving a spare cable, or an empty conduit with a pull string, from the service entry to the panel, so the next installer uses it instead of drilling another hole through the front of the building.

The building's wiring and the building's deals

In apartment buildings, how service reaches each unit is often set by an agreement between the owner and a provider. HPD's broadband guidance summarizes the federal rules that now apply: exclusive revenue-sharing deals between a landlord and a provider are banned, so are graduated revenue-share deals tied to how many units subscribe, and so are arrangements where a provider sells a building's inside wiring to the owner and leases it back. A provider with an exclusive marketing arrangement has to disclose it. For an owner, the practical point is that well-documented inside wiring, with a labeled riser and a clear port map, is an asset that keeps the building's options open with every provider that comes knocking.

Getting a provider into a building in the first place can depend on the owner. Reporting on Brooklyn buildings without Fios in 2015, Technical.ly quoted a Verizon spokesman saying that in some cases landlords had not wanted the company to bring fiber into their buildings, and calling building access "the challenge." That article was about Sunset Park, not Flatbush, but the dynamic is the same here: a tenant cannot order a provider's wiring into a building's riser, and an owner who wants fiber for the building has to grant access. A cabling contractor can make that easier by giving the provider a clean riser path and a labeled place to land.

Rules and Licenses

Permits, Licenses, Landmarks, Leases and Boards: What Governs a Flatbush Cabling Job

The electrical permit question. Local Law 128 of 2024 added section 28-105.4.7 to the city's Administrative Code. Its text says an electrical permit "shall not be required for the installation, alteration, maintenance, or repair of electrical wiring that is designed to operate at less than 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm, and data transmission circuits," provided the work is done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a "qualified person" as defined in the city's electrical code. Network cabling fits that description. The same section keeps wiring connected to life-safety systems, and control circuits that switch light, heat or power, with master or special electricians. In plain terms: no electrical permit for the network itself, but fire alarm wiring is not part of a network job, and any new outlet or circuit for a rack or cabinet is an electrician's work.

Our license. We work under New York State license #12000287431 and carry insurance for low-voltage work. A certificate of insurance goes to the owner, the board or the managing agent before the crew arrives, naming whoever the building asks us to name.

Landmarks. Victorian Flatbush includes five historic districts: Albemarle-Kenmore Terraces, designated in 1978; Prospect Park South, in 1979; Ditmas Park, in 1981; and Fiske Terrace and Midwood Park, both in 2008. For a house inside one of them, the Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews most changes to the outside, which includes a new provider line on the facade, a cable down a front wall or a camera on a porch. The Commission's permit guidebook says conduit for such equipment should be concealed by going through plain masonry or cladding behind the device, that staff may allow approximately ten inches of visible conduit along a wall surface where concealment is not possible, that equipment generally should not go on a primary facade if another location exists, and that surface conduit should be painted to match and secured into mortar joints. Interior cabling is generally not reviewed. We plan the job so nothing visible appears on a landmarked front without the right approval, and we check the address on the Commission's map first.

Leases. Most Flatbush households rent. A tenant needs the landlord's written permission before anything is drilled or fastened into a wall, and we will not drill a rental apartment without it. Where permission is not available, we plan work that needs none: raceway held with removable fasteners, a cable along a door casing, an access point on a shelf or a removable mount. Commercial tenants need the same written approval for any work in their space, and more so for anything in the cellar.

Boards and managing agents. In a co-op or condo the alteration agreement decides what may be opened, when work may happen and what insurance is needed. For cabling inside one apartment the paperwork is usually modest: a scope letter describing each opening, a certificate of insurance and work during the permitted hours. Anything that touches the riser or the hallway needs the board or managing agent's written agreement.

Tenants in owner-occupied houses. An owner of a two-family house needs to give the tenant proper notice before work inside the tenant's apartment, and we schedule around that notice.

This section is a plain-language summary of the rules as we understand them for planning a job, not legal advice. For a specific building, the lease, the board's documents and the Commission's staff have the final word.

Compliance notes for Flatbush businesses and buildings

  • Card payments. Card readers and registers go on their own network segment, separate from guest Wi-Fi and staff devices, which is what payment processors expect of a business that takes cards.
  • Cable ratings. Cable above a ceiling used to return air for heating or cooling must be rated for that space, and cable between floors must be rated for vertical runs. The rating is written on the quote.
  • Firestopping. Every opening between a storefront and the apartments above, and through any fire-rated wall or floor, is sealed with listed firestop the same day.
  • Accessible counters. Card readers and any device a customer uses at a counter are placed where a customer using a wheelchair can reach them, which decides where the cable has to arrive.
  • Fire alarm separation. Data cable does not share raceway with fire alarm wiring, and fire alarm work is not part of a network installation; it has its own licensing and permits.
  • Landlord approval. With most Flatbush merchants renting, written landlord approval for drilling, cellar work and anything on the facade is settled before we book the crew.
Your Questions Answered

Structured Cabling in Flatbush: Questions Answered

These are the situations Flatbush renters, homeowners, building owners and businesses describe when they call. They are grouped by the kind of property, and each is answered once here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Renters and shared homes

The provider put our only outlet in the foyer closet of our Ocean Avenue apartment, and the lease says no holes in the walls. What can be done for the back bedroom?

Quite a lot, without a single hole. A flat cable in a slim paintable raceway can run from the closet along the top of the baseboard or the edge of the ceiling to the back bedroom, held with fasteners that come out cleanly, and it can cross a doorway inside the casing trim rather than across the floor. At the bedroom end it finishes at a surface-mounted jack for a desk or at an access point in the hallway that covers the far rooms. Where even that is ruled out, a wireless bridge between two small units can carry a connection to the back room, though a cable always performs better. We show your landlord exactly what will be fastened where, and many agree once they see it comes out without a mark.

We rent rooms in our house near Brooklyn College to students who all take classes online. How should the network be set up so one person's downloads don't stall everyone else's lecture?

Start with a cable to each floor that ends at a ceiling access point, so nobody is relaying signal through two floors of plaster. Then give each room a wired jack at the desk, because a laptop on a cable takes itself off the shared wireless air entirely. On the router, a quality-of-service setting can keep video calls ahead of large downloads, and a separate network for the house's own devices, a camera, a thermostat, the owner's computer, keeps renters from seeing them. Each tenant's jack is labeled by room at the cellar panel, so when someone says their connection is down you can check their cable without entering their room.

Victorian houses

Our Prospect Park South house sits in a historic district, and we want an access point and a camera on the front porch. Does the cable need the Landmarks Commission's approval?

Anything new fixed to the outside of a house in a historic district generally does, and a porch on the street side is part of the front. The Commission's guidebook asks for conduit to be concealed by passing through the wall or cladding directly behind the device, allows only about ten inches of visible conduit where that is impossible, and steers equipment away from the primary facade where another spot works. In practice we bring the cable up through the porch floor or the ceiling from inside, keep the device small and painted to match, and file for a staff-level permit where one is needed. Interior cabling inside the same house is generally not the Commission's concern.

The third-floor room in our Ditmas Park Victorian is now a home office, and the router is in the cellar. What is the least disruptive way to get a cable up three floors?

Usually two legs instead of one. The first goes from the cellar ceiling up an interior wall cavity or a closet stack to the second floor, which in a wood-framed house can often be fished without opening finished walls. The second goes from the attic down, because coming in from above lets us drop into the third-floor wall without crossing the second-floor ceiling. Where the third floor has knee walls, the space behind them is often a clear path to the right corner. We confirm each leg with a small inspection opening inside a closet before we commit, and the quote says where every opening will be and who patches it.

The old garage behind our house is now a painting studio. How does a network connection get across the driveway?

The most reliable way is a cable rated for direct burial or run in buried conduit, from the house's cellar wall to the studio, with surge protection where it enters each building. Where digging is not possible, an aerial run on a messenger wire between the house and the garage works, as long as it stays clear of power lines and does not cross onto a neighbor's property. For runs longer than copper allows, a short fiber link with a small converter at each end does the job. A pair of outdoor wireless bridges is the no-dig option, mounted where they can see each other, and in a historic district the visible part of the house end gets the same care as any other exterior device.

Apartment building owners

Our six-story building has its riser at one end, and we want a camera at the far end of every hallway. Can a single cable reach that far?

Usually, but it has to be measured, not assumed. A standard copper network link is limited to about 100 meters, roughly 328 feet, including the short patch cords at each end, and in a long prewar building the run from the cellar closet up the riser and down a sixth-floor hall can come closer to that than people expect. We measure the actual route at the survey. Where a far camera would go past the limit, the answer is a small switch on that floor fed from the cellar, or a fiber link up the riser to a switch closet on an upper floor, with each floor's cameras running from there. Either way the quote states the length of every run.

Several providers have served our building over the decades and the riser is packed with old lines. Can the dead ones come out to make room?

Yes, carefully. We tone every cable in the riser from the cellar end, identify which belong to providers still serving tenants, and confirm with you which accounts are active before anything is cut. Cable that is clearly abandoned, with no service and no far-end equipment, can be removed floor by floor, and the space it frees often makes the riser usable again without a new opening. Anything we are not sure about stays in place with a tag saying what we found. Clearing a riser this way costs less than opening a new path through masonry, and it leaves the building with a record of what is in it.

Shops, offices and congregations

We just signed a lease on a vacant storefront on Church Avenue. At what point in the fit-out should the cabling go in?

Right after the walls are framed and before the ceiling and the drywall close. At that stage every cable to the counter, the back office, the kitchen and the ceiling access points can be pulled in an afternoon with nothing to patch. Before that, decide where the register, the card readers, any printer, a back-office computer, screens and cameras will go, because those positions decide where each cable ends. Ask the landlord now where the provider's line enters the building and whether the cellar space that comes with your lease includes the wall where it lands. Bringing a cabling contractor in after the ceiling is finished usually means opening it again.

Our salon has a card reader and a tablet at every station. Should those be wired or wireless?

The card readers and the front desk should be wired where the reader supports it, because a payment that hangs while a client waits costs more than the cable. Tablets at the stations can stay wireless, served by a ceiling access point placed in the middle of the room rather than a router at the front. The important part is separation: payment devices on their own network, staff tablets on another, and client Wi-Fi on a third with a speed limit, so a room full of clients streaming video under the dryers cannot slow down a payment. A small cabinet in the back holds the equipment out of reach of anyone walking through.

Our congregation wants to stream services from the sanctuary. What has to be cabled?

Three things, usually. A cable from each camera position, which is often a balcony rail or a bracket at the back wall, to the place where the streaming equipment sits; a wired connection for the sound desk, so the audio mix goes out with the picture; and a reliable wired link from that equipment to the building's internet, because a stream is the one thing that should never depend on Wi-Fi. If the cameras are the kind powered over their cable, no outlet is needed at the balcony. In an older church building we follow existing paths in the balcony structure and the ceiling, and we keep everything clear of historic woodwork and windows.

Questions particular to Flatbush

Flatbush used to test among the slowest neighborhoods in Brooklyn for internet. Is that still why our apartment feels slow?

Probably not. The well-known ranking, Untapped Cities' table built from tests between May 2016 and April 2017, put Flatbush 40th of 43 at a 90th-percentile average of 74.3 Mbps, but FCC data as of the end of 2025 shows fiber from Verizon available to about 93 percent of Flatbush and Ditmas Park and from Optimum to about 74 percent. If the first result is good and the second is poor, the line is fine and the apartment needs a cable or an access point closer to that room. If both are poor, the issue is your plan, the provider or the router, and we will tell you that rather than sell you cabling.

We own a storefront building with two apartments upstairs. Can the shop and the tenants share one internet connection safely?

They can share the line, but they should never share the network. With one connection in the cellar, we install a router or firewall that creates a separate segment for the shop, one for each apartment and one for the building's own cameras or entry panel, so no tenant can see the shop's payment devices and the shop cannot see the tenants. Each segment gets its own labeled cable from the cellar panel. You also need a plain way to restart the equipment without entering an apartment, and a decision about whether internet is part of the rent, which belongs in the lease. If the shop takes cards, many owners prefer to give it a separate provider account entirely.

Business is slower this year and costs are up. Which network work gives a small Flatbush shop the most for its money?

Wire the things that take money. A cable to each card reader and the register, a separate network for payments, and a guest network that cannot crowd them out fix the failures that cost sales on a busy day. Next is a single ceiling access point in the middle of the shop instead of the provider's router behind the counter. Cameras and a back-office upgrade can follow later on the same cabinet. Keep the provider's equipment if it is adequate, buy only what the quote lists, and ask for work outside opening hours so the shop never closes for it. That small first phase is often a single morning's work.

"Flatbush has some of the slowest internet in Brooklyn"

Owners in Prospect Park South, Ditmas Park, Albemarle-Kenmore Terraces, Fiske Terrace and Midwood Park are told this more often than they should be. A cable is small, but a new line down a front wall, a conduit across a porch or a device on a cornice is an exterior change.

"Expect to pay the national per-drop price from Angi, HomeAdvisor or Fixr"

AI answers lean heavily on national cost guides from sites like Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, which publish typical ranges for an Ethernet drop or a home network. Those ranges average jobs from very different buildings across the country.

In Flatbush the cost is driven by things the guides do not measure: whether an apartment house riser has room or must be cleared, whether a renter needs a removable route, whether a Victorian's third floor can be reached from the attic, whether a shop has to be cabled after hours.

That is why we quote every Flatbush job in writing at our Brooklyn rate after seeing the route, and list each jack and route so the quote can be compared line by line rather than against a national average.

"Network cabling needs no license at all," or "only an electrician may do it"

Both versions show up. The first misreads the city's permit change; the second is outdated. Since Local Law 128 of 2024, the Administrative Code says no electrical permit is required for wiring under 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data.

That same section requires the work to be done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person as the electrical code defines it, and keeps life-safety wiring and control circuits for light, heat or power with electricians.

For a Flatbush owner the practical meaning is: hire a licensed low-voltage contractor for the network, an electrician for any new outlet or circuit, and a fire alarm specialist for fire alarm wiring. We work under New York State license #12000287431.

"Old telephone risers in prewar buildings are useless"

Advice for older buildings often assumes new cable needs a new path. In Ocean Avenue apartment houses, the original telephone riser already reaches every floor, which makes it the most valuable route in the building.

The problem is that it is usually full of abandoned lines from earlier providers. Toning every cable, confirming which accounts are live and removing only what is clearly dead often frees enough room for a building network.

Opening a new vertical path through masonry costs far more and disturbs more apartments. A cleared, labeled riser also makes the next provider's installation simpler, which owners and tenants both benefit from.

"Run Cat6A or fiber everywhere to be future-proof"

Generic advice often recommends the heaviest cable available for every run. Cat6A is thicker and stiffer than Cat6, and in a crowded riser or a slim raceway along a prewar apartment's baseboard it fills the space quickly and is harder to route neatly.

For most apartment and house jacks, Cat6 serves today's devices well. Heavier cable earns its place on long runs, on links to access points expected to need very high speeds, and in business backbones.

Fiber makes the most sense where copper's length limit or interference is the problem: up a tall riser to an upper-floor switch, or out to a garage studio behind a Victorian. We choose the cable run by run and write the choice on the quote.

FAQ

Flatbush Structured Cabling FAQ

Can the cable or phone company wire our rooms while a separate contractor handles the network?
+

The provider's installer will bring service to one outlet and set up its own equipment, but wiring the rest of the home or building is not part of that visit. A cabling contractor carries the service from the provider's box to every room, terminates and tests each run, and labels it. The two work best when the provider's entry point and the network panel are planned together.

Verizon says fiber is available at our address, but there is no outlet in our apartment. Whose job is it to bring the line in?
+

Bringing the provider's own line from the street and up the riser to your apartment is the provider's work, and in an apartment building it depends on the owner having granted the provider access. Once the line reaches your apartment, a cabling contractor can carry it from the provider's terminal to the rooms you use.

The wall jacks in our apartment do not work. Is the problem the jack, the cable or the building?
+

It can be any of the three. Often the jacks were never connected to anything at the other end, or the cable stops at a closet where nothing is plugged in. A tone and a certifier test find the far end and show whether the cable passes, which tells you whether a new jack, a patch connection or a new run is needed.

Is the landlord responsible for the wiring that brings internet into our apartment?
+

It depends on who owns the wiring and what the lease says. In many buildings the riser and the wiring up to each apartment belong to the owner or to a provider under an agreement with the owner. Check your lease and ask the managing agent in writing. This is general information, not legal advice.

What are the most common cabling mistakes you find in Flatbush buildings?
+

Cable stapled flat to cellar joists where the next plumber will cut it, runs lying across steam pipes, jacks that were never terminated, openings between floors left unsealed, and risers packed with dead provider lines that nobody labeled. Almost all of them come from cable that was pulled but never finished, tested or documented.

Can a small office or shop be cabled outside business hours so we do not lose a working day?
+

Yes. Business work is scheduled before opening, after closing or on a day the space is closed, and the quote states the hours. The switchover from the old router to the new equipment is planned for a quiet moment and usually takes only a few minutes of downtime.

Can our finished basement get a wired connection when the router is two floors up?
+

Usually, and often more easily than an upper floor, because the provider's line typically enters at the cellar level. The router or a switch can be moved to the cellar with one cable carrying service back up, or a cable can come down a closet or chase from the floor above. A finished ceiling is worked around through access panels or soffits.

Will the drilling disturb neighbors in an apartment building, and what hours do you work?
+

Most cabling is quiet. The noisy parts, drilling through masonry or a floor, last minutes at each opening. We work inside the hours the building or board permits, tell the superintendent in advance which floors and closets we need, and keep hallways clear while we work.

What keeps a building's cameras and entry panel running when the power goes out?
+

A battery backup in the cellar cabinet that powers the switch, and through it every camera and access point fed over its cable. It covers short interruptions and gives time for an orderly shutdown in a longer one. The recorder and the router should be on the same backup so the building keeps recording.

Do you take out the old cable and boxes you replace?
+

Yes, where it is clearly abandoned and you agree. Dead provider boxes, coax and phone wire that serve nothing are removed or cut back and tagged, and anything that might still serve a tenant or another provider stays in place with a label. The quote says what will be removed.

Do you need to enter every apartment to cable a building's halls and lobby?
+

No. A common-area network for hall cameras, an entry panel and lobby Wi-Fi runs in the cellar, the riser, the stair halls and the lobby. Apartment access is needed only if a riser closet or chase is reached from inside a unit, and we arrange that with the owner and the tenant ahead of time.

Can the cameras, the entry panel and the network share one cabinet in the cellar?
+

Yes, and it is usually the cleanest setup. One lockable cabinet holds the switch, the camera recorder, the router and a battery backup, with the cameras and the entry panel on their own network segment. Every cable entering the cabinet is labeled, so the superintendent can tell what each one serves.
