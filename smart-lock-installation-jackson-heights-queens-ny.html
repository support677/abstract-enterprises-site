The Neighborhood

Why Jackson Heights Properties Need Smart Lock Installation That Starts With the Paperwork

Almost every keyless job north of Roosevelt Avenue begins with the same discovery: the door is not entirely yours. The Queensboro Corporation built Jackson Heights as a planned community between 70th and 92nd Streets, from Roosevelt Avenue up to Northern Boulevard, and in 1919 it offered Linden Court as the first building under its “Cooperative Ownership Plan” — one of the first cooperative housing schemes in the country, according to the history the Jackson Heights Beautification Group adapted from Daniel Karatzas’s book. A century later the cooperative model is still everywhere here. The JHBG history counts at least 60 Jackson Heights buildings converted to co-ops or condominiums during the 1980s alone, adding more than 5,000 tenant-owned apartments to a base of at least 5,000 co-op units that already existed. If you live in one of them, you hold shares and a proprietary lease, not a deed to your front door.

That changes the smart lock conversation in practical ways. The board and its managing agent decide what may be attached to a unit door, the proprietary lease usually entitles the corporation to a way in for emergencies and repairs, and the house rules set the hours when a contractor may work. One Jackson Heights co-op whose house rules are published online — the Allendale Apartments at 34-24 82nd Street — is a fair example of the genre: no construction or noisy installation except on weekdays between 8:30 AM and 5 PM, every contractor “screened and approved by Management”, apartment alarm systems installed only with written approval, and a statement that all apartment entrance doors are self-closing under Fire Department rules. A lock that ignores any of those lines can end up removed at the shareholder’s expense. We plan around them before the box is opened.

The doors themselves are old. Laurel Court, the first apartment building in Jackson Heights, went up in 1914; the block-long garden complexes — Hampton, Elm, Hawthorne, Laburnum and Cambridge Courts, The Chateau, The Towers — followed between 1920 and 1925, and the JHBG history notes that every apartment completed through 1921 was a walk-up. Many unit doors in those buildings are original or early replacements: thick wood or metal-clad slabs, often with decades of paint on their edges, mortise pockets cut for locks that have long since been swapped, and a closer at the top that has to pull the door shut against whatever we add. A motorised deadbolt is unforgiving on a door like that. If the bolt rubs, the door doesn’t latch, the self-closer fails its job and the battery drains. Fitting a smart lock here is half carpentry and half electronics, and the carpentry comes first.

Then there are the doors nobody owns individually. The lobby door where, as the Queens Daily Eagle reported in October 2020, a burglar walked into a 34th Avenue co-op at 3 a.m. and tore open about a dozen packages — the second break-in at that building since July. The mailroom at 83-10 35th Avenue that a lone burglar entered three times in one week in March 2025, according to QNS and the NYPD’s 115th Precinct. The fire door to the interior garden that house rules say must stay shut. Those are board decisions, and they are where keypads, fob readers and electric strikes do the most good in Jackson Heights. A smart lock on your own apartment door protects your apartment; it does nothing for the lobby someone was buzzed into.

Finally, the people. The Department of City Planning’s 2026 edition of The Newest New Yorkers counts more than 50,000 immigrants in Jackson Heights, nearly 60 percent of the population, with Colombians, Ecuadorians and Mexicans together making up about 43 percent of the foreign-born and a strong Bangladeshi commercial presence around Diversity Plaza. Households are large and often crowded, keys get copied for cousins and in-laws, and the shareholder on the stock certificate may not be the person who uses the door most. A code per person, a log that shows who came in, and a key the co-op can still use are what make keyless entry fit a Jackson Heights household.

Jackson Heights was built in recognisable waves, and each wave left its own kind of door. The building you live in tells us, before we arrive, roughly how thick the door is, what the board will care about and where the trouble is likely to be.

The Greystones of 1917, then Hampton, Elm, Hawthorne, Laburnum and Cambridge Courts: George H. Street entrances in front, the shared garden behind, and early-era unit doors.

Andrew J. Thomas Complexes

Linden Court, The Chateau with its slate mansard roofs and carved limestone entrances (1923), The Towers at 80th Street and 34th Avenue (1924, 96 apartments) and Spanish Gardens: detached buildings around a landscaped garden. Entrance surrounds are part of the protected streetscape; anything new on them is kept small and reversible.

Smaller buildings such as Hayes, Ivy and Cedar Courts and the 1914–1916 Laurel, Oban, Penrhyn, Plymouth and Willow Courts near 80th to 83rd Streets. Walk-up stairs, short landings and doors that have been re-locked several times over a hundred years.

1930s Elevator Buildings

Rental buildings from the 1930s — Fillmore Hall, with 132 units the largest Queensboro apartment house, Georgian Hall, Oak Hall, Buckingham Hall and the Mount Vernon among them. More apartments per entrance than the early walk-ups, so lobby and mailroom access matter as much as the unit lock.

Post-War Apartment Houses

The building boom the JHBG describes after 1947, when thirty-eight new apartment buildings were completed in 1950 alone, many along 34th and 35th Avenues. Typically steel doors in steel frames with standard bores, and mailrooms off the lobby.

English Garden Homes

The attached brick houses Queensboro began on 87th Street in 1924 and spread across more than fifteen streets in four years, with front and rear gardens and garages at the back. Owner-controlled doors, a front door facing the street and a rear door toward the garden and garage.

Shops at street level with apartments or offices above, along 37th Avenue, 82nd Street, 74th Street and Roosevelt Avenue. A residential street door beside a shop door, and two sets of people who should never share a code.

Apartments sublet by shareholders with board consent, and units still held by the sponsor or a holdover tenant after a conversion. The person living there, the shareholder and the board may all have a say over the same door.

Door and hardware details we check in every Jackson Heights building

Paint, swelling and the self-closer. On a unit door that has been painted every time an apartment changed hands, the edge, the stop and the strike can each carry a sixteenth of an inch or more of build-up. The door still closes for a hand-turned latch, but a motorised bolt touching paint on its way into the strike will strain, report a jam and run the batteries down. Because these doors are self-closing — the house rules we quoted say so, and HPD explains that apartment and hallway doors must swing closed and latch by themselves — the door has to latch from the closer’s pull alone, not from someone pushing it. We take the paint back to clean wood or metal at the contact points, reset the strike, and adjust the closer’s latch speed so the door finishes its swing firmly. Then we let it close twenty times on its own before the smart lock goes in.

Old holes and mortise pockets. A century-old unit door may carry the outline of three earlier locks: a mortise pocket for the original box lock, a bore for a later deadbolt and screw holes from a surface-mounted lock someone added in the 1970s. Most residential smart deadbolts want a standard round bore and backset. Where the existing bore is in the right place, we reuse it; where it isn’t, we fill and re-bore only after the board has seen what the hallway side will look like. If the door is the original mortise type and the board wants the hallway face unchanged, an interior-only retrofit unit that turns the existing deadbolt’s thumb-turn is often the cleaner answer.

The key the corporation keeps. Cooperative proprietary leases commonly require a shareholder to give the corporation a key to any lock they install, and New York courts have enforced that: the silo research for this page logs the Manhattan case 111 Tenants Corp. v. Stromberg, reported by The Cooperator, in which a shareholder was ordered to hand over a duplicate key. So nearly every smart lock we fit in a Jackson Heights co-op keeps a mechanical cylinder, and the shareholder gives the managing agent or super a key exactly as the lease asks. A code instead of a key is something only the board can agree to.

One motion out. Every lock we fit on an apartment door opens from inside with a thumb-turn or lever — no key, no code, no phone. HPD’s fire-safety guidance says double-cylinder locks are illegal on apartment doors, and in buildings where the stair is the only way out, there is no room for a second step at the door.

Most shareholders who rework their own door also ask what can be done about the front door. Compliance callouts for Jackson Heights business doors

  • Exit without knowledge. A door people use to leave a shop or restaurant must open from inside without a key, a code or a card. Keypads go outside; inside there is a lever, a thumb-turn or a push-to-exit device, and an electrified lock always has an independent way out.
  • Landmarks first on the facade. In the historic district, the LPC Permit Guidebook asks for photos and drawings before entrance doors or infill change, and where it allows small add-on hardware such as door actuators it wants them “as small as possible” and with no exposed conduit. We plan keypads and readers on the door, the frame or a non-decorative return rather than on decorative stone, and we tell you when a permit is likely to be needed.
  • Residents upstairs. Where a shop shares a building with apartments, the residential door is covered by the Housing Maintenance Code, and if tenants are given smart access, by the Tenant Data Privacy Act, Local Law 63 of 2021. Shop credentials never open the residents’ door.
  • Fail-safe or fail-secure, per door. When power drops, an electric strike or magnetic lock either releases or stays locked. Which is right depends on whether the door is part of the way out, so we specify it door by door and write it on the plan.

When a business outgrows one keypad — several doors, staff schedules, a camera timeline next to each door event — the work becomes access control. Our Queens access control installers take it from there, and commercial security cameras for Queens businesses put a picture beside every opening.

The shared doors a co-op board controls

A burglar got into the mailroom of a 35th Avenue building three times in one week — can our board put a keypad or fob reader on the mailroom door?

Yes, and in a lot of Jackson Heights buildings it is the single most useful keyless upgrade a board can make. The QNS report from April 2025 describes a lone burglar forcing his way into the mailroom at 83-10 35th Avenue just after midnight on March 8, taking a package with $500 of jewelry, then coming back on March 12 and again on March 14 for documents. The pattern — the same room, the same building, three times — is what happens when a shared interior door is held by a cheap lock and a key that has been copied for years.

A fob reader or keypad with an electric strike on the mailroom door does three things a keyed lock cannot. Residents and staff each carry their own credential, so a lost fob is cancelled in seconds instead of re-keying the room. The managing agent gets a record of which credential opened the door and when, which is what a police report or an insurance claim will want. And the door can be set to stay locked overnight except for staff. Because the mailroom is normally off the lobby rather than on the way out of the building, it is usually fitted fail-secure; we confirm that against the floor plan before ordering the strike.

Two cautions. A forced door beats any lock if the frame is weak, so we look at the jamb and the strike side first and reinforce it where needed. And a credential system on a common door collects data about residents; if any of them are tenants, the Tenant Data Privacy Act limits what a building owner may gather and keep, which is worth reading before the board decides what the log should record.

Couriers are supposed to use our building’s service entrance — can that door take a code that only works during delivery hours?

It can, and it closes a gap that the front door’s buzzer leaves wide open. The Queens Daily Eagle’s 2020 report on Jackson Heights package thefts quoted a resident at 79th Street and 37th Avenue saying many neighbours, particularly seniors, buzz people into the building without seeing who they are, and that thefts began a minute or two after UPS or FedEx drop-offs. House rules in some buildings already send messengers and trades to a service entrance instead — the Allendale Apartments’ rules name one on the south side of that building and limit deliveries to weekday and Saturday-morning hours.

A keypad on the service door lets the board give each regular carrier, the super and approved contractors their own codes, each limited to the days and hours the house rules allow. Outside those hours the codes simply don’t work, so a code that leaks is far less useful to a stranger. If the board prefers, a single courier code can be changed every month and posted to the carriers’ accounts, while residents keep separate credentials that work at all hours.

It only helps if parcels land somewhere secure once the courier is inside. We usually pair the service-door code with a locked package room or the mailroom door above, so the courier’s code opens the way to the drop point and nothing further.

Our house rules keep the door to the interior garden shut at all times — can residents open it with a code instead of the front-door key?

Yes. The block-long interior gardens are the reason Jackson Heights co-ops are called garden apartments, and the rules around them are strict for good reason: in many buildings the garden door is a fire door, it opens into a space shared by several buildings’ worth of neighbours, and if it is left open the garden becomes a back way into the building. The Allendale house rules say exactly that — the fire door to the garden must remain closed at all times, garden hours are 9 AM to sundown, and the building’s front-door key opens it.

A keypad on the garden side of that door, or a keypad lever on the building side, lets residents use the same personal code they use elsewhere, with a schedule that follows the house rule: open from 9 AM, closed to codes after sundown. A seasonal schedule can track the sunset roughly month by month. The door must still open freely from the garden side toward the building, or by a push bar, so nobody is ever trapped outside in an emergency; and it must keep its self-closer and latch, which is the whole point of a fire door.

We check one more thing: whether the garden door is also an exit route for any of the buildings around the court. If it is, its inside release stays mechanical and the electronics only control coming in.

Contractors keep propping the garden or service door open — can the door itself tell the super?

It can. Most access controllers and many connected smart locks support a door-position sensor — a small magnetic contact in the frame — that knows whether the door is actually shut, not just whether the bolt is thrown. Set a “held open” time of a few minutes, and if a contractor wedges the service door with a bucket or a mover props the garden door for an hour, the super’s phone gets a notification and a local chime can sound at the door.

This matters more in Jackson Heights than the technology might suggest. A door that is supposed to self-close and doesn’t is a fire-safety violation; HPD’s self-closing doors page explains that the city inspects 300 class A multiple dwellings a year for exactly this under Local Law 71 of 2022. It is also how a building gets burgled: a propped door is an open invitation that no lock can fix.

We wire the contact into the same controller or hub that runs the door’s code, set the held-open delay with the super, and leave the alerts going to two people so they don’t depend on one phone.

The laundry room is only open certain hours under our house rules — can its door follow that schedule on its own?

Yes. Some Jackson Heights co-ops restrict the basement laundry to daytime and evening hours — the Allendale rules, for instance, set 7 AM to 9 PM — and enforcement usually depends on the super remembering to lock up. A keypad lever or keypad deadbolt on the laundry door with a schedule does the job automatically: residents’ codes work from 7 AM and stop at 9 PM, while the super’s code works at all hours for repairs and machine service.

The same lock can keep a log, which helps when a machine is damaged or the room is left in a state. If the building’s laundry operator needs access to empty coin boxes or service machines, they get their own code limited to their visiting day. A basement door also needs a mechanical way out from inside at all times, so whoever is in the room when the schedule ends can still leave without a code.

For basement doors we prefer a battery keypad lever over anything that needs mains power, because a basement circuit shut off for work shouldn’t lock or unlock a door by accident.

Can an electric strike go into the old wood or stone jamb of our building’s original entrance without spoiling it?

Usually, but the entrance is the one place where we slow right down. Inside the Jackson Heights Historic District the street entrance is part of what the Landmarks Preservation Commission protects. Its Permit Guidebook explains that replacing historic front doors requires an assessment of their condition, and that where it allows small add-on hardware such as door actuators or push plates, they must be “as small as possible” with no exposed conduit. A strike set neatly into an existing jamb, with its cable concealed, fits that approach far better than a surface-mounted box on carved limestone.

Technically, a wood jamb takes an electric strike well once the old keeper is removed and the pocket squared up; the cable is fished inside the frame and dropped behind the trim. A stone or cast surround is different: we keep the strike in the door frame, not the stone, and route the cable through the door frame and the vestibule rather than drilling masonry. The keypad or reader goes on a plain return or inside the vestibule glass rather than on the decorative face.

Before ordering anything we photograph the entrance, mark where each part would sit, and give the board a drawing it can send to LPC if a permit is needed. Whether a permit is required for a given building is LPC’s call; ask the commission’s staff early, because the paperwork is quicker than the work.

Your own apartment inside a cooperative

Our board is drafting a smart-lock policy for shareholders — what should it cover?

A short written policy saves the board from deciding the same questions one shareholder at a time. In the jobs we see, the policies that work cover six things: whether smart locks are permitted on unit doors and what the hallway face may look like (finish, size, whether the existing cylinder must stay); how the corporation keeps emergency access, which in most proprietary leases means a mechanical key handed to the managing agent; that any lock opens from inside with a single motion and never needs a key or code to leave; that the self-closing function of the door is not impaired; who may install (licensed and insured, with a certificate naming the corporation and agent); and that the shareholder restores standard hardware or leaves the smart lock in place on sale, as the board prefers.

Some boards add a line about data: a shareholder’s own lock is the shareholder’s business, but if the building ever links common-door access to unit doors, the Tenant Data Privacy Act applies to any tenants in the building. Others add the contractor hours from the house rules, so nobody books a Saturday install by mistake.

We are glad to send the board our standard scope letter and a sample hallway photo of each lock type we fit, so the policy can name finishes and sizes instead of brands. The wording itself should come from the board and its counsel.

The board approved a one-year sublet of our apartment — how do we hand the door to the subtenant without giving away our own access?

Treat it as two credentials and one key you still control. Before the subtenant arrives, delete every code except yours, change your own administrator password, and create a new user for the subtenant with their own code or fingerprint and an end date set to the last day of the sublet. The lock’s app should stay in your account; the subtenant needs a code, not administrator rights. If the subtenant wants to use their phone, most apps let you share a guest or user invitation that you can revoke.

Keep the mechanical side tidy too. The corporation’s duplicate key stays with the managing agent as the lease requires. Give the subtenant one key for emergencies and write it into the sublease, and if the sublet ends badly, a single cylinder swap is cheap compared with re-keying a whole apartment.

One practical point many shareholders miss: if the lock is connected, its log will show the subtenant’s comings and goings. Tell them in writing that the lock records openings and decide in advance what you will and won’t look at. Board sublet approvals in Jackson Heights co-ops vary, and some buildings discourage subletting altogether, so the board’s written terms come first.

After a fire emptied part of our building, how do residents keep their apartments secured while contractors have the run of it?

This is a real Jackson Heights problem, not a hypothetical. THE CITY reported in March 2022 that after an April 2021 electrical fire at a six-storey, 133-unit building at 34th Avenue and 89th Street displaced more than 140 households, the tenant association counted at least 50 apartments burglarized while families were kept out and security guards and contractors had access. Damaged doors, emptied buildings and many people with keys are exactly the conditions in which belongings disappear.

If a building is emptied by fire, flood or a long renovation, a battery-powered smart lock with a log on each apartment door changes the picture: every opening is recorded by code, nobody needs a resident’s key, and contractors who must enter get a code for specific days that can be read back later. Where the original doors are damaged, a temporary solid door with a keypad deadbolt is quicker than waiting for a replacement and gives the resident, not the contractor, control. A door contact that alerts the resident’s phone when the door opens adds a second record.

All of this needs the owner’s or board’s agreement and must respect any access the building legitimately needs for repairs. But residents are entitled to ask for it, and a written log is the kind of evidence THE CITY’s tenants said they struggled to get recorded at the precinct.

Shops on the commercial streets

Our Roosevelt Avenue storefront sits under the elevated 7 train — does the constant vibration loosen a keypad or strike?

We have not found any manufacturer data or study that measures what an elevated line does to door hardware, so we won’t quote one. What we can describe is what we check on doors near the structure: screws that work loose over months, strikes that drift a fraction out of line, and closers that start to slam or fail to latch. All of those happen to busy shop doors anywhere; repeated shaking from passing trains can only add to them.

So on Roosevelt Avenue we install as if the door will be shaken: thread-locking compound on the keypad and strike screws, through-bolts rather than wood screws where the frame allows, cable runs clipped so they can’t chafe, and a strike set with enough tolerance that a slight shift doesn’t jam the bolt. A keypad mounted on the door itself gets an inspection at every battery change, and an electric strike is checked for alignment if the door starts to bounce.

The bigger Roosevelt Avenue issue is still people, not trains. Individual codes for each employee, a log the owner can check, and a back door that never opens from outside without a credential do more for a shop here than any bracket.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Smart Lock Installation in Jackson Heights

A note on method: the build environment for this page could not open Google’s AI Overview, so the headings below are not quotations from it. They are claims that turn up again and again in smart lock marketing, cost-guide sites like Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, and how-to articles — the material answer engines summarise. Each is followed by what we find on Jackson Heights doors.

“A smart lock is a renter-friendly upgrade you can do without asking anyone”

The claim is aimed at renters with a lease in a big rental tower. In Jackson Heights the most common home is a share in a cooperative, and the shareholder’s right to change a door is set by the proprietary lease, the house rules and the board, not by the product’s box.

House rules like those published for the Allendale Apartments on 82nd Street require written approval for installations, management screening of contractors and work only in weekday hours. A lock fitted without approval can be ordered removed, and the cost of putting the door back falls on the shareholder.

Ask the managing agent first, send them the scope and spec sheet, and book the work inside the permitted hours. It usually takes a few weeks and saves a fight.

“Installing a smart lock takes about 30 minutes with a screwdriver”

On a new, square door with a standard bore, that is roughly right. Jackson Heights unit doors are often a century old, painted many times over, cut for earlier locks and fitted with a closer that must still pull the door shut.

Most of our time goes on the door, not the lock: clearing paint from the edge and stop, squaring the strike, filling stray holes, and adjusting the closer so the door latches on its own pull. Skip those steps and the motorised bolt jams, the battery drains and the door may not self-close as the fire rules require.

A realistic estimate is one visit of an hour or two for a sound door and longer for one that needs work. We tell you which before you book.

“You need a Landmarks permit to put any lock on a door in a historic district”

This overcorrects. The Landmarks Preservation Commission regulates the exterior of buildings in historic districts — facades, entrances, storefronts, windows. Its Permit Guidebook puts doors and entrance infill under its rules and asks for photos and drawings when they change.

An apartment door off an interior stair hall is normally a matter for the co-op board, not the commission. The building’s street entrance, a storefront on 82nd Street or a keypad on a limestone surround is where LPC’s rules come into play.

When in doubt, the commission’s staff will tell you whether a permit is needed. We plan entrance hardware to be small, concealed and reversible, which is the direction the guidebook points.

“A smart lock solves package theft”

Package theft in Jackson Heights buildings happens in the lobby and the mailroom, not at apartment doors. The Queens Daily Eagle’s 2020 story described a burglar entering a 34th Avenue co-op at 3 a.m. and tearing open a dozen parcels by the mailboxes; QNS described a mailroom on 35th Avenue entered three times in one week in 2025.

A smart lock on your own door does nothing about either. What helps is control of the shared doors: a code or fob on the mailroom or package room, a service-entrance code for carriers that works only in delivery hours, and fewer strangers buzzed in from upstairs.

Those are board decisions. Shareholders can ask for them; we can price them.

“It’s your apartment, so you don’t have to give anyone a key”

General smart-home advice tends to treat the owner as sole master of the door. Co-op shareholders are usually bound to give the corporation access for emergencies and repairs, and the silo research behind this page records the Manhattan case 111 Tenants Corp. v. Stromberg, reported by The Cooperator, in which a shareholder was ordered to surrender a duplicate key.

Renters are in a similar position under state law: Multiple Dwelling Law section 51-c lets a tenant add a lock of their own but requires a duplicate key on the landlord’s request.

So in Jackson Heights we keep a mechanical cylinder in almost every unit-door smart lock and hand a key over the way the lease or the law asks. Whether a code can substitute for that key is for the board, in writing.

“Wi-Fi smart locks connect from anywhere in the apartment”

Product pages tend to assume a router somewhere near the door. In a prewar walk-up the router is often at the back of the apartment, the lock is on a door set into a masonry stair hall, and every neighbour’s network is competing for the same channels.

We test the signal at the door, not at the router, before choosing between a Wi-Fi lock, a Bluetooth lock with a plug-in bridge near the entrance, or a lock that works purely locally. A lock that only needs to open for the people standing at it doesn’t need Wi-Fi at all.

The final test is remote: phone on mobile data, outside the building, lock and unlock, and confirm the alert arrives. If it doesn’t, we change the connection, not the promise.

“Smart lock data stays private between you and the app”

For a lock you own on your own door, the data is between you and the manufacturer. Building systems are different. When a building owner puts smart access on common doors that tenants use, the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Act, Local Law 63 of 2021, limits what can be collected, requires consent before collecting certain data, and bars requiring smart access to enter a tenant’s own unit.

Jackson Heights co-ops often include sublet apartments and holdover tenants alongside shareholders, so a board installing fob readers on the lobby or mailroom should read that law before deciding what the log records and how long it is kept.

We set up building systems to record only what the board needs, give the managing agent the export controls, and leave privacy decisions where they belong — with the board and its counsel.

For Boards

A Shared-Door Checklist for Jackson Heights Co-op Boards

Before a board spends money on keyless common doors, it helps to walk the building with one question per door. This is the list we work through with board members and supers.

  • Is it a way out? Any door people use to leave the building, the garden or the basement keeps a single-motion inside release that works without power, code or key.
  • Does it have to self-close? Fire doors and doors to stairs and corridors must close and latch on their own; anything we add has to leave that working, and a held-open alert helps keep it that way.
  • Who really needs to open it? Residents, staff, couriers, the laundry operator, the exterminator — each group gets its own credentials and hours rather than one shared code.
  • What should the log record, and for how long? If any residents are tenants, read the Tenant Data Privacy Act first and decide the retention period in writing.
  • Is it on the street facade? In the historic district, plan small, concealed and reversible hardware and ask LPC staff early whether a permit is needed.
  • What happens when the power is off? Decide fail-safe or fail-secure per door, and keep at least one mechanical key route into the building for the super.
  • Who maintains it? Name the person who adds and removes users, and the person who gets the low-battery and held-open alerts when the super is off.

Boards that work through the list usually end up converting the mailroom and service door first, the garden door second, and the main lobby when the intercom is due for replacement.

Field Notes

Field Notes on Jackson Heights Co-op Doors

How our installers approach a prewar co-op unit door — the routine we follow on every one, not the story of a single visit

We start in the hallway, not at the door. Is the corridor uniform? Are the neighbours’ doors fitted with the same hardware? If every door on the landing carries the same brass cylinder, the board will notice a matte-black keypad, and we would rather suggest an interior retrofit than leave the shareholder with a removal notice.

Then we let the door close by itself, several times, and listen. A door that clicks shut on its closer is ready for a smart lock. One that stops a finger’s width short, or needs a push, is telling us the strike, the paint or the closer needs attention first.

Next we look at the edge of the door under a light. Layers of paint, a filled-in mortise pocket, a second bore that never matched the strike — each of those changes how we fit the lock.

Last, we ask who holds keys today and who will hold them tomorrow: the household, the super, the managing agent, a subtenant. The code plan comes out of that conversation, and the corporation’s key goes back to the agent before we leave.
