The Neighborhood

Why Pelham Gardens Properties Need Access Control

Ask three sources where Pelham Gardens is and you get three slightly different shapes. Wikipedia draws it with East Gun Hill Road on the north and east, Pelham Parkway on the south and the Dyre Avenue branch of the 5 train on the west, ending at the Esplanade, and names Eastchester Road as its main street. CityNeighborhoods.NYC uses Gun Hill Road to the northeast, Stillwell Avenue to the southeast, the parkway to the south and the Esplanade to the west. Both put it in Bronx Community Board 11 and the 10469 ZIP code, and the NYPD’s 49th Precinct, whose station house stands at 2121 Eastchester Road, patrols it. This page names streets instead of arguing over edges.

The name reaches back to Thomas Pell, who, Wikipedia records, bought the land from the Siwanoy on June 27, 1654, at the Treaty Oak in what is now Pelham Bay Park. The area belonged to Westchester County until it was transferred to New York City in 1895, and the oldest house in the area is described as a former farmhouse at the corner of Woodhull Avenue and Fielding Street. The two sources disagree on when the houses came. Wikipedia says significant development began in the 1920s with a construction boom in the 1950s; CityNeighborhoods places most construction between the 1930s and 1950s and describes Tudor Revival, Cape Cod and Colonial houses with pitched roofs, bay windows and private driveways. Either way, most houses standing today are roughly seventy to a hundred years old, and so are many of their doors and frames.

What those houses hold is the heart of this page. Wikipedia’s land-use paragraph is blunt: Pelham Gardens is dominated by single-family homes with some small apartment buildings and two- and three-family homes, and “many private homes have basement studio apartments but are classified as one family.” A New York Times “Living In” column from 2002, quoted in the same article, noted that interns and residents from the nearby medical centers rent apartments in Pelham Gardens’ two- and three-family houses; a 2011 column in the same series, which we could read only as a search excerpt, called the semidetached two-family one of the area’s numerous house types. Kevin Walsh of Forgotten New York described crossing from the apartment houses of Pelham Parkway and Allerton into “a land of well kept one- and two-family houses.” In other words: one building, often two or three households, and at least one of them living partly or wholly below the sidewalk.

Then there is the ground itself. Wikipedia describes the area as largely flat but with “a significant valley between Eastchester Road and Laconia Avenue, with the most significant low point on Seymour Avenue,” a dip it attributes, probably, to a glacier. Water runs downhill, and in a heavy rain the low blocks collect it. On October 31, 2025, News 12 Bronx reported that residents near Tiemann and Waring Avenues deal with stagnant storm water flooding the street and sidewalk whenever it rains, that one homeowner puts up barriers to keep it out of his basement, and that the water turns to ice in winter. The Department of Environmental Protection told the station that the street grade should send runoff to the catch basin on Tiemann Avenue, but that a lack of curb reveal and a new pedestrian ramp with a raised strip may be restricting drainage.

Eight months later the problem came from below. On June 26, 2026, News 12 reported that a brief but heavy rainfall had flooded at least ten homes along Allerton Avenue between Seymour and Morgan Avenues, with sewage-backed water rushing into basements for the third time that week. Residents said flooding had never been an issue before that Monday; a plumber they hired believed the problem came from the city sewer, the city told them the line was theirs, and DEP crews and a contractor returned to inspect. One homeowner said she had already spent about $3,000 pumping and cleaning. We cite these reports because they describe exactly the room where many Pelham Gardens houses keep their door controller, their lock power supply and, often, a tenant’s front door.

The citywide record explains why the basement door is not just an equipment question. When the remnants of Hurricane Ida struck on September 1, 2021, ABC7 reported that at least 13 people died in New York City and that eleven of them died in basement apartments that quickly filled with water. The DEP’s “Plan for Intense Storms” page now asks owners of multi-unit properties to find out whether anyone lives in a basement or cellar and, if so, to develop alerts, communication and evacuation plans, and to survey below-grade openings such as exterior stairs and sloped driveways.

The rules about basement homes are changing too. The Department of Buildings’ ancillary dwelling unit page describes Local Law 126 of 2024 as a pilot that lets some pre-existing basement and cellar apartments be brought into compliance gradually, and lists Bronx Community District 11, which contains Pelham Gardens, inside the pilot area. The same page says subgrade units are not permitted in FEMA’s Special Flood Hazard Area, the Coastal Flood Risk Area or DEP’s 10-Year Rainfall Flood Risk Area, and that pilot applications are not being accepted until the department’s rules are issued. So a Pelham Gardens owner with a basement studio is living with two open questions at once: whether the unit can be made legal, and how much water the block takes. A door system should not make either answer worse.

None of this makes Pelham Gardens a dangerous place to live; Wikipedia’s figures for the 49th Precinct show crime across all categories down 71.7 percent between 1990 and 2022, with 133 burglaries reported in 2022. Burglary still happens here, and the reported cases tend to come through the doors nobody watches. In April 2021, ABC7 reported that men forced open the rear door of a home near Gabriel Drive and Mace Avenue at about 12:30 a.m. Rear doors, side doors a few steps down from a driveway and basement doors at the bottom of a stair share the same weakness: they are out of sight of the street.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Pelham Gardens’ Architecture

Six kinds of building share these blocks. We describe them from the sources cited on this page and from what anyone can see from the sidewalk; no address mentioned on this page is an AESS customer, and every house is different once you open the basement door.

Detached one-family houses with a finished basement

The typical Pelham Gardens house: brick or stucco, a pitched roof, a short front lawn and a driveway along one side. The basement is often finished and lived in, sometimes as a family room and sometimes as a separate studio. Entry to it may be through the house, through a side door a few steps below the driveway, or from an outside stairwell at the back. Each of those is a different door with a different risk of water.

Semi-detached two-family houses

Pairs of houses that share a party wall, each with an owner’s unit and a rental unit above or below. Front walks and stoops are often side by side with the neighbor’s, which matters for where a call station goes. The second unit may have its own door at the front or reach the street through a shared vestibule.

Three-family houses

A smaller number of houses carry three households, one of which may be at basement level. Three families sharing a front door is where a single brass key stops working, because nobody can say how many copies exist; three call buttons and three sets of credentials put each household back in charge of its own visitors.

Small apartment buildings

Low walk-ups and garden-style buildings scattered among the houses and along the parkway edge, with a street door, an intercom panel and a cellar door to a boiler and refuse room. Where a building has three or more apartments the Multiple Dwelling Law applies, and where a building uses fobs, phone credentials or other smart access, the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law limits what data can be kept.

Houses converted to offices

Along Eastchester Road and East Gun Hill Road some houses carry a practice, an insurance office or a small business on the ground floor and an apartment above. The office has opening hours; the apartment does not. The basement often holds both the office’s storage and the building’s meters and boiler.

Houses of worship and community rooms

Churches, a parish hall and community rooms appear at the edges of the neighborhood, including Holy Rosary at East Gun Hill Road and Eastchester Road. Fellowship halls and storage are often in a lower level, and side doors are used only at service or meeting times.

How each building type changes the door hardware

On a detached house, the owner’s front door usually takes a mortise or cylindrical lockset in a wood frame; a keypad deadbolt or an electric strike behind a keypad works well, provided the door closes and latches on its own. The basement door is where choices narrow. Older basement doors are frequently steel or wood slabs in a steel or wood frame set into the foundation, often with a step down on the inside and no closer at all. A strike that unlocks when power is removed, a lever that always opens from inside and a closer strong enough to pull the door shut against wind down the stairwell are the usual combination. Where the basement door swings out into the stairwell, we check that water and leaves gathering on the landing cannot jam it.

The Low Door

Planning Entry Around the Water Line

Most access control guides start at the front door. In Pelham Gardens we start at the lowest one, because it is the door that combines the most risks: it is out of sight, it is the second household’s front door in many houses, and in some blocks it is the first place water arrives. The design question is simple to state. Where is the highest water this basement has seen, or could plausibly see, and what has to stay above it?

The answer starts with the parts that cannot get wet. A door controller is a circuit board. A lock power supply is a transformer, a charger and a battery in a metal box. A network switch, a recorder or a hub for battery locks is the same story. In many houses all of these end up on the basement wall at knee height, next to the electric panel, because that is where the power is. We put them on the first floor instead: in a hall closet, a utility cupboard, the back of a coat closet or an attic space reached from the top floor. The only things that stay in the basement are the parts that must be at the door: the lock itself, the reader or keypad, a door contact and the cable that joins them.

The cable route matters as much as the equipment. A cable run along the bottom of a basement wall sits in water the first time the floor drain backs up; a cable run in the joist space overhead does not. We bring the basement door’s cable down from the ceiling to the door frame, not up from the floor, and we protect the drop with raceway where it is exposed. Outside, a reader on a stairwell wall gets its cable through the wall at reader height, sealed, rather than through a sleeve at the bottom of the foundation where the stairwell drain is.

Then there is power failure, which in a storm is likely. Every electric lock either stays locked when power disappears (fail-secure) or releases (fail-safe). Neither is right for every door. For a door that someone must be able to leave in a hurry, what matters most is that the inside handle always works mechanically, with no power and no key; with that in place, the lock’s behavior only decides who can get in from outside during an outage. We go through that door by door with the owner and write the result on a chart, because the right answer for the basement door, the front door and the door between the basement and the house are usually different.

AESS method drawing; generic house, not any real property. Heights are illustrative.

What we keep below the water line, and what we never do

  • At the basement door only: the lock or strike, the inside lever, a weather-rated reader or keypad, a door contact and sealed cable. Each is chosen to survive damp air, and the reader is mounted as high on the stairwell wall as a person can comfortably reach.
  • Never on the basement floor or low wall: controllers, lock power supplies, batteries, plug-in transformers, network switches, Wi-Fi hubs for battery locks and recorders.
  • Never a lock that needs power or a key to get out: no double-cylinder deadbolts on a basement apartment’s exit door, no magnetic lock without a mechanical way out, and no keypad on the inside of a door someone sleeps behind.
  • Never a cable sleeve at the stairwell drain: wall penetrations go in high, sloped to the outside and sealed.

AESS example; the right answer differs house by house and is agreed with the owner in writing.

Working with the water work, not around it

Locks do not keep water out, and we do not sell them as if they did. The DEP’s guidance points owners toward backwater valves installed with a plumber, sealing poorly fitted doors and utility openings, retrofitting below-grade mechanical rooms and building curbs or bulkheads that steer rainwater away from below-grade stairs and driveways. Each of those touches the basement door, so we coordinate. A new threshold or a removable flood shield changes how far the door must swing and how the strike lines up; a bulkhead at the top of the stairwell changes where a person stands to use the reader. We prefer to come after the plumber and the mason have finished and before the drywall goes back, so that cable can be run where it will stay dry and the door can be adjusted to its final threshold.

Below-Grade Households

A Second Household Below the Sidewalk

When Wikipedia says that many Pelham Gardens one-family houses have basement studios, it is describing a household that often has no door of its own on any plan, no call button at the street and no lock that the owner did not install for some other purpose. The occupant may come and go through the owner’s side door, through the boiler room, or through a basement door that was meant for deliveries of heating oil. Visitors ring the owner. Packages land in the owner’s hall. In an emergency, the occupant’s way out may be the same stair the water comes down.

We cannot make a basement unit legal; that is the work of an architect, the Department of Buildings and, for many owners, the pilot program that has not yet opened. What we can do is make sure the door system respects three things whatever the unit’s status. First, the person living downstairs can always get out, with a single motion, without power and without a key. Second, the person living downstairs has their own credential, their own call button and their own door if the house allows one, so their comings and goings are not routed through the owner’s life or the other way round. Third, nothing we install would have to be torn out if the unit is later brought into compliance or if the owner decides the basement should not be lived in at all.

How the credentials divide in a house with a basement studio

A typical plan has four groups. The owner’s household can open every door and holds the administrator account. The basement occupant can open the street gate if there is one, their own entrance and any shared laundry or utility door, but not the owner’s front door or the stair into the owner’s floor. Service trades, the oil delivery, the meter reader and the boiler technician get codes that work only at the basement or utility door and only in a time window. A trusted relative or neighbor gets a code for emergencies that is logged when used. The door between the basement and the upper house, if there is one, is treated as a door between two homes: it locks from both sides, opens freely toward the exit route in an emergency, and is never the only way out of the basement.

When the occupant moves out, their credential is deleted that day and their mechanical key, if there was one, is rekeyed. Because a basement occupant here may be a medical resident on a fixed placement (the 2002 Times column noted interns and residents renting in local houses), a student or a relative rather than a long-term tenant, turnover can be frequent; a code list that the owner can change from a phone is worth more here than in a house with no rental unit at all.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control on Eastchester Road and Gun Hill Road

Pelham Gardens has no big shopping strip of its own; Wikipedia calls Eastchester Road the neighborhood’s primary thoroughfare, CityNeighborhoods describes East Gun Hill Road as a commercial corridor of groceries, bakeries and service businesses, and Wikipedia notes a high concentration of private medical facilities drawn by the nearby hospitals. The businesses we see from the sidewalk are mostly small: practices and offices in converted houses, shops at street corners, a few walk-up professional suites, and the parish buildings at the northern edge. Here are the places owners name when they call.

Eastchester Road

The neighborhood’s main north–south street, carried by the Bx31 bus, with the 49th Precinct station house at 2121 Eastchester Road toward its southern end. Houses along it carry offices on the ground floor and apartments above, and the side doors to their driveways and basements matter as much as the front.

East Gun Hill Road

The northern and eastern edge, served by the Bx28 and Bx38, with Holy Rosary Church at Eastchester Road, the Eastchester branch of the New York Public Library at 1385 East Gun Hill Road and the Baychester post office at 1525 East Gun Hill Road. Shops here have front doors on a busy road and back doors on quiet lots.

The Pelham Parkway edge

The southern border along Pelham Parkway, carried by the Bx12 and its Select Bus Service, where apartment buildings and houses with professional offices face the parkway’s service roads.

The Esplanade

The landscaped roadway on the western edge that covers the old New York, Westchester and Boston Railway in its tunnel, with the Pelham Parkway station’s only entrance in its median north of the parkway.

Converted-house medical offices

Practices that keep patient records, supplies or equipment in a basement that may take water, and whose patients include people who cannot manage stairs or a stiff door.

Parish and community buildings

Lower-level halls, food pantries and storerooms used on a timetable, where a side door opens for volunteers at set hours and must stay shut the rest of the week.

Rules a business door has to follow

  • Free egress: under the Building Code, an electrically locked door on a required exit must let people out without a key, special knowledge or effort, and a magnetic lock must release from the inside by motion sensor and button and on fire-alarm activation where an alarm exists.
  • Accessibility: a reader, button or keypad that the public uses goes within accessible reach, and an office serving patients should think about a powered door opener at the entrance they actually use.
  • Records: door logs at a medical office are business records; we set retention to what the practice needs and no more, and we never tie door events to patient files.
  • Apartments in the same building: a residential tenant above an office is protected by the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law if smart access is used on the building entrance, and the tenant’s door is never put on the office’s schedule.

For an office in a converted house, the after-hours plan is the part owners most often skip. When the last staff member leaves, the office door locks, the apartment door carries on as normal, and the basement door returns to its after-hours rule: tenant and owner only, plus a short window for any trade that has to read a meter or check the boiler. If water is a risk in that basement, records, supplies and the door controller all go up a floor, and the office manager gets the same water-sensor alert as the owner.

Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Pelham Gardens: Questions Answered

These are the questions Pelham Gardens owners put to us, grouped by what is really being asked. Each answer is written for this neighborhood’s houses; questions about cost, timing and paperwork are in the FAQ further down.

Water, the Stairwell and the Basement Door

Our basement floor drain pushed sewage into the room three times in one week. Which parts of a door system can stay in the basement, and which should move upstairs?

Only the parts that physically have to be at the door should stay: the lock or electric strike, the inside lever, the reader or keypad, a door contact and the cable joining them. Everything with a circuit board, a transformer or a battery should move up a floor, and that includes the door controller, the lock power supply and its standby battery, any plug-in adapter, the Wi-Fi bridge for battery locks and a camera recorder if there is one. The reason is not only that water ruins electronics. A power supply standing in sewage-backed water is a shock risk to whoever comes down to bail, and a battery that has been submerged should not be charged again.

In practice we find a spot on the first floor within about fifty feet of cable from the basement door, usually a closet against an outside wall, a cupboard under the stairs or the back of a utility room, and we run the cable down through the floor into the basement ceiling and across to the door. The basement end is then nothing but hardware rated to get damp, and replacing it after a bad night costs a fraction of replacing a controller.

Where should a reader or keypad sit on an outside basement stairwell whose drain clogs and fills the bottom steps?

As high as a person standing on the stairs can comfortably reach, on the side wall rather than beside the door at the bottom. On most outside stairwells that means a point on the wall two or three steps up from the landing, at the height a light switch would be for someone standing on that step. The visitor or resident presents the credential, the strike releases, and they walk down the last steps to a door that is already unlocked. The reader never sits at the level where water and leaves collect, and it is out of the splash from the drain.

We use readers and keypads rated for outdoor exposure, but the rating describes rain and dust, not standing in water, so height is still the main protection. The cable enters the wall behind the reader, sloped to drain outward and sealed. If the stairwell has no wall high enough, a short post at the top of the stair works too; then the door below is released from the street level, which also means a visitor does not have to go down a dark stair before being let in.

We slide a flood barrier into the basement doorway when heavy rain is forecast. Will it stop an electric lock or a door sensor from working?

It can, depending on which side of the door it sits and how the door swings. A barrier slotted into the frame on the outside of an in-swinging door does not touch the lock, but it does mean the door cannot be used as an exit while it is in place, which matters if anyone sleeps in the basement. A barrier on the inside of an out-swinging door blocks the door from opening at all. Neither affects a magnetic door contact much, but the household will receive “door held open” or “door forced” alerts if the door is jostled while the barrier goes in.

When we design for a house that uses a barrier, we do three things. We confirm with the owner that the basement has a second way out while the barrier is in place, or that nobody sleeps there on those nights. We add a “storm mode” that silences door alerts for the basement door while the barrier is fitted. And we position the strike and contact so the barrier channels do not foul them; if the mason is adding permanent channels, we fit the hardware after they are set.

We are rebuilding the basement walls after the backups. How should new door wiring be run so the next flood does not ruin it?

From above, never from below. Run the cable for the basement door through the ceiling joists, drop it inside the wall only for the last few feet to the door frame, and keep every box at least door-handle height. Ask the contractor to leave an empty conduit or a pull string from the first-floor equipment closet down to the basement door head, so cable can be pulled later without opening the new drywall. If there will be a water sensor beside the door, run its cable the same way and mount the sensor on the floor with only the probe at the bottom.

Getting Out Fast

Someone lives in our basement studio. Which electric lock is safe on the door at the bottom of the outside stairs if water comes in at night?

Any lock whose inside handle opens the door with one motion, no key, no code and no power. That rules out double-cylinder deadbolts, keypads on the inside face and magnetic locks that rely on a button or sensor alone. What remains is a lever-operated lockset or mortise lock with free egress, paired either with an electric strike or with an electrified lever on the outside only. The occupant pushes the lever and walks out, whatever the electricity is doing, and whatever the keypad outside is displaying.

The second choice is how the outside behaves in an outage. If the strike is fail-secure, the door stays locked to the stairwell during an outage and the occupant re-enters with a key; if fail-safe, the outside opens freely while power is off. For a basement apartment we usually choose fail-secure for the outside function, because the inside always opens anyway, and we give the occupant a mechanical key as the backup. We also put a contact on the door so the household knows if it was left open while someone fled.

If the basement door unlocks when the power fails, how is the rest of the house kept shut during the outage?

By treating the door between the basement and the upper house as a separate lock with its own rule. Where the owner wants the basement entrance fail-safe, for instance because elderly relatives use it and cannot manage a key, the inner door at the top of the basement stairs becomes the security boundary. It gets a fail-secure lock from the basement side with free egress toward the exit, so anyone in the house can still get down and out, but an outage does not open a path from the street into the living floors.

If there is no inner door, which is common where the basement is open to the house, we usually recommend against a fail-safe outside lock on the basement entrance and use a fail-secure strike with a mechanical key override instead. The point of the exercise is that no single power failure opens the whole house, and no single power failure traps anyone inside.

Can the basement tenant’s credential also open a door upstairs, so there is a dry way in and out during a storm?

Yes, if the house has a route that suits it, and in a valley block it is often a good idea. The basement occupant’s credential can be allowed at the side door or a vestibule door upstairs, either all the time or only when the owner switches on a “storm route.” From there an inside stair leads down to the studio, and the occupant does not have to use the outside stairwell while it is full of water.

Can the system that runs our doors also send a storm warning to the person living in the basement?

It can send a message the owner chooses to send, and it can send an automatic alert when a water sensor by the basement door detects water. Most current access control apps let the administrator push a notice to users of a particular door or group; we set up a basement group containing the occupant and anyone else who sleeps downstairs. The water sensor goes on the floor beside the basement door with its electronics mounted higher, and it notifies both households, not just the owner.

The Basement Unit and the Rules

Our block may be inside the city’s 10-year rainfall flood area. Does that affect legalizing our basement studio, and should the door work wait?

It affects legalization, according to the Department of Buildings, which says subgrade units are not permitted in DEP’s 10-Year Rainfall Flood Risk Area, nor in FEMA’s Special Flood Hazard Area or the Coastal Flood Risk Area, even though Community District 11 is otherwise inside the basement pilot area. You can look your block up on the DEP stormwater flood map, and an architect or expediter can tell you what that means for your house. We are not the people to answer the legal question, and we do not install anything that presents a basement as a legal apartment.

The door work does not need to wait for that answer if what you need now is a safe exit and a secure entrance. Free-egress hardware, a reader on the stairwell, cable from above and equipment kept upstairs are right whether the basement becomes a legal unit, stays a family room or becomes storage. What should wait is anything tied to a new entrance or layout that an approved plan might change, such as cutting a new doorway or mounting a call station for a unit that may not exist next year.

Our plumber is putting in a backwater valve next month. Should the door installation come before or after?

After the valve, and after any other water work at the basement door. A backwater valve itself is inside the house drain and does not touch the door, but plumbers often open floors near the foundation wall, move floor drains or add a sump pit, and masons may add a curb or bulkhead at the top of the stairwell or rebuild the bottom landing. Any of those can change the door threshold, the swing clearance and where cable can safely run. FloodHelpNY describes the valve as a device on the pipe joining the home’s fixtures to the city sewer that reduces the risk of sewage backing up in heavy rain; it is a plumber’s job, and if the basement door needs a lock in the meantime we fit a temporary battery lock.

Offices, Damp and Batteries

Our office occupies the ground floor of a converted house on Eastchester Road, with a rented apartment above. Once the office closes, which doors lock and who still reaches the meters in the basement?

The office’s front door locks at closing and accepts only staff credentials until morning. The apartment’s door is unaffected by the office’s hours; the tenant’s credential and call button work around the clock. The basement door, if it is reached from outside, goes to its after-hours rule: the owner, the building’s maintenance person and, during a set window, the utility and boiler trades. If the basement is reached only through the office, the meter reader needs a scheduled appointment or a lockbox key, because handing out an office credential to every utility worker defeats the point.

We write those rules as a short table the owner, the office manager and the tenant each sign off on, so nobody is surprised. The table also says who gets which alerts: a door forced at night goes to the owner and the office manager, a water sensor in the basement goes to everyone, and the tenant is never sent office activity.

Our basement stays damp all summer. How long will a battery keypad lock last on a door that is always humid?

Usually less long than the box suggests. The batteries themselves are not much affected by humidity, but corrosion on contacts, keypads that wick moisture between the buttons and motors that strain against swollen doors all shorten the life of a battery lock. On a door that sees a dehumidifier most of the year we plan battery changes twice a year rather than once, and we choose models whose battery compartment is on the inside, away from the damp stairwell air.

If the door is in a stairwell where condensation runs down the walls, we would rather fit a wired strike with the electronics a floor up and only a sealed reader at the door. It costs more at installation and less every year after, and it never leaves the occupant at the bottom of a stair with a keypad that has gone flat.

Choosing between a strike and a magnetic lock on a basement door

For a residential basement door we almost always prefer an electric strike. It leaves the original latch and lever in place, so the inside always opens mechanically, and it can be set to keep the outside locked when power is lost. A magnetic lock holds a steel plate to an electromagnet on the head of the door and releases only when its power is cut, so it needs a motion sensor and a push button inside, and in a damp stairwell its plate and magnet corrode. Magnetic locks have their place on glass office doors; they rarely belong at the bottom of a Pelham Gardens basement stair.

For the owner’s front door, the choice is between a wired strike behind a keypad or reader and a good battery keypad deadbolt. Both work; the wired option costs more and lasts longer, the battery option goes in faster and leaves the frame untouched. On semi-detached houses with old wood frames we look at the frame before recommending a strike, because a frame that has shrunk away from the masonry will not hold one.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Pelham Gardens

We could not open Google’s AI Overview from the environment this page was written in, so we do not quote it. What follows answers claims we hear repeated by chatbots, cost-guide sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, and national security brands, and checks each one against how houses on these blocks are actually built and used.

“Put the control panel somewhere central, like the basement”

Generic installation advice treats the basement as the natural home for a controller, a power supply and a recorder: it is near the electric panel, out of the way and usually unfinished. On a house that has never taken water, that is reasonable. On a block where News 12 has reported sewage rushing into basements three times in a week, it puts the most expensive and least water-tolerant part of the system in the one room most likely to flood.

A controller that has been underwater is usually a write-off, and so is its standby battery. Worse, if the controller runs every door in the house, losing it in the basement means losing control of the front door and the side door as well as the basement door, on the very night the household needs them most.

Our rule in Pelham Gardens is the reverse of the generic one. Equipment goes upstairs; cable comes down from the ceiling to the doors that need it. The basement end is cheap to replace, and the brain of the system stays dry.

“Fail-safe locks are the safe choice”

The name suggests that a fail-safe lock is always the safer option. It only describes what the outside of the door does when power is cut: a fail-safe lock unlocks, a fail-secure lock stays locked. Safety for the person inside comes from the inside handle, which should open mechanically regardless of power, and that is true of both types when they are installed correctly.

On a basement entrance that opens directly into a lived-in house, a fail-safe lock can mean that every storm outage leaves a door from the stairwell into the house unlocked for hours. That is not safer; it just moves the risk from the person inside to the house as a whole.

The real question is which door forms the boundary during an outage, and that is answered door by door on a written chart, not by picking one lock type for the whole house.

“Weatherproof readers can go anywhere outdoors”

Outdoor readers carry ingress-protection ratings that describe resistance to dust, rain and water jets. A product page that lists a high rating can make it sound as if the reader can sit anywhere. A rating tested against spray is not a promise about sitting at the bottom of a stairwell in a few inches of storm water mixed with leaves, salt from winter sidewalks and whatever the drain pushes back up.

In a stairwell, mounting height protects the reader better than any rating. We put readers well above the landing, bring cable in from behind and seal the penetration, and, where the stairwell has no suitable wall, move the reader to the top of the stair.

“Access control costs $1,500 to $5,000 per door”

Cost-guide figures of a few thousand dollars per door circulate widely, and they are not wrong as averages. They describe commercial installations with readers, controllers, power supplies and software on every door. A Pelham Gardens house rarely needs that. It may need one wired basement entrance, a keypad deadbolt on the front door and a two-button call station, which together cost less than several “average” doors.

What the averages leave out is the work particular to these houses: moving equipment out of the basement, running cable through the ceiling instead of along the floor, adjusting a basement door that has swollen in damp air, or coordinating with a plumber’s threshold. Those are real costs, but they are small next to replacing a flooded controller every few years.

“A smart lock is all a basement apartment needs”

National brands sell a single smart lock as the complete answer for a rental unit: codes from a phone, logs and remote unlocking. A battery smart lock is a good tool, and it is often part of our plan. On its own it does not give a basement studio its own call button at the street, a way for visitors to reach the occupant rather than the owner, or a signal to the household if the door is left open in the rain.

It also adds a battery and a radio to the dampest door in the house. Many smart locks keep their batteries on the inside, which is good, but the keypad on the outside faces the stairwell air every day of the year.

“ADT, Ring or SimpliSafe cover access control too”

Monitored alarm brands and do-it-yourself kits can add a doorbell, a smart lock or a door sensor to an alarm plan, and for some houses that is enough. Their kits are designed for one household and one front door. They do not usually separate a basement studio’s visitors from the owner’s, set different power-failure behavior for different doors, or let a tenant manage their own door without touching the owner’s account.

They also come with monthly plans. A local access control system can run without a subscription, with the owner holding the administrator account; any monitoring or cloud service is optional and priced separately.

“Pelham Gardens is a quiet area, so security is mostly about the front door”

Pelham Gardens is generally described as stable and family-oriented, and the 49th Precinct’s long-term crime trend is down sharply. That leads some advice to suggest a doorbell camera at the front and little else. The reported incidents that do occur here have tended to come through the rear: ABC7’s 2021 report described a rear door forced open after midnight.

Rear doors, side doors and basement doors are also where daily life happens: a tenant’s entrance, the family’s way in from the driveway, the trades’ way to the boiler. A system that controls only the front door leaves the busiest doors on brass keys whose copies nobody can count.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Shelf by the Electric Panel

This is a note on method, not a story about a particular house. When we walk a basement anywhere in the low part of the northeast Bronx, the first thing we look at is not the door. It is the shelf, hook or bare stretch of wall next to the electric panel, because that is where earlier installers tend to leave things: a doorbell transformer, a plug-in power supply for a keypad, a small router, a battery box, a coil of spare cable on the floor.

Then we look for the water line. Most basements that have taken water carry a mark of it somewhere: a stain on the bottom of the drywall, a rust ring on a boiler leg, a cardboard box that has been dried and kept. We ask the owner where the water reached and photograph the mark. That height goes on the handover record, and everything electronic goes above it, with a margin.

Owners are sometimes surprised by how little ends up in the basement after that: a lock, a reader, a contact and a sensor. It is the least dramatic part of the job and the one that decides whether the doors still work the morning after the next storm.

Our homeowners policy has no sewer-backup rider. Does that change what you would put in the basement?

It makes the case for keeping equipment upstairs stronger, because a flooded controller or power supply would be replaced at your own cost. We cannot advise on insurance, but we can design so that the only door parts in the basement are inexpensive ones and list their replacement cost on the handover record.

Can you work on a basement door in the days right after a sewage backup, before it has been cleaned?
+

Three things, on the spot rather than in a leaflet. How to open their door from the inside with no power, and how to get out by the other route if there is one. A new hole through a foundation wall is needed only when the reader sits outside on a stairwell wall that has no other route, and then we core it at reader height, slope it to drain outward and seal it on both sides. Holes near the bottom of a foundation wall invite water and we avoid them.

The door at the bottom of our basement stairs swings out into the stairwell. Does that matter for an electric lock?
+

It matters more for the door than for the lock. An outswinging door can be blocked by water, ice or leaves collected on the landing, and its hinges face the stairwell, so we check the swing clearance and recommend non-removable hinge pins. The strike or lock works the same way in either swing, but an outswing door often needs a different strike model and a latch guard. We look at the landing and the drain before choosing hardware.

Our semi-detached house shares a front walk with the house next door. Where should our call station go so the neighbors' visitors don't ring us?
+

On your side of the shared walk, as close to your own door as a visitor naturally stops, with your house number clearly visible beside it. If the two front doors sit side by side under one porch, a station on your door frame or on the wall at your side of the porch is better than one on a shared post. Each button should ring only your household, and the camera should be aimed at your doorstep rather than across the neighbor's.

Do you need to go inside the basement studio to install, and how do we arrange that with the tenant?
+

Usually yes, briefly, because the lock and contact are on the studio's door and the inside lever has to be tested from inside the unit. The owner should give the occupant notice and agree a time; we bring the work order, wear shoe covers and keep the visit as short as the door allows. If the occupant prefers to be present, we can schedule for early morning or evening within our usual hours.

Our driveway slopes down to a garage under the house and rain runs into it. Can the garage door opener be part of the access system?
+

Often, yes. A garage door opener can be triggered by a credential, a phone or a code through a relay, so the household uses the same app for the garage as for the house doors. On a sloped driveway we mount any keypad or reader at the top of the slope rather than beside the garage door, and the relay and power above the floor. The DEP lists sloped driveways among the below-grade openings where water can enter, so the opener's own safety sensors and battery backup should be checked after every heavy storm.
