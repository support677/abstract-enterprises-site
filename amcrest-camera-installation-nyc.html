The Neighborhood

Why NYC Properties Need Amcrest Camera Installation

New York City was stitched together from very different places, and its buildings still show the seams. On January 1, 1898, according to the city's archaeology collection, Manhattan, Brooklyn, the Bronx, Queens and Staten Island joined into one city after a consolidation vote that passed by 64,744 to 64,467. The housing those five places had already built did not change overnight, and much of it is still standing. The Rent Guidelines Board's 2026 Housing Supply Report says 54 percent of the city's units are in buildings constructed before 1947, out of 3,705,000 housing units counted in 2023. The Citizens Housing and Planning Council puts it another way: more than 80 percent of the city's housing was built more than 50 years ago, in types that run from Riverdale mansions and Midland Beach bungalows to Sunnyside garden apartments, brownstones, tenements and high-rises near Central Park. When someone in New York buys an Amcrest kit, the camera in the box is the same one sold everywhere in the country. The wall it is going onto is not.

The Architectural League of New York traces two of the most common types. Rowhouse neighborhoods filled in between the Civil War and the First World War, using a house type that first appeared in numbers in Manhattan in the 1820s and 1830s. Tenements went up quickly and in huge numbers between 1860 and 1910, first in Manhattan and Brooklyn and later in the Bronx. A real-estate guide adds the prewar apartment types most New Yorkers know from the inside: four- to six-story walk-ups built before 1929, eight- to twelve-story elevator buildings from the 1910s and 1920s, and the larger doorman buildings of the 1920s and 1930s. One renovation firm describes prewar walls as often 12 to 18 inches of masonry. We cannot verify that figure for any one building, but it matches what our drill bits meet in many of them, and it is the first reason we wire Amcrest cameras with PoE cable instead of trusting WiFi. A camera that streams over the air through a brick party wall and two plaster partitions will drop frames, lose its connection and fill the app with gaps exactly when something happens.

The second reason is the kind of trouble cameras are bought for here. CBS New York reported a lobby package thief at a building at 36th Avenue and 31st Street in Astoria, where police said the suspect used a screwdriver to disable the lock on the door. In Park Slope, the same outlet reported thieves cutting open packages and taking things out of them while still inside a lobby. In Harlem, CBS covered a theft of furniture from the lobby of the Robeson on Lenox Avenue, where a key code is needed to enter and police said there was no forced entry; a tenant there said her family had its own door camera so they could see what went on in front of their apartment. Gothamist, citing SafeWise, put the value of packages stolen in the city close to a billion dollars, and Security.org's national survey found apartment residents more than three times as likely as single-family residents to have had a package stolen in the previous three months, 10 percent against 3 percent. The New York Attorney General's package-theft alert tells New Yorkers to use a doorbell camera or video surveillance to deter thieves or help police identify them. Lobbies, vestibules, mail areas and stoops are where most city camera systems earn their keep.

Amcrest fits that job in a particular way. According to Amcrest's product listing at Adorama, its cameras can record to a microSD card (sold separately), an FTP server, a network storage drive, a local computer or any Amcrest NVR, and they work with Blue Iris recording software. In plain terms, the footage can stay inside the building on hardware the owner controls, without a monthly cloud plan. That matters more in New York than people think. The NYC Council's data team, using Census survey figures, found that 8.1 percent of households had no internet access in 2021 and 24.5 percent had no cable, fiber or DSL broadband subscription, with 19 community districts above one-third. A camera system that only works when the cloud is reachable is a weak choice for a building whose owner lives elsewhere, or a walk-up where the internet line is a single cellular router in the super's apartment. An Amcrest NVR in a basement closet keeps recording when the internet goes down; remote viewing comes back when the connection does.

There is a second side to Amcrest that a buyer in the city should hear plainly before the drill comes out. Tec-Tel's NDAA Section 889 guide lists Amcrest as a US-distributed Dahua OEM, meaning cameras built on Dahua hardware and sold under the Amcrest name. The Intercept reported in 2021 that an Amcrest representative confirmed the products are not NDAA compliant, while Amcrest describes them as "conceptualized and designed by Amcrest in Houston, TX." The FCC's Covered List names Dahua, not Amcrest, and limits Dahua's entry to equipment used for public safety, security of government facilities, physical surveillance of critical infrastructure and other national security purposes. For a homeowner in Bay Ridge or a co-op in Washington Heights, that usually does not stop the install. For a building paid for partly with federal money, or an owner thinking about replacement parts five years from now, it is a real question. The next section lays out what we know and what we do not.

Then there are the rules that sit on top of the hardware. No general city law requires apartment buildings to have cameras, according to a security vendor's summary of building requirements; what the law does require is intercoms and lighting. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires intercoms in Class A multiple dwellings with eight or more units built or converted after January 1, 1968. Lighting matters to cameras directly: a camera aimed down an unlit corridor records very little that anyone can use. The Landmarks Preservation Commission writes security cameras into its rules on equipment attached to facades in historic districts. Co-op and condo boards often treat a building-wide camera replacement as something that needs their approval. The city's Tenant Data Privacy Act sets consent rules when entry systems collect data about tenants. And New York's recording laws decide whether a camera's microphone should be switched on at all. Each of those changes where an Amcrest camera can go, who has to agree, and how it is configured.

We also want to be clear about what our research for this page could not find. We did not pull HPD violation counts, 311 complaint counts or Department of Buildings permit data about cameras, so this page does not quote any. We could not read Reddit threads, Google's People Also Ask boxes, Bing or DuckDuckGo directly, and Yelp turned up praise for other local installers but no complaints we could cite. Where a claim on this page comes from a single vendor rather than a law, a regulator or a newspaper, we say so in the sentence. Where a vendor claim could not be checked, such as a reported "Local Law 50" camera-signage rule or a reported change to the Real Property Law forcing landlords to allow tenant cameras, we have left it out entirely.

New York City facts that shape an Amcrest install

  • An old housing stock: 54 percent of the city's 3,705,000 housing units are in buildings from before 1947, per the Rent Guidelines Board's 2026 Housing Supply Report.
  • Two dominant historic types: rowhouses from the Civil War to the First World War and tenements built between 1860 and 1910, per the Architectural League of New York.
  • A large protected stock: the Landmarks Preservation Commission protects 1,460 individual landmarks, 121 interior landmarks and 12 scenic landmarks, plus its historic districts, according to Wikipedia's summary of LPC designations.
  • Internet gaps: 8.1 percent of NYC households had no internet access in 2021 and 24.5 percent had no wired broadband subscription, per the NYC Council's data team.
  • Intercoms are required, cameras are not: MDL §50-a covers Class A buildings of eight or more units built or converted after January 1, 1968; no general city law mandates cameras.
  • A voluntary police link: 311 says businesses with cameras can register camera locations with the NYPD at NewYorkCityConnect.org, and amNY reported a 2024 pilot in nine precincts letting businesses share live feeds by choice.

What city owners often add once the cameras are in. Once a building's Amcrest system is recording the lobby and the door, the next request we usually hear is about the door itself: an intercom that actually works, or a key-fob reader so the super is not cutting new keys every month. Those are separate services with their own quotes, and the Tenant Data Privacy Act rules described further down this page apply when entry data is collected. If you are thinking about either, mention it when we come out, so the cable for a future door station goes in during the same visit rather than through a second set of holes later.

What "Dahua OEM" means in a city building

An OEM camera is one built by one manufacturer and sold under another company's name. Tec-Tel's NDAA guide lists Amcrest as a US-distributed Dahua OEM, and both ICTally and Cargotrans describe Amcrest and Lorex as brands that have historically sold cameras built on Dahua hardware. Amcrest's own statement, as quoted by The Intercept, is that its products are "conceptualized and designed by Amcrest in Houston, TX." Both things can be true at once: a company can design and specify products in Texas and source the hardware from a factory partner. For installation, what matters is that Amcrest cameras share a great deal with Dahua cameras in how they are configured, how their firmware behaves and how their recorders talk to them. That shared lineage is one reason a technician who knows Dahua recorders can usually work through an Amcrest system quickly.

Private homes and private buildings

The FCC's Covered List entry for Dahua covers equipment used for public safety, security of government facilities, physical security surveillance of critical infrastructure and other national security purposes. A camera over the stoop of a Crown Heights rowhouse or in the lobby of a privately owned Queens walk-up is none of those things. One camera-checking site, Blinkless, states that the FCC rules do not revoke equipment already installed and do not ban private use. We have not seen any source saying a private New York owner must remove an Amcrest system. So for private homes and private buildings, we install Amcrest the way we install any PoE camera brand, and we keep the paperwork tidy so the owner knows exactly what is on the wall.

Buildings that use federal money

NDAA Section 889 is about the federal government buying, and paying for, covered equipment. That is why Tec-Tel and The Intercept focus on whether Amcrest products can be sold to the government. If a building's security upgrade is being paid for in part with federal grants or loans, the safe move is to ask the agency providing the money before buying any Amcrest equipment, and to get the answer in writing. We do not try to answer that question for a board or a nonprofit; we flag it at the first call and, if the answer is no, we quote a compliant system instead. It is far cheaper to change brands on paper than to rip out a finished install.

The parts question

The practical worry for most owners is not legality but supply. If the FCC's 2026 action limits the import and marketing of some Dahua-based equipment for certain uses, an owner who loses one camera in a few years may find the exact model harder to buy. That is a reason to plan, not to panic. We record every camera's model number and firmware version on the hand-over sheet, keep cable runs to standard network cable that any PoE camera can use, and set up the recorder so a replacement camera from another line could be added if it ever came to that. The cable in your walls is the expensive part of the job, and it is brand-neutral.

Decide what each camera is for

Before you count cameras, write one line for each spot: "see faces coming through the vestibule," "see who takes packages from the mail table," "see the whole back yard," "read a plate at the garage gate." A face camera and a wide yard camera are different lenses at different heights. A camera with no clear purpose tends to end up aimed at a ceiling or a wall. This list is also what a co-op board or a skeptical tenant will want to see, because it shows the cameras are about doors and packages, not people's private lives.

Look at the other side of every wall

In a rowhouse or a tenement, the wall you want to drill through to bring a cable inside may have a kitchen, a bathroom or a neighbor's apartment on the other side. Before we arrive, look at what sits behind each spot where a cable would come through: pipes, an electrical panel, a gas meter, a stair stringer. We scan and probe on the day, but a photo of the far side tells us where to be most careful and sometimes points to a better route through a closet or a cellar ceiling.

Know your network

Amcrest cameras and recorders are network devices, and two buyer reports we read show why that matters. One Blue Iris user reported cameras that kept changing their network addresses, which breaks recording software that expects each camera to stay put. Another buyer complained that a camera was talking to an address that was not on their network. Tell us who supplies your internet, where the router is, and whether anyone else manages it, such as a landlord or a building IT person. That lets us plan fixed addresses for every camera and decide how much of the system should be allowed to reach the internet at all.

New York packs nearly every building type in American history into five boroughs. Brownstones: stone, stoops and the cellar route

A brownstone facade is soft sandstone laid over brick, and it is unforgiving. Drill too fast and the face spalls; drill into a carved lintel and the damage is permanent. On a brownstone we put cameras where they do the most good with the least visible hardware: under the stoop looking at the parlor-floor door and the garden-level gate, inside the vestibule looking at the inner door and the mail slot, and at the back of the house looking across the yard. Cable from a stoop camera usually goes straight through the wall into the cellar, then along the joists to wherever the recorder will live. Amcrest's turret guide calls for a 1-inch paddle bit on through-wall runs in wood; in masonry we use a bit sized for the cable and the connector, then seal the hole against weather. In a historic district, the camera's place on the facade is not just our call, which the next section covers.

Tenement walk-ups: one stair, many doors

A walk-up has one main path: the street door, the vestibule, the mailboxes and the stair. That makes coverage simple and placement delicate. A camera at the street door, one on the mailboxes and, where the owner wants them, one per landing aimed down the stair rather than at any apartment door covers the building's movement without singling anyone out. Seneca Security warns that a camera aimed specifically at one tenant's door, without covering the wider hall, can look like harassment in a legal dispute. We plan every landing camera to show the stair and the hall, not a particular door. Cable in these halls usually runs in a small surface raceway painted to match, because opening plaster-on-brick walls up four or five floors costs more than the camera system and rarely ends cleanly.

Prewar co-ops: boards, risers and paperwork

The larger prewar buildings are usually co-ops, and their boards treat building-wide security changes as alterations. Seneca Security notes that camera system replacements and new access control may require board approval. Boards often want a contractor's certificate of insurance, set hours for noisy work and use of the service elevator, and a plan of where each camera and cable will go. Inside, these buildings can have plaster on metal lath, terracotta block or solid masonry, and old telecom risers that may or may not have room. We ask the managing agent for the building's alteration rules at the first call, send the camera plan and insurance papers ahead of the visit, and check in with the super on arrival. A board that sees a clear plan, with each camera's purpose written down, usually approves faster.

HDFC and small co-ops: local recording on a budget

The Comptroller's office describes HDFC co-ops as largely prewar multifamily walk-up buildings woven into the streetscape. Many are run by volunteer boards watching every line of the budget. This is where Amcrest's local recording makes the most sense: an NVR with a drive in the cellar, PoE cameras at the door and the mailboxes, and no monthly bill. We keep these jobs simple, write down every login, and set up at least two board members with viewer accounts so the system does not live on one person's phone.

Houses in the outer boroughs: driveways and alleys

Landmarks and Facades

Cameras on Landmarked Facades: How LPC Rules Shape an Amcrest Install

For a brownstone owner in a historic district, the most important decision about an Amcrest camera is not the model. It is which wall it goes on. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's 2018 rule amendments wrote down its staff practice on equipment attached to facades and areaways, naming intercoms, light fixtures, security grilles, mailboxes and security cameras among them. The proposed rules described in the Commission's August 2018 revisions would prohibit many of these objects on a primary facade, the side facing the street, unless there is no feasible alternative or the location is required by an agency or a utility-like entity. The Commission's Permit Guidebook has a section on installing security cameras, including small dome cameras finished to match the facade.

This is not a theoretical concern. The Commission's public hearing calendar for July 16, 2013 lists an application for 52 West 76th Street, an 1887–89 rowhouse in the Upper West Side/Central Park West Historic District, to legalize security cameras and an intercom installed without LPC permits. Legalizing work after the fact costs time and money that a phone call beforehand would have saved. The Commission's FasTrack program issues permits for many simple exterior work types on non-visible facades within 10 working days, according to its 2023 rules. In practice, that pushes us toward cameras inside the vestibule, under the stoop or on rear and side walls, which is often better coverage anyway.

What a storefront Amcrest system has to do

A shop camera has three jobs that a home camera rarely has. It must capture a usable face at the door, not just a shape against the light; it must show hands at the register clearly enough to settle a dispute about a transaction; and it must keep recording overnight when the gate is down and the lights are off. The recorder goes somewhere a person grabbing things in a hurry cannot easily take it, on battery backup, with remote viewing set up on the owner's phone. We schedule the drilling for the hours the store is closed and leave the floor clean before opening.

Rules and practical limits for business cameras

  • Audio off by default. RecordingLaw notes that cameras recording sound must meet New York's one-party consent rule under Penal Law §250.00. A shop camera recording customers' conversations at the counter, where the owner is not part of the conversation, is the situation that rule is about. Amcrest cameras with microphones are set to video only unless the owner has a lawful reason and has thought it through.
  • No cameras where people expect privacy. New York Penal Law §250.45, as Impera Security summarizes it, makes it a crime to install a camera where a person has a reasonable expectation of privacy without consent. Restrooms and changing areas are never covered.
  • Posted notice helps. According to Ambient.ai's summary, Penal Law §250.65 protects systems where a written notice is posted conspicuously stating that video surveillance is in use for security, or where the cameras are clearly obvious. We recommend a sign at the entrance.
  • Biometrics are different. The city's biometric identifier law, Local Law 3 of 2021, requires covered commercial establishments that collect biometric data from customers to post notices at entrances, but its notice provision generally does not apply to information collected through photographs or video recordings, according to the city's rules page and a Carlton Fields summary. If a business turns on face recognition features, that changes and needs legal advice.
  • Roll-down gates and sightlines. A 2009 city law required storefront gates to be at least 70 percent transparent by July 1, 2026. NY1 reported on July 2, 2026 that the City Council unanimously passed Int. 910, repealing that deadline so a compliant gate is needed only when a gate is replaced for other reasons, and sent it to the Mayor. Either way, a solid gate blocks an inside camera's view of the sidewalk; we place cameras with the gate down in mind.
  • Sharing with police is optional. 311 says businesses can register camera locations with the NYPD at NewYorkCityConnect.org and share live video in emergencies. amNY reported that the Connect New York program lets businesses choose how and when footage is accessible. We set up whatever the owner decides and nothing more.
  • Help with costs may be coming. The City Council's June 30, 2026 press release describes Int. 553-A, which would require Small Business Services to create a program giving small retail owners financial help to buy and install security technology, and Int. 623-2026, introduced in February 2026, would create a pilot installing cameras and plexiglass in some small businesses. Neither is a program you can apply to on the basis of this page; check with SBS.
  • Electrical work is separate. PoE cameras take their power from the network cable, so most Amcrest jobs need no new outlets. Where a recorder closet needs a new receptacle, that circuit is a licensed electrician's job and our quote says so.

Field Notes: The Hour Before Any Amcrest Camera Goes on a City Wall

We do not publish stories about a particular customer's building unless we can document them, so this is not a tale about one address. It is the routine our technicians follow on a typical city Amcrest job, whether it is four cameras on a Bed-Stuy rowhouse or twelve in a walk-up in Inwood.

Apartments, co-ops and condos

Can you put Amcrest cameras into a Manhattan co-op or condo building?

Yes, once the board or managing agent has approved the work. Building-wide camera replacements usually count as alterations, so we ask for the building's rules and insurance requirements at the first call, send a certificate of insurance and a plan of every camera and cable ahead of time, and work inside the building's permitted hours. Inside a single apartment, a shareholder or owner's own cameras raise different questions; aiming them out into a hallway shared with neighbors is a common-area matter the board controls. How many Amcrest cameras does a typical Manhattan apartment need?

Often fewer than people expect. Inside a single apartment, one camera covering the entry door from inside, and perhaps one on a terrace or back room with a fire escape, is usually enough. Interior cameras in a home you share raise their own privacy issues, and anything aimed at the common hallway needs the building's approval. For a whole building, the count is driven by doors and paths, not square footage: each street entrance, the lobby or vestibule, the mail or package area, the elevator lobby, service and cellar doors, and any yard or roof access.

Does a Manhattan camera install need a permit?

It depends on where the camera goes and what building it is on. Our brownstone is in a historic district. Does LPC have to approve the cameras?

For cameras on the exterior, plan on it. Interior cameras, such as a dome on the vestibule ceiling, generally do not need LPC review unless the work affects the exterior or the interior itself is designated. Owners, landlords and tenants

As a landlord, can I install cameras in my building's common areas?

Generally yes. Common areas such as lobbies, mail areas, stairs and hallways are the owner's to secure, and a Justia answer from April 2026 about a New York co-op said video surveillance is legal as long as the camera is not aimed into an apartment when its door is open. Seneca Security advises owners of rent-stabilized buildings to write down the security reason before installation, using incident reports, police reports or insurance recommendations, and to tell tenants in advance. We place every hallway camera to cover the hall and the stair, not one tenant's door, which keeps the system defensible.

What happens if tenants object to new cameras in the halls?

Objections usually come down to two worries: that a camera is aimed at someone's door, or that the footage will be used against a tenant. The placement answer is to cover the hallway as a whole, which Seneca Security notes is what separates security from something that looks like harassment. The data answer is to decide in advance who can see footage, how long it is kept and how it is exported, and to tell tenants. If the cameras connect to a smart entry system, the city's Tenant Data Privacy Act, as HPD explains it, requires consent before collecting certain data and bars using it to harass or evict. That law is a matter for the owner's lawyer; our part is a system configured to match the policy.

Recording, storage and the network

What does cloud storage per camera cost with Amcrest, and do I need it?

You do not need it to record. Amcrest's listing at Adorama says its cameras can record to a microSD card, an FTP server, a network storage drive, a local computer or an Amcrest NVR, and they work with Blue Iris. Most of our city installs record to an NVR in the building, which has no per-camera monthly charge. Cloud plans are sold by Amcrest, not by us, and their prices change, so we do not quote them on this page. If you want an off-site copy because the recorder could be stolen or flooded, we can set that up with whichever plan you choose, alongside local recording rather than instead of it.

Should I use an Amcrest NVR or run the cameras on Blue Iris?

Both work. An Amcrest NVR is a single box built for the job, simple for a super or a busy owner, and it powers PoE cameras directly. Blue Iris runs on a Windows computer and offers more control, but the computer has to stay on, updated and secure, and someone has to look after it. One Blue Iris user reported Amcrest cameras changing their network addresses, which is why we set a fixed address for every camera either way. For a small co-op or a shop, we usually suggest the NVR; for an owner who already runs a home server, Blue Iris can make sense.

Why do Amcrest cameras sometimes vanish from the recorder after a few days?

The most common cause we see is addressing. If cameras get their network address automatically from a router, a router reboot or replacement can hand them new addresses, and a recorder or Blue Iris looking for the old ones loses them. The Blue Iris user report in our research describes exactly this. The fix is to give each camera a fixed address, or a reservation in the router, and record them on the hand-over sheet. Weak or damaged cable runs are the second cause, which is why we certify every run before we hang a camera on it.

A buyer said an Amcrest camera talked to an address outside their network. Should that worry me?

It is worth taking seriously, the same way you would with any internet-connected camera. Cameras with cloud and app features reach out to servers on the internet; whether that traffic is expected is a question about which features are turned on. On our installs we change default passwords, turn off features the owner does not need, and where the owner wants, keep the cameras on a network segment that cannot reach the internet directly, with only the recorder allowed out for remote viewing. That way the cameras do their job inside the building and nothing else.

What AI Gets Wrong About Amcrest Camera Installation in NYC

We could not capture Google's AI Overview or its People Also Ask boxes for this page from our build environment, so we will not pretend to quote them. What follows are claims we see repeated in quick online answers about Amcrest and about camera installs in New York, set against what the sources and our own field practice say. Marketplaces such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish national and regional cost guides that feed many of those answers; they are useful for a rough idea, but they are not a quote for a wall in Harlem or a cellar in Astoria.

"Amcrest cameras are banned in the US."

This is the claim we hear most, and it is not what the sources say. Amcrest does not appear on the FCC Covered List by name. Dahua does, and the FCC's own entry limits Dahua to equipment used for public safety, security of government facilities, physical surveillance of critical infrastructure and other national security purposes.

The real limits are narrower. Amcrest is listed under Dahua on NDAA Section 889 lists, which concern federal purchasing, and ICTally and Cargotrans report that the FCC acted in July 2026 against continued import and marketing of some previously authorized Covered List equipment, for Dahua only for certain security uses. Blinkless says the rules do not revoke installed equipment or ban private use.

For a private home or building in New York, that usually means the install can go ahead. For anyone using federal money, the funding agency gets the last word, and we raise that at the first call.

"Amcrest is a WiFi brand, so there is nothing to wire."

Amcrest sells WiFi and SmartHome cameras, but it also sells PoE turrets, domes, bullets and NVRs, and its own installation guides describe running cable along the surface or hidden inside walls and ceilings. For a city building, the PoE line is usually the better choice.

The reason is the building stock. More than half the city's units are in buildings from before 1947, and prewar masonry walls are thick. A WiFi camera on a brownstone stoop talking to a router two floors up and one party wall over is asking for dropped footage.

A PoE cable carries both power and video, needs no outlet at the camera, and keeps recording through WiFi trouble. The cable is the work; the camera is the easy part.

"You need a cloud subscription to record."

Not with Amcrest. According to its Adorama listing, the cameras can record to a microSD card, an FTP server, a network drive, a local computer or an Amcrest NVR, and work with Blue Iris. A subscription is a choice, not a requirement.

In a city where the NYC Council's data team found 24.5 percent of households without a wired broadband subscription in 2021, local recording is a practical advantage. The NVR in the cellar keeps recording while the internet is down.

What a subscription adds is an off-site copy. If the recorder could be stolen or flooded, that can be worth having alongside local recording, and we will set it up if you choose a plan.

"Any Amcrest app works with any Amcrest camera."

Amcrest runs more than one app. A Walmart listing for one SmartHome-series camera says it works only with the Amcrest SmartHome app and has view-only access in the Amcrest View Pro app. A buyer who mixes product lines can end up with two apps on one phone.

That matters most in buildings where several people need to view footage: a board, a super, an owner who lives elsewhere. Giving each of them two apps and two logins is how systems stop being used.

We read every model number before we mount anything and tell you which app each camera needs. If a mixed order would be awkward to live with, we say so while there is still time to return something.

"You can re-aim the camera from your phone later."

Only some cameras can. Amcrest's turret guide states that turret models cannot pan, tilt or pivot automatically from the app or web interface. The aim you set when the camera goes up is the aim you keep until someone climbs a ladder.

In a narrow walk-up vestibule or under a brownstone stoop, a few degrees decide whether the camera sees faces or the tops of heads. Getting that right takes standing in the spot by day and checking it again with the lights off.

That is why our process includes a night check of every view before we leave, and why we ask the owner to look at each picture with us.

"Cameras in New York need no approval at all."

There is no general city law requiring cameras, and private cameras are legal in many places, but approval is often needed from someone. On a landmarked facade, the LPC's rules cover security cameras; the Commission's July 16, 2013 calendar shows a rowhouse owner on West 76th Street applying to legalize cameras installed without permits.

In co-ops and condos, boards often treat camera systems as alterations. In rentals, a tenant mounting a camera in a common hallway generally needs the landlord's permission. Audio recording falls under Penal Law §250.00.

The approvals are rarely hard to get, but they are much easier before the drilling than after. We ask who has to say yes at the first call.

Every Amcrest job in the city follows the same eight steps, whether it is two cameras on a Queens driveway or sixteen in a Bronx elevator building. The order matters most in old masonry buildings and shared buildings, where skipping the planning is the usual cause of a camera that sees the wrong thing or a hole that should not have been drilled.

Default passwords changed, separate viewer accounts, the right Amcrest app on each phone, audio off unless lawful and wanted, internet access limited to what viewing needs.

Every run certified, day and night views checked, a test clip exported, recorder and cables labeled, and a sheet listing every camera, address, model and login left with you.

FAQ

NYC Amcrest Camera Installation FAQ

Is it still legal to buy and install Amcrest cameras in New York in 2026?
+

For private homes and private buildings, the sources we read do not say otherwise. Amcrest is not named on the FCC Covered List; Dahua is, and its entry is limited to public safety, government facility security, critical infrastructure surveillance and national security uses. One source says the 2026 FCC rules do not revoke installed equipment or ban private use. Anyone spending federal grant or loan money should check with the funding agency first, because Amcrest appears under Dahua on NDAA Section 889 lists.

Where are Amcrest cameras actually made?
+

Amcrest says its products are conceptualized and designed by Amcrest in Houston, Texas, as quoted by The Intercept in 2021. NDAA compliance lists such as Tec-Tel's describe Amcrest as a US-distributed Dahua OEM, meaning the hardware is built by Dahua, a Chinese manufacturer, and sold under the Amcrest name. Both descriptions appear in the sources, and we record the exact model of every camera we install so you always know what is on your wall.

Are home and building security cameras legal in New York at all?
+

Yes, video cameras for security are legal in most places a property owner controls. The limits are about privacy and sound. Penal Law section 250.45 makes it a crime to put a camera where someone has a reasonable expectation of privacy without consent, such as a bathroom, and audio recording must meet the one-party consent rule under Penal Law section 250.00. That is why we set cameras to video only unless the owner has a lawful reason for sound.

Does city law make apartment buildings put in cameras?
+

No general city law requires cameras in apartment buildings, according to the building-requirements summaries we read. What the law does require is intercoms in Class A buildings of eight or more units built or converted after January 1, 1968, under Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a, along with lighting. Cameras are a choice an owner or board makes, usually after package thefts or break-ins, and good lighting is what makes their footage useful.

Who in a building should be allowed to see the camera footage?
+

That is the owner's or board's decision, and it should be written down before the system goes live. Most buildings limit live viewing to the owner or managing agent and the super, with exports for police or insurers handled by one named person. On an Amcrest NVR we create a separate account for each person with only the rights they need, so access can be removed when someone leaves without changing everyone else's login.

Should an owner tell tenants before cameras go up in the common areas?
+

It is good practice, and vendors who advise rent-stabilized owners recommend notifying tenants in advance and documenting the security reason in writing. Posting a clear sign that video surveillance is in use also matters, because Penal Law section 250.65 protects systems where a notice is conspicuously posted or the cameras are plainly obvious. We are not lawyers; we can give you camera positions and a plan to share with tenants.

Can my landlord stop me from mounting my own camera?
+

Inside your own apartment, a camera that sits on a shelf and does not need drilling is usually your business. A camera mounted on a hallway wall or aimed down a shared corridor is on common space, and a landlord or board generally controls that, which means you need permission in writing. We do not mount tenant cameras in common areas without the owner's written approval, and we suggest a no-drill indoor camera when approval is not given.

Does a company installing cameras in NYC need a license?
+

Yes. The city's business portal says a license from the New York State Department of State is required to install, service or maintain security or fire alarm systems, with education and exam requirements, while master electricians register instead. AESS holds NYS license number 12000287431. Homeowners can install their own cameras, but anyone doing it as a paid business should be able to show you a license number.

I mounted some Amcrest cameras myself and they keep failing. Can you take the system over?
+

Yes. We start by tracing and testing every cable, because a weak run is the most common hidden cause, then check addressing, firmware, the recorder's drive and the app setup. Cameras that move to new network addresses after a router restart are a frequent culprit and are fixed with fixed addresses. If a camera was mounted into a surface that will not hold, we remount it. Repair work is priced from your borough's Amcrest price page.

Can an Amcrest system record around the clock without paying for a plan?
+

Yes. With PoE cameras on an Amcrest NVR, continuous recording is limited by the size of the hard drive, not by a subscription. We size the drive to the number of days you want kept and set the recorder to overwrite the oldest footage when it fills. Motion-only recording stretches the same drive further. Cloud backup is optional and sold by Amcrest, not by us.

Will a WiFi Amcrest camera get a signal through a prewar building's walls?
+

Sometimes, but it is the weak point of many city installs. Prewar buildings often have thick masonry walls that block WiFi, and a camera on the far side of a party wall or two floors from the router tends to drop out. Where we can, we run a PoE cable instead, which carries power and video together. Where a cable truly cannot go, a WiFi camera close to the router or a network extender in the right spot can work, and we test the signal before we commit.

Is it safe to view my Amcrest cameras from my phone when I am away?
+

It can be, if the system is set up with care. We change every default password, create separate viewer accounts, keep firmware current at hand-over, avoid opening recorder ports straight to the internet, and where you want it keep the cameras on a network segment that only the recorder can talk to. Remote viewing then goes through the recorder and the app, rather than each camera being reachable from outside.

Can the cameras be shared with the NYPD, and do I have to?
+

Sharing is voluntary. 311 says businesses can register camera locations with the NYPD at NewYorkCityConnect.org and share live video in emergencies, and amNY reported that the Connect New York program lets businesses choose how and when footage is accessible. A state bill to make camera registration mandatory was introduced several times but we found no evidence it passed. We set up only what the owner chooses.
