Plain Terms

Elevator Access Control, Explained for a Rockland Board Buying It for the First Time

Elevator access control is a way of making your elevator ask a question before it moves: who are you, and where are you allowed to go? Without it, the elevator in your Rockland building answers anyone who reaches the lobby. With it, the car waits for an approved fob, card, code, phone or face before it will carry someone upstairs, and it can limit each person to the floors that belong to them.

The equipment is modest. A reader goes in the elevator car, usually on or beside the button panel, or at the lobby or garage call button. A small access control panel goes in the machine room or another secure closet, with a battery backup. Between the panel and the elevator controller sits a set of switches, called relays, or an interface board that does the same job. When a resident presents an approved credential, the panel closes the right switch for a few seconds, and the elevator controller accepts a call to that resident's floor. When a stranger presses the button without one, nothing happens.

Nothing about the elevator itself is rebuilt. The motor, the cables, the doors, the safety circuits and the controller stay exactly as they are. Our equipment sits beside the elevator and talks to it through a connection that your building's licensed elevator contractor makes and approves. That division of work matters in New York State, where the people who touch the elevator's own systems must hold a license from the Department of Labor, and we explain it in detail further down.

For a Rockland building that has never had it, elevator access control solves a short list of real problems:

  • Strangers who slip in behind a delivery can no longer ride to the upper hallways or the roof door.
  • A tenant who moves out takes nothing with them; their fob stops working the moment the manager deletes it.
  • Garage levels in waterfront and transit buildings stop being a back door into the residential floors.
  • A medical or professional building can keep each practice's floor private after hours without a guard.
  • The board or owner gets a record of who used the elevator and when, which settles disputes quickly.

What it does not do is replace good doors. The lobby door, the garage door and the stair doors are still the first layer. Elevator access control is the second layer, and in a short Rockland building it is often the most cost-effective second layer, because a single reader in a single car protects every floor above the lobby.

Why Rockland Is Different

One Car, a Few Floors, a Village Downtown: How Rockland Elevator Buildings Differ From the City's

Most of what is written online about elevator security is written for Manhattan towers: banks of six cars, destination dispatch, a security desk staffed around the clock. Very little of it fits a Rockland building, and following it can lead a board to buy far more system than it needs.

Here is what we usually find when we survey an elevator building in Rockland County.

A single elevator car

The majority of Rockland elevator buildings we see have one car. That simplifies the design enormously. One reader in one car, wired to one small panel, protects the whole building. There is no second car for a stranger to wait for, and no bank of hall stations to coordinate.

Three to six floors

A building on Main Street in Nyack or Lafayette Avenue in Suffern may have a lobby, two to four residential floors and a basement. Floor-by-floor restriction across five floors needs only a handful of relays, so even the more advanced design stays affordable here. Often, though, the board's actual goal is simpler: keep non-residents out of the car entirely. A single reader that enables the whole car does that.

A garage or a parking level

Waterfront buildings in Haverstraw advertise indoor garage parking. Transit-area rentals in Suffern and Pearl River pair surface lots with garages. New construction such as 30 West in Haverstraw includes a garage that the public will use too. Any time the elevator stops at a parking level, that stop deserves its own decision, and in many Rockland buildings it is the most important decision on the job.

Older riders

Senior and 55-plus buildings make up a large share of Rockland's elevator stock. The credential has to work for someone with arthritis, limited vision or a walker, and the reader has to sit within comfortable reach for a rider in a wheelchair. That is a design requirement, not an afterthought.

No full-time staff

Most Rockland elevator buildings are run by a volunteer board, a small owner or a managing agent who covers several properties. The system must be simple to run from a laptop or a phone, and a lost fob must be easy to cancel without calling us.

Weather that reaches the machine room

The river towns sit on the Hudson, and much of the county loses power in a bad ice storm or nor'easter. Where the access panel goes, and what the elevator does when power fails, matters more here than in a city high-rise with a generator in the cellar.

None of this makes Rockland work harder. It makes it different. The right Rockland elevator access control design is usually smaller, simpler and more focused than the city version, and a good installer should tell you so rather than quoting a tower system for a four-story building. Our Hudson Valley elevator access control page covers the wider region; this page is about Rockland's own buildings.

How It Works

How a Fob Becomes a Floor in a Rockland Elevator

It helps to follow a single ride from start to finish, because once a board sees the path, the choices on the quote make sense.

Step one: the reader sees the credential

A resident of a four-story building in Pearl River steps into the car and holds a fob near the reader. The reader picks up the fob's number by radio over a distance of an inch or two. A keypad reads a code instead; a phone reader picks up a Bluetooth or NFC signal; a face reader compares the rider to an enrolled photo. Whatever the credential, the reader's only job is to identify it and pass the number on.

Step two: the signal travels to the panel

The reader's wire runs down the elevator's traveling cable, the flexible bundle that hangs from the car and follows it up and down the hoistway, to the machine room. If the traveling cable has spare conductors, we use them. If it does not, the elevator contractor may add a cable, or we may use a wireless bridge designed for elevators. This single question, whether there are free conductors in the traveling cable, decides more about the cost of a Rockland job than any other.

Step three: the panel decides

The access control panel checks the number against its list. Is this fob active? Is it allowed in this elevator? Is it allowed at this hour? Which floors may it reach? The answer takes a fraction of a second.

Step four: the elevator is allowed to answer

If the fob is approved, the panel closes a relay for each floor that resident may reach, usually for five to ten seconds. The relays connect to inputs on the elevator controller that the elevator contractor has designated for security. While a relay is closed, the floor button works. When it opens again, the button goes back to doing nothing for anyone else.

Step five: the ride is logged

The panel records the event: which credential, which elevator, which time, and whether access was granted or refused. A board member or manager can look this up later, which is useful when a dispute or an incident needs facts.

Two things are worth noticing. First, nothing in that chain touches the elevator's safety circuits. Our relays only tell the controller which calls it may accept; the controller still decides how the car moves. Second, the design scales gently. A Level 1 job uses one relay that enables the whole car. Floor-by-floor control adds a relay per controlled floor. In a five-floor Rockland building, the difference between those two is a few relays, a little more programming and a longer conversation with the elevator contractor, not a different system.

Floor Control

Floor-by-Floor Restriction in a Three- to Six-Story Rockland Building

Floor restriction means a credential reaches only certain floors. A resident of the third floor can ride to the lobby, the third floor and perhaps the laundry level; a resident of the fourth floor reaches the fourth; the superintendent and the managing agent reach everything. It is the most requested feature in larger buildings, and in Rockland it is worth thinking through carefully, because a short building does not always need it.

When a Rockland building needs floor restriction

  • Mixed-use buildings where a store, office or medical practice occupies one floor and apartments occupy the others.
  • Medical and professional buildings where each practice wants its own floor closed after hours.
  • Buildings where the garage or basement holds storage cages, bike rooms or mechanical spaces that residents should not reach freely.
  • Senior and supportive buildings where staff, program spaces or records rooms are on a floor residents should not visit unaccompanied.
  • Larger co-ops, such as the Main Street co-ops in Spring Valley, where the board wants individual floors private.

When a single reader is enough

In a three-story Nyack building where every floor is residential and every resident is trusted to visit neighbors, restricting each person to their own floor adds cost and friction without much benefit. A single reader that enables the whole car for any resident keeps strangers out, which is usually the actual goal. If the board later wants floor restriction, the same reader and panel can usually be expanded.

In-car control versus hall-call control

There are two places to control access. In-car control puts the reader inside the elevator and restricts which buttons work. Hall-call control puts a reader beside the call button on a particular landing, such as the garage, so the car cannot even be summoned to that landing without a credential. Rockland buildings often need both: a reader in the car for the residential floors and a reader at the garage hall station to stop the parking level from being an open door.

What floor restriction cannot do

It cannot stop someone from riding along with a resident who is allowed to go to a floor. It cannot lock the stairs, which must remain usable as an exit. And it cannot override firefighters, who take control of the elevator in an emergency and ignore our system entirely. Boards that understand these limits make better decisions about where to spend the rest of the security budget.

Counting the relays

For a rough sense of scale, the number of relays equals the floors you want to control multiplied by the number of cars, plus any hall stations with readers. A six-story, single-car building with every floor controlled needs six relays, plus one for a garage hall-call reader. That is a small, tidy panel. We count them exactly on the survey and list them line by line on the written proposal.

Building Types

Rockland Building Types and the Elevator Access Control Each One Usually Needs

Rockland's elevator buildings fall into a handful of recognizable types. Each type tends to need the same starting design, and knowing which one you are in saves time on the survey.

Village downtown buildings over stores: Nyack, Suffern, Pearl River, Spring Valley, Haverstraw

Main Street in Nyack, Lafayette Avenue in Suffern, Central Avenue in Pearl River and Main Street in Spring Valley all have apartments over stores, and the newer ones have an elevator. Nyack Point Apartments at 263 Main Street is a three-story building; Diana Place at 259 Main Street lists an elevator; the village's own zoning analysis counted eleven new units approved across six small projects in the downtown mixed-use zone over two years. These are small buildings with big problems for their size: a street door that opens onto a sidewalk with heavy foot traffic, a ground-floor tenant with customers coming and going, and apartments above that need to stay private. The usual design is a reader in the car that enables the residential floors, with the store or office floor either open during business hours or on its own credential group.

Waterfront buildings in Haverstraw and Nyack

The Waterfront at Harbors in Haverstraw is built as elevator buildings with only two homes per floor and indoor garage parking, on the river at its widest point. In buildings like this, the elevator opens almost directly into each home's hallway, so the elevator is effectively the front door of every apartment. That makes a reader in the car, and a reader at the garage landing, a high priority. It also makes panel placement important, because the garage level of a waterfront building is the last place you want electronics during a storm surge.

Senior and 55-plus buildings

Nyack Plaza's senior homes sit in its elevatored tower. Esther Gitlow Towers on Lafayette Avenue in Suffern lists controlled access and elevators. West Haverstraw Senior Citizens Apartments on Route 9W and Maplewood Gardens in Nanuet appear on the state's senior housing referral list. In Pearl River, the Town of Orangetown adopted a zone change in 2025 for twenty 55-plus rentals downtown. These buildings need a credential older residents can use without frustration, a reader at a height a wheelchair user can reach, and a plan for aides, family and visiting nurses. Some senior buildings in Rockland are only two stories, such as the residential buildings at Vincent's Village in Nanuet; where those have a lift or elevator, one reader often covers it.

Co-ops and condominiums

Spring Valley's Hickory House is a 178-unit shareholder co-op on Main Street, and condo communities such as the Harbors on the Haverstraw waterfront are governed by elected boards. Co-op and condo boards usually want an audit trail, a clear policy on who gets fobs, and a system the managing agent can run. Floor restriction is common in larger co-ops; whole-car enable is often enough in smaller ones.

Transit-area rentals

176 Lafayette in Suffern advertises a secure elevator building with keyless entry and intercom access, within half a mile of the Suffern train station. The 109 units proposed near the Pearl River Metro-North station would be the same kind of building if approved. New rentals like these often arrive with a cloud-based door and intercom platform already chosen by the developer. The best time to add the elevator is before the cab is finished, so the platform's elevator module, the traveling cable conductors and the controller inputs are all specified together.

Medical and professional buildings

Medical offices cluster near Montefiore Nyack Hospital and Good Samaritan in Suffern, and outpatient practices have been opening across the county. We name those hospitals only as landmarks; the work we describe is in the independent medical and professional buildings around them. A two- or three-story medical building with an elevator typically has several practices on different floors, each with its own hours. Floor restriction by practice, with the lobby and ground floor open during the day, is the usual answer.

Office buildings and campuses

Rockland's office stock runs from small professional buildings to the 21-story tower at Blue Hill Plaza in Pearl River and the office and light-industrial parks around Orangeburg and Nanuet. Multi-tenant office buildings often want after-hours floor restriction tied to each tenant's badge, so the building can stay open in the morning and lock down at night without a guard.

Codes and Approvals

Permits, Code Officials and Privacy Rules for Elevator Access Control in Rockland

One of the most common questions we hear from Rockland boards is who has to approve this. The honest answer is that it depends on where the building is, because Rockland does not have one building department.

The Uniform Code, enforced locally

New York State's Uniform Fire Prevention and Building Code applies everywhere outside New York City, but it is enforced by the local town or village. Rockland has five towns, Clarkstown, Haverstraw, Orangetown, Ramapo and Stony Point, and a long list of incorporated villages within them, from Nyack and Piermont to Suffern, Haverstraw and West Haverstraw. A building in an incorporated village usually deals with the village building department; a building in an unincorporated hamlet such as Nanuet, Pearl River or New City deals with the town.

A Rockland exception: Spring Valley

In the Village of Spring Valley, Rockland County's own Office of Buildings and Codes administers and enforces the Uniform Code and the Energy Code, issuing permits and certificates of occupancy and scheduling inspections. A Spring Valley co-op or rental therefore talks to the county office, not a village building department.

Fire inspectors

Several villages assign Uniform Code fire prevention duties to their own fire safety inspector; the Village of West Haverstraw's code, for example, gives enforcement of the fire prevention code to its Fire Safety Inspector. Because an elevator reader must never interfere with firefighters' service, the local fire official may want to see how the system behaves in recall.

What usually needs a permit

In our experience, adding readers and low-voltage wiring to an existing elevator is treated differently from altering the elevator itself, and the elevator contractor's connection may be treated as an alteration under its license. Practices vary by municipality, and we do not guess. Before we schedule a Rockland job, we ask the elevator contractor how it handles the controller connection with the local code official, and we check with the building department if anything about the job could count as an alteration to fire-rated construction, such as a new penetration through a rated wall.

Privacy: NYC's Local Law 63 does not apply here

You may read that apartment buildings must follow special rules before using fobs or biometrics. Those rules, in New York City's Local Law 63 of 2021, apply only to buildings in the five boroughs. They do not apply in Rockland County. We still follow sensible privacy practice in every Rockland building: collect only the data the system needs, tell residents in writing what is recorded and who can see it, delete departed residents' credentials promptly, and get clear consent before enrolling anyone's face or fingerprint. Boards that want face readers should still put a written policy in place, because residents will ask.

Fire, Power and Weather

Fire Recall, Ice-Storm Outages and River Flooding in Rockland Elevator Buildings

An elevator reader is low-voltage equipment, but it sits inside a life-safety system. Three situations need a decision before the first reader is mounted.

Fire: the elevator stops listening to us

When the building's fire alarm triggers recall, or firefighters take the car with their key, the elevator controller goes into firefighters' service and ignores ordinary calls. Our relays become irrelevant. That is the correct behavior, and the elevator contractor confirms it during commissioning. We never wire anything that could hold a car at a floor or prevent recall. If a Rockland building's fire alarm is old or its recall wiring is uncertain, the elevator contractor will want to look at it, and that is a conversation worth having anyway.

Power failure: decide the answer in advance

A long outage is not unusual in Rockland. When the power goes, the elevator stops unless the building has emergency power for it. Our panel runs on a battery for a period that depends on the battery size and the number of readers. The board decides in advance what happens if the battery runs down: the readers can fail secure, so the car stays restricted, or fail open, so any rider can use it when power returns. Senior buildings usually choose fail open for the car and keep the lobby door secure, so residents are never stuck waiting for the system to recover. We set the choice in writing, test it, and label it in the panel.

Water: keep the brains above the river

Waterfront buildings in Haverstraw and Nyack, and low-lying sites anywhere along the river or its creeks, should never have the access panel in a garage or basement if there is a dry, secure room higher up. In most single-car buildings the machine room is at the top or beside the hoistway, which is fine. Where the machine room is at the bottom, we look for an alternative location for our panel, such as an electrical closet on an upper floor, and run the cable accordingly. A panel ruined by a storm surge leaves the car either open or locked, and neither is what a board wants on the morning after a storm.

Accessibility: the override that should exist

A resident who needs the elevator should never be trapped behind a dead reader. Every Rockland system we install includes a defined way for the superintendent or manager to put the car in free mode, either in software or with a keyed switch in the machine room, and the instructions go into the binder we leave on site.

Credentials

Fobs, Cards, Codes, Phones and Faces: Choosing an Elevator Credential for Rockland Riders

The credential is what residents feel every day, so it deserves more thought than it usually gets. Here is how each option tends to work out in Rockland buildings.

Key fobs

A small plastic tag on a key ring. Easy, cheap to replace, and familiar to most residents. Fobs are the default in Rockland apartment and co-op buildings, and they work well for older riders as long as the reader is within easy reach and gives a clear light and tone. We recommend secure, encrypted fobs for new systems, because the oldest 125 kHz proximity fobs can be copied cheaply.

Cards

The same technology in a wallet-sized card. Offices and medical buildings often prefer cards because staff already carry an ID badge, and the elevator can read the same badge that opens the office door.

PIN codes

A keypad in the car. Codes are free to issue and impossible to lose, which is why some senior buildings like them, but shared codes spread, and many older riders struggle with small keypads in a moving car. We use individual codes, never one code for the whole building, and pair the keypad with a fob reader so residents can choose.

Phone credentials

A phone app that presents a credential by Bluetooth or NFC. Rental buildings with app-based door systems like phone access because move-ins and move-outs are handled remotely. Phones are a poor sole credential in senior buildings and for anyone who does not keep their phone charged, so we always keep a fob option.

Face, fingerprint and iris readers

Biometric readers are Level 2 and cost more. They suit buildings where credentials are frequently shared or lost, and some offices and medical buildings. In apartment buildings, residents need to be told clearly what is stored and must agree before enrolling. Face readers also need decent lighting in the car.

Mixing credentials in one building

Nothing forces a building to pick just one. The same reader can usually accept a fob from one resident, a phone from another and an individual code from a third, all managed in the same list. That flexibility is especially useful in Rockland buildings that house several generations: grandparents who want a fob, adult children who want their phone, and a weekly aide who gets a time-limited code. What matters is that every credential belongs to one named person and can be cancelled on its own.

Our usual Rockland recommendation

For most Rockland apartment, co-op and senior buildings: encrypted fobs, with individual PIN codes as a backup for residents who prefer them. For offices and medical buildings: cards, often the same card that opens the suite. For new transit-area rentals: phone credentials with a fob option. Face readers where the board has a specific reason and a written privacy policy.

Visitors and Deliveries

Guests, Aides, Deliveries and Garage Arrivals in Rockland Elevator Buildings

Once the elevator is restricted, everyone who is not a resident needs a way up. Planning for them is what separates a system residents like from one they prop open.

Guests

The cleanest answer is an intercom that can release the visitor's floor. When the resident answers the lobby intercom and opens the door, the system also enables the elevator to that resident's floor for a minute or two. That requires an intercom that knows which apartment answered, which most current video intercoms do and most older audio systems do not. Where the intercom cannot do it, the resident can come down to meet the guest, or the system can issue a temporary code or phone pass for a set day and time.

Home health aides and visiting nurses

In Rockland's senior and 55-plus buildings, aides and nurses are part of daily life. Each aide can receive a credential limited to one floor and set hours, which expires automatically when the care arrangement ends. The family or the resident's care agency asks the manager, the manager issues it, and nobody has to share a resident's own fob.

Deliveries

Most buildings keep deliveries in the lobby or a package room and do not let couriers ride up at all. If a building wants deliveries brought to the door, the intercom can release the floor for that single delivery, or a time-limited courier code can work only during set hours.

Contractors, movers and the superintendent

Move-in days, plumbers and painters can receive temporary credentials limited to the floors they need for the days they need them. The superintendent and managing agent have credentials that reach every floor, including mechanical levels. Every credential, temporary or permanent, shows up in the log.

Garage arrivals

This is where Rockland buildings differ most from city buildings. In a waterfront building with indoor parking, or a transit-area rental with a garage, residents arriving by car never pass through the lobby. A reader at the garage hall-call station lets them call the car with the same fob they use upstairs, while stopping anyone who walked into the garage behind a car from summoning the elevator. In buildings where the garage is shared with the public, such as the new 30 West development in Haverstraw with its garage for tenants and the public, this reader is not optional. It is the main control.

Emergency responders

Police, ambulance and fire crews must be able to reach residents quickly. Firefighters use the elevator's own fire service key, which bypasses our system completely. For ambulance calls, many Rockland buildings place a lockbox or a responder credential at the entrance under the local fire department's guidance. We set up whatever arrangement the building and its responders agree on, and we never configure the elevator in a way that would slow a medical call.

Upgrades and Repair

Upgrading, Replacing and Repairing Older Elevator Systems in Rockland

Many Rockland elevator buildings already have something: a brass key switch in the car, a keypad installed when the building opened, or an early fob system whose installer has retired or stopped answering. Those systems are where a lot of our Rockland work starts.

Key switches

A keyed switch in the car that enables a floor or the whole car is the oldest form of elevator access control. It works until the keys spread, and once they do, the only fix is to change the cylinder and reissue keys. Replacing a key switch with a reader keeps the same idea, but each credential can be cancelled individually. In many cases the reader can use the same wiring path to the controller that the key switch used, which the elevator contractor confirms.

Shared keypad codes

A keypad with one building code is better than nothing, but in a few months the code is known to delivery drivers, former tenants and their friends. Upgrading to individual credentials means replacing the keypad, and often its controller, with a reader that supports fobs and personal codes. We collect the resident list, issue the new credentials, and set a switch-over date residents know about in advance.

Early fob systems

Older fob systems often run on proprietary software on one aging computer in the management office. If that computer dies, nobody can add or remove a fob. We can usually replace the controller with a current platform and, where the new readers support the old fob format, keep residents' existing fobs during a transition. Installers on access control forums point out that the old 125 kHz, 26-bit format is the one to confirm before promising that fobs can be reused, and we check it on the survey.

Repairs

When an elevator reader goes dark but the car itself runs normally, the problem is usually ours to solve: a failed power supply or battery, a damaged reader, a loose connection at the terminal strip or a credential problem. We test our output at the relay. If our equipment is switching correctly and the floor still will not register, the fault is on the elevator side and we bring in the elevator contractor with a clear note of what we found. We take over and repair systems installed by other companies in Rockland, subject to what we find on the first visit.

Maintenance

An elevator access system needs little maintenance, but the battery should be tested and replaced on schedule, the software kept current, and the credential list cleaned of departed residents. We can do this on a visit booked in advance or teach the manager to do most of it.

Glossary

Elevator Access Control Terms for Rockland Boards, Owners and Managers

  • Car operating panel: the button panel inside the elevator car, where an in-car reader usually mounts.
  • Hall-call station: the up and down buttons at a landing such as the lobby or garage; a reader here controls who can summon the car to that landing.
  • Floor-enable relay: a switch, closed by the access panel, that allows one floor button to register for a few seconds.
  • Interface board: a board that does the work of many relays at once, used in larger floor counts.
  • Traveling cable: the flexible cable that hangs from the car and carries power and signals to and from the machine room.
  • Machine room: the room holding the elevator's controller and, often, our access panel.
  • Firefighters' service: the elevator mode that recalls the car in a fire alarm and gives firefighters control; it overrides access control.
  • Fail open and fail secure: what the readers do when they lose power, either allowing anyone to ride or keeping the car restricted.
  • Credential: anything that identifies a rider: fob, card, code, phone or biometric.
  • Audit trail: the log of every credential used, where and when.
  • Uniform Code: New York State's building and fire code, enforced locally by Rockland's towns and villages, and by the county in Spring Valley.
