The Neighborhood

Why Hudson Valley Properties Need eufy Camera Installation

The Hudson Valley was settled long before anyone thought about wireless signals, and its houses still show it. Hudson River Valley Heritage describes the Dutch as the first colonists after Henry Hudson's voyage of 1609, and says that by 1750 most of the region had been settled in a network of small local communities that still survives. Wikipedia describes the valley running from the Capital District around Albany and Troy south to Yonkers in Westchester. Inside that stretch you find the oldest surviving group of colonial stone houses in the Hudson Valley in Hurley, Kingston's Stockade District, which the National Park Service history calls the largest identified area of early Dutch settlement in New York State, and the corner of Crown and John Streets, where Hudson Valley Magazine notes early Dutch stone houses stand on all four corners. Those walls were built to keep weather out. They also keep radio signals out, which is the first thing anyone installing eufy cameras here has to plan around.

Most of the region's houses are not quite that old, but they are old enough. Pattern for Progress, the regional planning group, reports that across the Hudson Valley a majority of housing units were built before 1970, and a significant portion before 1939. In Beacon, a real estate profile counts 49.6 percent of properties as detached single-family homes and around 35.9 percent built before 1940. A Hudson Valley renovation contractor describes the common older types as Beacon Victorians, Fishkill farmhouses and Poughkeepsie colonials, often with original woodwork and stone foundations. A Times Union piece on historic homes notes that Hurley, Stone Ridge, Hyde Park and Kingston have many stone houses and that most historic homes in the valley date from the 1800s. Then there are the newer subdivisions, like The Reserve in New Windsor, which a local listing site describes as a community of 375 single-family homes. A eufy system on each of those is a different job.

What sets a eufy install apart from a wired camera system is how much rides on a single box indoors. Most eufy battery and solar cameras talk to a HomeBase rather than straight to your router, and that link is the weak point. A troubleshooting guide we read describes it as a proprietary signal weaker than ordinary Wi-Fi and suggests keeping cameras within about 30 feet of the HomeBase indoors. Another notes that thick brick walls, large metal objects and dense foliage can block it. eufy's own support pages add that the HomeBase is designed for indoor use between 32°F and 122°F, while the battery cameras are rated from -4°F to 122°F. Put those facts on a Hudson Valley property and you get the real work: a HomeBase that cannot live in an unheated barn or a cold mudroom, cameras that may sit 150 feet away on a detached garage, and fieldstone or brick between them. Choosing where that box goes, and whether a second one or a wired access point is needed, decides whether the system stays online through the first winter.

The second thing that rides on a eufy system is the internet. Owners can review clips stored on a HomeBase, but viewing and alerts from away depend on eufy's cloud connection, and one owner on Slickdeals put the problem plainly: if that cloud link is broken for any reason, every camera shows as offline, even though they are physically up and connected on the home network. That matters here more than in the city. Hudson Valley One reported in 2023 that 1,070 properties in Ulster County still had no broadband reaching them, and that in Shandaken even cell service struggles. Mid-Hudson News reported in September 2025 that New York's ConnectALL program was committing nearly 400 million dollars to reach unserved and underserved locations, including sections of Putnam, Dutchess, Orange, Ulster and Westchester. So the first question on a north-county call is not which camera you want. It is what internet you have, and how well it holds up when the weather turns.

The weather does turn. Times Hudson Valley reported that as of noon on the Sunday after the July 4, 2026 storms, Central Hudson had 669 active outages affecting 41,323 customers across the region, and Central Hudson itself later said the hardest-hit areas were in Dutchess and Ulster. Mid-Hudson News reported that after a September 2026 nor'easter, Central Hudson crews restored power to more than 24,000 customers in the Mid-Hudson Valley. Daily Voice reported during Tropical Storm Isaias in August 2020 that NYSEG had 34,150 of its 38,693 Putnam customers without power at one point that morning. A eufy battery camera keeps its charge through an outage, but the HomeBase and the router do not, and when they go dark the cameras have nowhere to send anything. One troubleshooting site recommends a modest battery backup for the router and HomeBase for exactly that reason. We size and fit that backup on most valley installs, and we test what happens when the power comes back.

Then there is trust, which is a bigger part of eufy conversations in New York than anywhere else. In January 2025 the New York Attorney General announced a 450,000-dollar settlement with three companies that distribute eufy home security cameras. The National Law Review's account of it says the investigation found that video streams were sent without end-to-end encryption and that active feeds could be reached without authentication by anyone who had the matching web address. The settlement requires appropriate encryption for video storage and transmission and regular vulnerability testing. The Mozilla Foundation's review of the eufy S350 indoor camera notes that it does offer end-to-end encryption for cloud video but that it is not on by default. In March 2026, National Today reported that Representative Elise Stefanik and Senator Rick Scott asked the FCC and the Commerce Department for a formal investigation of Anker, eufy's parent company; Tech Times reported in October 2026 that no enforcement action had been announced and that Anker plans to fold the eufy and Soundcore names into a single Anker brand by the end of the year. None of that means you should not own eufy cameras. It means the settings matter, and setting them correctly is part of our job.

Finally, the reasons people call. Hudson Valley Post reported a January 2025 State Police alert about residential burglaries in Dutchess, Westchester and Putnam counties, with troopers advising residents to install or switch on security systems and cameras. News 12 Westchester reported in July 2026 that neighbors near Tower Hill Drive in Port Chester showed footage of someone trying car doors around 4 a.m. Hudson Valley Post reported a Poughkeepsie porch thief recorded on Fitchett Avenue and De Laval Place, and News 12 reported in June 2026 that porch theft reports in Westchester were down but still happening. Patch and local radio reported that police in Scarsdale found a hidden camera in a resident's bushes, placed, they said, by an organized burglary group watching the house. At the same time, residents across the valley have pushed back on public surveillance: Hudson Valley One covered Kingston residents raising privacy concerns about city cameras in September 2026, the Times Union reported Dutchess County removing its Flock license plate cameras, and Mid-Hudson News reported Ulster County Executive Jen Metzger's October 2026 order barring county departments from buying or deploying Flock and similar AI-assisted surveillance. People here want to watch their own property without watching the neighbors. That shapes how we aim every camera.

We also want to be clear about what our research did not find. We looked for Reddit threads, Patch posts and Yelp reviews about eufy installations specifically in the Hudson Valley and found none we could cite. We found no published count of how many valley homes use eufy, and no source describing how often HomeBase signals fail through particular wall types here. Where this page talks about stone or plaster walls cutting signal, it draws on the troubleshooting sources named above and on what we find in houses of that age generally, not on a survey of Hudson Valley homes. The only way to know your property is to test it, which is what the first part of every visit is for.

  • Stone and masonry are common: Hurley, Stone Ridge, Hyde Park and Kingston have many stone houses, the kind of walls that block the HomeBase-to-camera link.

What Hudson Valley owners often add once the cameras are up. After a eufy system has run through one season, the next request is usually about the network behind it: a cable run to the barn or garage so a second HomeBase or access point can serve the outbuildings, or a battery backup sized to carry the router and HomeBase through a short outage. If you already know the property has a building a long way from the house, say so when you book, so the network side can be planned in the same visit rather than after the first storm.

Westchester and Rockland: tight lots, neighbors close by

In the southern two counties the usual eufy job is a doorbell, two to four outdoor cameras and maybe a floodlight camera over the driveway, on a house with a neighbor 30 or 40 feet away. The internet is usually a cable or fiber connection that holds up well, so the network side is simpler than further north. What takes care is aim. A camera over a Rockland garage door can easily take in the neighbor's side yard and bedroom windows, and in a region where people have spent 2026 arguing about public cameras, that is a fast way to start a feud. We set each camera's field of view and activity zones so they cover your doors, your driveway and your walkway, and we mask anything beyond the property line in the app where the model allows it. Doorbells are the other common call. The houses here range from 1920s colonials to 1980s raised ranches, and the existing doorbell transformer may or may not meet the power a eufy wired doorbell needs, which we check before the old button comes off. Westchester requires electricians to hold a county trade license and Rockland licenses wiring at 50 volts or more, so where a hardwired floodlight needs a new box or circuit, that part goes to a licensed electrician and our quote says so.

Orange and Putnam: driveways, barns and trees

North of the Thruway's southern stretch, lots open up. A typical Orange County call might be a house set back 200 feet on a gravel drive with a detached garage and a horse barn, and a typical Putnam call a wooded lot on a slope with the house in shade most of the winter afternoon. Two eufy problems show up here that rarely matter in Westchester. The first is distance: a camera on a barn is often too far from a HomeBase in the house to hold a steady link, especially through a stone or brick foundation wall. The answer may be a second HomeBase in a heated space in the outbuilding, an outdoor-rated access point fed by a cable run, or simply a different camera position. The second is trees. A solar panel that gets full sun in July can sit in shade from November to March, and a camera aimed down a drive in winter starts sending motion alerts every time leaves and branches move in spring. We walk the property with that in mind, and in Putnam, where NYSEG and Central Hudson outages have been long and wide, we plan the battery backup first.

Dutchess and Ulster: old stone, long winters, thin internet

The two northern counties are where eufy installations need the most planning. Stone walls in Hurley, Stone Ridge, Hyde Park and Kingston block the HomeBase signal far more than the drywall of a modern house. Winters are the longest in our area, and eufy's own rating for the HomeBase, indoors and above freezing, rules out the unheated porch or summer kitchen where some owners would like to put it. The internet can be anything from fiber in Poughkeepsie to a satellite dish or cellular hotspot on a mountain road. And many of the properties are second homes, which means nobody is there to notice a camera has gone offline until the owner opens the app on a Friday night and sees nothing since Tuesday. We plan these jobs around three questions: where the HomeBase can live warm and within reach, what happens to the system when the internet drops, and how the owner will know something has failed. Dutchess has no county electrician license we could find, but towns such as Fishkill require electrical work to be inspected by an approved independent agency, and Ulster has its own county licensing law; we tell you which applies to any hardwired part.

Walk the property after dark once

Daylight hides the problems a camera has at night. Walk your doors and driveway once after dark and notice where the porch light falls, where the light from the neighbor's house reaches, and where it is fully black. A camera aimed into a bright porch light at night shows a white glare and a silhouette, and one aimed across a dark lawn depends entirely on its own infrared, which reaches only so far. Notice too what moves: a flag, a hedge, a tree limb near a light. Those are the things that will trigger false alerts all winter. Send us those notes with the photos and we will place the cameras around what you saw rather than what the box suggests.

Decide who will watch, and from where

A eufy system for a house you live in full-time is set up differently from one for a weekend place in Ulster or a rental cottage. If the owners will be two hours away most of the week, alerts matter more than recordings, and so does knowing when the system has gone offline. If guests will stay, they need to know where outdoor cameras are, and indoor cameras need much more thought. If more than one person should get alerts, each needs their own sign-in shared from the owner's account, not one password passed around. Tell us who will use the system and we will set up the accounts, the alerts and the sharing that fit.

eufy Camera Installation for the Hudson Valley's Architecture

The six counties hold almost every kind of building New York has, from Dutch stone houses to new subdivisions. For a eufy system, the building changes three things: how far the HomeBase signal travels through it, where cameras can be fixed without damage, and who has to approve anything visible from the street. These are the types the sources on this page describe, and how we handle each.

Colonial Stone Houses

Hurley, Stone Ridge, Hyde Park and Kingston have many. Walls a foot or more thick stop most wireless signals, so HomeBase placement and sometimes a second HomeBase or wired access point decide the whole plan.

Farmhouses with Outbuildings

Fishkill farmhouses and their Orange and Dutchess cousins, often with a barn, a garage and a shed spread across the lot. Distance and stone foundations are the challenge; winter sun on solar panels is the other.

Victorians and Village Houses

Beacon Victorians and the houses on village main streets: wood frame, plaster on lath, deep porches and decorative trim. Cameras go into sound wood, not brittle trim, and doorbell wiring is often decades old.

Historic Rowhouses

Newburgh's East End and Kingston's districts, where visible exterior changes can need review. Cameras are placed where the street sees as little as possible, and window or under-eave mounts are considered first.

Suburban Colonials and Ranches

The most common Westchester and Rockland house. Shorter distances and better internet make these the simplest eufy jobs, with care taken over neighbors' sight lines and doorbell power.

New Subdivisions

Communities like The Reserve in New Windsor, with 375 single-family homes. Modern wiring and siding make mounting easy; homeowner association rules on exterior fixtures are the thing to check first.

On stone walls. A Dutch stone house was built from field and quarry stone laid in lime mortar, and its walls can be far thicker than the length of any wireless troubleshooting guide's assumptions. The troubleshooting sources we read say thick brick walls can block the link between a eufy camera and its HomeBase; old stone does at least as much. Inside, the HomeBase will happily reach cameras in the same room and the next, but a camera on the outside of a stone wall may lose it entirely. We do not guess at this. We place the HomeBase where it will live, then carry a camera to each proposed outdoor spot and read the signal in the app before any mount goes up. Where the stone wins, we have three choices: move the HomeBase closer to a window or door opening where signal leaks through, add a second HomeBase on the other side of the wall, or run a cable so an access point can sit outside the stone. Fixing to stone needs care too. We prefer wooden eaves and frames where they exist, and where a camera has to go on the stone itself we drill into sound stone, not the soft lime mortar joints, and seal the hole against water.

On historic districts. Kingston's Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews exterior work on historic properties visible from the public right-of-way before a building permit is issued, and its guidance names electrical and mechanical equipment that will be visible from the street. Newburgh's preservation guide says owners making exterior changes in the East End must get a Certificate of Appropriateness from the Architectural Review Commission. Neither source we found names security cameras specifically, so we do not promise that a camera needs review or that it does not. What we do is plan for the least visible option first: under eaves, on rear walls, inside porches, or a window-mounted camera that sits inside the glass, which eufy says works best on a first- or second-floor window. When eufy fits a business, and when it does not

A eufy camera on a HomeBase records clips when it detects motion rather than recording every second on most battery models, which is fine for seeing who came to the back door of a café at 3 a.m. and less fine for reviewing exactly what happened at a register over an hour. Battery cameras also need recharging, and a business owner who already works long days may not want to take down cameras from a high wall every few months in winter. On the other hand, eufy's lack of a monthly fee for local storage on a HomeBase, its simple app and the ease of adding a camera make it attractive for a single-location shop or office. We ask what the cameras need to prove, who will look at the footage and how often, and whether there is reliable power and internet at each spot, and we recommend eufy only where the answers point that way. Where they do not, we will say so plainly before you spend anything.

Rules and practical limits for business cameras

  • Tell employees. Under New York Civil Rights Law §52-c, as Outten & Golden explains it, employers must give notice of electronic monitoring and post it in a conspicuous place visible to employees. We remind every business owner of this before cameras go live.
  • No cameras in restrooms or locker rooms. Labor Law §203-c prohibits cameras in restrooms, locker rooms and similar spaces regardless of any posted monitoring policy. We will not install one there.
  • Licensed installers. New York requires a state license to install camera systems used to detect or monitor intrusion, theft or movement, and the state alarm installers' association warns unlicensed installers can face fines of up to ten thousand dollars. Our work is done under NYS license #12000287431.
  • Hardwired lighting is electrical work. A floodlight camera that needs a new junction box or circuit is line-voltage work. Westchester, Rockland, Orange, Putnam and Ulster license electricians at county level, and towns like Fishkill require third-party inspection. That part goes to a licensed electrician.
  • Audio is usually off. A camera that records conversations among people who have not agreed to it raises eavesdropping questions under New York law. On business installs we leave microphones off unless you have a clear, lawful reason and notice is posted.
  • Village lighting codes. New Paltz, Cold Spring, the Town of Chester and Irvington all have outdoor lighting standards that limit glare and light crossing property lines. A floodlight camera on a downtown building is aimed and shielded with those in mind.

Field Notes: Carrying a Camera Around the House Before Drilling Anything

We do not publish stories about a particular customer's property unless we can document them, so this is not about one address. It is the routine our technicians follow on the kind of property the Hudson Valley is full of: an old house with thick walls, a detached garage or barn, a router in a back room and a pile of eufy boxes on the kitchen table.

We start indoors and decide where the HomeBase will live: a room that stays heated all winter, close enough to the router for a cable, away from metal shelving and the microwave. Then we switch on one camera and walk it around the outside to every spot the owner wants covered, holding it where it will be mounted and reading the signal in the app. At the front door it is usually fine. Round the back of a stone wall it often falls away. At the barn it may vanish. We note each reading and only then talk about mounts. If a spot fails, we try the HomeBase in a second room, then look at where a cable could take an access point outside the wall. We check the sun on each solar spot against the low winter sky, not the summer one. And before we leave, we switch off the router and HomeBase at the outlet, wait, switch them back on, and watch every camera come back. A system that recovers on its own is the one the owner never has to call about.

Your Questions Answered

eufy Camera Installation in the Hudson Valley: Questions Answered

Signal, walls and the HomeBase

Our farmhouse has thick fieldstone walls. Will the HomeBase inside reach a camera mounted out on the barn?

Possibly not, and we find out before mounting anything. The troubleshooting sources we rely on describe the HomeBase-to-camera link as weaker than ordinary Wi-Fi and say thick masonry, metal and dense foliage all block it, and a barn across a yard adds distance on top of the stone. We carry a camera to the barn and read the signal in the app with the HomeBase where it will live. If it is weak, the usual answers are a second HomeBase inside a heated part of the barn, or a buried or overhead network cable to the barn so an access point or HomeBase there connects back to the house. Sometimes moving the house HomeBase to a room with a window facing the barn is enough. The test tells us which, and the quote reflects it before you commit.

The app says the eufy cameras are offline, yet the house Wi-Fi works perfectly. What is going on?

Usually one of three things. First, the HomeBase may have lost its own connection to the router, often after the internet provider swapped the router or pushed an update that changed settings; the house Wi-Fi works for phones, but the HomeBase is still looking for the old network. Second, the cameras may have lost the HomeBase link because of distance, cold or a new obstacle such as a metal shed or a refrigerator moved against the wall. Third, as one owner described on Slickdeals, if the cloud connection between your system and eufy's servers is broken, every camera can show offline in the app even though it is still running at home. We check the chain in that order: router, HomeBase, then each camera. Most of the time the fix is a settings change or a HomeBase that needs to sit somewhere better.

Why does one camera drop off for a few minutes and then come back on its own, over and over?

An intermittent camera is almost always sitting on the edge of its range. On a good day the signal is just strong enough; when someone runs the microwave, a car parks in the driveway between the camera and the house, wet leaves hang on the trees after rain or the temperature drops, it slips below the line and reconnects a few minutes later. Battery level plays in too, since a weak battery can struggle to hold a marginal connection. We check the signal reading for that camera over a few minutes, look at what sits in the path, and either move the camera a few feet, move the HomeBase, or add a second HomeBase or an access point. Repeated reconnecting also drains the battery faster, so fixing the range usually fixes the battery complaint at the same time.

Winter, batteries and solar

How much does a Hudson Valley winter actually hurt a eufy battery camera?

eufy's support page says its battery cameras operate from -4°F to 122°F because of the lithium-ion battery inside, and that the HomeBase is designed for indoor use from 32°F to 122°F. It also recommends raising motion sensitivity in winter, because cold air and heavy clothing make people harder for the motion sensor to pick up. In practice, batteries hold less charge in the cold and drain faster, and a camera on a north-facing wall in Ulster will need charging more often in January than in June. We place battery cameras where they are sheltered from the worst wind, set winter motion sensitivity as eufy suggests, and talk honestly about which spots should have a solar panel and which should have a plug-in or wired camera instead.

Will a eufy solar panel really keep a camera charged on a shaded Hudson Valley lot in December?

Sometimes yes and sometimes no, and the owners who have tried it say so. One S220 owner wrote on Slickdeals that in winter they still had to charge the camera by hand every two or three weeks, while another said that after adding an external panel their cameras stayed at full charge almost every day except in heavy rain or very cloudy weather. A Tom's Guide reviewer of the S330 measured the solar panel adding about 20 mAh on a cloudy day against about 280 mAh on a bright sunny one. That gap is the whole story here: in December the sun is low, the days are short and many valley lots are shaded by hillsides and trees. We judge each solar spot against the winter sky, not the summer one, and if a spot will not keep up we say so and suggest another position or another power source.

When a nor'easter dumps snow, does the solar panel warm up enough to clear itself?

We have found no source saying eufy's solar panels heat themselves, and a panel's job is to make electricity, not warmth, so we plan on the assumption that snow sits on it until it slides or melts. That is why we angle panels steeply enough to shed snow where the bracket allows, keep them clear of roof edges where snow and ice fall from above, and avoid mounting them so high that clearing one with a soft brush is impossible. After a storm that covers the panel, the camera simply runs on its battery until the panel clears. A camera with a well-charged battery going into the storm rides out a few days of covered panel easily; one that was already low in a shaded spot may not, which is one more reason to choose winter-sunny spots in the first place.

Internet and power

Our road in Ulster still has no broadband. Can a eufy system do anything useful there?

Some things, with clear limits. Hudson Valley One reported in 2023 that 1,070 Ulster properties had no broadband reaching them, and that in Shandaken even cell service struggles. A eufy HomeBase can store clips locally, so cameras can still record events at the property, but remote viewing and alerts need an internet connection, and without one you will only see what happened when you are there or when a connection is available. If you have satellite internet or a cellular hotspot, the system can work over it, but data limits and outages matter. We look at what connection you have, how it behaves in storms, and whether the state's ConnectALL buildout, which Mid-Hudson News reported in 2025, is likely to reach your road, and we design for what is actually available rather than what the box assumes.

After the July Central Hudson outage our cameras never came back on their own. How do we stop that next time?

The cameras ran on their batteries, but the router and HomeBase went dark with the house, and when power returned something in the chain did not reconnect cleanly. Often the router came back before the internet provider's line did, or the HomeBase started up before the router was ready and gave up. A battery backup on the router and HomeBase, which one troubleshooting site recommends for exactly this, carries them through short outages so nothing needs to restart. For longer ones, we check that the router and HomeBase are set to recover automatically, and we test it by switching them off at the outlet and watching the system come back. Times Hudson Valley's count of 41,323 customers affected at one point after the July 4, 2026 storms shows how often this matters here.

Doorbells

Our 1950s house has the original doorbell wiring. Will a wired eufy doorbell run on it?

It may, but the transformer is often the weak link. eufy lists its wired doorbell as needing 16 to 24 volts AC at 30 VA or more, and as one owner put it on Slickdeals, a home built a few decades ago probably does not have a transformer putting out enough. The old wires themselves are usually fine at doorbell voltage if they are intact. We test the voltage at the button, look at the transformer's rating and the chime, and if the transformer is too small, replacing it is a simple job where it sits on an accessible box; where it is buried or tied into line-voltage wiring that needs an electrician, we say so. If the wiring is unreliable or missing, a battery doorbell avoids the issue entirely. eufy also says its wired doorbell will not run on building intercom power, which matters in some village apartment buildings.

What stops someone from simply pulling the doorbell camera off the wall and walking away with it?

Mostly the fact that the video of them doing it is already saved by the time they get it off. A doorbell camera records the approach and the face before anyone touches it, and with the system set up properly that clip is stored on the HomeBase indoors or sent to the cloud, not kept only on the doorbell. We also make the doorbell harder to take: mounting plates fixed into solid framing rather than siding alone, the security screws eufy supplies tightened properly, and the doorbell registered to your account so it cannot simply be reset and used elsewhere. On a long rural driveway where a doorbell might be the only camera, we suggest a second camera that can see the door, so a stolen doorbell is still on video from another angle.

Privacy and the law

After the New York Attorney General's 2025 action against eufy, is it sensible to put these cameras on our house?

That is a decision for you, and we will give you the facts rather than a sales pitch. The Attorney General's office announced in January 2025 that three distributors of eufy cameras paid 450,000 dollars after an investigation found video sent without end-to-end encryption and, in some cases, active feeds reachable without authentication. The settlement requires appropriate encryption and regular vulnerability testing. Mozilla's review says the S350 indoor camera offers end-to-end encryption for cloud video but leaves it off by default. In 2026, two members of Congress asked federal agencies to investigate eufy's parent company Anker, and Tech Times reported no enforcement action had followed. If you go ahead, we switch on every encryption and sign-in protection the models offer, keep indoor cameras out of private rooms, and explain what is stored where. If you would rather not, we install other brands too.

If police ask for our eufy footage after a break-in on our road, are we required to hand it over?

Not voluntarily, in most cases. A Consumer Reports explainer carried by WLOS answered this question with yes and no: you can choose to share, and police can obtain footage through legal process such as a warrant or subpoena. On another brand, Hudson Valley Country reported that Ring ended its feature letting police request footage through the app, so police now need a search warrant there. Your eufy clips belong to you; you decide whether to download and share a clip with police when a neighbor's car is broken into, and many people do. We show you how to find and export a clip quickly at handover, so if you want to help an investigation you can, without giving anyone wider access to your account.

Can our outdoor eufy cameras record sound, or does New York law say to switch the microphones off?

The cautious answer is to switch audio off outdoors and in shared spaces. A Justia Ask A Lawyer answer for a New York homeowner advised disabling audio on outdoor and garage cameras unless a consenting participant, including you, is part of any recorded conversation, because recording conversations you are not part of can raise eavesdropping problems. Video of your own property is a different matter. Separately, New York Penal Law §250.45 makes it illegal to use an imaging device to record a person in a bedroom, bathroom, changing room or fitting room without consent. We set audio off by default on outdoor eufy cameras and on any indoor camera that might pick up guests or workers, and we leave the decision to switch it on with you after explaining why we did.

If the front is the only option, we suggest checking with HLPC staff before we drill, and we give you photos and specifications of the proposed camera to send them.

+

Is eufy a sensible choice for a house up here, or should we look at something else?
+

It suits many valley homes: no monthly fee for local storage, easy battery cameras and a simple app. It suits them less where the internet is unreliable, where stone walls or long distances make wireless hard, or where you want continuous recording on many cameras. The 2025 Attorney General settlement over encryption is also worth weighing. We install other brands, so we will tell you honestly which fits your property.

How does eufy stack up against Blink for a weekend house?
+

Both make battery cameras that are easy to mount. The main practical difference people weigh is storage: eufy's pitch is local storage on its HomeBase without a monthly fee, while Blink owners commonly rely on a cloud plan or a local storage module. For a weekend house the bigger questions are the same for either brand: how reliable your internet is, whether the hub stays above freezing, and how you will know a camera has gone offline while you are away.

Are eufy doorbells wired or battery powered?
+

eufy sells both. The wired doorbell runs on existing doorbell wiring and needs 16 to 24 volts AC at 30 VA or more, and it will not run on building intercom power. Battery doorbells such as the E340 need no doorbell wiring and are recharged by hand. In older houses with weak transformers or unreliable wiring, a battery doorbell often saves trouble; where the wiring is sound, wired means no charging.

Can a nearly flat battery make a eufy camera show as offline?
+

Yes. As the battery runs low, a camera may stop holding its connection to the HomeBase and show offline in the app before it dies completely. Cold makes this worse, since lithium batteries lose capacity in freezing weather, and a camera on the edge of its range uses more power reconnecting. Charge it fully, then if it keeps happening we look at range, winter placement and whether a solar panel or plug-in power suits that spot.

Does a eufy floodlight camera over the garage need an electrician?
+

It depends on the model. Hardwired eufy floodlights need a weatherproof junction box with 120-volt power, and where there is no existing fixture, the box and wiring must be installed by a qualified electrician, who in Westchester, Rockland, Orange, Putnam and Ulster holds a county license. The plug-in Floodlight E340 has a three-prong plug and a 20-foot cord, so it only needs a suitable outdoor outlet.

We rent out a cottage on our land. Where can indoor eufy cameras legally go?
+

Never in bedrooms, bathrooms or changing areas. New York Penal Law section 250.45 makes it illegal to use an imaging device to record a person in a bedroom, bathroom, changing room or fitting room without consent. Outdoor cameras on entrances and driveways are common, and guests should be told where they are. We keep audio off and suggest outdoor-only coverage for rental units.
