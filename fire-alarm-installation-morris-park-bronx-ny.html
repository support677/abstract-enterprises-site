The Neighborhood

Why Morris Park's Hospital Row, Office Towers and Avenue Storefronts Need Fire Alarms Planned Around Many Tenants

Morris Park is in the East Bronx, in Community District 11. Wikipedia draws it between Pelham Parkway and Neill Avenue on the north, Eastchester Road on the east, the Amtrak Northeast Corridor tracks and Sackett Avenue on the south, and Bronxdale Avenue and White Plains Road on the west, with about 10,289 residents on roughly a third of a square mile. CityNeighborhoods.nyc describes the residential core as two-story brick homes, detached houses and low-rise apartment buildings put up mostly between the 1920s and 1940s. The street grid has an unusual origin: John A. Morris opened the Morris Park Racecourse here in 1889, it hosted the Belmont Stakes into the 1900s, and after a fire in 1910 the property was cut into building lots (Wikipedia; Forgotten New York).

The houses on those lots are not what this page is about. Smoke alarms inside one- and two-family homes follow separate rules and are not part of this service. The fire alarm work in Morris Park sits in three other places, and each one is a different kind of building.

The first is the eastern edge, which Forgotten New York calls Hospital Row. Jacobi Medical Center at 1400 Pelham Parkway South opened in 1955, has 457 beds, runs the Bronx's only burn unit and a Level I trauma center, and houses FDNY EMS Station 20 on its grounds (Wikipedia). Across Eastchester Road are the Albert Einstein College of Medicine and Montefiore's Weiler hospital, including the 220-million-dollar Price Center research building of 2008 (Albert Einstein College of Medicine). Calvary Hospital at 1740 Eastchester Road provides hospice and palliative care with 225 beds on its Bronx campus (Wikipedia). The Bronx Psychiatric Center finished a 350-million-dollar rebuild in December 2015 with 436,310 square feet of new inpatient, residential and central services buildings (DASNY).

Next to them is the Hutchinson Metro Center. Its core began as Richard Meier's Bronx Developmental Center of 1977, a state facility for 380 children with disabilities; the state sold it at auction in 2001, and the converted building opened as office space in 2003 (Lehman College). The campus now counts more than 80 businesses, health care providers, schools and government agencies, a nine-floor, 285,000-square-foot Tower One and a Residence Inn by Marriott (Bronx Times). Montefiore's 11-story, 280,000-square-foot Tower Two holds an ambulatory surgery center with 12 operating rooms and four procedure rooms. Montefiore's then-CEO Dr. Steven M. Safyer described it as "a hospital without beds" (Montefiore, May 2013). The Department of City Planning counted more than 16,000 people coming into the half mile around the future Morris Park station to work each day, with about 4,500 at the Metro Center alone.

The third place is Morris Park Avenue itself. The Morris Park Business Improvement District covers just over a mile of the avenue and 188 businesses (Bronx Times via Welcome2TheBronx, 2017), and its director called them "shockingly healthy" during the pandemic (Bronx Times, January 2021). Most are family-owned: pasticcerias like Conti's, which dates to 1921, pizzerias, trattorias, tax offices and doctors' offices, many in two- and three-story buildings with apartments upstairs. In all three places, one building often holds many occupants who don't control the fire alarm.

Morris Park at a glance

  • Where: East Bronx, Community District 11; roughly Pelham Parkway to the rail tracks and Sackett Avenue, Bronxdale Avenue and White Plains Road to Eastchester Road (Wikipedia), with Hospital Row and the Metro Center on the eastern edge.
  • Residential core: mostly one- to three-story buildings, per the Department of City Planning's station study, with some apartment buildings, including Art Deco walk-ups on Cruger Avenue (Forgotten New York).
  • Health care and research: Jacobi (457 beds), Calvary Hospital (225 beds), Bronx Psychiatric Center (156 inpatient beds plus an 86-bed children's center), the Einstein campus and Montefiore Weiler (Wikipedia; DASNY).
  • Office campus: Hutchinson Metro Center, with Tower One at 285,000 square feet and Montefiore's 11-story Tower Two (Bronx Times; Montefiore).
  • Fire service: Squad 61 and Battalion 20 at 1518 Williamsbridge Road, Engine 90 and Ladder 41 at 1843 White Plains Road; the 49th Precinct is at 2121 Eastchester Road (Wikipedia).
  • Coming change: a Metro-North station near Morris Park and Bassett Avenues and a 46-block East Bronx rezoning approved August 15, 2024 (6sqft; CityLand).

Building Types

Commercial Fire Alarm Systems for Morris Park's Building Types, From Surgery Floors to Bakery Kitchens

Commercial fire alarm systems are required by a building's use and size under the NYC Building Code. These are the building types that bring Morris Park owners, tenants and facility managers to a fire alarm design.

Hospitals and inpatient units

Hospitals are institutional occupancies, and a summary of the NYC Building Code by NY Engineers lists Group I buildings among those that need a fire alarm in every case. On Hospital Row, the work we see is rarely a whole new hospital. It is a floor renovation, a new imaging room, a lab moved from one wing to another, a nurse station relocated. Each of those changes devices on a large existing system that the hospital's engineering department runs, and each has to be filed, installed in phases while patients stay in the building, and tested without disturbing the floors around it.

Hospice and long-stay care

Calvary Hospital's 225 beds on Eastchester Road are for patients receiving end-of-life care (Wikipedia). In a building like that, the fire alarm has two audiences: staff who must act, and patients and families who should be disturbed as little as safely possible. Health care codes allow notification in patient areas to be built around trained staff, with coded announcements, chimes and visible signals, rather than full horns in every room. Which approach is allowed in which room is a design decision made by the engineer, approved by the FDNY, and then rehearsed by staff. Nursing home fire alarm installation and assisted living fire alarm installation follow the same logic in smaller buildings.

Psychiatric inpatient buildings

The Bronx Psychiatric Center's newer buildings include 156 inpatient beds and the 86-bed NYC Children's Center Bronx Campus, with secure indoor and outdoor recreation areas and electronic monitoring (DASNY). Secure units mean locked doors, and locked doors mean the fire alarm has to work with the locking hardware: which doors release on alarm, which stay under staff control, and how staff get people out. Devices in patient areas are chosen and mounted so they can't be used to cause harm.

Medical office towers and ambulatory surgery

An office building is a business occupancy. Tenants such as doctors, physical therapists and administrators fit out suites on the landlord's system. A surgery suite is different: when patients are under anesthesia and can't leave on their own, the space is treated as ambulatory health care, which the NY Engineers summary of the code lists as its own fire alarm trigger. Montefiore's Tower Two at the Metro Center, with 12 operating rooms in an 11-story building, is the clearest local example. In a building that tall, the tower's system is also a high-rise system, with voice messages floor by floor and a fire command station for the FDNY.

Research labs

The Price Center on the Einstein campus was built for genetics, stem cell, imaging and computational research (Albert Einstein College of Medicine). Labs bring fume hoods, high air-change rates, freezers, compressed gases and equipment that can't lose power without warning. Fast-moving air dilutes smoke before a ceiling detector sees it, so detector type and location are chosen around the airflow, and the fire alarm's control of fans and dampers is coordinated with the lab's exhaust so a test doesn't pull a hood off line by surprise.

Avenue storefronts with apartments upstairs

Most buildings on Morris Park Avenue are two or three stories, with a store or restaurant on the ground floor and offices or apartments above. Many don't need a building-wide fire alarm system at all; the NY Engineers summary sets thresholds for mercantile spaces and for apartment buildings by size and height that small buildings often fall under. What they do need is a clear answer, from the design professional, written on paper, before a new restaurant installs its hood or a landlord adds a floor. A pizzeria or bakery that grows into the second floor, or a building that becomes a medical office, can cross a threshold without anyone noticing.

New mid-rise buildings near the station

The 2024 rezoning lets apartment and mixed-use buildings rise near the Morris Park station on land that was zoned for commercial and manufacturing use (6sqft). Filings labeled Morris Park by Marketproof include a seven-story, 70-foot building with 89 apartments at 800 Brady Avenue (filed January 5, 2026) and a nine-story, 105-foot building with 106 units at 626 Pelham Parkway South (filed March 27, 2026). The first sits under New York City's 75-foot high-rise line; the second sits well above it.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Find the Building Engineer Before You Find the Ceiling

In a neighborhood like Morris Park, the first phone call on a tenant job usually isn't to the tenant. It's to the building engineer or property manager who controls the base system. A suite on the fourth floor of an office tower may need six new devices, but those devices have to be programmed into a panel the tenant doesn't own, under drawings the tenant has never seen, on a system whose central station account is in the landlord's name. If that conversation happens after the ceiling grid goes up, the suite opens late. If it happens first, the landlord's vendor, the tenant's design professional and the installer agree on device addresses, cable routes and a test date before anyone climbs a ladder.

The second habit is to ask what can't be interrupted. In an office, a fire alarm test is a nuisance. In a dialysis unit, a lab with live experiments or a floor where patients are under anesthesia, the fans, door releases and elevator recall the fire alarm controls have to be tested in a sequence the clinical staff have agreed to. We write that sequence down with them.

The third is on the avenue: look at what the kitchen cooks before choosing the detector. A ceiling detector placed for a former clothing store won't last a week above a new pizza oven. These are working habits, written in general terms; we don't describe specific clients' buildings on this page.

Your Questions Answered

Morris Park Fire Alarm Questions From Practice Managers, Lab Directors, Restaurant Owners and Developers

Hospital Row and the Metro Center

Montefiore called its 11-story surgery tower at the Metro Center "a hospital without beds." Is a building like that designed under hospital fire alarm rules or office rules?

Usually a mix, decided space by space. The building itself is an office building, a business occupancy, and as a tower over 75 feet it also carries high-rise fire alarm features. The surgery floors inside it are different: where patients are put under anesthesia and can't leave on their own, the space is treated as ambulatory health care, which the NYC Building Code, as summarized by NY Engineers, lists as its own fire alarm trigger. It is not a hospital, because no one stays overnight, but the surgical suite's design borrows more from health care than from a typical office floor. The design professional sets the classification of each area on the drawings.

Which fire alarm devices can go inside the MRI magnet room of an imaging center on the Metro Center campus?

Very few, and only ones rated for that environment. The magnet's field can pull ferrous parts and can stop ordinary devices from working, so detectors, strobes and their mounting hardware inside the room have to be chosen with the imaging vendor's site plan in hand, or kept outside the room where the code allows the space to be covered from outside. Cable entering the room passes through the room's shielding at locations the vendor approves. Bring the vendor's drawings to the first design meeting, not to the installation.

The psychiatric center's units keep doors locked for patient safety. How does a fire alarm unlock exits on a locked inpatient unit?

Through a planned interface, not an automatic release of every door. The design states which doors unlock on alarm, which remain under staff control with keys or remote release, and how staff move patients to safety. The fire alarm sends a signal through a control module to the locking hardware for the doors that release, and the acceptance test proves each one. These arrangements are reviewed by the FDNY and match the facility's written fire safety plan.

In a hospice where patients are near the end of life, are full horns used in patient rooms, or something quieter?

Health care buildings can be designed so that notification in patient areas is aimed at trained staff, using coded announcements, chimes and visible signals, while staff areas and public spaces get full notification. That is a design choice the engineer makes within the code and the FDNY approves; it depends on staff being present and trained. In a building like Calvary Hospital, with 225 beds of end-of-life care, the plan is about getting staff moving without frightening families who are sitting at a bedside.

A clinical research site on Waters Place may schedule overnight study visits. Does having participants sleep there change the fire alarm?

It can. An office suite where people come and go during the day is designed for people who are awake. Rooms where participants sleep, even a few nights a month, bring sleeping-area questions: smoke detection in those rooms, notification loud enough to wake someone, and staff on duty who know what to do. Tell the design professional about overnight use at the start, because it can change both the classification of the space and the device layout.

Can the devices in one lab on the Einstein campus be shut off while that lab is renovated, without disabling the whole floor?

Usually, yes, on an addressable system. The devices in the work area can be disabled or covered individually while the rest of the floor stays live, with the FDNY notified and a fire watch where the code requires one. The bypass is logged, and every device is put back in service and tested before the room reopens. On an older zoned system, isolating one room is harder, because a zone often covers several rooms; that is one practical reason labs push for addressable systems.

Morris Park Avenue storefronts

Our two-story building on Morris Park Avenue may add a floor of apartments. Would the addition bring a fire alarm requirement the building doesn't have now?

Possibly. The NYC Building Code's triggers for apartment buildings, as summarized by NY Engineers, look at how many stories the dwelling units sit above the lowest exit and how many units there are, and a building with a store and two floors of apartments can reach a threshold that a store and one floor did not. An addition is new work, so it is designed to current code, and the design professional decides whether the new floor needs only its own smoke alarms or a building system that also covers the store. Ask before the architect finishes the addition drawings.

The Morris Park Business Improvement District covers about a mile of storefronts. Can several owners on the avenue share one inspection and testing day?

Yes, in the sense of one contractor scheduling neighboring buildings on the same day, which saves travel and time. Each building's system is still separate, with its own report, its own records and its own central station account.

Our family's pasticceria building on Morris Park Avenue has never had a fire alarm system. Does selling the business force the new owner to install one?

A sale by itself doesn't change what the code requires. What changes requirements is a change of use, an alteration or an addition. If the buyer keeps the bakery as it is, the building's situation stays the same. If the buyer turns the upstairs into a clinic, combines two storefronts or adds seating, the design professional looks at the building again. Buyers often ask for a written fire alarm analysis during due diligence, so it is worth having one ready.

Near the new station

Density near the Morris Park station was cut back during the rezoning talks. If a planned building comes in shorter than first drawn, what changes in its fire alarm?

Height is the big one. A building drawn at nine or ten stories and redrawn at six or seven may drop below New York City's high-rise line, an occupied floor more than 75 feet above fire department access. Below the line, the system usually drops voice evacuation and the fire command station and becomes simpler. Fewer units and less commercial space can also change other thresholds. Whenever the massing changes, the fire alarm design is checked again before filing.

A one-story storage building on Eastchester Road is being sold for redevelopment. Should its fire alarm stay monitored right up to demolition?

Yes, as long as the building is standing with stock, people or equipment in it. A required system that is taken out of service has to be reported to the FDNY, and an empty building with a dead fire alarm is exactly where fires go unnoticed. Keep the central station account active until the building is cleared and the demolition contractor takes over, then close the account and notify the FDNY as required.

Our tower floor at the Metro Center has open-plan desks under sprinklers. Does the whole floor need smoke detectors?

Often not. In a sprinklered office building, the sprinklers do the detecting in most open areas through their waterflow switches, and the fire alarm adds manual stations, notification and smoke detection where the code calls for it, such as elevator lobbies, the area around the panel, duct detectors and some special rooms. That changes if the tenant adds a server room, a lab, a sleeping room or a health care space. The design professional's layout shows which rooms need detectors; more is not automatically better.

Equipment

Types of Commercial Fire Alarm Systems We Install in Morris Park

What is a commercial fire alarm system? It is the set of detectors, manual stations, horns, strobes and speakers wired to a fire alarm control panel that watches a whole building or tenant space, warns the people in it, operates other building systems and reports to a central station that calls the FDNY. How does a commercial fire alarm system work? A device senses smoke, heat, flowing sprinkler water or a pulled handle; within seconds the panel sounds notification, recalls elevators, closes smoke doors, shuts down fans and transmits the alarm. Commercial fire detection systems come in a few types, and these are the ones that show up in Morris Park.

Addressable

What is an addressable fire alarm system? Each device has its own address, so the panel can say "smoke detector, Suite 410, procedure room 2" instead of "fourth floor." Addressable fire alarm installation, addressable fire alarm system installation and addressable fire alarm panel installation are the standard for towers, hospitals and new buildings.

Conventional

What is a conventional fire alarm system? Devices are wired in zones, and the panel knows only which zone is in alarm. Conventional fire alarm installation, conventional fire alarm system installation and conventional fire alarm panel installation still suit a small store or a three-story walk-up with few devices.

Choosing between them

What is the difference between addressable and conventional fire alarms? Addressable systems name the device and warn when a detector gets dirty; conventional systems name a zone. In a medical tower with forty suites per floor, that is the difference between one door and forty.

Voice evacuation

Fire alarm voice evacuation installation and fire alarm emergency voice communication for high-rise towers, hospitals and large assembly rooms, with fire alarm speaker installation and fire alarm speaker strobe installation so messages can differ by floor.

Intelligent detection

Intelligent fire alarm installation and an intelligent fire detection system use detectors that adjust for their surroundings and report drift, which helps in kitchens, mechanical rooms and labs where ordinary detectors slide toward false alarms.

Air sampling

Aspirating smoke detection installation, an air sampling smoke detection system, VESDA installation and VESDA service for labs with high air change rates, server rooms and imaging equipment rooms where early warning matters and spot detectors are hard to reach.

Devices

Fire Alarm Devices in Morris Park Buildings and Where Each One Goes

Fire alarm equipment installation in Morris Park ranges from a single heat detector over a bakery oven to hundreds of devices on a hospital floor. Fire detection device installation and fire alarm initiating device installation cover the devices that tell the panel something is wrong; notification appliances tell people.

Smoke detectors

FAQ

Morris Park Fire Alarm Installation FAQ

Does a hotel on the Hutchinson Metro Center campus need a detector in each guest room as well as the building fire alarm?
+

Yes, in general. Hotels are a transient sleeping use, and the codes call for smoke alarms inside each guest room that wake the occupant, plus a building fire alarm with detection and notification in corridors and common areas where the occupancy requires it. The two do different jobs: the room alarm warns the guest, and the building system warns everyone and calls the FDNY through the central station. The design professional sets the exact layout.

Does a medical office building's fire alarm have to be tested more often because patients are inside?
+

Not as a rule. The testing intervals come from NFPA 72 and the NYC Fire Code by type of device, and an outpatient medical building generally follows the same schedule as any other building with the same equipment. Hospitals and some licensed health care facilities may have added requirements from their regulators. What patients do change is how testing is scheduled, so clinics aren't disrupted.

What should a lab manager tell us before a fire alarm test so experiments and freezers aren't affected?
+

Tell us which rooms have work that cannot be interrupted, which equipment is on circuits tied to the fire alarm, and whether any fans, dampers or door releases serve the lab. Most tests can be planned so that interfaces are proven without shutting down sensitive equipment for long, but that has to be agreed before the test day, not during it.

Is a dental office that uses nitrous oxide treated like a surgery suite for the fire alarm?
+

Not automatically. What matters is whether patients are rendered unable to leave on their own. Light sedation where patients stay responsive is different from general anesthesia. The design professional decides from the clinical program whether the suite is an ordinary business occupancy or ambulatory health care, and the fire alarm design follows that decision. Tell the architect exactly what sedation you plan to offer.

Do the conference centers at the Metro Center need different fire alarm notification from the office floors around them?
+

They can. A large conference room can be an assembly space, and the NY Engineers summary of the code sets a fire alarm trigger for assembly occupancies at an occupant load of 300. A room full of people at a presentation also needs notification that can be heard over a microphone and seen from every seat. The building's system usually covers it, with speakers and strobes sized to the room.

Our dialysis treatments can't be stopped halfway. How is a fire alarm test scheduled in a dialysis unit?
+

Around the treatment schedule, agreed with the clinical staff in advance. Most testing can be done when chairs are empty, early in the morning, late in the evening or on a day the unit is closed. Where a device in the unit has to be tested during hours, staff are told exactly when and what they will hear. The FDNY acceptance test for new work is booked the same way.

Does a parish school like St. Clare of Assisi follow a different fire alarm test schedule from the church next door?
+

The device testing schedule is set by NFPA 72 by type of device, so a smoke detector in the school and one in the parish hall follow the same intervals. Schools also hold the fire drills required by the Fire Code, and every school needs a fire alarm system whatever its size, which is not true of every church hall.

Does a hospital helipad like Jacobi's connect to the building fire alarm?
+

It can. A rooftop helipad has its own fire protection requirements under the Fire Code, and where it includes a fixed suppression system or detection, those are normally monitored by the building's fire alarm so a discharge or trouble shows on the panel. The hospital's design professional and engineering staff decide what is connected. Jacobi's helipad is noted by Wikipedia; we describe the general arrangement, not that hospital's system.

Can a fire alarm panel go inside a tenant suite in a tower, or does it have to be at the building's fire command station?
+

The building's main panel or its annunciator stays where the FDNY expects to find it, usually at the fire command station or main entrance. A tenant suite usually gets devices and modules on the building's loops, and sometimes a networked node or power supply in a closet, but not a separate stand-alone panel. Where equipment goes is shown on the approved drawings.

If the new Metro-North station brings more patients to a clinic nearby, does the clinic's occupant load change its fire alarm?
+

Not by itself. Occupant load is calculated from floor area and the use of each room, not from how busy the clinic is. It changes when the floor plan changes, for example a larger waiting room or a new group therapy room. If the clinic grows or rearranges its space to handle more visitors, ask the design professional to check the numbers again.

Can a tenant suite's devices report to the tenant's own central station instead of the building's?
+

Generally no. Devices on the building's system report through the building's panel and its central station connection, and the FDNY approves that arrangement as one system. A tenant that wants its own notifications can ask the landlord about extra contacts on the building's account. A truly separate tenant system is unusual and has to be designed and approved as such.

Does a cafeteria kitchen inside a hospital or office building need the same detection as a restaurant on the avenue?
+

The physics is the same, so the approach is similar: heat detection rather than smoke detection near cooking, and monitoring of the hood suppression system by the building's fire alarm. The difference is scale and ownership. An institutional kitchen is usually already part of a large addressable system run by the building's engineers, so changes are filed as modifications of that system.
