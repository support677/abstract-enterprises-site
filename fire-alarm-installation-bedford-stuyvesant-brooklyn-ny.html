The Neighborhood

Why Bedford Stuyvesant Properties Need Fire Alarm Installation

Bedford Stuyvesant is mostly old housing, and old housing is where fire detection is most often missing, out of date or wrong for the way the building is now used. Wikipedia's account of the neighborhood describes roughly 8,800 buildings built before 1900, and the FDNY's risk assessment for the district says 81 percent of its residential buildings went up before 1938. The masonry row houses began in the 1870s, and brownstones for the upper-middle class kept rising into the late 1910s. Those houses were designed for one family, a coal furnace and gas or kerosene light. Today the same house might hold an owner's duplex on the garden and parlor floors, two rented apartments above, a gas boiler in the cellar, three gas ranges, a window air conditioner in every bedroom, and an e-bike charging in the front hall. The alarm plan has to fit the house as it is lived in, not as it was built.

The second reason is the record. Patch reported in September 2022 that Bed-Stuy had eight fire deaths in the previous fiscal year, the most of any neighborhood in Brooklyn, across 539 structure fires, and an earlier Patch report counted ten Bed-Stuy fires in eleven days one winter that left 52 people without homes. Since then, News 12 Brooklyn and Brooklyn Paper have reported a three-story brownstone on MacDonough Street burning on all three floors just before midnight in April 2025, with a 77-year-old woman killed; a fire at 922 Lafayette Avenue that spread to three neighboring houses in June 2025; a fire behind a cellphone shop at 1515 Fulton Street that reached the three apartments above it in June 2026; and fires at 1112 Bedford Avenue and 808 Marcy Avenue in 2026. We describe those fires further down only as the outlets did. None of the reports says whether a detector worked.

The third reason is that the rules have changed under owners who have not moved in decades. Battery smoke alarms installed today have to be sealed ten-year units under Local Law 112 of 2013, according to the Department of Buildings. Fire alarm filings moved from the Buildings Department to the FDNY in June 2019. And on January 1, 2027, about seven weeks after this page was reviewed, natural gas alarms become due in covered homes with gas piping. The Buildings Department's October 13, 2026 announcement lists apartment buildings and private dwellings, including one- and two-family homes, among the buildings covered. A Bed-Stuy brownstone with two rental floors and a gas boiler is squarely inside that rule.

Finally, a large share of the neighborhood sits inside landmark districts, which makes owners nervous about drilling anything. The Stuyvesant Heights district, the expanded Bedford-Stuyvesant/Stuyvesant Heights district designated in 2013 with about 825 buildings, the Bedford Historic District designated in 2015 with about 800 buildings, and the 36 Queen Anne row houses of Alice and Agate Courts all fall under the Landmarks Preservation Commission. As we explain in the landmarks section, almost all detection work happens inside and never touches the facade, but the FDNY filing for a building system in a district does need a specific landmark statement, and it is worth knowing that before an engineer is hired.

Fire Alarm Installation for Bedford Stuyvesant's Architecture

Seven kinds of building account for almost every fire alarm job we see in Bedford Stuyvesant. They differ in who owns them, who sleeps in them and what their walls and floors are made of, and they fall under different city rules. Those differences settle the plan long before any brand or model comes into it.

The brownstone kept for one family

Three to four stories over a cellar, an open stair from the garden floor to a skylight, wood joists between brick party walls, and usually a renovation somewhere in its recent past.

The rowhouse with rental floors

An owner's duplex on the garden and parlor floors with one or two apartments above, one front door, one stair, and a cellar holding the boiler and meters for everyone.

The three- and four-family house

Three or more apartments make it a multiple dwelling, which brings HPD's full detector duties, self-closing doors and the fire safety notice on every apartment door.

The 1890s French flat

Four- and five-story apartment houses of the kind the Landmarks Commission describes in the expanded Stuyvesant Heights district, with a shared stair, several apartments per floor and a cellar below.

The building with a store at street level

A shop on Fulton Street, Nostrand Avenue or Broadway with apartments over it, closed from evening to morning while families sleep upstairs.

The church, sanctuary or storefront congregation

Converted rowhouses, storefronts and old sanctuaries, often with a gas boiler below, a basement hall in regular use and parts of the building closed for repairs.

Senior, supportive and public housing

Buildings such as Stuyvesant Gardens on Malcolm X Boulevard and NYCHA's Marcy and Tompkins Houses, where the building fire alarm belongs to the owner and is serviced under its own contracts.

The brownstone kept for one family: an open stair is a chimney

The defining feature of a Bed-Stuy brownstone, for fire detection, is the stair. In most of these houses it rises from the garden floor or the parlor floor through every level to the top, often under a skylight, with no door anywhere along it. A fire in the garden-floor kitchen or a cellar boiler room sends heat and smoke up that open shaft faster than through any other part of the house, and the top-floor bedrooms sit at the end of it. The April 2025 MacDonough Street fire, which Brooklyn Paper reported had all three floors burning when firefighters arrived just before midnight, is the kind of fire that moves through an open house that way. The paper did not report how it started or whether any alarm sounded, and we draw no conclusion about that house.

What we take from the layout is placement. An alarm at the head of every flight, where smoke arriving from below collects first. An alarm inside every bedroom and in the hall outside it, because a Brownstoner poster answering an owner's question put it plainly: "A hallway detector does NOT cover the bedroom. You need both." Carbon monoxide near the sleeping areas on each bedroom floor and in the cellar at a sensible distance from the boiler. And above all, linking: when the kitchen alarm on the garden floor goes off, every alarm in the house must go off with it, because nobody on the fourth floor will hear a single alarm two floors below with the door shut and a window unit running. In a one-family house that is the single most valuable change we make.

The renovation: fifteen devices on the drawings, and what to do about the linking limit

Gut renovations are common in Bed-Stuy, and they are the best time to put in hardwired, linked alarms, because the walls and ceilings are open and cable can go anywhere. They also produce a question we hear often, and that a Brooklyn owner raised on the Brownstoner forum: the architect's drawings for a single-family brownstone showed fifteen smoke and CO devices, and the owner understood that all of them had to be interconnected but that the alarms they wanted would only link twelve. Another poster replied that fifteen "does seem a bit excessive for a single-family," and someone else suggested putting the extra devices on an interlock relay on a second circuit.

Our approach is practical. First we check the count against the house: one in each sleeping room, one outside each sleeping area, one on every level including the cellar, CO where the code and the fuel-burning appliances call for it, and a combination device wherever smoke and CO are both wanted in the same spot, which often brings a fifteen-device plan down to something smaller without losing any coverage. Then we check the maker's interconnect limit for the exact model, which differs between product lines. Where a large house genuinely needs more devices than one interconnected string allows, the manufacturers' own relay modules link two strings so that an alarm on either sets off both. We put the final count and the wiring arrangement on the device list so the electrician, the architect and the inspector are working from the same page.

The rowhouse with rental floors: one stair, several households

The most common arrangement we see is an owner living on the garden and parlor floors and renting one or two apartments above. Everyone uses the same front door and the same stair. The cellar holds the boiler, the water heater and the gas meters for the whole house. A fire in the owner's kitchen on the garden floor threatens the tenants on the top floor, and a fire in a tenant's kitchen threatens the owner's family. Detection that stops at each apartment door leaves the shared stair, the shared hall and the cellar, where fires often start out of everyone's sight, without any warning at all.

So in a house like this we plan two layers. Inside each apartment go the devices HPD requires and the ones that wake people: smoke within fifteen feet of each sleeping-room entrance, carbon monoxide where any fuel burns, and from January 1, 2027, natural gas detection near the gas appliances. In the shared spaces go linked alarms at the bottom and top of the stair, on each landing and in the cellar, so a fire anywhere in the common areas sounds on every floor. We write down which devices the owner maintains and which ones each tenant is expected to test monthly, because HPD's page assigns monthly testing and battery replacement to tenants and replacement at end of life to owners, and a written split avoids arguments later.

The three- and four-family house: when the house becomes a multiple dwelling

The moment a Bed-Stuy rowhouse has three apartments, it is a multiple dwelling, and the owner's duties grow. HPD's detector page requires at least one approved, working smoke detector in each unit within fifteen feet of the primary entrance to each room lawfully used for sleeping, CO detection in each unit if the building has a fossil-fuel burning device, natural gas detection where there is gas piping from January 1, 2027, a notice given to an adult in each apartment, a notice posted in the building's entrance area, and records of the posting date, each device's install date and the end of its useful life. HPD's fire safety page adds that every residential building with three or more apartments must have self-closing apartment doors and a Fire Safety Notice posted on the inside of every apartment door.

Those items work together. A self-closing door keeps a fire in one apartment out of the stair long enough for the others to leave, and the alarms in the stair tell them to go. When we survey a three- or four-family house we check the doors, the posted notices and the detectors in one walk, and our device list carries the dates HPD wants kept. The other question these houses raise is the roof. A Brooklyn tenant on the Brownstoner forum described a top-floor apartment in a three-family brownstone whose only second way out was a heavy roof hatch reached by a ladder, with no fire escape and no sprinklers, and posters split on whether that counts as a second exit. We cover what detection can and cannot do in that situation in the questions below.

The 1890s French flat: a small apartment building inside a row of houses

The Landmarks Commission's designation report for the expanded Stuyvesant Heights district describes French flats of four to five stories from the 1890s alongside the row houses, many with shared entrances through a vestibule into a hallway. Inside, they work like small apartment buildings: one stair, several apartments on each floor, a cellar with the boiler and storage, sometimes a store cut into the ground floor. The FDNY's assessment counts multi-family walk-ups as 43 percent of the district's residential buildings, so these and the later walk-ups are a large share of the work.

In a French flat the plan splits cleanly. In every apartment, HPD's detectors and their records. In the halls, the stair and the cellar, whatever the building already has: sometimes an old panel with hall detectors and bells, sometimes linked alarms someone added, sometimes nothing. A like-for-like repair of an existing panel is ordinary service work. Adding a building system where there was none, or moving or replacing a panel with a different one, is a design change that a registered design professional files with the FDNY before installation. We tell the owner in writing which of those their building needs before we price it, and in a district building we flag the landmark statement the FDNY filing will need.

The building with a store at street level: the hours nobody is downstairs

The FDNY assessment says 11 percent of the district's residential buildings mix homes and businesses, and almost all of them are on the commercial streets. The June 2026 fire at 1515 Fulton Street near Throop Avenue is the local example: News 12 Brooklyn reported a four-story building with a clothing store and a cellphone shop sharing the ground-floor unit and three apartments above, a fire that started behind the cellphone shop and reached the apartments, more than 140 fire and EMS members on scene, and the Red Cross registering eight adults and six children for help. That fire broke out shortly after 1:30 in the afternoon, when the shops were open and people were awake. The same fire at three in the morning, with the gates down, would have been a very different story.

So for a building like that, the store's detection has to do two jobs: notice a fire in a back room nobody is watching, and warn the families upstairs. We plan detection in the store that sounds where the apartments can hear it, or reports to a central station that can send help, and we choose devices that will not false-alarm on the steam of a laundromat, the dust of a stockroom or the cooking of a takeout counter. Where the store is a business that has a fire alarm system and fuel-burning equipment, the FDNY's carbon monoxide rule may also apply, as we explain in the commercial section.

The church, sanctuary or storefront congregation

Bed-Stuy has a great many congregations, and their buildings have their own fire record. News 12 reported a second-floor fire at Bethel of the Nazarene Church in November 2024 that damaged the ceiling of the main room. In June 2025, First Responder News reported an all-hands fire with heavy damage at Iglesia Pentecostal "El Redil" at 162 Tompkins Avenue, which firefighters kept from spreading to the buildings beside it. And in 2017, Brownstoner reported that the synagogue of B'nai Adath Kol Beth Yisrael at 1006 Greene Avenue, whose congregation had been meeting in the basement while the sanctuary above stood unused, was destroyed by a fire that local reports said a maintenance worker started accidentally while loosening roof tiles with a torch.

Churches raise questions houses do not. Parts of the building may be closed for years while services move to the basement. Boilers run hard during long services and evening programs. Volunteers do maintenance. And whether a congregation needs a building fire alarm system at all depends on its size, occupant load and construction, which is a design professional's call, while the FDNY's carbon monoxide rule for fire-alarm-protected places of worship with fuel-burning equipment applies once a system is in. We help trustees sort out which of those situations they are in, install household-type detection where that is the right answer, and install and pre-test to approved drawings where a filed system is required.

Senior, supportive and public housing: the building system belongs to the owner

Larger buildings have engineered fire alarm systems that were designed, filed and inspected, and work on them goes through the owner, the managing agent and the building's fire alarm company. The November 2025 fire at Stuyvesant Gardens, a senior building at 159 Malcolm X Boulevard, shows how those systems and sprinklers behave together: News 12 reported that the fire stayed in the first-floor unit where it started, that two people were taken to the hospital, and that a sprinkler outside the unit kept running for hours and flooded the hallway. A relative told the outlet her mother "was stuck inside and there was a lot of smoke" and stayed safe in her bedroom.

NYCHA's Marcy Houses, opened in 1949 with 27 six-story buildings and 1,705 apartments, and Sumner Houses, finished in 1958 with 13 buildings, are public housing where the building fire alarm is NYCHA's. Residents report problems through their development's management, and a private installer does not work on those systems unless the housing authority hires one. The $1.85 million Representative Jeffries secured in 2024 for fire detection components at Marcy and Tompkins Houses is that kind of landlord work. What residents of any large building can do on their own is make sure the smoke and CO alarms inside their apartment work, and report a missing one in writing.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Light a Smoke Pencil at the Cellar Door and Watch Where It Goes

Start below the garden floor. In a Bed-Stuy rowhouse the survey begins in the cellar, at the boiler, the water heater and the gas meters. The technician notes every appliance that burns fuel, every room the gas piping feeds, and anything stored against the boiler, because the cellar is where fires grow unseen and where carbon monoxide begins. From there we follow the gas up the house to every range, dryer and gas fireplace, room by room, because each of those rooms needs a natural gas alarm in a covered home from January 1, 2027.

Find the air path. Before deciding where a detector goes on each landing, we look at how air moves through the house. Open stairs, old dumbwaiter openings, gaps around pipes that run floor to floor, and the skylight at the top all set the route smoke will take. Where a homeowner is curious, a few seconds with a smoke pencil at the cellar door shows the draft better than any drawing, and it usually ends up at the top of the stair. That is where the most important alarm in the house goes.

Count the households and the beds. On each floor we ask who sleeps there, including a relative on a sofa bed or a tenant nobody mentioned. The June 2025 fire on Lafayette Avenue ended, according to News 12, with inspectors finding an illegal basement apartment in one of the neighboring houses. If the number of households in a house does not match what its owner expects, we say so, because detection planned for two families will miss the third.

Shut every door and sound the far alarm. Last, with every bedroom door shut and any window air conditioner running, a second person sets off the alarm farthest from the top-floor bedroom, usually the one by the garden-floor kitchen. If it cannot be heard clearly in that bedroom, the plan needs linking or another alarm, whatever the minimum would allow. In four-story houses with thick brick and plaster, that test fails more often than owners expect.

Historic Districts

Fire Alarms Inside Bed-Stuy's Landmark Districts: What the Landmarks Commission and the FDNY Each Ask For

Owners in Stuyvesant Heights, the Bedford Historic District and Alice and Agate Courts often assume that any work on a landmarked house means a long Landmarks Commission process. For fire detection, that is rarely true, but there are two specific points where landmark status does matter, and getting them right at the start saves weeks.

Most detection work is interior work that never needs the Commission. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's page on permit types says interior work in a historic district building needs an LPC permit in three situations: when the interior work requires a permit from the Department of Buildings, when it affects the exterior of the building, giving HVAC louvers and vents as examples, and when it affects a space designated as an interior landmark. Sealed ten-year battery alarms, wireless linked alarms and carbon monoxide and gas alarms mounted inside on ceilings and walls fall outside all three in ordinary houses. So a family in a landmarked brownstone can have its alarms replaced and linked without filing anything with Landmarks.

Hardwired work during a renovation is where to check. A gut renovation of a district brownstone almost always has DOB filings and an LPC permit already, because the work goes well beyond alarms. If new hardwired alarms are part of an electrical job that the electrician files separately, the question to ask is whether that filing brings the interior work into the first of LPC's three categories. We do not guess at that for a particular house. We ask the electrician or architect to confirm it with LPC before the filing goes in, and we route cable so that nothing at all appears on the front or rear facade.

The exterior is where the Commission's rules bite. The same LPC page says a permit is needed for any work that affects the exterior of a building in a historic district, even on parts not visible from the street. A monitoring antenna on a cornice, a strobe or horn on the front of the house, a conduit run down the rear wall or a keypad box at the areaway gate would each be exterior work. In practice we avoid all of them in district buildings. Cellular communicators sit inside, cable travels in closets and chases, and notification for a store or church goes inside the space it protects.

The FDNY filing for a building system needs a landmark statement. When a building in a district needs a filed fire alarm system, such as a French flat getting new hall detection, a church or a store with a panel, the FDNY's Local Law 195 FAQ says buildings in historic districts and individual landmarks need a sealed, signed statement that the work is exempt and the exterior will not be altered, and the FDNY's fire alarm tips guide lists "Landmark certification as required" among the documents in the plan package. January 1, 2027

Natural Gas Alarms Become Due in Bed-Stuy Homes on January 1, 2027: Who Is Covered and What to Install

On October 13, 2026, the Department of Buildings announced that owners of covered residential buildings with gas piping must install code-compliant natural gas alarms in each dwelling unit, near the gas appliances, by January 1, 2027, under Local Law 102 of 2025. As this page was reviewed, that date was about seven weeks away. The release lists the buildings covered: apartment buildings, private dwellings including one- and two-family homes, hotels and motels, lodging and rooming houses, and single room occupancy buildings. HPD's detector page describes the same requirement for multiple dwellings and for one- and two-family homes the owner does not occupy, wherever there is gas piping. If you live in your own one-family house, ask before assuming either way; if any part of your house is rented, plan on gas alarms.

What counts. The Buildings Department says the alarms must be listed to UL 1484 or UL 2075, and that hardwired, plug-in and battery-powered models are all allowed. The city does not endorse brands, but the release names battery-powered models it has identified as UL 1484 listed from five makers: Aware Buildings, DeNova, Heiman, Nexelec and Universal Security Instruments, and it says that list is partial. HPD allows a combined device that covers smoke, CO and gas, as long as the installation still meets each requirement it is meant to cover, and every device has to include an end-of-life alarm. A smoke or CO alarm that does not list natural gas among its functions does not count toward this rule.

Where they go in a brownstone. HPD's page puts gas detection in the area where the natural gas burning device is located, and, in Class A and B multiple dwellings, within fifteen feet of sleeping-room entrances if a fuel-gas appliance is in that room. In a typical Bed-Stuy rowhouse that means the cellar where the boiler, the water heater and often a gas dryer sit; each apartment's kitchen with its gas range; and any room with a gas fireplace insert or gas logs, which many owners added in parlor fireplaces over the years. A three-family brownstone with a gas boiler and three gas ranges is usually four gas alarms at minimum, before any gas dryer or fireplace.

Paperwork and enforcement. HPD asks owners to give at least one adult in each unit a natural gas notice along with the smoke and CO notices, to post notices in the entrance area of a multiple dwelling, and to keep the date each notice was posted, the date each device was installed and the expiration date of each device's useful life. The Buildings Department says HPD code enforcement staff will begin inspecting for compliance next year, and that DOB and National Grid are handing out a limited number of free detectors to small property owners at outreach events, first come, first served. A tenant in a Class A building can be charged $25 for a single-function detector, $50 for a two-function device or $75 for a three-function device, with a year to pay, under HPD's schedule.

One visit per apartment, not three. Getting into every apartment is the slowest part of detector work in a rented rowhouse. If a smoke alarm is near the end of its ten years, or a CO alarm went missing at the last turnover, it costs less and bothers tenants less to fix that in the same visit as the gas alarm, with one notice and one updated record. We price smoke, CO and gas together per apartment, on our landlord rate of $400 to $800 per unit, and the device list we leave carries the install and expiration date of every device by apartment and room.

Your Questions Answered

Fire Alarm Installation in Bedford Stuyvesant: Questions Answered

These are situations Bed-Stuy homeowners, landlords, tenants and church trustees bring to us that the sections above do not already settle. Each is answered here in full and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

The layout of the house

Our kitchen sits in a one-story rear extension off the garden floor, and the bedrooms are two and three floors up. Does the smoke alarm belong in the extension or at the foot of the stair?

Usually neither on its own. Where a rowhouse has a kitchen built out into the yard on the garden level, with a flat roof and its own ceiling height, that extension is often the most likely place in the house for a fire to start, because the range, the toaster oven and sometimes a gas dryer are all there. A smoke alarm inside a small kitchen will sound every time something sears, so owners pull it down, and an alarm nobody can live with is the same as no alarm. We put a photoelectric alarm or a heat alarm in the extension, set back from the range rather than over it, as a Brownstoner poster advised another owner for the kitchen floor, and a smoke alarm in the room the extension opens into, near where the stair begins. Then we link both to every alarm upstairs. The point of the extension device is not to wake the people in the kitchen; they already know. It is to set off the alarms in the top-floor bedrooms the moment a fire takes hold downstairs, while the stair is still clear. If the extension also has a gas range, it needs a natural gas alarm under the 2027 rule in a covered home, and a combination device can keep the number of things on that ceiling down.

We are turning our one-family brownstone into a two-family, with a rental duplex on the top two floors. What changes for detection once a second household moves in?

Three things change. First, the law: a two-family that is partly rented brings HPD's carbon monoxide duties and, from January 1, 2027, natural gas detection, along with the notice to an adult in the rented unit and the records of install and expiration dates. Second, the cost to the tenant: in a rented one- or two-family home, HPD's page lets an owner charge the tenant $25 per CO or gas detector, with a year to pay. Third, and most important in practice, the layout. In a one-family house every alarm is one family's business. In a two-family the tenant's duplex has its own kitchen and its own bedrooms, but it still shares the stair and the front door with you, and the cellar boiler still serves both homes. We plan each apartment's own smoke and CO alarms first, then decide with you how the shared stair and cellar are covered and whether those alarms should link into both apartments, so that a cellar fire reaches the tenant's bedrooms and a fire in the tenant's kitchen reaches yours. If the conversion is part of a filed renovation, the architect's drawings set the minimum; we make sure the installed layout matches them and still passes the closed-door listening test in each bedroom. If the conversion changes the Certificate of Occupancy, that is the architect's filing, not ours, and the alarms should go in to the final layout rather than the old one.

Getting out, and gathering in

The top-floor apartment in our three-family brownstone has no fire escape, only a ladder to a roof hatch. What can a fire alarm do for the tenants up there?

It can give them more time, and time is what that apartment is short of. A Brooklyn tenant described exactly this arrangement on the Brownstoner forum, a top-floor unit in a three-family brownstone whose only other way out was a heavy hatch reached by a ladder in the hall, with no fire escape and no sprinklers, and the replies split: one poster said a hatch counts as a second means of egress, another said "Hatches are meant for firemen to come into a building, not for occupants to escape," and another noted that "Houses aren't required to constantly be upgraded to meet code." Whether that hatch is a legal exit is a question for an architect, the Department of Buildings and the FDNY, not for an alarm installer, and we would not tell a landlord or a tenant that an alarm fixes it. What detection can do is make sure the people on that floor hear about a fire below them as early as possible, while the main stair is still usable. That means alarms on every landing of the shared stair and in the cellar, linked into the top-floor apartment, plus the apartment's own smoke and CO alarms, so a fire on the garden floor wakes the top floor in the first minute rather than when smoke reaches their door. We also check that the hatch can actually be opened from below by the people who live there, and we tell the owner in writing if it cannot.

A church study group of twenty or thirty people meets in our parlor most weeks. Does that change anything about the alarms in our house?

It does not turn your house into a church, and we would not tell you it needs a building fire alarm system because neighbors gather in your front room. A house used occasionally for a meeting is still a house. What it changes is how we think about the parlor floor on those evenings. Twenty or thirty people in a double parlor, many of them visitors who do not know the house, means more people who would need to find their way down the stoop or through the garden-floor door in a hurry, and more people who would not know what your alarm sounds like. So we make sure the parlor floor has a smoke alarm that every other alarm in the house sets off, so a fire in the garden-floor kitchen or the cellar is heard upstairs at once, and we place it where people sitting in the front room can hear it over conversation and music. If you have gas logs in the parlor fireplace, the room should have CO and natural gas detection as well. And if the gatherings grow into regular services with a set schedule and an expected crowd, that is the point to talk with a design professional about whether the use of the building has changed, which is a different conversation from an alarm visit.

Churches and larger buildings

Our congregation has moved services to the basement while the sanctuary upstairs is closed for repairs. Does the empty sanctuary still need detection?

Yes, and in some ways it needs it more than when it was full. A sanctuary in regular use has people in it who would see smoke. A closed one has nobody, and a fire that starts there, in old wiring, in stored materials or during repair work, can burn for a long time before the congregation below notices. Brownstoner's report on the 2017 fire at 1006 Greene Avenue described exactly that arrangement, a congregation meeting in the basement under a sanctuary that had not held services for about fifteen years, and local reports blamed a maintenance worker's torch on the roof. We make no claim about that building's alarms. The planning lesson is simple: detection in a closed part of a building must report to the part people use. In a congregation without a building system, that means smoke detection in the sanctuary and its roof space linked to alarms in the basement hall, so a fire upstairs is heard downstairs during a service. Where the building has a fire alarm panel, the sanctuary's devices should stay in service and supervised through the closure, and any work that takes them out of service should be arranged with the fire alarm company of record. Repair crews using flame or hot tools should not be working in a space whose detection has been switched off for their convenience without someone watching it.

In the Stuyvesant Gardens fire, a sprinkler outside the burning unit kept running for hours and flooded the hallway. Does the fire alarm have anything to do with when a sprinkler stops?

Not in the way most people expect. A sprinkler head opens because of heat at that head, not because a smoke detector or a fire alarm panel told it to, and once open it keeps flowing until the water supply to it is shut off. News 12 reported that at Stuyvesant Gardens on Malcolm X Boulevard in November 2025 the fire stayed in the first-floor unit where it started, two people were taken to the hospital, and a sprinkler outside the unit ran for hours and flooded the hallway, leaving some residents unable to get back in for a time. What the fire alarm system does in a sprinklered building is report the water. The FDNY's fire alarm guide lists an operational sprinkler system among the conditions for inspecting a new alarm, and in buildings where the sprinkler is supervised by the fire alarm, the waterflow and valve devices tell the panel and the monitoring center that water is moving, which is how the fire department learns a head has opened. Shutting the water off after a fire is a decision for the fire department and the building's staff, made at the sprinkler valve, once the officer in charge is satisfied the fire is out. For a building like that, the useful questions for an owner are whether the waterflow signal reaches the monitoring center, whether the staff know where the control valves are and who is allowed to close them, and whether the panel's devices in a flooded hallway were checked before the building was put back in normal service.

Old filings and unfinished jobs

A fire alarm job for our building was filed with the Buildings Department years ago and never finished. Does it have to start over with the FDNY?

Not necessarily. The FDNY's Local Law 195 FAQ, written when fire alarm filings moved from the Department of Buildings to the FDNY, says applications filed before June 2, 2019 stay on the DOB process and are signed off under the procedures in place for them, and that post-approval amendments for those jobs go through DOB. Applications filed after that date go through the FDNY under an FDNY project number. So the first step is finding out exactly when your job was filed and what state it is in, which your original architect or engineer, or a filing representative, can look up. Some owners come to us holding a set of approved drawings from years ago and a contractor who has moved on. If the old job is still alive on the DOB side and the design still matches the building, it can sometimes be completed as filed. If the building has changed since, with apartments added or combined or a store opened below, the old design may no longer fit, and a change of engineer or a post-approval amendment under the FDNY process requires a new TM application and fee, according to the same FAQ. Either way, we survey the building against the old drawings and tell you, in writing, which devices are in, which are missing and what no longer matches, so your design professional can decide which path is quicker.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Fire Alarm Installation in Bedford Stuyvesant

AI summaries and national cost guides from sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr are written for an average American house on an average street. A Bed-Stuy rowhouse with tenants upstairs, a landmark designation and a gas boiler is not that house. These are the shortcuts we see mislead owners here most often.

"Fire deaths happen even in homes with alarms, so the type of alarm matters most"

The FDNY's own numbers for this district point somewhere else. Its Community Risk Assessment for Brooklyn Community Board 3 counts twelve residential fire deaths over five years and says every one of them happened without a working smoke detector. Citywide the figure is 66 percent.

That does not mean the type of alarm is irrelevant. Photoelectric sensing, combination devices and linking all matter. But in Bed-Stuy the first problem is not which alarm; it is whether there is a working alarm at all, in the right place, and whether it can be heard on the top floor.

So the first thing we do on any visit is find the alarms that are missing, dead, past their date or pulled off the ceiling because they kept going off. Fixing those comes before any upgrade.

"Brownstones are single-family homes, so a few smoke alarms are all the law asks for"

Many Bed-Stuy brownstones are not single-family homes any more. The FDNY's assessment counts 44 percent of the district's residential buildings as one- and two-family houses and 43 percent as multi-family walk-ups. A rowhouse with an owner's duplex and two rented apartments is a three-family, and three apartments make it a multiple dwelling.

A multiple dwelling brings HPD's full detector rules: smoke within fifteen feet of every sleeping-room entrance in every unit, CO where fuel burns, gas detection from 2027, posted notices, records of install and expiration dates, self-closing doors and a Fire Safety Notice on every apartment door.

A rented two-family carries CO and gas duties too. The legal minimum depends on who lives in the house, not on what it looks like from the sidewalk.

"Anything you do to a landmarked house needs Landmarks Commission approval"

Not for most fire detection. The Landmarks Commission's permit page limits interior work that needs its permit to work that requires a DOB permit, work that affects the exterior and work in a designated interior landmark. Alarms mounted on interior ceilings in an ordinary house are none of those.

The Commission does care about the outside. Any exterior work on a building in a historic district needs a permit, including parts nobody sees from the street, so outdoor strobes, antennas and conduit on a facade are where trouble starts.

For filed building systems, the FDNY wants a sealed, signed statement that the work is exempt and will not alter the exterior. That is a document, not a delay, if the design keeps everything inside from the start.

"Natural gas detectors are for big apartment buildings, not houses"

The Buildings Department's October 2026 announcement lists private dwellings, including one- and two-family homes, among the buildings whose owners must install natural gas alarms near gas appliances by January 1, 2027, alongside apartment buildings, rooming houses and single room occupancy buildings.

HPD's page frames the house rule around one- and two-family homes the owner does not occupy. Either way, a Bed-Stuy rowhouse with a rented floor and gas piping is covered, and that describes a very large share of the neighborhood.

The same announcement says HPD inspectors will begin checking for compliance next year. Waiting for a violation to arrive is the expensive way to find out.

"A sprinklered building does not need much detection"

Sprinklers and alarms do different jobs. A sprinkler controls fire where heat reaches it; an alarm tells people to leave. Patch reported that in the February 2023 e-bike battery fire on the Bed-Stuy and Bushwick border, the building's sprinkler system had no effect because the fire spread so quickly.

News 12's report on the November 2025 Stuyvesant Gardens fire describes a fire that stayed in one unit in a sprinklered building, yet a resident's relative still described "a lot of smoke." Smoke travels where water does not.

A sprinklered building still needs working smoke and CO alarms in each apartment, and where it has a fire alarm system, the sprinkler's waterflow and valve devices should report to it so the fire department hears when water is flowing.

"Fire alarm plans are filed with the Department of Buildings"

That was true for a long time, and older architects and contractors still say it. The FDNY's Local Law 195 FAQ explains that fire alarm applications filed after June 2, 2019 go through the FDNY, under an FDNY project number that replaces the DOB job number.

The FDNY's tips guide then sets the path: plans by a registered professional engineer or architect on a TM-1, an initial review of up to eight weeks, about twenty to twenty-four weeks overall, and an inspection that needs live central station monitoring and an S-97 or S-98 holder present.

Jobs filed with DOB before that date stay on the DOB process. Knowing which one applies to a stalled Bed-Stuy renovation is often the difference between finishing and starting over.

"Any smoke alarm from the hardware store will do"

Not in New York City. The Department of Buildings' bulletin on Local Law 112 of 2013 says battery-powered smoke alarms installed under it must be listed to UL 217, use a non-removable, non-replaceable battery lasting at least ten years and give an audible warning at end of life. A cheap alarm with a nine-volt door does not meet that.

The same bulletin says required alarms must be replaced when they pass the manufacturer's suggested useful life, and HPD requires every device to have an end-of-life alarm and never to be painted over.

For businesses with a fire alarm system, the FDNY adds that required detectors cannot be swapped for stand-alone alarms at all. The device on the shelf has to match the building it goes into.

FAQ

Bedford Stuyvesant Fire Alarm Installation FAQ

How many recent Bed-Stuy fire deaths happened in homes without a working smoke detector?
+

All of them, on the FDNY's figures. Its Community Risk Assessment for Brooklyn Community Board 3, based on FDNY data for 2020 to 2024, counts twelve residential fire fatalities in five years and says 100 percent happened without a working smoke detector, compared with 66 percent citywide. Checking that every floor has a working, in-date alarm is the most useful fire safety step a Bed-Stuy household can take.

When will HPD start checking buildings for natural gas alarms?
+

The Department of Buildings said in its October 13, 2026 announcement that HPD code enforcement staff will begin inspecting for compliance next year, after the January 1, 2027 deadline. An owner who installs the alarms, gives each unit the notice and records the install and expiration dates before then has nothing to fix when an inspector arrives.

Are the free gas alarms the city and National Grid are handing out good enough to use?
+

They can be. The Buildings Department says DOB and National Grid are giving a limited number of free detectors to small property owners at outreach events, first come, first served, and that any alarm used must be listed to UL 1484 or UL 2075. Check the listing and the end-of-life date on the box, and place one near each gas appliance in every covered unit.

Do hardwired smoke alarms also need sealed ten-year batteries?
+

No. The Department of Buildings bulletin on Local Law 112 of 2013 says its sealed ten-year battery rule applies to smoke alarms that may take their primary power from batteries, and not to alarms powered by building wiring. Hardwired alarms usually carry a backup battery, and they still have to be replaced when they pass the manufacturer's suggested useful life.

Our building's fire guard holds an F-78 certificate. Can he clean the smoke detectors connected to our panel?
+

He can maintain and clean smoke detectors, according to the FDNY, but not carbon monoxide detectors. The FDNY's guidance says S-97 and S-98 Certificate of Fitness holders can maintain, test and clean both smoke and CO detectors, while F-78 and S-78 holders can maintain and clean smoke detectors only. Testing and programming stay with an S-97 or S-98 holder.

Our managing agent handles everything. Does the owner personally have to sign the FDNY fire alarm application?
+

The owner's signature is required. The FDNY's Local Law 195 FAQ says the owner must sign the TM-1 application, in its Section 17. The managing agent and the design professional can prepare everything else, but the owner, or someone with legal authority to sign for the owning company or estate, has to sign before the filing goes in.

Are churches and nonprofits exempt from the FDNY's fire alarm filing fees?
+

No. The FDNY's Local Law 195 FAQ says city agencies and the School Construction Authority are exempt when they file directly with their own engineers or architects, and states that places of worship, private schools and nonprofit organizations are not exempt. A congregation should budget for the filing fee alongside the design and installation.

Our church's approved fire alarm plans went missing when the old trustees left. Can we get them back?
+

Yes, through a refiling rather than a lookup. The FDNY's Local Law 195 FAQ says lost approved plans are replaced by filing a new TM-1 under the same FPIMS number, paying the fees, and resubmitting signed and stamped plans for restamping. Unofficial approval letters are posted online and official copies are mailed, so the approval letter itself may be easier to find than the drawings.

How long does an FDNY Project Authorization for fire alarm work stay valid?
+

Two years, renewable, according to the FDNY's Local Law 195 FAQ. That matters for long Bed-Stuy renovations and church projects that stop and start with funding. If the work will run past two years from the authorization date, the design professional should plan the renewal well before it lapses.

Why does the FDNY filing for our building ask for an asbestos report?
+

Because the FDNY's Local Law 195 FAQ requires an ACP-5 asbestos report with fire alarm filings, using the same standards as the old Buildings Department process. Installing a system means drilling and opening ceilings and walls, and in buildings as old as most of Bedford Stuyvesant's, the filing has to show that work was checked for asbestos first. The design professional or an asbestos investigator prepares it.

Where is the FDNY office that handles fire alarm inspections in Brooklyn?
+

The FDNY's fire alarm tips guide gives the Fire Alarm Inspection Unit's address as 9 MetroTech Center in Brooklyn, on Tuesdays and Thursdays from 9 a.m. to noon, and says inspections are requested with form B-45. The inspection itself happens at your building, with an S-97 or S-98 holder present and the panel free of trouble.

Our new natural gas alarm is sounding. What do we do first?
+

Leave. The Department of Buildings and the FDNY say that if a gas alarm sounds or you smell gas, everyone should leave the area immediately and call 911 once they are somewhere safe, then contact the gas utility. Do not switch lights on or off or look for the leak yourself. Call us only after the fire department or the utility has cleared the house.
