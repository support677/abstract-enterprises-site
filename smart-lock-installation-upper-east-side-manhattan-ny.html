The Neighborhood

Why Upper East Side Homes Need Smart Lock Installation Planned Door by Door

The Upper East Side is not one housing market with one kind of door. Wikipedia's account of the neighbourhood draws it from 59th Street up to 96th Street and from Fifth Avenue and Central Park across to the East River, taking in Lenox Hill, Carnegie Hill and Yorkville, all inside Manhattan Community District 8 and the 19th Precinct. Walk east along almost any street in the 70s and the doors change every few hundred feet: limestone co-op entrances with uniformed staff on Fifth and Park, brownstone and brick townhouses on the side streets between Madison and Lexington, five- and six-storey walk-ups toward Second and First, and post-war and new towers out on York and East End. Each of those doors is governed by somebody different — a co-op board and its house rules, the Landmarks Preservation Commission, a landlord, a condo's managing agent — and each has a different set of people who need to get through it.

It is also a large and an older neighbourhood. NYU's Furman Center, in its May 2026 profile of the Upper East Side, estimates 224,027 residents in 2024, the fourth-largest population of the city's 59 community districts. Its figures show 23.9 percent of residents aged 65 or older and about half of all households, 50.5 percent, made up of one person living alone. The homeownership rate, 37.9 percent in 2024, is higher than the citywide 32.7 percent, which still leaves most households renting. Read together, those numbers sketch the requests a lock plan here has to handle: someone living alone who wants a relative in another city to be able to let a plumber in; a family with young children and household staff who each need a different door schedule; a renter who wants something smarter on the inside of a door the landlord owns.

Much of the area's building stock is protected. Wikipedia's summary of the Landmarks Preservation Commission's designations lists the Upper East Side Historic District, designated in 1981 and running from 59th to 78th Street along Fifth Avenue and as far east as Third Avenue in places, expanded in 2010 with 74 more buildings; the Metropolitan Museum Historic District on Fifth Avenue between 79th and 86th; the Park Avenue Historic District, designated in 2014 with 64 properties between 79th and 91st; the Carnegie Hill Historic District, about 400 buildings north of 86th; and the small Treadwell Farm and Henderson Place districts. The Commission's own rules list repairing or replacing door hardware — hinges, knobs and handles — among work that needs no approval, but they carve out “ornate historic exterior hardware on special doors”, and anything that changes the look of the door itself falls under a permit. A smart lock on a landmarked townhouse is therefore a design question before it is a hardware question.

Newer doors are arriving too. The first phase of the Second Avenue Subway opened on January 1, 2017 with new stations at 72nd, 86th and 96th Streets, and the Furman Center counts 4,025 homes added in buildings of four or more units on the Upper East Side between 2010 and 2025, with permits for another 338 issued in 2025 alone. Those buildings arrive with steel unit doors, bored deadbolts and, often, an entry app chosen by the developer — a very different starting point from a 1910 co-op door three blocks west.

The neighbourhood's own crime stories point at the door, not the lobby. In September 2019 Patch reported Manhattan prosecutors' case against an 82-year-old man charged with eight counts of burglary after he slipped past doormen at buildings on East 79th and East 68th Streets and stole from apartments while residents were away on vacation; he was finally caught when a doorman on Third Avenue turned him away. That December, the 19th Precinct warned of a burglar climbing to fire escapes in the East 80s and 90s between Lexington and First Avenues and entering through unlocked windows during working hours. In March 2023 officers arrested a man found inside the vestibule of a four-storey Yorkville walk-up on East 82nd Street near East End Avenue, trying to kick in the inner door. A uniformed doorman, a vestibule and a locked street door all help; none of them replaces a properly hung, properly locked apartment door, and only one of those three stories was about a door at all.

Residents talk about the same things online. On the r/uppereastside forum, recent threads ask for a locksmith who can re-key Mul-T-Lock cylinders that came with a key-control card, whether anyone on the UES will copy a “do not duplicate” key, and what to do about a package thief working East 75th Street. Those questions tell us what people here already have on their doors — high-security, restricted-key cylinders in a lot of co-ops — and what they worry about in buildings without staff. A good smart lock plan keeps the first and helps with the second.

Upper East Side figures behind a smart lock plan

  • 224,027 residents (2024): the Furman Center's population estimate for the Upper East Side community district, fourth-largest of the city's 59.
  • 23.9 percent aged 65 and over: the share of residents in that age group in the Furman Center's 2024 figures — locks here often have to work for people who would rather not use an app.
  • 50.5 percent single-person households: half the neighbourhood lives alone, which makes a trusted second person on the lock's account more than a nicety.
  • 37.9 percent homeownership: above the city's 32.7 percent but still a minority, so plenty of keyless projects start with a renter's interior-only lock.
  • About 400 buildings in the Carnegie Hill Historic District alone, and 74 buildings added to the Upper East Side Historic District in 2010 — a large share of street doors here sit under Landmarks rules.
  • 8.8 serious crimes per 1,000 residents (2025) on the Furman Center's measure, against 12.3 citywide — a safer-than-average neighbourhood whose burglary stories still turn on unlocked windows and unattended apartments.

So the job on the Upper East Side is rarely “put a smart lock on the door.” It is: keep the board's key system and the hallway look intact on a co-op door; keep the Landmarks-protected face of a townhouse door untouched while the inside gets clever; give a household's staff exactly the doors and hours they need; and make a walk-up's street door close and lock behind the last person through it. The sections below take each of those in turn.

Keyless projects here often grow into the lobby. Co-op boards that start by approving one shareholder's unit lock frequently ask about the front entrance next; our Manhattan access control installers handle fob readers and controllers for building doors, and our intercom installation crew in Manhattan replaces worn lobby panels so the release and the unit lock fit one plan.

How we approach each type of door

Co-op unit doors. We start from the cylinder. Many co-ops have standardised on a patented, restricted-key system so keys cannot be copied at a hardware store, and the shareholder holds a card or certificate that controls duplication. The cleanest smart conversion keeps that cylinder exactly where it is: an interior unit that turns the existing thumb-turn, or a smart deadbolt body that accepts the building's cylinder where the manufacturer allows it. Consumer Reports makes the point that a retrofit smart lock's resistance to kicking, picking and drilling “is entirely dependent on the deadbolt it's paired with”, so on these doors a retrofit inherits the building's high-security hardware rather than replacing it with something weaker. We check the throw of the existing bolt, the clearance between the thumb-turn and the door's panel mouldings, and whether a mortise lock body below needs a different approach altogether.

Townhouse street doors. On a landmarked house the first decision is what not to touch. The exterior escutcheon, knob or lever, letter slot and knocker stay as they are, and the electronics go on the interior face, inside the vestibule, or on a secondary door that is not part of the protected facade. Where a keypad outside is genuinely wanted, we put the options and photographs together for the owner or their architect to take to the Commission rather than drilling first and asking later. Doors in these houses are often thick, hand-made and slightly out of square after a century of seasons, so we measure thickness, backset and bolt alignment at the door rather than trusting a spec sheet.

Townhouse secondary doors. The door under the stoop, the garden door and any roof bulkhead are where most keyless convenience actually goes. These doors are less visible, often plainer, and they are the ones household staff use every day. Keypads, card readers and app control on these doors give a family the benefits of a smart lock without asking anything of the street facade.

Walk-up building doors. On a five-unit building the street door does most of the security work, and it only works if it closes. We adjust or replace the closer so the door shuts and latches on its own from any opening angle, fit an electric strike or electrified lock with a keypad or fob reader, and leave mechanical exit from inside untouched. Unit doors stay with the tenants; where an owner wants to offer smart unit locks, they are offered, not required, and a working physical key still comes with every apartment.

Tower unit doors. These are the most straightforward doors on the Upper East Side to fit, and the most awkward to connect. Bored steel or solid-core doors take a smart deadbolt in the existing holes; the work is in placing a bridge or a Thread border router where the signal reaches through concrete, and in deciding whether the lock should be enrolled in any platform the building runs.

Codes and rules that bear on commercial doors here

  • Magnetic locks on egress doors. The NYC Building Code's section on electromagnetically locked egress doors (1010.1.9.9) allows them on doors to tenant spaces in several occupancy groups only with door-mounted release hardware that cuts power with one hand, unlocks when power is lost and is released by any panic hardware too. On a gallery's glass door that usually means a lever or bar with a built-in switch, not just a button on the wall.
  • Rated doors and frames. NFPA 80 limits job-site preparation of fire-rated assemblies to round holes; other cut-outs count as field modifications needing the listing laboratory's approval. A practice's corridor door in a residential building is often rated, so we match the strike or electrified lever to the label instead of cutting the frame.
  • Landmarks. A storefront inside a historic district is part of the designated property. The Commission's rules let hardware such as knobs and handles be repaired or replaced without approval, but readers, keypads and new holes on a protected facade are a different matter, so we look for an inside-the-glass or vestibule location first.
  • Accessibility. Operable hardware on a public entrance should not require tight grasping, pinching or twisting of the wrist, and is mounted between 34 and 48 inches above the floor under the 2010 ADA Standards; we place readers and push buttons with that in mind.
  • Apartments upstairs. Where the shop and the apartments share a street door, the residential side keeps its rights. The city's Housing Maintenance Code still requires each apartment entrance to have its own key lock, and tenants can add their own lock under the Multiple Dwelling Law; a shop's keypad never becomes the only way upstairs.

If a ground-floor tenant is also thinking about cameras or an alarm, our commercial security systems team for Manhattan plans the lock, the reader and the camera at the same walkthrough.

Your Questions Answered

Smart Lock Installation on the Upper East Side: Questions Answered

These are the questions that come from this neighbourhood's own doors: landmarked townhouses, pre-war co-ops with restricted keys, households that travel, walk-ups without staff, and practices and galleries on the ground floor. Borough-wide topics — renters' rights, co-op key rules, building entry platforms, office towers — are on our Manhattan smart lock installation page and the five-borough guide it links to. Cost, coverage and booking questions are in the FAQ near the bottom. Nothing here is legal advice.

Townhouses in the historic districts

Can the ornate brass knob and escutcheon on our townhouse door stay exactly as they are once the lock goes smart?

Yes, and on most landmarked houses that is the plan we would propose first. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's rules treat repairing or replacing ordinary door hardware — hinges, knobs, handles — as work that needs no approval, but they expressly exclude “ornate historic exterior hardware on special doors”. If your outside hardware is the decorative original, the safest course is to leave every piece of it in place and do all the smart work on the inside face of the door, where nobody on the sidewalk can see it.

In practice that means one of three things. If the door already has a separate single-cylinder deadbolt, an interior retrofit can turn its thumb-turn and leave the key cylinder outside untouched. If the only lock is an old mortise lockset, a mortise-format smart lock or kit can sometimes replace the inside works while keeping the outside trim. If neither suits, the smart lock can go on the vestibule door or the door under the stoop instead, and the street door stays a beautifully kept mechanical lock. We photograph the exterior before and after so the record shows nothing changed.

We employ a housekeeper, a nanny and a driver — should each of them have the townhouse's main door, or only the service entrance?

That is a household decision, but the lock can enforce whatever you decide, and most families end up separating the doors. A common arrangement is that staff who arrive daily use the entrance under the stoop or the garden door with a personal code or phone key, limited to their working days and hours, while the parlour-floor street door is kept for the family and guests. The driver, who mostly needs to drop bags and children, may only need a code during school runs.

Giving each person their own credential, rather than a shared “staff code”, is what makes the system useful. The entry log then says who opened which door and when, you can change one person's hours without touching anyone else's, and when someone leaves your employment their access ends with one tap instead of a locksmith visit. We set it up with you, name every credential by person, and leave a single sheet showing who can open what — worth keeping with the household's other employment papers.

Pre-war co-ops on Fifth, Madison and Park

We had to show a key card to get copies of our high-security key — does going smart weaken that duplication control?

Only if the smart lock throws the old cylinder away. Restricted-key systems such as the Mul-T-Lock and similar cylinders that residents on r/uppereastside ask about are valuable precisely because copies can only be cut against a registered card. A keypad deadbolt that replaces your cylinder with the manufacturer's own standard keyway quietly swaps that controlled key for one that any key shop can copy. Many boards also want the building's key system to stay on every unit door.

The way round it is to keep the cylinder. An interior retrofit mounts over the inside of your existing single-cylinder deadbolt and turns it, so the restricted key, its card and the outside of the door all stay exactly as they were. Some smart deadbolts can also take a compatible high-security cylinder in place of the standard one; whether yours can is a question for the lock maker and the cylinder supplier, and we confirm it before you buy. Either way, the backup key for the door remains the controlled one, and the codes and phone keys are added on top of it rather than instead of it.

Is a lock that clamps onto the inside of our deadbolt as strong as the deadbolt itself?

It is exactly as strong as the deadbolt, no more and no less. Consumer Reports puts it plainly in its lock-buying guide: a retrofit smart lock's resistance to kicking, picking and drilling “is entirely dependent on the deadbolt it's paired with”. The retrofit is a motor and a radio sitting on your thumb-turn; the bolt, the cylinder, the strike and the frame are what stop a door being forced.

On a good pre-war co-op door with a high-security cylinder and a full-length bolt, that is excellent news — you keep a strong lock and add convenience. On a tired door it means the smart part will not fix what is already weak. So we check that the bolt extends fully into the frame, that the strike is fixed into solid material with long screws, and that the door is not sagging so far that the bolt only just catches. If any of those are wrong, they get corrected first, because a motorised bolt grinding against a misaligned strike also flattens batteries in weeks instead of months.

Our pre-war apartment has a second door from the kitchen to the service hall — should it be smart as well?

Often it should, and it is frequently the more useful of the two. In many classic layouts the kitchen door opens onto a back stair or service elevator, and it is the door that deliveries, porters and household help used to use. If the front door becomes keyless and the kitchen door keeps an old key that has been copied for years, the weak point has simply moved to the back of the apartment.

Two rules govern what we fit there. First, the door must stay openable from inside in one easy motion without a key; the city's fire-safety guidance says apartment doors should open from inside without a key and bars double-cylinder locks, and a service door that leads to a stair is very likely to be someone's way out in an emergency. Second, it is the building's door frame and often a rated assembly, so the board or managing agent will want to know what is going on it. Within those rules, a keypad or retrofit on the service door with its own log and auto-lock is a sensible pairing with the front door, set to match the hours staff actually use it.

Decades of paint have built up on our pre-war door's edge — does that matter for a motorised bolt?

It matters more than most people expect. Layers of paint on the door edge, the strike and the frame stop narrow gaps closing, drag on the bolt as it travels and can pack the strike pocket so the bolt cannot extend all the way. A person turning a key feels that and pushes the door; a small motor simply strains, reports a jam or stops short, and drains its batteries doing it.

So on older co-op and townhouse doors the preparation is part of the job. We open the strike pocket to full depth, ease paint off the bolt path and the latch edge, check the hinges are tight and the door hangs evenly, and adjust the strike so the door latches without being leaned on. Where the building's painter is due, we ask that the hardware edges be masked next time. It is unglamorous work, and it is the reason a smart lock on a hundred-year-old door can run as smoothly as one on a new condo door.

Households that are often away

When we tell the front desk we're going away, should the cleaner's and walker's codes be paused too?

If nobody should be coming in while you are gone, yes. Most buildings have a routine for residents who are away — holding packages, watching for leaks — and the lock can follow the same routine. The case Manhattan prosecutors brought in 2019 against a man who walked past doormen on East 79th and East 68th Streets and stole from apartments while their residents were on vacation is a reminder that an empty apartment is what such a burglar looks for.

With a smart lock, “away” can be a setting rather than a hope. Before you leave, regular service codes are suspended for the dates you are gone, and only the people you choose — the person watering the plants on Thursdays, say — keep access, on their own schedule. Every opening sends you a notification wherever you are, and if a code is tried repeatedly the lock tells you that too. When you are back, the normal schedules resume without anyone needing to be re-enrolled. We set up the away pattern with you once, so it is a two-minute job each trip.

If I'm suddenly admitted to one of the York Avenue hospitals, how does a friend get in to feed the cat?

This is one of the most practical reasons people living alone on the Upper East Side choose a smart lock, and it works as long as two things are set up in advance. First, a second administrator: someone you trust, ideally nearby, who can issue a code or a phone key from their own phone if you cannot. Second, the lock has to be reachable remotely, through its own Wi-Fi, a bridge or a Thread border router that stays powered.

Then the sequence is simple. From a hospital bed, you or your second administrator create a code for your friend that works only for the days you are away; your friend lets themselves in; you see each visit in the app; the code expires on its own. There is no envelope of keys at the desk and no need for the building to open your door. If you would rather the desk did it, a sealed emergency code can also be lodged with the building under its rules. We write the plan on the handover sheet, including who the second administrator is.

Yorkville walk-ups and newer towers

As the owner of a small Yorkville walk-up, should the money go on the street door or on each apartment door?

For an owner, the street and vestibule doors come first. They are yours to control, they protect every tenant and the stair, and they are where trouble starts: in March 2023 police arrested a man inside the vestibule of a four-storey walk-up on East 82nd Street near East End Avenue who was trying to kick in the inner door. A street door that closes and latches by itself every time, with a good strike, a keypad or fob reader and a working intercom release, does more for the building than any single apartment lock.

Apartment doors are a different matter. City rules require the owner to provide a key lock on each unit entrance and let tenants add their own lock as well, and the Tenant Data Privacy Act stops owners from making a smart system the only way into a tenant's home. So smart unit locks are something to offer, not impose, and a physical key always comes with them. Our usual advice is to fix the building doors properly, then let tenants who want keyless entry add an interior lock on their own side.

People keep slipping into our walk-up behind residents — can the street door be made to close and lock faster?

Usually yes, and it is mostly mechanical. Tailgating depends on how long the door hangs open after a resident goes through. A closer that is set too slow, has lost its fluid or fights a sagging door lets the door drift for several seconds and often leaves it resting just short of latched. A correctly adjusted closer brings the door back firmly through its final few inches so the latch snaps home every time.

Then the lock helps. With an electric strike or electrified lock, the door relocks the moment it closes, and a door position contact can tell the owner if it has been propped or left ajar. Some keypads and readers can also log each entry, which makes a pattern of tailgating easier to see. What a lock cannot do is stop a resident holding the door for a stranger, so we suggest a short note to tenants once the new door is in. For package theft specifically, a vestibule that locks behind the courier — or a delivery code that only works on the outer door — is worth discussing at the walkthrough.

Burglars in the East 80s and 90s came in through fire-escape windows — does a smart front door do anything about that?

Not directly, and we would rather say so. In late 2019 the 19th Precinct described a burglar who reached fire escapes from the street or the roof between Lexington and First Avenues and tried windows until one was unlocked, during working hours. A smart lock on the apartment door would have been irrelevant to how he got in, although an entry log and an “unlocked” alert can at least show whether the door was used on the way out.

The fire-escape window needs its own answer. City fire-safety guidance prohibits key-locked gates on fire-escape windows, because those windows are a way out, so any gate there has to open from inside without one. A window sensor on a simple alarm, or a camera inside the apartment pointing at the window, adds warning without blocking the exit. When we visit for the door, we are happy to look at the fire-escape window too and tell you which of those our alarm installation team in Manhattan can add.

Practices and galleries on the ground floor

Our practice occupies the ground floor of a Park Avenue co-op — whose rules govern a keypad on the practice's own street entrance?

Several sets at once, which is why we plan these doors with the practice and the building together. The co-op's house rules and its alteration process apply because the door is part of the building; if the building sits within the Park Avenue Historic District or another district, any visible change to the entrance is a Landmarks matter too; and once a powered lock is on a door that patients use to leave, the city's building code on egress applies. A practice's lease may also say who may alter the entrance.

The design that usually satisfies everyone keeps the facade as it is and moves the technology inside: a reader or keypad just inside the street door or in the vestibule, an electric strike in the frame that matches the frame's label, a release button at reception and free exit through the door in one motion. Staff each get their own credential, after-hours openings are logged, and the medication or records room can have its own lock on a separate schedule. We put the drawings and specifications together for the board's alteration review so the practice is not left to explain hardware it did not choose.

Our Madison Avenue gallery keeps borrowed works in a back room — can that door keep its own record of every opening?

Yes. The front door of a gallery and the room where works on loan or on consignment are kept do different jobs, and they should be on separate credentials. The front door opens for staff in the morning and closes behind the last visitor; the back-room door is opened by far fewer people, and every opening is worth knowing about. A keypad or card lock on that door, with its own user list and audit trail, gives the director a record of who went in and when.

We set the back-room door to relock automatically, alert the director if it is held open, and keep its credentials separate from the general staff code, so a part-time gallery assistant can open the shop without having access to the store. Where a lender or the gallery's insurer asks how access to stored works is controlled, the log and the user list answer the question. If the room has a solid or rated door, the lock is chosen to match it rather than the other way round.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Smart Lock Installation on the Upper East Side

A note on method: the tools used to research this page could not display Google's AI Overview, so none of the claims below is quoted from it. Each heading paraphrases a statement published by a national cost guide, a lock maker or a consumer publication — the sort of line answer engines repeat — and the source is named underneath, followed by what changes on an Upper East Side door.

“Expect to pay $250 to $400 for a smart lock installation”

Fixr's national cost guide, last updated in January 2025, gives a range of $250 to $400 with an average around $300, and handyman labour at $50 to $100 an hour. Angi, HomeAdvisor and similar sites publish ranges in the same territory. Those figures are honest averages for a standard bored deadbolt on a suburban house.

A townhouse on East 70th Street is rarely one door, and a co-op on Park Avenue is rarely one visit. A typical townhouse plan touches the street door, the door under the stoop and the garden door; a co-op job adds the board's paperwork and a slot inside the building's working hours. Walk-up street doors and practice entrances are electric-strike jobs, which AESS publishes at $1,875 to $3,125 at the Manhattan rate.

None of that makes the national figure wrong. It makes it the price of a different job. If a quote for an Upper East Side door starts from it, ask what happens to the paperwork, the second door and the old paint on the frame.

“A retrofit leaves the outside of your door untouched”

That is August's description of its interior-mounted locks, and it is accurate — which is why we recommend retrofits so often on landmarked townhouses and on co-op doors with restricted keys. Nothing changes on the corridor or street face, and your existing key carries on working.

What the line leaves out is that a retrofit is only as strong as what it is fitted to. Consumer Reports notes that a retrofit's resistance to kicking, picking and drilling depends entirely on the deadbolt beneath it. August also says its retrofits suit single-cylinder deadbolts only, and some need clearance around the thumb-turn that a panelled pre-war door may not offer.

So “untouched” outside does not mean “nothing to check” inside. We look at the bolt, the strike, the panel mouldings and the swing before recommending one.

“A bridge a few steps from the lock is all remote access needs”

August's support pages ask for its Connect bridge to sit within 10 to 15 feet of the lock and to see a strong Wi-Fi signal where it is plugged in. In an open-plan condo that is a matter of finding a socket in the hall.

Classic Upper East Side apartments are long, with thick plaster walls, a front door at the far end of a gallery and the router often in a back bedroom; a townhouse's service door may be a floor below the nearest outlet. The bridge's location becomes a small design exercise, and sometimes a reason to choose a Thread lock with a border router instead.

We test remote unlocking over mobile data, away from the apartment's Wi-Fi, before we pack up. If it works from the street, it will work from another country.

“Matter makes every smart lock work with every ecosystem”

Matter is a real step forward. Lock makers describe Thread as a low-power, self-healing mesh and say a Matter lock can be shared with up to five ecosystems at once. That is useful in a household where one person uses an iPhone and another uses Google.

The fine print matters. Yale's set-up guide says a Thread border router has to be in place first and that the router should match the app you are using — a Nest Hub for Google Home, a HomePod mini or Apple TV for Apple Home. Not every feature in a maker's own app is available through every ecosystem, either.

For a family with staff, we normally keep the maker's app as the place where codes and schedules live and add Matter for voice and automation on top, rather than relying on a single ecosystem to do everything.

“Batteries last up to six months”

Schlage quotes up to six months of battery life with typical use for its Encode Wi-Fi deadbolts. Other makers quote similar or longer figures, and they are fair for a family front door used a handful of times a day.

A townhouse service door used by a housekeeper, a nanny, a dog walker and several deliveries is not typical use, and neither is a pre-war door whose bolt drags on old paint. Both use the motor more, and both run batteries down faster than the box suggests.

We set low-battery alerts to reach two people, fix the alignment so the motor is not straining, and agree a date-based battery change for busy doors rather than waiting for the warning.

“Installing a smart lock is an easy DIY job”

Product reviewers often say a retrofit needs a screwdriver and half an hour, and on a modern door in a house that can be true. The hardware is designed to be fitted by its owner.

On the Upper East Side the half hour is the easy part. A co-op wants to know who is working on the unit door and whether they are insured. A landmarked townhouse owner needs to know whether a planned change counts as ordinary repair or needs the Commission. A walk-up owner has egress and self-closing duties on the street door. None of those appear in the box.

Many residents do fit their own retrofit, and we are glad to set up or tidy one afterwards at our programming rate. The judgement calls are where a licensed installer earns the fee.

“Connect your smart lock to your alarm system for full protection”

National brands such as ADT, Ring and SimpliSafe all market lock integration. Ring, for example, says compatible Z-Wave locks can arm and disarm Ring Alarm, but its support page also says a subscription is required and that only codes created in the Ring app work with the feature.

That can suit a single-family house well. For a co-op apartment or townhouse with staff, it can mean the lock's codes have to be managed in the alarm company's app and depend on its plan, and that the people who install it will not deal with your board, your Landmarks question or your building's key system.

If you want lock and alarm linked, we choose hardware that keeps codes under your control first, then connect it. A local, licensed crew that knows the neighbourhood's buildings is the part a national brand cannot ship.

Glossary

Upper East Side Door Terms, Explained

  • Parlour floor: the raised main floor of a townhouse, reached by the stoop; its street door is usually the most decorative and the most likely to be a protected feature.
  • Under-stoop entrance: the door at street level beneath the stoop, historically the service entrance, and today the door most households use every day.
  • Areaway: the sunken space in front of a townhouse's under-stoop entrance, usually behind a low iron gate.
  • Vestibule: the small lobby between a walk-up's street door and its inner door, where the mailboxes and intercom panel tend to sit.
  • Special door: in the Landmarks rules, a door distinguished by fine craftsmanship, distinctive materials such as bronze or carved wood, unique form or age; its ornate exterior hardware is not covered by the no-approval repair provision.
  • Permit for Minor Work: the Landmarks Preservation Commission's staff-level permit for exterior work that does not need a Buildings Department permit and is restorative or appropriate — door replacement in an existing opening is one of its examples.
  • Restricted or high-security keyway: a patented cylinder whose keys can only be cut by authorised dealers against a registered card, common on co-op unit doors.
  • Retrofit lock: a smart lock that mounts over the inside of an existing deadbolt and turns it, leaving the outside and the key unchanged.
  • Mortise lock: a boxed mechanism that sits inside a slot chiselled into the edge of a thick door, with one faceplate holding both latch and bolt — what most heavy pre-war and townhouse doors on these blocks were built with.
  • Backset: how far in from the edge of the door the lock's main hole is centred; makers of smart deadbolts usually support the two common sizes, 2-3/8 and 2-3/4 inches, and old doors sometimes match neither.
  • Service door: in a pre-war apartment, the second door from the kitchen to the back stair or service elevator.
  • Thread border router: a hub or smart speaker inside the home that links a Thread lock to the internet.
  • Request-to-exit: a button or sensor that tells a powered lock someone is leaving, so the door releases for them.

Field Notes

Field Notes From Upper East Side Doorways

How our installers work an old Upper East Side door — general practice, not an account of one job

On a townhouse we look at the outside before we touch the inside. We photograph the street face of the door, the escutcheon, the knocker and the letter slot from the sidewalk, because the most important promise on a landmarked house is that none of it will look different when we leave.

Then we open the door and watch it close. A century-old door that has to be lifted or leaned on to latch will defeat any motor, so hinges get tightened, the strike gets moved and the paint comes off the bolt path before a lock leaves its box.

On a co-op door the first thing we ask for is the key. If it is a restricted, card-controlled key, the plan is built around keeping that cylinder, not replacing it.

Finally we sit down with whoever will run the lock — often not the owner, but the house manager or a grown child — and set up each person's code together, so the plan is theirs before we go.

Will you service a smart lock that another company installed in our apartment?
+

Yes. We diagnose and repair locks fitted by others, including retrofits residents put in themselves. If the lock only needs re-pairing or new codes, that is a programming visit at $265 for the first hour; if hardware has to be corrected or replaced, we quote it before doing the work.

Our elevator opens onto a landing we share with just one neighbour — does our apartment door still need a proper lock?
+

Yes. A semi-private landing is still common space: building staff, the neighbour's household and their visitors can all reach it. Your apartment door should lock like any other unit door, and a smart lock there adds an entry log and auto-lock without changing the building's elevator controls.

Can the lock's app account be set up in the name of our estate manager or family office?
+

It can, but we recommend the owner holds the primary administrator account and the estate manager or family office is added as a second administrator. That way access survives a change of staff or advisers, and you can always remove anyone without losing control of your own door.

Do you install locks that open with a tap of an iPhone or Apple Watch?
+

Yes. Some smart deadbolts support Apple Home Key, which unlocks the door with a tap of an iPhone or Apple Watch; Schlage lists it on its Encode Plus, for example. It works alongside codes and keys, so people without an iPhone still get in, and it needs an Apple home hub set up in the home.

What happens to our codes if the lock's maker shuts down its cloud service?
+

Codes stored in the lock itself and Bluetooth unlocking from a phone at the door generally keep working without the maker's servers, while remote access, alerts and app-based changes may stop. We favour locks that keep codes on the device and support Matter, which allows local control through more than one home platform.

Our building supplies Wi-Fi to every apartment — can the lock use it, or do we need our own?
+

Sometimes. Building-wide Wi-Fi often isolates devices or asks for a sign-in page that locks and bridges cannot complete, and some locks only join 2.4 GHz networks. We test it at the door; if it will not hold the lock reliably, a Thread lock with a border router or a small router of your own is the dependable option.
