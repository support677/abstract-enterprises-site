New York School Camera Law, Applied in Putnam

Facial Recognition: Off the Table Everywhere

The State Education Department’s September 2023 determination binds every school in New York — district, charter, parish, independent, and residential program alike: facial-recognition technology may not be purchased or operated. Haldane’s river village and the biggest district in the county answer to the identical rule. Conventional video was never restricted — cameras, recording, motion analytics all remain fully lawful — and every system we deliver ships with face-matching disabled at the firmware level, that state documented in the commissioning file so the question stays answered.

Lawful Coverage and the Felony Line

Recording in bathrooms, changing areas, residential living quarters, or any space carrying a reasonable expectation of privacy is a felony under Penal Law 250.45, and schools get no exemption. Everything else is lawful ground: entrances, corridors, stairs, lunchrooms, gyms, lots, driveways, fields, playgrounds, barns and paddocks, mechanical rooms, perimeter. Classroom interiors we route to your board or director rather than defaulting into — our standing county drawing covers each classroom doorway from the corridor, entries and exits logged, no lens inside the room, with any deliberate exception initialed on the plan. On residential program campuses, coverage stops at living-space thresholds, drawn precisely and documented for licensors and counsel.

When Footage Joins a Student’s Record

FERPA can pull identifiable video into a student’s education record once it informs a disciplinary decision — bringing parental viewing rights and disclosure limits with it — and New York’s Ed Law 2-d imposes binding contract terms on any outside vendor holding student-identifiable data. For residential and special-education programs, state licensing reviewers add their own documentation expectations on top. Our default architecture keeps all of it manageable: recorder on premises, drives on the school’s inventory, a written review-and-release procedure at handover, off-site copies only under a 2-d-conforming agreement — so the eventual hard conversation opens a binder instead of a scramble.

Who Pays for the Work, and What Each Program Demands

District buildings tap the state’s $2 billion Smart Schools bond, which funds camera projects so long as facial recognition never enters scope. The county’s nonpublic and residential-program schools use NPSE reimbursement where eligible, with federal nonprofit-security and school-safety grants stacking above and importing the §889 hardware exclusions when they do. Small schools rarely have a grants office — so the proposal we deliver is already formatted to the target program’s checklist, itemized and forwardable by whoever wears that hat this year.

Field Notes: A Farm Campus, Twenty-Two Cameras, and the Four-Minute Find

Twenty-two cameras at a residential special-education campus in the eastern county — a farm-based program where barns, paddocks, and animal facilities are part of the school day, and where a portion of the students live on grounds. The director’s brief had two halves that pulled against each other. Half one: her population includes elopement risk, and the state reviewers who license residential programs expect an institution to account for a student’s movements across a big, open, wooded property. Half two: this was a home as much as a school — living quarters lawfully and rightly off-limits to any lens, therapy-animal spaces to be treated with respect, and nothing about the system allowed to make the campus feel watched in the wrong way. Most vendors had answered one half and failed the other. The design work was making both true at once.

We drew it from the thresholds outward. Every building entrance and exit covered — dorm-wing doors included, lenses facing the doorway from outside, never into living space, each position photographed and initialed on the plan for the licensing file. Corridors in the school and program buildings ran unbroken. Outside, the grounds got a connective tissue of coverage: the paths between buildings, the barn aisle and paddock gates, the pond approach, the treeline fence where the property met state land, and the long gravel entrance drive back to the road. Classification was tuned across two evenings because this property’s wildlife traffic was the heaviest we’d calibrated all year — deer in numbers, turkeys, a coyote with a schedule — until the animal stream went silent and only people and vehicles rang through. The office got the one-page operating card; three staff members rehearsed the export drill; the reviewer got a documentation packet that mapped every lens to a written reason.

The system earned its keep on a November evening, eight weeks in. A resident student slipped out a dorm-wing door at dusk — the door event and doorway camera logged it the moment it happened — and the on-duty staff had the grounds view open inside a minute. The timeline told the story in order: down the path past the barn, along the paddock fence, toward the pond approach, where the treeline camera held him in frame, stationary and safe. Staff reached him in under four minutes, calm, coat around his shoulders, walked back before full dark. No injury, no search party, no flashing lights — and afterward, an incident report built from an exact timeline instead of estimates, which the state reviewer read and closed without a follow-up visit. The director’s note to us said the part worth repeating: the system found him fast, and it did it without ever pointing a camera where a camera didn’t belong. That balance was the entire assignment.

Nowhere in the county, or the state. Regular video sits outside the order entirely; cameras, recorders, and motion analytics stay fully legal. We ship every system with face-matching dead at the firmware level and log that fact in your commissioning paperwork.

Can cameras go inside classrooms?

Legally, usually yes; by our default, no. That call belongs to your board or director. Which spaces can never be recorded?

Bathrooms, changing areas, residential living quarters, and any space with a reasonable expectation of privacy — recording there is a felony under Penal Law 250.45, with no school exemption.
