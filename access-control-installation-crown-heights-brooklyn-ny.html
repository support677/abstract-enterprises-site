The Neighborhood

Why Crown Heights Properties Need Access Control

Crown Heights runs from Washington Avenue east to Ralph Avenue and from Atlantic Avenue south to Empire Boulevard, a little over a square mile and a half split between two community boards and two police precincts, the 77th on Utica Avenue to the north and the 71st on Empire Boulevard to the south. What matters for a door, though, is not the map but the way people share buildings. The neighborhood holds very few single-entrance houses with one family and one key. It holds prewar apartment houses with a hundred or more households and a single lobby, row houses divided into flats a century ago, storefronts with apartments above them on Franklin, Nostrand, Kingston and Utica, and a wave of new construction coming to Atlantic Avenue. Every one of those has a door that many people use, and that is the problem access control is built to solve.

The most common complaint we hear from Crown Heights tenants and managers is not a break-in in the old sense. It is the stranger who walks in through a door that somebody opened for them. In August 2025 News 12 Brooklyn showed security video from 240 Crown Street, a building of about 135 apartments, in which a man pushing a small child in a stroller was let in by someone at the door, waited in the lobby until it was quiet, and took two packages off a pile on the lobby floor. One resident described it as "good intentions, bad results." A few years earlier CrownHeights.info reported two thieves loading "dozens of packages left unattended" from the lobby of a building on Brooklyn Avenue into bags before a resident came downstairs, filmed them, and they rode away on electric bikes. On the neighborhood's own Reddit forum, a thread about package theft in large prewar buildings says much the same thing in residents' words: random people get buzzed in and walk out with packages.

The second complaint is the door itself. In June 2022 tenants of a 60-apartment complex on Lefferts Avenue rallied over defective locks on the doors into the rest of the building, a month after a robbery in the front vestibule. "That front door is not secured properly," one tenant told Patch, and a woman who had lived there for about forty years added that the frame could be kicked open. The landlord's staff said locksmiths were sent whenever young people broke the locks. That pattern, a lock replaced again and again in a door that no longer closes true, is the one we see most often in Crown Heights' older buildings, and no reader or fob fixes it by itself. HPD treats a building door that fails to close and latch on its own as a Class C, immediately hazardous condition, and in fiscal year 2025 the agency issued 72,714 self-closing door violations across the city.

The third thing that shapes this work is who is allowed to decide. A large share of Crown Heights' apartment buildings are rent-stabilized, and New York's housing agency has for years attached conditions when owners switch tenants from keys to fobs: fobs free to lawful occupants, extra fobs for caregivers and regular visitors, and limits on what personal information can be collected. Since 2021 the city's Tenant Data Privacy Law has added written policies and data limits for any building that uses fobs, apps or other smart access. A row-house owner in the Crown Heights North Historic District, meanwhile, answers to the Landmarks Preservation Commission for anything mounted on the front of the house. None of this makes access control hard to install. It makes it a job where the paperwork and the people come first and the hardware second.

And the building stock is about to change. In May 2025 the City Council approved the Atlantic Avenue Mixed-Use Plan, which rezones 21 blocks of Crown Heights and Bedford-Stuyvesant that had been held since the 1960s to one- and two-story industrial and storage buildings. The plan allows about 4,600 new apartments, 1,900 of them permanently affordable, and requires active ground floors with shops or other commercial use. A few blocks south, the old Bedford Union Armory reopened in 2021 as the Major R. Owens Health and Wellness Community Center, part of a project that includes 415 apartments. Thousands of new households will soon share lobbies, package rooms, bike rooms and garages in this neighborhood, and the cheapest time to decide how those doors work is before the walls close.

Two-door lobby control

Most prewar lobbies on Crown Street, Montgomery Street and Lefferts Avenue already have the two doors; they just are not working as a pair.

A station or directory panel at the street door that lets a resident see and speak to a visitor before releasing the door, plus a fob reader in the same housing. Calls go to tenants' phones, to an indoor screen, or to a landline for residents who do not use smartphones.

A keypad or slim reader at the parlor-floor stoop door and a second at the garden door under the stoop, with an electric strike in each frame and separate codes for the owners and for each apartment. Building Specialists

Access Control for Crown Heights' Architecture

Wikipedia's account of the Crown Heights North Historic District alone lists rowhouses, townhouses, two-family houses, semi-attached and freestanding houses, flats, apartment buildings and institutional buildings, built over almost ninety years. Across the whole neighborhood we sort them into six kinds of entrance, and each one changes where the lock goes, where the cable runs and who holds the administrator login.

Prewar elevator apartment houses

Five- and six-story buildings from the 1910s to the 1930s on the side streets between Eastern Parkway and Empire Boulevard and along the parkway itself, often with a hundred or more apartments, a deep vestibule, mailboxes inside the inner door and a cellar laundry. The street door, inner door, cellar door and roof door are the usual four.

Walk-ups and courtyard buildings

Smaller four- and five-story buildings, sometimes set around a courtyard with a gate at the sidewalk. The courtyard gate becomes the first door, the building door the second, and the stair to the roof the one that must never be locked from inside.

Single-family row houses

Limestone, brownstone and brick houses in the historic districts north of Eastern Parkway and on blocks south of it, one household, a stoop door to the parlor floor and a garden door below. One controller, two doors, and the old key kept as a backup.

Row houses divided into apartments

The same houses split into two, three or four flats, sometimes decades ago. One street door, several households, bell buttons added one tenant at a time and a key that has been copied for every one of them. Individual codes or fobs per apartment end that.

Stores with apartments above

Commercial-residential buildings on the avenues, many from the late 1880s to the early 1930s, with a shop on the ground floor and a separate street door to the apartments. Two systems, or one system with two administrators, so the shop and the tenants never share codes.

New mid-rises and towers

Buildings finished in recent years near Atlantic Avenue, the Armory and the Botanic Garden, and the ones still coming under the 2025 rezoning: glass storefront entrances, package rooms, amenity floors, garages and hundreds of users, run on networked readers and phone credentials.

What the materials mean for the install

Limestone and brownstone facades are the most delicate surfaces in the neighborhood, and the LPC Permit Guidebook is clear that equipment should not go on or in front of the primary facade if there is another option, and that cable should be concealed and run through plain masonry rather than decorative stone or ironwork. In practice that means the reader goes on the return of the doorway or inside the vestibule, the cable drops straight into the cellar behind the stoop, and nothing is drilled through carved stone. Brick on the side streets is simpler: fixings go into mortar joints, so a device removed years later leaves a joint to repoint, not a broken brick.

The doors themselves are the bigger variable. Prewar apartment buildings often still have their original heavy wood or steel street doors in frames that have been bored for four or five generations of locks; before a strike goes in we fill or replace the damaged section of jamb so the strike has solid material to hold. Original glazed inner doors with brass hardware can take a strike in the frame without the door being touched. New buildings bring the opposite problem: aluminum storefront doors with stiles too narrow for a conventional lock, which is why the lock in those buildings usually lives in the panic bar or the frame header.

The Door Somebody Opens for You: Courtesy Entry and the Two-Door Lobby

Look closely at the two Crown Heights package thefts above and neither one involved breaking anything. On Crown Street, according to the News 12 report, someone simply let the man in. On Brooklyn Avenue the report does not say how the pair got into the lobby at all. That is typical of large buildings everywhere, and it is why the first question we ask a board or a managing agent is not "which reader?" but "what happens after someone gets through the street door?"

In most of the big prewar buildings here, the answer used to be built into the architecture. A street door opens into a vestibule, the mailboxes sit in the vestibule or just beyond it, and a second, glazed door separates the vestibule from the lobby and the stairs. Over decades the inner door is propped, its closer fails, its lock is taken out because tenants kept getting stuck, and the building ends up with one door doing the job of two. Restoring that second door, with its own reader and its own closer, means a stranger who talks his way past the first door, or follows a neighbor through it, is standing in a small, well-lit space with a camera on him and nowhere to go. A resident who opens the street door out of kindness has not opened the building.

Where the packages go is the second half of the answer. A pile of boxes on the lobby floor is visible from the street through the door glass and reachable by anyone who gets inside. We would rather see deliveries go to a room off the vestibule that couriers can reach with a time-limited code and residents open with their own fob, so the lobby proper is never part of a courier's route. Where a building has no spare room, a lockable cabinet inside the vestibule, or a closet under the stair, does the same job at smaller scale. None of this needs a security guard, and none of it relies on residents remembering not to hold the door.

There is a human side we do not pretend hardware can fix. People hold doors for neighbors with groceries, for parents with strollers, for older tenants with canes, and they should. What a good door system does is make that kindness cheap. If the inner door has its own reader, holding the street door costs nothing. If the video panel lets a resident see who is calling before buzzing, saying no is easy. And if the building's written door policy, which the city's privacy law already requires an owner to give tenants, also says plainly that deliveries go to the package room and visitors are buzzed in by the person they are visiting, everyone has something to point to.

Closer, Frame, Then Reader: Why the Door Has to Pass HPD Before the Electronics Matter

HPD's guidance on self-closing doors is short and unforgiving. Apartment doors, hallway doors and stairwell doors in residential buildings must close and latch on their own, and inspectors check building entrances too. A defect that stops a door from closing itself is a Class C, immediately hazardous violation; the owner has 14 days to fix it and certify the repair, and if the owner does not, HPD can do the work itself and bill the property. The agency says it issued 72,714 self-closing door violations in fiscal year 2025 and spent more than $6.7 million on repairs owners left undone. It also picks about 300 buildings a year for proactive self-closing inspections.

For access control that rule is a gift, because the condition that earns the violation is exactly the condition that makes a fob reader useless. A reader on a door that does not latch is a decoration. So every Crown Heights job starts at the hinges. We close the door ten or a dozen times from different angles and speeds, watch whether the latch catches every time, check the closer's arm and the speed and latching adjustments, look at whether the door has dropped and rubs the frame, and press on the strike side of the jamb to see whether the wood is still sound. On the Lefferts Avenue complex in the Patch report, tenants said locks would not be secure until the whole door was replaced, and that the frame could be kicked. When we see a frame like that, we say so in writing and price the frame or door repair as its own line, before any electronics.

There is a second link between the two. A door with an electric strike that latches every time, a closer that is adjusted rather than wedged, and a door position contact that reports when the door has been held open gives an owner something HPD-related work rarely does: a record. The system's log shows when the street door was propped and for how long, which makes it far easier to find the cause, whether a tenant moving furniture, a contractor or a closer that has started to fail, before an inspector does.

We do not file with HPD, certify violations or speak for the agency. What we hand over is documentation: photos of the door before and after, the hardware installed, and the settings, so whoever certifies the correction has what they need.

Fobs in a Rent-Stabilized Crown Heights Building: What Owners Have Been Told to Provide

This section is not legal advice, and we are not lawyers; it summarizes what published rulings and the city's own page say, so that an owner planning a change and a tenant receiving one start from the same facts. The details of any building's case belong to the owner's attorney, the tenants' attorney and the agency.

The state's housing agency, DHCR, has repeatedly approved owners' plans to replace key locks with fobs and old intercoms with newer systems, but with conditions. In one 2017 administrative review, summarized by Landlord v. Tenant, an owner wanted to replace a wired intercom with a phone-based intercom and the building's key lock with fobs. DHCR approved the change and then rejected the owner's appeal of two conditions: under the agency's standard policy, tenants could receive up to four additional fobs at no charge for employees, such as a home attendant, or for guests who visit regularly or help care for the tenant or the apartment; and because the new intercom needed a touch-tone landline, the owner had to give tenants a permanent $15 monthly rent reduction to offset the line's cost, unless it later installed wireless units that removed that cost. Brick Underground's 2018 summary of other DHCR rulings added that fobs must be free to lawful occupants, that the charge for a lost fob is capped, and that the switch cannot be used to collect new identifying information from tenants.

The city's Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, sits on top of that. HPD's page explains that owners of buildings using smart access, which includes key fobs, cards, apps and biometrics, must get tenants' consent, give them a written privacy and data retention policy, collect only a short list of data, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, never sell it, and never use it to track tenants outside the building, to harass, or to monitor relationships. The owner also cannot require smart access to get into a tenant's own apartment. Elsewhere in Manhattan, a 2019 lawsuit over a keyless entry system ended with the landlord agreeing to give the tenants who sued physical keys, a reminder that a credential tenants cannot use is a credential that ends up in court.

What this means for the install is concrete. We set up the system so that it can produce the number of fobs each apartment is entitled to without anyone fighting about it, so that the fields it stores are the minimum the law allows, so that logs are deleted on a schedule rather than kept forever, and so that a resident who cannot or will not use a phone still has a fob and, where the building requires it, a working key path. We give the owner a plain-language description of what the system records and for how long, which becomes the basis of the policy the owner hands tenants. And if a tenant association asks us what a fair system looks like, we will describe the same thing to them.

Four Thousand Six Hundred New Apartments on Atlantic Avenue: Decide the Doors on the Drawings

The stretch of Atlantic Avenue the Council rezoned in May 2025 has, in 6sqft's description, been dominated for decades by vacant parking lots, self-storage buildings and auto shops, because zoning since the 1960s allowed only one- and two-story industrial and storage buildings there. The new rules allow about 4,600 apartments across 21 blocks and require active ground floors. That combination, residential floors above shops, workshops or community space, with a busy avenue on one side and residential side streets on the other, is the hardest kind of building to secure as an afterthought and one of the easiest to secure on paper.

On paper means a door schedule. Before the electrical drawings are finished, every door that will be controlled is listed with what it does: the residential street door and vestibule, the package room, the bike room, the trash and recycling room, the garage door and its pedestrian door, the roof, the amenity spaces, the stairs that must always open from inside, and every ground-floor commercial door. For each one we note the lock type, whether it fails secure or fails safe in a power cut, whether the fire alarm releases it, the cable and conduit it needs, and who will administer it. Ground-floor tenants who sign their leases later are given their own administrator account and their own door group on the same platform, so the residential manager never has to issue a shop employee a fob, and the shop never sees the residents' list.

The Bedford Union Armory project a few blocks south shows why the planning matters. 6sqft described 415 apartments there, about 250 of them affordable to households at or below 60 percent of area median income and more than 160 at market rate, sharing a site with a 60,000-square-foot community center that has a pool, basketball courts, a soccer field and space for nonprofits. When housing and a public-facing facility share a block, the line between public and private is drawn by doors, and the decisions about those doors last as long as the building. Making them early costs a meeting; making them after move-in costs core drilling.

For new buildings we also plan for what HPD-marketed and lottery buildings go through in their first year: hundreds of households moving in over a few months, credentials issued in batches as leases begin, and privacy paperwork that has to be ready before the first fob is handed out.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control in Crown Heights

Wikipedia names Franklin, Nostrand, Kingston and Utica Avenues and Atlantic Avenue as Crown Heights' commercial streets. Most of the businesses on them share a building with apartments, and that single fact drives almost every commercial job here: the shop's doors and the tenants' door have to be controlled separately, by different people, with neither able to open the other's.

Franklin Avenue

Brownstoner's history of Franklin at Park Place calls it the main commercial street of that part of Crown Heights, lined with commercial-residential buildings from the late 1880s to the early 1930s. Today it is restaurants, bars, cafes and shops, which means staff doors that open before dawn and close after midnight, cellar stairs shared between neighbors, and back gardens behind the dining rooms.

Nostrand Avenue

Groceries, takeout counters, salons and small offices under apartments, from Atlantic down past Eastern Parkway. The requests are a staff keypad on the back or cellar door, a reader on a stockroom that holds cash or stock, and a clean separation from the residential street door next to the shopfront.

Kingston Avenue

A busy shopping block close to Eastern Parkway with stores, offices and institutions on the same buildings' ground and upper floors. Upstairs offices with a shared stair, rooms used by many people for a few hours, and doors whose hours change through the week and the year are the common jobs.

Utica Avenue

The eastern spine of the neighborhood, with the 77th Precinct's station house at 127 Utica and the Crown Heights–Utica Avenue subway station. Barbershops, beauty supply stores, clinics and daycares in mixed-use buildings ask for staff-only doors and entries that let parents or patients in without letting anyone wander upstairs.

Atlantic Avenue

Auto shops, self-storage, wholesalers and parking lots today, and under the 2025 plan a strip of new buildings with required active ground floors. Bay doors with a personnel door beside them, yards behind fences, and, increasingly, commercial spaces at the base of residential buildings.

Shuls, churches and community spaces

Houses of worship of every size are spread through the neighborhood, many in converted row houses or storefronts. In 2022 the Community Security Initiative offered small shuls in Crown Heights and other Brooklyn neighborhoods new doors, locks and cameras followed by access control training, so many of these doors are now being upgraded.

Two businesses, one cellar: the shared-space problem on the avenues

The single most common commercial puzzle in Crown Heights is a space two parties need. A restaurant and the apartments above share a cellar where the boiler and the meters sit. A bar and a salon share a back yard. A grocery's cellar is reached through steel doors in the sidewalk that the landlord's porter also uses. The old answer was a key everyone had copies of, which meant nobody could ever say who had been down there. The better answer is one controlled door with two or three groups of users on it, each group administered by its own party, and a record that shows which group opened it and when. Where a landlord holds an emergency right of entry, the landlord gets a credential that works at any hour and shows up in the log as the landlord, which tends to settle arguments rather than start them.

The second puzzle is hours. A cafe opens at six, a bar closes at four, a salon works by appointment, and a daycare has drop-off and pickup windows. Each staff door can follow its own business's schedule, with an opener's code that works from early morning and a closer's code that works until the night ends, and with deliveries given a time-limited code for the stockroom only. The residential door upstairs keeps its own schedule, which, in a building with apartments, is locked all day every day.

Rules that apply to Crown Heights shop, office and mixed-use doors

  • The way out always works. The NYC Building Code requires an electrically locked door on an exit path to unlock when it loses power and when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate, and a magnetic lock to be released by hardware on the door that works with one hand. Customers and staff leave without a key, card or code, every time.
  • Equipment listed for the job. Locks and controllers on exit doors should carry a UL 294 access control listing. We use listed equipment and keep the listing sheets in the job file for the landlord, the insurer or an inspector.
  • The apartments' street door stays locked. Under Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a, entrance doors of class A multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968 must be self-closing and self-locking and kept locked except when an attendant is on duty, and buildings with eight or more apartments need an intercom with voice and a remote release. Older buildings must install them when a majority of tenants ask in writing. A shop cannot put the residential door on a daytime unlock schedule.
  • Smart access in buildings with homes. Where apartments share a door that uses fobs, cards or an app, the city's Tenant Data Privacy Law applies to that door: consent, a written policy, minimal data and deletion of authentication data within 90 days unless anonymized. A shop's own staff door is not covered by that law, but a shared residential entrance is.
  • Landmarked storefronts. In a historic district the LPC reviews visible changes to the facade, and its guidebook prefers equipment that is discreet and off the primary facade where possible, with cable concealed and fixings kept out of decorative surfaces.
  • A licensed installer. New York State requires a Department of State license for businesses that install, service or maintain security systems for others. AESS works under license #12000287431.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Crown Heights: Questions Answered

Large and rent-stabilized apartment buildings

The landlord's new lobby panel rings apartments over a phone line, and several of us had already cancelled our landlines. Who is supposed to carry that cost in a stabilized building?

The published rulings point toward the owner. In a 2017 case summarized by Landlord v. Tenant, DHCR approved an owner's switch to a telephone-based intercom and fobs, but required a permanent $15 monthly rent reduction because the new system needed tenants to keep a touch-tone landline, and said the owner could ask again only if it installed wireless units that removed the landline cost. Your building's own case depends on what the owner filed and what DHCR approved, so the place to raise it is with DHCR or a tenant attorney, not with the installer.

From the installation side, the landline problem is optional. Most current panels can call residents' mobile phones through an app or a regular phone number, ring an indoor screen in the apartment, or ring a landline for residents who keep one. If we are designing the system, we choose a panel that can do all three, so nobody is forced to pay for a line they do not want and nobody is forced onto a smartphone they do not use.

My mother's home attendant comes six days a week. In a rent-stabilized building, can the landlord refuse her a fob or charge for one?

The same 2017 DHCR ruling is the closest published guide. The agency's standard policy, as Landlord v. Tenant reported it, lets tenants receive up to four additional fobs at no charge for employees or for people who visit regularly or help care for the tenant or the apartment, and employee fobs can be set to expire and be renewed at the tenant's option. A home attendant fits squarely in that description. The owner may set reasonable rules for others. Again, the final word on any one building belongs to DHCR.

What we do on our side is make the right answer easy. An attendant's fob can be set to work only on the days and hours of her shift, can show in the log as "home care, apartment 4F" rather than under her name if the building's policy prefers that, and can be switched off in seconds if the agency sends someone new. None of that requires the attendant to download anything or hand over identification beyond what the law allows.

The old intercom transformer in our cellar also powers the street-door release. If the building adds fobs, does that transformer stay, and what happens to the apartment handsets?

It depends on whether the intercom itself is staying. If the board or owner wants to keep the existing handsets for now and add fobs at the door, the usual approach is to leave the intercom on its transformer, give the new electric strike and reader their own battery-backed power supply, and wire the intercom's door-release output into the new controller as one more way to open the door. The tenants press the same button they always have, and the door opens the same way, but a power failure or a dying transformer no longer takes the door down with it.

If the intercom is being replaced too, the transformer is retired with it. Either way, we test the old wire pairs to the apartments before anyone decides, because in many 1920s and 1930s buildings the riser is in better shape than the handsets, and keeping a sound riser can make a video upgrade much cheaper than it first looks.

After a stranger was buzzed into our building, the board wants the entry panel to save a picture of every visitor. What can a building lawfully keep from its door panel?

Many video entry panels can save a snapshot each time someone presses a call button, and that can be useful after an incident. In a building with apartments, though, the Tenant Data Privacy Law shapes what the owner may collect through smart access, how long it may be kept, and what it may be used for, and the building's written policy should say plainly what the panel records. Images of visitors and records of residents' entries are exactly the kind of data that policy has to cover. We are not lawyers; a board that wants to store visitor images should have its counsel review the policy before the feature is switched on.

Technically, we can set the panel to save call snapshots only, not continuous video; to keep them for a short, fixed period and then delete them automatically; and to limit who can see them to one or two named administrators. That is usually enough to answer the question "who rang 3C at 2 p.m.?" without building an archive nobody needs.

Row houses in and around the historic districts

Our house still has its original carved front door, and our restorer says no new holes in it. Can a fob or code system still control that door?

Yes, in most cases without touching the door at all. The part that releases the door is the electric strike, and it goes into the frame, not the door, so the original lock and latch stay exactly as they are. The reader or keypad goes on the masonry return of the doorway or inside the vestibule, the cable drops from the strike into the cellar through the frame or the wall behind it, and the door itself never sees a drill. On a pair of doors we work on the frame or the header in the same way.

The one thing we check carefully is the latch. An old mortise lock whose latch is worn short, or a door that has shrunk and no longer meets the frame squarely, will not hold reliably in any strike. A restorer can usually rebuild the latch or the meeting edge, and we schedule our work after theirs so the strike is fitted to the door as it will finally sit.

We rent out the garden apartment under our stoop. Can the tenant run a keypad on that door, while we keep a way in for a burst pipe?

Yes. The garden door can sit on its own controller, or on its own account on the house's controller, with the tenant as the administrator who adds and removes codes for their household and guests. The owners' codes do not appear in that list, and the tenant's do not appear in the owners'. For emergencies, the owner keeps a separate credential, or simply the mechanical key, which the log records when it is used electronically. New York's Multiple Dwelling Law section 51-c already lets a tenant add their own lock to their apartment door, provided the owner gets a key on request, so this arrangement follows a principle the law already recognizes.

The thing to settle in writing, in the lease or a short side letter, is when the owner may use the emergency credential and how the tenant will be told. A keypad that records every use makes that agreement easy to keep.

New buildings under the Atlantic Avenue plan and around the Armory

Our new building's ground-floor commercial space is not leased yet. What should be wired now for a tenant we have not met?

Wire for the doors, not the tenant. Every exterior door into the commercial space, and any door from that space into a shared corridor, trash room or cellar, should get conduit to the head of the frame, a pull box near the strike side, a cable path back to the building's security room, and a spare position on the controller. That costs little while the walls are open and saves a later tenant from surface-mounted raceway across a new storefront.

What should not be installed is the commercial tenant's actual reader and lock, because a pharmacy, a restaurant and a fitness studio want very different hardware. When the lease is signed, the tenant gets its own door group and administrator on the building's platform, or its own system on the prepared cable, and the residential side is never touched.

Our project puts a community facility and apartments on the same site. Should they share one access platform or run separate systems?

One platform with strictly separate administration is usually the better answer, as long as the two operators agree on who maintains the controller and who pays for the service. A shared platform means one set of cables and controllers, one place to see a forced door, and a fire alarm release that is wired once and tested once. Separate administration means the community facility's staff manage their members and hours, the residential manager manages residents, and neither can see or change the other's lists.

Separate systems make sense when the operators are completely independent, when one of them must keep records under its own rules, or when one may change hands. In either case the boundary doors between public and residential space, the ones a visitor to the pool or the gym should never pass, need to be identified on the drawings first. That list, not the brand of reader, is what decides the job.

Shops, offices and institutions on the avenues

Our Nostrand Avenue store reaches its cellar through steel doors in the sidewalk. Can a hatch like that be on access control, and should it be?

It can, but a sidewalk hatch is rarely the right first door. The steel leaves sit in the weather, take foot traffic and are usually secured from below with a bar or a padlock, so a powered lock in that position has a hard life. The better sequence is to control the door at the bottom of the cellar stair from inside, and the door from the cellar into the shop, so that whoever comes down through the hatch, whether a delivery crew or someone who forced it, cannot go any further without a credential.

If the hatch must be controlled, for instance because deliveries arrive before the store opens, we fit a contact so the system knows when it opens and pair it with a code for the delivery company that works only for the delivery window. A hatch that reports when it opens at 3 a.m. is worth more than one with an expensive lock nobody can monitor.

The back door of our Utica Avenue shop opens onto the yard where the building's tenants keep their trash cans. Who should control a door that two parties need?

Whoever is responsible for the yard, which is usually the building owner, should control it, with the shop and the tenants each given their own credentials. The shop's staff code works during business hours, the tenants' fobs or a key path give them the yard whenever they need it, and the owner's porter gets a code for collection mornings. Each group can be switched off without affecting the others, and the log shows which group used the door.

If the shop wants to stop the yard door being used as a back entrance to its stockroom, the more important door is the one between the stockroom and the yard corridor, which the shop can control on its own system. Sorting out which door belongs to whom before anything is installed is most of the job.

Our salon on Franklin Avenue shares a cellar stair with the bar next door. Can one cellar door hold both businesses' codes and keep a record of each?

Yes. A single reader or keypad can hold separate groups for each business, each with its own hours and its own administrator, and every entry is recorded against the group that used it. The salon's staff can reach the cellar during salon hours, the bar's staff during the bar's hours, and the landlord at any hour. If something goes missing from the cellar, both businesses can see who was down there, which in our experience ends most disputes before they start.

What matters as much as the reader is the door. Cellar doors on the avenues are often light interior doors with worn hinges, and a door that does not close and latch by itself records nothing useful. We usually replace or reinforce the door and add a closer as part of the same job.

Equipment

Types of Access Control We Install in Crown Heights

Every system on this page is built from the same six kinds of parts. What changes from a Park Place row house to a 135-apartment building on Crown Street is which version of each part, and how many.

Electric strikes

The piece in the frame that lets the existing latch go when a valid credential is shown. It suits most prewar wood and steel doors because the door and its original lock stay as they are. Fail-secure strikes keep the door locked in a power cut while the inside knob or lever still opens it.

Electrified panic bars and mortise locks

For aluminum-and-glass entrances in new buildings, double doors, and doors that carry heavy traffic, the lock lives in the panic bar or the mortise body instead of the frame. These are the doors where a power door operator for wheelchair users is also common.

Readers and keypads

Fob and card readers, keypads, and combined units, in slim versions for a narrow row-house doorway and vandal-resistant metal housings for a busy avenue. We fit readers that use encrypted credentials rather than the old 125 kHz fobs, which can be copied with cheap equipment.

Video panels and indoor screens

Door stations with a camera and a call button for each apartment or a directory, calling residents' phones, indoor touchscreens or landlines, and releasing the door from wherever the call is answered.

Closers, contacts and exit devices

The parts that make the door behave: a properly sized closer, a door position contact that reports a door held open, a request-to-exit sensor or push button, and a release for the fire alarm. Most of the failures we are called for start here.

Controllers and management

The battery-backed box that makes the decisions, kept in a locked, dry spot in the cellar or a closet, managed from a phone or a browser. Local systems carry no monthly fee; cloud management adds remote administration across several buildings, usually for a subscription.

An alarm plan, a doorbell camera or a controlled door: what each one does in a Crown Heights building

Many Crown Heights households already have a national company's alarm, such as ADT or SimpliSafe, or a doorbell camera such as Ring. Those products are good at what they are designed for: an alarm tells someone when a protected door or window opens while the system is armed, and a doorbell camera shows and records who comes to a door. Neither one decides who gets in. In a building where many people share the entrance, that decision is the whole problem, and it takes a lock that relocks every time, a list of who may open it, and a way to change that list without changing every key.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Crown Heights

These are generalizations we see repeated in quick online answers, consumer articles and national cost guides like those on Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. We do not attribute any particular claim or figure to any one source; we set each one against what Crown Heights doors and Crown Heights rules actually look like.

"Putting fobs on the door makes the building secure."

Fobs control who can open a door that is shut and latched. They do nothing about a door that is propped, a door that drifts closed without catching, or a door that a resident opens for a stranger. Both of the Crown Heights lobby thefts in recent news, on Crown Street and on Brooklyn Avenue, came down to someone being inside the lobby who should not have been; neither required defeating a lock.

What actually changes outcomes is the combination: a door that closes and latches every time, a second controlled door between the vestibule and the lobby, a package room that keeps deliveries off the lobby floor, and an alert when a door is held open. The fob is one part of that, and often not the most important one.

That is also why our quotes in older buildings so often include a closer, a frame repair or an inner-door strike that nobody asked for. Without them the reader is a nicer way of unlocking a door that was never really locked.

"A national per-door average tells you what a Crown Heights lobby will cost."

National guides mix very different jobs. Some security-door figures cover replacing a door, not adding electronic access; some per-door figures for wired access control run into thousands of dollars once cable and a controller are included; and forum posts from installers quote yet another range. None of them knows whether your building has one door or four, a cellar you can run cable through, or a frame bored out by a century of locks.

On our Brooklyn list, which applies to Crown Heights without a surcharge, a single controlled door with a reader or keypad, strike, wiring and ten fobs is $1,500 to $2,500, a video entry panel replacing a buzzer is $1,500 to $3,000, and a full building system is $15,000 to $40,000. Those are installed prices for the kinds of doors we see here.

The number that actually matters is written after a visit, because the door, the frame and the route to the cellar decide where in each range a job falls.

"Once there's a fob system, the owner can record and keep whatever it likes."

Not in a New York City building with apartments. The Tenant Data Privacy Law requires owners using smart access to obtain consent, give tenants a written privacy and retention policy, collect only a short list of data, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, and never sell it or use it to track tenants outside the building. The owner also may not make smart access the only way into a tenant's own apartment.

Generic answers often skip this because most of the country has no such rule. For a Crown Heights owner, ignoring it means a system that may have to be reconfigured after it is installed, and a policy that tenants can challenge.

We set up the system to collect only what is needed, to delete logs on a schedule, and to give the owner a clear description of what it records, which is the starting point for the policy the law requires.

"Switching a stabilized building from keys to fobs is just a hardware decision."

It is also a services decision. DHCR has approved such changes with conditions, including free fobs for lawful occupants, up to four extra fobs at no charge for employees or regular caregivers and visitors, caps on charges for lost fobs, and in one case a permanent rent reduction where a new intercom required tenants to keep a landline. Owners who skip those conditions invite complaints.

The practical consequence for the design is that the system must be able to issue several credentials per apartment cheaply, must not depend on every tenant having a smartphone, and must not require personal identification beyond what the rules allow.

We plan for that from the first visit, and we tell owners plainly that the agency, not the installer, decides what a particular building owes its tenants.

"Wireless smart locks are the easy answer for a row house split into flats."

A battery deadbolt is a fine lock for one household's own apartment door. On the shared street door of a divided row house it is a weak answer: it usually runs from one brand's app with one owner account, it cannot give each apartment its own administrator, it depends on batteries that fail in the cold, and it rarely relocks reliably on a heavy old door that has to close against a stiff closer.

The shared door of a multiple dwelling also has to be self-closing and self-locking under the state's Multiple Dwelling Law where that section applies, which is a job for a wired strike on a properly adjusted door rather than a deadbolt that throws only when it is told to.

The sensible split in most divided Crown Heights houses is a wired, battery-backed strike and reader at the street door, with whatever lock each household prefers on its own apartment door.

"Magnetic locks are the strongest, so use them everywhere."

Magnetic locks hold well, but they hold only while powered, so they release in a power cut, and the NYC Building Code requires a release on the door itself that works with one hand and frees the door immediately, plus release on loss of power. On an exit door that is exactly right; on a street door that must stay locked when the power goes out on a winter night, it is the wrong choice unless something else holds the door.

For most prewar Crown Heights doors an electric strike is simpler and better: it uses the door's own latch, keeps the door locked from outside in a power failure, and always opens from inside with the knob or lever. Maglocks have their place, on some glass doors, gates and interior doors, but not by default.

Whichever lock is used, nobody is ever locked in. Every electrically controlled door we install opens from the inside without a key, a card or a code, and we prove it with the power off before we leave.

"Cameras in the lobby will solve package theft."

Cameras record. In the Crown Street theft News 12 reported, security video showed exactly what happened, and the packages were still gone. A Crown Heights resident writing on Anash.org recommended cameras, delivery lockers and calling 911 when a theft is in progress, which is good advice, and the lockers are the part that prevents rather than documents.

Access control is the other preventive piece: a package room or cabinet only couriers and residents can open, a second door between the vestibule and the lobby, and a record of which courier code opened the package room and when.

The best results come from both together. A camera over the package room door, and a reader that opens it, together answer "who took it?" in seconds instead of hours of scrubbing through footage.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Walk Through the Inner Door Before Pricing the Outer One

This is how we approach a large prewar building here, not the story of one job. The street door is what the board asks about, so we look at it last. We start in the vestibule: is there an inner door, does it have a closer, does it latch, where are the mailboxes, and where do couriers leave packages? Then the cellar: where the old intercom transformer is, whether there is a dry wall for a controller, and how the cable will reach the entrance. Then the roof door, which must open from inside without a key. Only then the street door, its frame and its closer.

Done in that order, the walk-through usually changes the job. A board that called about one reader on the street door often ends up with a cheaper closer and strike on the street door and a reader on the inner door, because the inner door is where a stranger who has been let in actually stops.

Can the package room's entry record help settle an argument about a delivery that never arrived?
+

Often, yes. If couriers get their own time-limited codes and residents their own fobs, the record shows which code opened the package room and when, and a camera over that door shows who carried what. That will not find every missing box, but it usually tells a manager whether the package was ever delivered to the room.

If the controller in the cellar stops working, are residents locked out of the building or locked in?
+

Never locked in: every controlled door opens from inside by hand. With a fail-secure strike, the street door stays locked from outside, and residents use the emergency key cylinder until the controller is repaired. The battery backup covers ordinary power cuts, so a true controller failure is rare and is usually fixed on the first service visit.

When a building changes from keys or old fobs to new fobs, can the old and new both work for a few weeks?
+

Yes. Keys keep working through the cylinder until the owner decides to rekey it, and many readers can accept old and new credentials side by side during a set changeover period. We agree the cut-off date with the owner or board in advance, so residents who were away get their new fobs before the old ones stop.

Phone keys or fobs: which do larger Crown Heights buildings usually end up using?
+

Usually both. Phone keys are convenient for residents who want them and cost nothing to replace, while fobs serve older residents, children, caregivers and anyone without a smartphone. A building that relies on phones alone shuts some tenants out, so we set up readers that accept either.

For a prewar building, is a wired system or a wireless one the better choice?
+

Wired, for the entrances. A wired strike and reader on a battery-backed controller keep working through weak Wi-Fi, cold weather and battery failures, which matters on the doors everyone depends on. Wireless locks can make sense for interior doors that are hard to reach with cable, such as a storage room at the far end of a cellar.

Can residents add their own phones to the system, or does the manager have to do it for each one?
+

Usually the manager sends each resident an invitation by email or text, and the resident accepts it in the app on their own phone. Nobody hands over a phone or a password. The manager decides who is invited and can cancel an invitation or a phone key at any time.
