The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Chinatown Properties Need Access Control

Brooklyn Chinatown grew faster than its buildings did. When Paul Mak founded the Brooklyn Chinese-American Association in 1988, his own survey two years later counted, in his words to CBS New York in May 2026, “about 3,000 Chinese people in the neighborhood.” He now estimates the area at more than 200,000. Shiu Kan Leung told the same reporter that when he opened an electronics shop on Eighth Avenue in 1980 it was the first Chinese-owned store on the avenue. Open City’s reporting describes Sunset Park today as home to one of the largest Chinese communities in New York City, larger than Manhattan’s Chinatown, with most of it living around Eighth Avenue. Wikipedia places the Chinatown itself along Eighth Avenue from about 40th Street to 65th Street.

The houses those families moved into were not built for them. Wikipedia’s history of Sunset Park dates most of its row houses and industrial buildings to the decades between the 1890s and the 1920s, when the elevated railways and then the subway reached this part of Brooklyn. A municipal archives essay comparing the neighborhood’s 1940s and 1980s tax photographs with the street today found that the change had been cultural rather than architectural: by and large, the same buildings are still standing. So a door that was hung for one family a hundred years ago is now the front door for two or three households, a store, a stockroom and the relatives who come and go between them. That single fact is the reason most of our calls from this neighborhood are about access control rather than locks.

A key does not scale. In a two-family house on a side street, the owner’s household, the tenant’s household, a grandparent who lives with one of them, adult children who moved to Bensonhurst or Queens, a home attendant, a cousin who helps in the store downstairs and the person who reads the meters may all hold a key to the same street door. Nobody remembers who copied one. When a tenant moves out, the key usually leaves with them, or is handed to the next tenant, or both. Changing the lock means cutting a new set for everyone. Access control replaces that one shared key with a separate code, fob, card or phone credential for each person, each of which can be stopped on its own the same day without touching anyone else’s, and every opening is recorded against the credential that made it.

The avenue adds a second set of doors. Eighth Avenue’s restaurants, bakeries, groceries, seafood and produce shops start their day long before the families above them do, and much of that start happens at the back: a kitchen door to a rear yard, a cellar stair under the sidewalk, a stockroom behind the counter. The doors that matter most to a business on the avenue are the ones customers never see. On Thanksgiving morning in 2018, Patch reported, a man broke the rear window of Happy House Seafood Restaurant on Eighth Avenue at about 5:10 a.m. and took $200 from the register. In October 2025 News 12 reported that police were looking for one man tied to a number of Sunset Park burglaries, including one at Ocean Eats on Fourth Avenue where he took the register and $1,000. Neither story was about a fancy lock being defeated. Both were about the back of a business at an hour when nobody was there.

We do not sell any of this with fear, because the numbers do not support it. The 72nd Precinct, which polices Sunset Park, recorded 1,645 burglaries in 1990 and 107 in 2025, a fall of 93.5 percent, according to its own CompStat report; for the year to November 8, 2026, it counted 89 burglaries against 90 the year before. What has not changed is the everyday problem: too many keys, too many people sharing a door, and no record of who opened it. Access control is the answer to that problem whether or not crime is falling, and we would rather install one good door than four that nobody maintains.

There is one more reason, and it is the most important one on this page. On November 5, 2026, six people died in an early-morning fire in a two-story row house on 58th Street near Seventh Avenue. Insurance Journal reported that firefighters reached people trapped behind window bars by way of a rear entrance, and that the Department of Buildings had received 2025 complaints that the house had been illegally divided, complaints that were closed after inspectors could not get inside; NY1 reported that there was no indication the conversion played a role in the fire, and the cause was still under investigation when this page was updated. We mention it here because every lock we install in this neighborhood is a lock on a building where people sleep, and the first rule we follow is that no door we control will ever keep anyone in.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Brooklyn Chinatown’s Architecture

In this neighborhood the building, not the brochure, chooses the hardware. These are the six kinds of property we are called to most often around Eighth Avenue, what each one usually needs at the door, and why.

Avenue buildings with a shop below

Three- and four-story brick buildings with a store on the ground floor and apartments above, reached through a narrow residents’ door beside the shop window. Two separate systems on one wall: the store’s, and the residents’, with nothing shared but the brick.

Side-street row houses shared by families

Brick and frame two- and three-family houses from the early 1900s on the numbered streets east and west of the avenue, often with an owner’s household, a tenant household and relatives in between. One controlled street door with a credential for every adult, and plain keyed locks on each apartment door.

Houses being put back to legal use

Houses a previous owner divided into far more rooms than the building allows, now being returned to their legal number of apartments. Door plans drawn to the legal count, with free exit from every room and no per-room locks that would turn a family house into a rooming house again.

Century-old cooperatives and walk-ups

Four- to six-story buildings from the 1910s and 1920s, including the cooperatives on 43rd Street that are among the oldest in the city. A reader and strike at the street door wired into the existing intercom, then the cellar, roof and laundry doors as the board or owner is ready.

Infill condos with steel frames

Narrow new buildings that replaced a house on the avenue, such as the six-story brick-and-steel condo at 5213 Eighth Avenue. Storefront-style aluminum doors, steel stud walls and a developer’s system that may or may not be handed over properly.

Restaurants, bakeries and groceries

Ground-floor food businesses with a dining room or sales floor at the front and a kitchen, cold storage, stockroom and cellar behind. Heavy-duty strikes on kitchen doors, supplier codes on receiving doors and staff schedules that start before dawn.

What the walls and frames mean for the install

Most of the brick here is a century old, and the mortar is softer than the brick. We fix stations and readers into the mortar joints rather than the face of the brick, so if the equipment is ever moved the repair is a joint to repoint rather than a cracked face brick. Where a building’s front has been covered in stucco, siding or tile, we find what is behind the covering before we drill, because the fixing has to reach something solid.

Old wood frames are the hardest part of most jobs. An electrician’s forum we read while preparing this page put it well: on an apartment entry, the electric latch is the difficult piece, and one owner had skipped the unlock function entirely because his antique door was not a standard size. We see the same thing. A jamb that has been bored for three or four generations of locks has very little wood left where the strike body has to go, so we fill or splice that section of the jamb first and only then cut the strike pocket. Kitchen doors are usually the opposite problem: hollow-metal doors in steel frames that have been slammed, kicked and greased for years, where the frame may be bent and the latch no longer lands squarely. Those get straightened and fitted with a heavy-duty strike, or with a lock in the door itself with power passed through the hinge.

Aluminum storefront doors on newer condos and on some shops have stiles too narrow for a conventional lock. On those we put the lock in the frame header or in an electrified panic bar, and on frameless glass shop doors we use brackets that clamp the hardware to the glass rather than drilling it.

Three Households, One Street Door: Credentials for Shared Houses

The most common Brooklyn Chinatown house we work on is not one family’s home. It is a brick row house on a numbered street, built for one or two households, that now holds an owner’s family on one floor, a tenant family on another, and a set of relatives who belong to neither floor exactly: a grandmother who lives with the owner but cooks for the tenant’s children, a brother who stays three nights a week, an aide who comes in the morning. Every one of them uses the same street door, and every one of them, at some point, was given a key.

We start every one of these jobs the same way, by asking the owner to write down who should be able to open the street door today. Not who has a key, which is a longer list and an unknowable one, but who should. Then we sort that list by household, because the household is the unit that comes and goes together. When a tenant family moves out, every credential belonging to that household is ended in one action, on the day the lease ends, and nobody in the owner’s household notices anything has changed. When a relative stops visiting, their code stops working, and nobody has to explain why the locks were changed.

Each household also needs to stay private from the others. A system that lets the owner read every time the tenant’s teenager came home at midnight is not a system we will set up, and in a building with three or more apartments the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law limits what an owner may collect and keep in any case. What the owner does need is control of who holds a credential and an alert if the street door is forced or left standing open. Those two things can be given without a live feed of anyone’s comings and goings, and that is how we configure shared houses by default: users grouped by household, door-forced and door-held alerts to the owner, and an entry log kept short and opened only when there is a real reason to look.

What stays the same

The apartment doors upstairs keep their own ordinary keyed locks unless the owner and tenant both want something else; access control belongs at the shared entrance, not on someone’s home. The original street door key keeps working, so a flat battery or a forgotten code never locks a household out. And in a house where somebody upstairs prefers to buzz a visitor in rather than use an app, the call button or the old buzzer stays in service alongside the new credentials.

What we will not do

We do not install keypads or readers on bedroom doors in a rented house. A lock on each room is the clearest sign that a house is being let room by room, which is exactly the kind of subdivision that left 25 single-room units inside a two-family house at 818 55th Street, and it creates doors that someone may have to get through in a hurry. Where a family wants privacy for a teenager’s room or a relative’s room in their own home, an ordinary privacy lever that opens from outside with a simple tool is the right answer, and it costs a few dollars, not a reader.

Every Lock Opens From Inside: Free Exit After the 58th Street Fire

The fire on 58th Street on November 5, 2026 was a two-story wood-frame row house built in 1915, one of a block of 25 such houses, according to Insurance Journal’s account. Kevin Woods, the FDNY’s chief of fire operations, said crews met “fire on every floor.” Firefighters used a rear entrance to reach people trapped behind window bars. Six people died. We do not know, and nobody had yet said, what part any door, lock or bar played, and we will not speculate about a tragedy that was still under investigation. But it has changed the first question owners on these blocks ask us, and the answer deserves a section of its own.

The question is whether an electronic lock can trap people inside. Installed properly, it cannot, and the rules exist precisely so that it cannot. An electric strike, the part most of our jobs use, controls only whether the latch can be pushed in from the outside. The lever or knob on the inside works mechanically, as it always did, whether the power is on, off or failing. Anyone inside opens the door with one ordinary movement, no key, no code, no card, no special knowledge. That is what we fit on every residents’ door, every shared-house street door and every door from a sleeping area to the outside.

Magnetic locks are a different device and need different handling. A magnetic lock holds the door shut with power, so it has to release when the power fails, when the fire alarm or sprinklers operate, and when someone uses the release built into the door hardware. The New York City Building Code sets out exactly how: a sensor on the inside, a push-to-exit button within reach that cuts the lock’s power, and listed equipment. A magnetic lock done that way is lawful on many commercial doors. A magnetic lock wired to a cheap button, or one without fire alarm release, is not, and we remove them when we find them. We never put a magnetic lock, a double-cylinder deadbolt or a keyed inside lock on a door that is a way out of a home.

The rear door is a way out too

Many row houses here have a rear door to a yard, and in a house with families upstairs that door may be the second way out. Owners often want it locked tight against the yard, because a yard is where a burglar can work unseen. Both goals fit on one door: a lock that holds against anyone outside, a door contact that alerts the owner if it is forced or left open, and an inside lever that opens it in one movement. What does not fit is a padlock, a chain, a bar across the inside or a lock that needs a key to get out. If we find one of those on a door that people may need to escape through, we point it out in writing, whether or not it is part of the job.

The power-off test

The last thing we do on every job is take all the power away, mains and battery, and open every controlled door from the inside by hand. Then we restore the battery alone and do it again. If a door fails either test it is not finished. The owner watches the test, so the household knows from their own eyes, not our word, that the door lets them out.

Kitchens That Open Before Dawn: Back Doors on the Avenue

An Eighth Avenue restaurant is two buildings in one. The front is public for twelve hours a day. The back, the kitchen, the yard door, the cellar stair and the cold storage, is busiest when the front is dark: produce and seafood deliveries in the early morning, prep cooks arriving at different times, a closing cook taking out the last of the trash after midnight. The Happy House break-in that Patch reported in 2018 happened at about 5:10 in the morning through a rear window. That is the hour, and that is the side of the building, that most restaurant owners here ask us about.

Keys are the weak point in a kitchen. Kitchen staff change often, and in a family business keys pass between relatives, part-timers and the cousin who covers on holidays. When a cook leaves, the key often stays in a jacket pocket in another restaurant’s locker room. A reader or keypad on the kitchen door ends that: each cook gets a code or card that works only on the days and hours they are scheduled, a departing employee’s credential is cancelled before they leave the building, and if the yard door is opened at 3 a.m. the owner gets an alert instead of a surprise.

Kitchen doors are also hard on hardware. Grease, steam, heat from the line, hand trucks banging the frame and a door that is propped open with a milk crate for most of the service will kill a light-duty lock within a year. We use heavy-duty strikes and stainless or sealed readers, mount the controller in the office or a locked enclosure well away from the heat, and fit a door closer that actually closes the door. We also add a contact that reports when the door has been held open longer than the owner allows, because the propped kitchen door is the most common reason a good lock protects nothing.

What a reader cannot do

A reader on the kitchen door does nothing for a rear window, a roof hatch or a skylight, and we say so plainly. Those need bars, grilles, sensors or an alarm, and the bars on any window people might need to escape through have to open from inside. Access control is one part of the back of a restaurant, the part that decides who walks through the door, and it works best alongside a camera on the yard and an alarm on the openings that are not doors.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control in Brooklyn Chinatown

The Brooklyn Chinese-American Association’s history, as CBS New York told it in May 2026, is partly a history of merchants’ safety: a crime-victims hotline with translation, a bilingual officer assigned in 1990 to walk Eighth Avenue and hear store owners’ complaints, and work with police that one business owner, Danny Tsoi, said left “no more gangsters” demanding protection money. The businesses that came out of that history are still mostly family-run, and their door problems are family-sized: too many keys, too few people at the back of the building, and a lot of cash and stock behind doors that customers never see.

The food blocks of Eighth Avenue

Restaurants, bakeries, dim sum houses, seafood and produce shops packed shoulder to shoulder along the avenue. Kitchen and yard doors on shift schedules, receiving doors that suppliers can open at a set hour, and cellar stairs that should never be left open during service.

Groceries and supermarkets

Larger stores with loading doors, stockrooms, walk-in coolers and an upstairs or rear office. Staff-only readers on the stock and office doors, and alerts when a loading door stays open after the last delivery.

Offices and agencies over stores

Travel and tax offices, accountants, language schools, associations and home care agencies on the floors above the shops, several of which may share one street door. Suite readers that the tenant controls, separate from the landlord’s street door.

Health and home care services

Home care agencies have opened offices in Sunset Park to serve an aging population, and clinics sit among the stores. Records rooms on their own credential, staff access that ends the day someone leaves, and front doors that patients with walkers can manage.

Event halls and their side exits

Banquet halls and large restaurants that host weddings, birthdays and association dinners, with several exits required for the crowd. Exit hardware that stays legal, alarms that sound when a side exit is used as a loading door, and controlled entry for caterers.

The planned Eighth Avenue Center at 62nd Street

A development planned for 62nd Street and Eighth Avenue that has been described as a 17-story office building, a 10-story hotel, condominiums and a shopping center. Large new projects like that bring networked access control with them; the small buildings around them keep needing it one door at a time.

Jewelry, gold and phone shops

Stores that keep valuable stock at the counter are the businesses most likely to want a locked front door during opening hours. In February 2023 CBS New York reported that a jewelry store employee on Fifth Avenue near 49th Street, a few blocks west of the Chinatown blocks, was grazed by a gunshot during an attempted robbery; the suspect left empty-handed. Buzz-in entry, where the door stays locked and staff release it after seeing the customer through the glass or on a station screen, is a well-established answer, and on a glass shop door the lock can be clamped to the glass rather than drilled into it. What a buzz-in door must never do is trap customers or staff inside: the inside push bar or lever opens it freely at all times, and the release button is placed where staff can reach it without leaving the counter.

Rules that apply to Brooklyn Chinatown business doors

  • Exits open from inside. Under the NYC Building Code, an electrically locked door on a path of exit must release on power failure and when the fire alarm or sprinklers operate, and a magnetic lock needs a release in the door hardware that works with one hand. A shop’s, kitchen’s or hall’s exit opens from inside without a key, card or code.
  • Listed equipment. Controllers and locks on exit doors should carry a UL 294 listing for access control; we use listed parts and keep the listings in the job file for your insurer or landlord.
  • Residents’ doors above shops. Under Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a, the street entrance of a class A multiple dwelling built or converted after January 1, 1968 must be self-closing and self-locking, and older buildings must add one when a majority of tenants ask in writing. A store owner who also owns the building cannot leave the residents’ door unlocked during business hours unless an attendant is on duty.
  • Self-closing doors are inspected. Local Law 71 of 2022 requires HPD to inspect self-closing doors in class A multiple dwellings, and a failure to maintain them is a class C, immediately hazardous violation. A reader is pointless on a residents’ door whose closer has failed, so we check the closer first.
  • Tenant data. Where a building with three or more apartments uses fobs, apps, codes tied to a person or biometrics, the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law requires consent, a written privacy and retention policy, minimum data, and destruction of authentication data within 90 days unless anonymized. A shop’s own staff door is not covered; a shared residential door is.
  • A fob is a key. amNY reported that the Department of Buildings treats fobs, cards and metal instruments that fit mechanical locks as keys, and that traditional keys are required. In residential buildings we always leave a mechanical way in alongside any electronic credential.
  • Who may do the work. New York State requires a license to install or service security systems for others; AESS works under license #12000287431. Low-voltage cable for access control is our work; any new 120-volt circuit for a power supply is done by a licensed electrician under the electrical code, and we tell you up front when a job needs one.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Brooklyn Chinatown: Questions Answered

Houses, households and the people who run them

A tenant in our two-family asked us to put a keypad on her bedroom door. Should we do it?

No, and we will not install one. A separate lock on each bedroom is how a house gets turned into a rooming house, which is the kind of subdivision the Gotham Gazette described at 818 55th Street, where a two-family was split into 25 single rooms. It also puts an electronic lock on a door someone may need to leave in seconds, at night, in smoke. If the tenant wants more privacy, the honest fixes are a privacy lever on the bedroom door that opens from outside with a simple tool, and a properly controlled street door so strangers cannot walk in.

If the real issue is that the apartment is shared by people who are not one household, that is a question about the lease and the building’s legal use, and no lock will settle it. We are glad to advise on the street door and the apartment entrance, which are the right places for control.

Our rear yard door is the second way out for the families upstairs. Can it be locked against the yard and still open instantly from inside?

Yes. Fit a lock that the outside cannot open without a credential and the inside opens with one movement of a lever or push bar, every time, with or without power. An electric strike does this naturally, because it controls only the outside; a heavy-duty mortise lock with a free inside lever does it too. Add a door contact so the owner hears about a forced or propped door, and a closer so the door does not hang open in the wind.

Take off anything that defeats the inside: chains, hasps and padlocks, slide bolts high on the door, double-cylinder deadbolts and bars across the frame. If the yard side really needs more protection, put it on the yard itself, with a better gate and lighting, not on the door people escape through.

The owner of our building spends every winter out of the country. How do tenants get credentials issued or cancelled while she is away?

Choose a system managed from the cloud, so credentials are issued and cancelled from a phone anywhere, and name a second administrator who is in the neighborhood, usually a family member or the super. The owner keeps full control and sees every change; the local administrator handles the day-to-day: a new tenant’s fobs on the day the lease starts, a lost fob cancelled the same afternoon, a contractor’s code for a week. Phone credentials can be sent to a new tenant by text, so nobody needs to wait for someone to hand over a fob.

The door itself keeps working if the building’s internet drops; only remote changes wait until it returns. We set the local administrator up with their own login, so nobody shares the owner’s password.

The relative who set up our building’s door app is moving away for a year. How do we hand over control without losing the user list?

Add the new person as an administrator before the old one leaves, check that they can issue and cancel a credential themselves, and only then reduce or remove the departing relative’s rights. The user list, schedules and door settings live on the system, not on anyone’s phone, so nothing is lost when an administrator changes. What is easily lost is the account that owns the system, the one tied to the original email address and the billing; that should belong to the building owner, not to whoever happened to set it up.

When we install a system we register the owner account in the owner’s name, give the owner the recovery details in writing, and add other administrators below it. If an existing system was set up the other way round, we can usually move it.

Several people in our house work restaurant night shifts and come home after 2 a.m. Can the street door reader stop beeping and clunking so it doesn’t wake everyone?

Mostly, yes. Most readers and keypads let the confirmation beep be turned down or off and the light used instead, and a quiet strike with a soft-close closer makes far less noise than an old latch slamming into its keeper. The loudest part of many old doors is the door itself: a closer adjusted to finish the last few inches slowly does more for a sleeping household than any electronics.

We would not turn the sound off on a door contact’s held-open warning, because that sound is what reminds the last person in to make sure the door shut. It can be set to a short chirp rather than a long tone.

Restaurants, bakeries and groceries

Our seafood and produce arrive before 5 a.m., before anyone from the store is there. Can a supplier’s driver leave the delivery inside without getting onto the sales floor?

Yes, if there is a space to make into a receiving area: a vestibule at the back door, a caged section of the stockroom or a cellar landing. The supplier’s driver gets a code that opens the outer door only, only during the delivery window, and the inner door to the store stays locked to everyone but staff. The two doors can be interlocked so the inner one will not release while the outer one is open. A camera over the receiving area, tied to the door log, shows what was delivered and when.

Each supplier should have its own code, so a code that leaks can be changed for one company without affecting the others, and so the log shows which supplier opened the door. Cold goods still need to reach a cooler quickly, so the receiving area should be the shortest walk to it.

We want only the kitchen manager and the fish cutter to open our walk-in cooler. Can a walk-in door take a reader, and what happens to someone inside?

A walk-in cooler door can be put on a reader or keypad from the outside, usually with an electrified lock or a strike suited to the door’s heavy hinged frame. The inside safety release that every walk-in has, the knob or push plate that opens the door from inside, must stay exactly as it is and work at all times. We never fit anything that could hold a person inside a cold room, and we test the inside release with the lock powered before we leave.

The cold and condensation are hard on electronics, so the reader goes on the warm side, the controller stays well away, and cable is sealed where it passes into the box. If your cooler maker’s instructions limit what can be fitted to the door, we follow them.

Caterers at our banquet hall keep using the emergency exits as a loading door. What can we do that keeps the exits legal?

Give the caterers a better door and make the exits announce themselves. A designated loading entrance with its own code, issued to each caterer for the hours of their event, takes away the reason to use the exits. On the exits themselves, a local alarm on the panic hardware sounds whenever the door is opened, so staff hear it at once, while the door still opens freely the instant anyone pushes the bar.

What you cannot do is lock, chain or wedge an exit from a place of assembly, or slow it down without an approved arrangement. If your hall has a fire alarm system, any change to exit hardware is checked against it before we touch the door.

Our counter handles cash all day. Can a code be set so that, if someone forces us to open the back room, it opens the door and quietly calls for help?

Some keypads and controllers support a duress code: a code that works like a normal one at the door but also sends a silent signal, either to a monitoring service or to the owner’s phone. Whether it helps depends on who receives the signal and what they do, so we set it up only alongside a monitoring arrangement or a clear plan for the person who gets the alert. Not every model offers it, and we will tell you before you buy which ones do.

For most cash businesses the bigger gain is simpler: a back room that opens only for the people on shift, a door contact that reports it left open, and a camera on the counter.

Kitchen staff at our restaurant change almost every month. What stops the credential list filling up with people who left?

Give every credential an end date when you issue it. A new cook’s code can be set to expire at the end of the trial period or the season, and renewed in a tap if they stay; anyone who leaves without notice simply stops getting in on that date. A monthly report of who used the kitchen door, and who has not used it at all, shows the names to remove. Most owners find the list stays honest once removing someone takes ten seconds rather than a locksmith.

For a family business we also separate family members from staff, so a cousin covering on a holiday can be added for the day without becoming a permanent user.

Offices and choosing a system

Our home care agency’s office is upstairs over a store, and aides come in at every hour to drop off paperwork. Who should get a credential, and to which door?

Split the office into the parts aides need and the parts they do not. Aides usually need the street door and a front room or a drop box, during posted hours; coordinators need the whole suite; and the room where client files are kept should open only for the few people whose job is to handle them, with every opening logged. Aides who visit rarely can be buzzed in from the station rather than given a standing credential.

If the street door is shared with apartments or other offices, the landlord controls it and decides whether your aides get a credential there; the suite door and the records room are yours to control. We design both together so the two systems do not fight each other.

Two quotes for our shop’s back door are more than a thousand dollars apart. What should each quote list so we can compare them?

Ask both installers to list, door by door: the lock type and model, and whether it holds locked or releases when the power fails; the reader or keypad model and whether its credentials are encrypted; the controller and how long its battery backup lasts; who supplies the 120-volt power and whether an electrician is included; the number of credentials; whether a cloud subscription is required and what it costs a year; what happens to the door from the inside; and the warranty on parts and on labor.

Very often the cheaper quote is a battery smart lock with no controller, no backup and no door contact, and the dearer quote is a wired, monitored door. Both can be fair prices for different things. Once the lists are side by side, the gap usually explains itself.

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Brooklyn Chinatown

These are the generalizations that turn up in quick online answers, consumer articles and national cost guides of the kind Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish. Where we quote a figure, we name the guide it came from. Each claim is set against what a door around Eighth Avenue actually needs.

“Access control costs $1,500 to $5,000 a door.”

Swiftlane’s 2026 cost guide gives $1,500 to $5,000 per door installed for standard access points, and Safe and Sound’s gives $1,500 to $3,500 per door fully installed for a card or mobile setup. Those are honest national ranges, and they are wide because they have to cover everything from an office suite to a hospital corridor.

A Brooklyn Chinatown owner does not need a national range. On our Brooklyn list, a single controlled door with a reader or keypad, an electric strike, the wiring and ten fobs is $1,500 to $2,500; a keypad on a kitchen, cellar or service door is $1,500 to $2,000; and a video entry panel with fob access replacing a buzzer is $1,500 to $3,000.

What moves a job within those ranges here is almost always the frame and the cable route: a century-old jamb that needs splicing, a finished cellar ceiling, or a cable that has to cross from the store’s side of the building to the residents’ side.

“A basic keypad lock is about $500.”

Safe and Sound’s guide puts the bottom of the range at about $500 for a basic keypad. That figure describes a battery keypad deadbolt, the kind sold in hardware stores, fitted to a door.

On a single-family house’s side door that can be enough. On the shared street door of a three-household row house it usually is not: a battery lock has no controller, no backup power, no door contact and, on most models, no way to group users by household or end a whole household’s access at once.

We will happily fit a good keypad deadbolt where it suits the door, labor-only if you have bought it. We just will not call it an access control system.

“Fobs are cheap, so hand them out freely.”

Swiftlane’s guide prices encrypted 13.56 MHz fobs at $5 to $15 each in bulk, and it is right that the fob itself is cheap. That leads some answers to suggest issuing extra fobs to whoever asks.

The fob is cheap; the person holding it is not. In a house where three households share a door, every spare fob is a key nobody is tracking. The point of the system is that each credential belongs to a named person in a named household, so it can be stopped on its own.

We issue fobs by name and by household, record who has each one, and set end dates for anyone who is not a permanent resident.

“Landlords can use entry logs however they like.”

Some answers present a building’s entry log as a management tool with no limits. amNY’s 2019 reporting on keyless entry quoted the New York Civil Liberties Union’s Lee Rowland saying it is “fundamentally inappropriate for anyone to be told that a condition of living somewhere is giving up their data.”

New York City has since written limits into law. Under the Tenant Data Privacy Law, an owner of a multiple dwelling using smart access needs tenants’ consent, a written policy, minimum data and destruction of authentication data within 90 days unless anonymized, and may not use the system to track tenants outside the building.

We configure residential systems to collect the least they need and give owners the policy text the law expects.

“Electronic locks are a fire risk.”

After a fire like the one on 58th Street in November 2026, it is understandable that some people assume any electronic lock makes a building more dangerous. That is not how a properly installed system works.

A fail-secure electric strike controls only the outside of the door; the inside lever stays mechanical and opens with no power at all. Magnetic locks, which do need power to hold, are required by the Building Code to release on power loss, on fire alarm and from hardware on the door.

The real fire risk on a door is the improvised kind: a chain, a padlock, a bar across the inside, a double-cylinder deadbolt. We point those out on every visit.

“Crime in Sunset Park is the reason to install access control.”

Some answers open with crime rankings. The 72nd Precinct’s own CompStat report shows burglary down 93.5 percent between 1990 and 2025, and essentially flat in 2026, 89 cases against 90 by early November.

Those numbers say nothing about which of your doors is propped open, how many copies of the street door key exist, or whether last year’s tenant can still get in. Those are the problems access control solves, and they exist on safe blocks too.

We plan from the doors and the people who use them, and we leave the crime statistics to the precinct.

“Packages are safe once the street door has a reader.”

A reader helps, because a door that relocks behind every person keeps casual walk-ins out of the hallway. But News 12’s 2025 report on Sunset Park’s first NYCLocker quoted a resident, Kevin Hernandez, saying “anything we order, it’s always opened,” and parcels left inside a common hallway are still within reach of anyone who lives in or visits the building.

The fix that works is a place for parcels that only the courier and the recipient can open: a locked package room or locker, or a parcel locker like the city’s on Sixth Avenue.

Where a building has room for a package closet, we can put it on a courier code and the residents’ fobs, and leave the rest of the building closed to couriers.

Our Installation Process

The same eight steps cover one kitchen door and a whole walk-up. In Brooklyn Chinatown, steps two and four carry the most weight: working out who controls each door in a building shared by a store and several households, and proving every door lets people out before anything is locked.

1. Door photos and who uses each door

2. Who controls which door

We confirm who owns each door and who decides who gets in: the building owner, a commercial tenant, a board or each household, and how many legal households the building has.

3. Site walk with the doors closed

A technician checks each door closes and latches on its own, inspects frames and hinges, finds a cable route and a dry spot for the controller, and tests phone signal at each door.

4. Exit test before anything is locked

Every door to be controlled is checked as a way out: the inside handle, lever or bar must open it in one movement with no key, code or card, and stay that way after the work.

5. Written door schedule and Brooklyn price

A door-by-door schedule lists the lock, the reader, who gets which credential, what each door does in a power cut and the price from the Brooklyn list.

6. Hardware, cable and the controller’s own circuit

Frames repaired where needed, strikes and readers fitted, cable run out of sight, and the controller and battery mounted high on a dry wall, powered from the owner’s or common circuit.

7. Credentials, schedules and the household briefing

Each person gets their own code, fob, card or phone key, staff and supplier schedules are set, alerts configured, and each household or manager shown how it works.

8. Power-off test and handover

Every door tested on battery and with all power removed to prove it still lets people out, then administrator accounts and a one-page guide are handed over.

Before the Lunar New Year and before summer: a five-minute door check

Twice a year, open each controlled door and let it close by itself: does it latch without a push? Check the battery level of any keypad in the app before the cold weeks. Read the user list and remove anyone who no longer needs access: a tenant who moved, a cook who left, a supplier you dropped, a relative who has gone home. Open each door from the inside and make sure nothing, a chain, a bolt, a stack of boxes, stands in the way. If anything drags or sticks, a call now is cheaper than a lockout later.

FAQ

Brooklyn Chinatown Access Control FAQ

Is a restaurant that wants readers on both its kitchen door and its cellar stair priced as one job or two?
+

Usually one. Two or more doors on one controller fall on the multi-door line of our Brooklyn list, $4,000 to $15,000, rather than being two single-door jobs added together, because the controller, the power supply and the programming are shared. Where the kitchen door is the only door, the keypad line of $1,500 to $2,000 applies. The written price after the visit shows each door separately so you can see what each one adds.

Our restaurant closes for several days around the Lunar New Year. Can the system tell us if any door opens while we are shut?
+

Yes. A holiday schedule can suspend every staff and supplier credential for the dates you choose, so the only people who can open the doors are the owners. Any opening during those days, whether by an owner’s credential or by force, sends an alert to the owners’ phones, and a door left open sends one too. When you reopen, the normal schedules return by themselves without anyone reprogramming them.

We rent our store. Do we need the landlord’s permission to put a reader on the back door?
+

Almost always, yes. Most commercial leases treat drilling a frame, running cable and adding a power supply as an alteration that needs the landlord’s written consent, and the landlord may also need a key or credential for emergencies. Ask before you book. We can send the landlord a short description of the hardware, where the cable will run and an insurance certificate, which usually makes the conversation quick.

Can the same card that opens our kitchen door also record when each cook clocks in?
+

The door log records when each card was used at each door, and many systems can export that as a report, which some owners use as a rough check on arrival times. It is not a payroll time clock, though: a cook who is let in by a colleague, or who props the door, will not appear. If you need hours you can pay from, use a proper time clock or point-of-sale clock-in and treat the door log as a cross-check.

What should we do with all the old street door keys once everyone has a code or fob?
+

Re-key the mechanical cylinder that stays on the door, so the old keys stop working, and give new keys only to the owner and one emergency holder, kept somewhere safe. Until the cylinder is re-keyed, every old key in circulation can still open the door no matter how good the electronics are. Collecting the old keys is worth doing, but re-keying is what actually ends them.

Our shop’s back door has an old mortise lock that the whole family has keys to. Can it be put on a keypad without changing the lock?
+

Often, yes. An electric strike fitted to the frame can work with the existing latch, so the keypad releases the door while the old lock body stays where it is. If the lock is too worn, or its latch does not line up with a strike, an electrified mortise lock of the same size can replace it in the same pocket. Either way, re-keying the cylinder is what stops the family’s old keys, so decide who still needs one.

A delivery hand truck smashed the keypad on our kitchen door. Is that covered?
+

No. Physical damage, like a cart hitting a keypad or a door being forced, is outside the product warranty, which covers normal wear on AESS-supplied parts. A replacement keypad is quoted as a repair. Where a door takes regular knocks from carts or racks, a steel guard or a reader mounted a little higher and to the side of the traffic path costs far less than replacing a keypad every year, and we will suggest one.

What will the written door schedule show us before we agree to the work?
+

Each door on its own line, with the lock and reader to be fitted, whether the door stays locked or releases in a power cut, how it opens from inside, who gets which credential, any repair the frame or closer needs first, whether an electrician is needed for power, and the price for that door. It also lists anything we noticed on doors outside the job that could stop someone getting out, so you have it in writing either way.

My father lives upstairs with a landline and no smartphone. Can the street door station still ring him?
+

Yes, in two common ways. Some door stations can call an ordinary phone number, including a landline, so he answers the door by picking up his phone and presses a key to release it. Others work with a simple indoor handset or screen in the apartment, which suits someone who prefers a button on the wall to a phone. We ask about every person in the household at the visit so the station is set up for the way each of them answers the door.

Can a reader go behind the glass of our storefront so it is not out on the sidewalk?
+

Sometimes. Some readers can read a card or phone through ordinary glass from a short distance when mounted directly behind it, which keeps them away from rain, stickers and tampering. It does not work through metal-coated or very thick glass, or where a security film has been applied, so we test the actual pane on the visit. Where it will not read, a slim reader on the frame mullion is the next best place.
