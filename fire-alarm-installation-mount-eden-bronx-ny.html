The Neighborhood

Why Mount Eden's Hospital Blocks, Rezoned Jerome Avenue and Shopping Streets Need Fire Alarms Planned Around How Each Space Is Used

Mount Eden is a small neighborhood in Bronx Community District 4, which it shares with Highbridge and the Concourse. Sources draw its edges differently: one CityNeighborhoods outline runs from the Cross Bronx Expressway south to East 167th Street and west to the Edward L. Grant Highway, another puts the Grand Concourse at its eastern edge. On this page Mount Eden means the core that most descriptions share, the blocks south of the Cross Bronx Expressway and north of East 170th Street, between Jerome Avenue and Webster Avenue, with the Grand Concourse running through the middle.

The name goes back to Rachel Eden, who bought the sloping eastern part of the old Anderson Farm in 1820, according to NYC Parks. The parkway named for her got its planted center malls between Walton and Weeks Avenues in 1926. NYC Parks describes those malls as lying next to "Lebanon and Mount Eden Hospitals," and that hospital is still the neighborhood's anchor.

BronxCare's Concourse division stands at 1650 Grand Concourse, at Mount Eden Parkway. Lebanon Hospital moved to the site in 1946, and today the campus includes a 17-story tower with medical, specialty, outpatient and emergency services. BronxCare reports about 127,000 emergency department visits a year across its system, more than 4,500 employees and 859 beds, and it has applied to the New York State Department of Health to add 36 medical-surgical beds in available space at the Concourse division. Practices and pharmacies cluster around a hospital that size, often on the ground floors of apartment buildings never designed for patients.

The second force is the Jerome Avenue rezoning. The city approved the Jerome Avenue Neighborhood Plan in March 2018 (NYC Department of Housing Preservation and Development), and its fact sheet allows buildings of 11 to 16 stories along the elevated line, up to 19 in a few places, with wider commercial uses on Jerome Avenue, East 170th Street and Mount Eden Avenue. Before that, a 2015 city study of the stretch of Jerome and Cromwell Avenues south of the Cross Bronx counted 48 auto-related facilities in 26 blocks and noted "an excessive amount of petroleum and chemical storage in the area" (NYC Mayor's Office of Environmental Remediation). The first new building went up at 1325 Jerome Avenue, where a lot of five older buildings and several auto businesses was cleared for a tower of 14 to 15 stories, about 159 feet in its filing, with 255 apartments. Welcome2TheBronx called it "the tallest building in Mount Eden once completed." Farther up the avenue, 1351 Jerome Avenue added 11 stories and 131 apartments.

The third is the older fabric between them. The Department of City Planning found that more than 80 percent of the housing around the Jerome Avenue corridor was built before 1950, mostly six- to eight-story apartment buildings, and the 2015 study notes that Mount Eden "was spared the well-known abandonment" that emptied other South Bronx neighborhoods in the 1970s and 1980s. Its shopping streets are East 170th Street east of the el, which the same study calls "a robust commercial corridor" of clothing stores, pharmacies, variety stores and food vendors, and Mount Eden Avenue, which planners list as a vibrant local retail street. On September 17, 2025, a fire that started in a botanica on East 174th Street forced the families living above it out, and News 12 Bronx reported that "residents were seen on fire escapes climbing down to get out of their homes and to the ground." The FDNY needed about an hour to bring it under control.

This page covers commercial and code-required fire alarm systems only: the building systems in medical buildings, apartment buildings, mixed-use buildings, schools, stores, garages and new construction. Smoke alarms inside individual one- and two-family homes follow separate rules and are not part of this service.

  • Hospital: BronxCare Concourse division, 1650 Grand Concourse, with a 17-story tower and emergency services; 36 more medical-surgical beds proposed there (BronxCare; Wikipedia).

  • Parks: Claremont Park, 38 acres by the city's count, and the Mount Eden Malls along the parkway (NYC Mayor's Office of Environmental Remediation; NYC Parks).

Building Types

Commercial Fire Alarm Systems for Mount Eden's Building Types, From Walk-Up Storefronts to a Hospital Tower

Commercial fire alarm systems are required by what a building is used for and how big it is, so two buildings on the same block of Mount Eden can need very different systems. These are the types that bring owners and tenants here to a fire alarm design.

The hospital campus

BronxCare's Concourse division is a working hospital with an emergency department, inpatient floors and outpatient services. Hospitals are institutional occupancies, and the NY Engineers summary of the NYC Building Code lists every Group I occupancy as requiring a fire alarm, with voice communication and carbon monoxide detection among the expected features. Fire alarm work inside a hospital is run by the hospital's own facilities department, its design professionals and its approved contractors, under state health rules on top of the city code; we describe it here so neighbors understand the building next door, not to offer hospital work we haven't been asked to do. Hospital fire alarm installation always starts with the hospital's own procedures.

Medical offices and clinics around the hospital

The more common job is a practice moving into a storefront or an upper floor near the hospital. A doctor's or therapist's office is normally a business occupancy. The NY Engineers summary lists a sprinklered business building as needing a fire alarm when it holds at least 500 occupants, more than 100 above or below the level of exit discharge, or a fire area containing an ambulatory health care facility. That last condition is the one to watch in Mount Eden: once a practice treats patients who can't get themselves out, such as people under sedation or recovering from procedures, its space can cross into ambulatory health care, and the fire alarm requirement follows. The design professional makes that call from the practice's actual services.

New rezoning towers on Jerome Avenue

The buildings rising under the 2018 plan are tall apartment buildings with retail, community space and, in at least one case, a school in the base. At about 159 feet in its filing, 1325 Jerome Avenue is well above the 75-foot line at which the NYC Building Code treats a building as a high-rise, measured from the lowest level of fire department vehicle access. Section 403 lists automatic smoke detection, a fire alarm, emergency voice/alarm communication and a fire command center among the features of a high-rise, and the FDNY's 2019 bulletin on command center locations covers residential buildings taller than 125 feet. An 11-story building like 1351 Jerome Avenue is also above 75 feet, so high-rise rules shape its design from the first drawing.

Prewar apartment buildings

Most of Mount Eden's housing is older: five- and six-story walk-ups and elevator buildings from the 1910s through the 1930s. Under the NYC Building Code, as summarized by NY Engineers, an R-2 apartment building needs a fire alarm when dwelling units sit three or more stories above the lowest exit discharge or when it has 16 or more units, so most of these buildings carry some system, often a small one serving cellars, halls and stairs. The work is usually an upgrade: a panel replacement, new corridor devices or a store tenant's devices added to the building's loop. Grand Concourse buildings up to East 174th Street sit inside the Grand Concourse Historic District, so exterior work there may need Landmarks Preservation Commission review.

Stores and small commercial buildings

The NY Engineers summary puts mercantile spaces under a fire alarm requirement when a single fire area exceeds 12,000 square feet, a fire area sits more than three stories above grade, the combined fire area exceeds 24,000 square feet or a fire area below grade exceeds 1,500 square feet. Most single shops on East 170th Street fall below those lines on their own. What brings them into fire alarm work is the building above them: a store in an apartment building with a system usually gets devices that report to the building's panel, and its stockroom in the cellar is often where they go.

Schools in other people's buildings

The code requires a fire alarm in every school, whatever its size. In Mount Eden the notable example is Zeta Charter Schools' Pre-K and first-grade campus at 1325 Jerome Avenue, about 19,000 square feet on the ground and cellar floors of the new apartment building, with 12 classrooms, a cafeteria-gymnatorium and an outdoor playground and amphitheater, according to the architect's project page. A school like that is part of the building's fire alarm design from the start.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Ask Who Can't Leave Before You Ask Where the Panel Goes

On a mixed street like the ones around Mount Eden Parkway, our technicians open every survey with the same question: who in this building would need help getting out? A dental suite with patients in reclined chairs, a therapy office with people using walkers, a pre-K class napping after lunch and an upstairs apartment with an elderly tenant all change what the fire alarm has to accomplish in its first minute.

The second habit is to read the lease before the ceiling. In buildings where a medical practice, a store and the apartments each have a different landlord contact, the drawings have to say whose devices are whose, who holds the central station account and who gets the trouble call at night.

The third is to look next door. A new tower on Jerome Avenue may share a lot line with a body shop or a parts warehouse that has no fire alarm at all, and the new building's air intakes, its cellar walls and its detection near those walls should be planned with that neighbor in mind. These are general working habits; we don't discuss clients' buildings here.

Your Questions Answered

Mount Eden Fire Alarm Questions From Practice Managers, School Operators, Developers and Shop Owners

The hospital edge

BronxCare has applied to add 36 medical-surgical beds in available space at its Concourse division. When unused rooms in a working hospital become a bed unit, how is the fire alarm extended without taking the rest of the building off line?

In phases, with the existing system left in service everywhere else. New devices for the unit are installed and wired while that area is closed off, then programmed into the panel and tested before patients arrive, and the hospital's own procedures decide how the rest of the floor stays protected while walls are open. That work belongs to the hospital and its contractors; for neighbors, the useful lesson is that additions are designed, filed and tested as a modification of the approved system, never simply wired in.

Our practice on the Grand Concourse treats some patients who can't get out of the chair on their own after a procedure. At what point does a medical office stop being ordinary business space for the fire alarm?

When it starts serving people who are rendered incapable of getting themselves out, such as patients under general anesthesia or deep sedation, its space can become an ambulatory health care facility, and the code's business-occupancy rules then call for a fire alarm in that fire area. The design professional reviews the services you actually provide, the number of such patients at a time, and where the suite sits in the building. An exam-room practice where every patient walks in and out stays ordinary business space; a procedure suite often does not.

A hospital's emergency department never closes. Why does a building like that test its fire alarm a few devices at a time instead of all at once?

Because there is never a moment when nobody is inside, and an alarm that sounds everywhere at once would disrupt patient care across the building. Required tests still happen, but they are scheduled by area, with staff told in advance, the central station put on test, and notification silenced or limited where the design allows. Every device still has to be proven over the inspection cycle.

With ambulances heading to the emergency department on the Grand Concourse all day, how do residents and staff nearby tell their building's fire alarm from a siren outside?

A building fire alarm uses a distinct pattern, three pulses and a pause, repeated, and it comes with flashing strobes or, in a voice system, a spoken message. A siren rises and falls and stays outside. The design professional sets the sound levels above the room's normal noise, which near a busy hospital includes traffic, so the alarm is clearly louder than the street.

An imaging practice near the hospital wants to line an X-ray room with lead. Does the shielding change anything for the fire alarm?

The room still needs whatever detection and notification the design calls for, and the shielding affects how the devices get there. Every opening in a lead-lined wall or ceiling has to be planned so radiation protection isn't compromised, so the fire alarm cable route, back boxes and device locations are coordinated with the physicist's shielding plan before the walls close. Strobes have to be visible from where staff and patients actually stand, which in an imaging room is often behind a control window.

Rezoned Jerome Avenue

About 300 Pre-K and first-grade children attend a school in the ground floor and cellar of the 14-story building at 1325 Jerome Avenue. Do those classrooms report to their own fire alarm or to the building's?

That is decided on the building's approved drawings, and the common answer is one fire alarm system for the whole building, with the school's devices, notification and annunciation designed for a school. The school still has its own needs: notification that reaches every classroom and the gym, a display staff can read at the school's office or entrance, and drills that don't confuse residents upstairs. Whatever the arrangement, the school and the building owner both need to know who calls the central station, who meets the FDNY and who pays for repairs inside the school's space.

Is a classroom below street level treated differently by the fire alarm than one on the ground floor?

The code requirement for a school fire alarm doesn't change by floor, but a cellar classroom has practical differences the design has to respect. Children leave by stairs, not straight out a door, so notification has to start early and reach them without delay; strobes matter because young children may not respond to sound alone; and the stairs and exits must be clear on the drawings. The design professional plans the exit path first, and the fire alarm follows it.

Our new building on Jerome Avenue is financed through a city housing program. Does city financing add fire alarm requirements beyond the building code?

The code sets the minimum, and the agency or lender can ask for more in its own design standards or loan documents, so read them before the drawings are final. None of those replace the FDNY filing, the Project Authorization or the acceptance test, which every new building on the avenue goes through the same way.

Our new building went up where auto repair shops stood, and the garages on both sides are still operating. What should our fire alarm designer know about them?

Your fire alarm protects your building, not the garage next door, but the neighbors affect your design. Exhaust and fumes drifting toward your air intakes can reach duct detectors and cause false shutdowns, so intake locations and detector choice are planned with the neighbors in mind. Cellar walls along the lot line may need detection suited to fuel odors and dust, and the lot line itself is where a neighbor's fire would first threaten your building, so the building's sprinkler and fire alarm coverage there should be solid.

The stores in a new Jerome Avenue building were finished as empty shells before any tenant signed. When a tenant finally moves in, who pays to fit its space into the building's fire alarm?

The lease decides, and it should say so before anyone signs. The building's approved system usually covers an empty shell with basic devices; a tenant's layout, walls, kitchen or treatment rooms then require new devices, programming and a filing as a modification of the approved system. Many landlords build the panel with spare capacity and charge the tenant for the work inside the space; others include a fit-out allowance. Either way, the tenant's contractor works from the building's approved drawings and coordinates with the building's fire alarm company, so one set of records stays accurate.

Shops under apartments

When the September 2025 fire started in a botanica on East 174th Street, residents above it climbed down the fire escapes. Does a building fire alarm warn the apartments when the fire is in the store below?

If the building has a system and the store's devices report to it, yes: detection in the store sets off the building alarm, and notification sounds in the halls and, where designed, the apartments. Many older buildings with small stores have no such link, so the families upstairs depend on their own apartment smoke alarms and on noticing smoke. When a store renovates, or the building upgrades its panel, ask the design professional whether the store can report to the building's system; it is often the cheapest way to give upstairs residents a head start.

BronxCare runs more than 30 practices out in the community. If a hospital-run practice opens in a storefront under apartments, does the hospital's own fire alarm standard come with it?

The city code applies to the storefront the same way it applies to any practice of that type, and the building owner's approved system is what the space ties into. A hospital system may have its own internal standards for equipment, monitoring or testing that go further, and those become part of the tenant's scope if the landlord agrees. The practical work is coordination: the hospital's facilities staff, the landlord's fire alarm company and the design professional agreeing on one set of drawings, so the storefront doesn't end up with two incompatible systems.

Equipment

Types of Commercial Fire Alarm Systems We Install in Mount Eden

People who ask what is a commercial fire alarm system are usually describing it already: the building-wide network of detectors, manual stations, horns, strobes and speakers wired to a control panel that watches the building, warns the people inside, runs other equipment and reports to a central station. If you are wondering how does a commercial fire alarm system work, the sequence is short. A detector senses smoke or heat, or someone pulls a station; the panel checks the signal, then sounds notification, recalls elevators, releases door holders, stops fans and sends the alarm to the central station, which calls the FDNY. Commercial fire detection systems come in a few kinds, and these are the ones that fit Mount Eden's buildings.

Addressable

Anyone asking what is an addressable fire alarm system wants to know why it costs more to design. Each device has its own address, so the panel reports "cellar classroom 3" or "suite 2B" instead of a zone. Addressable fire alarm installation, addressable fire alarm system installation and addressable fire alarm panel installation are standard in the new Jerome Avenue towers.

Conventional

As for what is a conventional fire alarm system, the devices are wired in zones and the panel reports only which zone is in alarm. Conventional fire alarm installation, conventional fire alarm system installation and conventional fire alarm panel installation still make sense for a single small shop or office.

Choosing between them

On what is the difference between addressable and conventional fire alarms, the short answer is precision and supervision: addressable panels name the device and warn when a detector is dirty, which saves time in a building with a school, a clinic and apartments under one panel.

Voice evacuation

Fire alarm voice evacuation installation and fire alarm emergency voice communication for high-rise buildings and larger schools, with fire alarm speaker installation and fire alarm speaker strobe installation so a message can tell the school to leave and the residents upstairs to wait for instructions.

Intelligent detection

Intelligent fire alarm installation and an intelligent fire detection system use detectors that adjust for slow drift and report when they need cleaning, which helps in storefronts under the el and cellars beside garages.

Air sampling

Aspirating smoke detection installation, an air sampling smoke detection system, VESDA installation and VESDA service for imaging and server rooms in medical buildings, high-ceiling spaces and rooms where spot detectors are hard to reach. Data center fire detection installation for a practice's or school's network room often starts here.

Devices

Fire Alarm Devices in Mount Eden Buildings and Where Each One Goes

Fire alarm equipment installation in Mount Eden ranges from a lead-lined imaging room on the Grand Concourse to a parts room on Jerome Avenue. Fire detection device installation and fire alarm initiating device installation cover the devices that tell the panel something is wrong; notification appliances tell the people.

Smoke detectors

Commercial smoke detector installation covers corridors, exam-room hallways, electrical and telecom rooms, classrooms and lobbies as the design requires. Addressable smoke detector installation and photoelectric smoke detector installation are the usual choice. Commercial smoke detector replacement, commercial smoke detector testing and commercial smoke detector repair are routine in older buildings, and smoke detector circuit troubleshooting on a conventional loop often turns up one bad splice in a damp cellar. The FDNY's design tips call for detectors at least 36 inches from supply diffusers and return openings, which matters when a medical suite adds ceiling air outlets during a fit-out.

FAQ

Mount Eden Fire Alarm Installation FAQ

Why does a new building's elevator machine room get a heat detector right next to the sprinkler head?
+

Where a machine room or hoistway has sprinklers, the elevator's power is usually arranged to shut off before water reaches the equipment, and a heat detector set below the sprinkler's temperature tells the fire alarm to trip that power. The design professional shows the detector, the shutdown relay and the smoke detector for elevator recall on the drawings, and all three are proven at the acceptance test.

Can the stair pressurization fans in a new 14-story building be started by the fire alarm?
+

Yes, where the building is designed with them. The fire alarm sends the start signal through a control module, the fans' status reports back to the panel, and the fire command area gets manual controls. The smoke control sequence is written by the design professional and tested with the mechanical contractor, so both trades must be on site for that part of the FDNY test.

What are the phone jacks in the stairwells and lobby of a new high-rise, and are they part of the fire alarm?
+

They are firefighter phone stations, part of the emergency voice and communication system in many high-rises. Firefighters plug in a handset and talk to the command area while they work. They are supervised by the fire alarm like any other circuit, so a damaged jack shows as a trouble, and they are tested at the acceptance test and in later inspections.

How are fire alarm devices in a school gymnatorium protected from balls and climbing?
+

Detectors, strobes and speakers in play spaces get wire guards listed for that device, or a model built for impact, and they are placed where they won't be hit by equipment or used as a handhold. Guards must not block a detector's airflow or a strobe's light, which is why they are chosen from the manufacturer's listed accessories rather than made on site.

Do the outdoor playground and amphitheater of a school in a residential building need fire alarm devices?
+

Open-air play areas generally aren't fitted with fire alarm devices, but the people in them still have to know about an alarm. The school's emergency plan covers how staff outside are told, and where the design professional decides that an exterior horn or strobe is needed so children and teachers outdoors hear the building's alarm, it is shown on the drawings.

The school in our building has triple-pane windows to block subway noise. Does that make the fire alarm easier or harder to hear inside?
+

Usually easier. Notification is designed to be clearly louder than the normal sound in each room, and quieter rooms need less to stand out. The windows don't stop the building's own horns or speakers, which are inside. The design professional still checks sound levels in each classroom, and the acceptance test confirms them.

Why is there a smoke detector right above the fire alarm control panel in our cellar?
+

NFPA 72 calls for detection at the panel location when that room isn't staffed around the clock, so a fire near the panel is reported before it can damage the panel itself. In a cellar beside storage or a boiler room, the design professional may choose a heat detector instead where smoke detectors would false-alarm.

How does a firefighter outside a closed store with a roll-down gate on East 170th Street know the alarm came from inside it?
+

The building's panel or annunciator tells them which space is in alarm, so the display at the entrance firefighters use is the first stop. Where the design calls for it, a remote indicator near the storefront shows that the store's devices are in alarm. Keys or emergency access for the gate are a matter for the owner's emergency plan, and the central station's call list should include someone who can open it.

Can a horn strobe be mounted on a glass storefront wall?
+

Not directly on the glass. Devices need a solid mounting surface and a listed back box, so they go on a wall, a column or a mullion where the manufacturer's instructions allow, at the height the code requires. The design professional picks locations where the strobe is seen from inside the store and the cable can reach without running across the glazing.

Our waiting room has rows of fixed seats. Does that make it an assembly space for the fire alarm?
+

Not by itself. A waiting room that serves a medical practice is usually part of the practice's business occupancy. Assembly rules turn on how many people gather and for what, so a large room used for events, classes or worship is reviewed differently. The design professional calculates occupant load from the floor plan and use.

In a dental or procedure room, where does the strobe go so a patient lying back in the chair can see it?
+

Strobes are placed so their light reaches every part of the room, including people lying down, using the spacing and wall or ceiling locations NFPA 72 allows for the room's size. In treatment rooms that often means a ceiling-mounted strobe or one positioned in the patient's line of sight. Staff are still the patients' first warning, so the practice's emergency plan says who helps each chair.

Does the generator test in a new building affect the fire alarm?
+

It shouldn't cause an alarm, but switching power can produce a short trouble signal if the panel or a power supply sees the change. The panel's own batteries carry it through the transfer. If every monthly generator test leaves a trouble on the panel, have the fire alarm company check the power supply settings and the batteries rather than ignoring it.
