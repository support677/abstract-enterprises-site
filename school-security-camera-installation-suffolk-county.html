The Vocabulary on Your Quote, Explained Plainly

NVR / DVR

Recorder generations. NVRs accept digital IP camera streams over network cable; DVRs digitize old analog coax. What’s in your closet decides whether the project is a swap, a hybrid, or a clean start.

PoE

Power over Ethernet: one cable feeds a camera both juice and data, letting a head reach a dugout roof or gate post without hiring an electrician to chase power across the property.

WDR

Wide dynamic range — the imaging trick that holds a readable face in a dark doorway against a sunlit parking lot behind it. Every south-facing Suffolk entrance needs it.

Person / Vehicle Detection

Classification that alerts on humans and cars while ignoring everything else. In a county where deer walk school fields nightly, this single setting decides whether staff trust the alerts or mute them.

Retention

How many days of video exist before the recorder overwrites itself. We build Suffolk schools to a thirty-day floor, expandable to sixty or ninety with drive capacity.

NDAA §889

The federal exclusion list. If NPSE, SSBA, or federal safety money funds the purchase, certain manufacturers are off the table — our default hardware clears the rule before it’s asked.

EoC

Ethernet-over-coax: adapters that push modern IP video down cable installed fifteen years ago. When the old copper meters clean, your invoice shrinks and your ceilings stay shut.

IK10 / IP66

Impact resistance and weather sealing. Gyms and any camera a person can reach get the first; anything tasting salt off the Sound or the Atlantic gets the second, with stainless hardware.

LPR

License-plate reading at driveways, lot entrances, and wherever buses and vans sleep. Every vehicle that enters becomes a searchable record with a timestamp attached.

New York Camera Law for Suffolk Schools, in Plain Language

The Facial-Recognition Door Is Shut

Since the State Education Department’s September 2023 determination, no New York school — district, charter, Catholic, independent, or religious — may purchase or operate facial-recognition technology. A Huntington academy and a Montauk district building answer to the identical rule. Ordinary video — cameras, recorders, motion analytics — was never touched by the order and remains fully lawful. Our installs ship with any face-matching capability disabled in firmware, and the disabled state is written into your commissioning file so the question dies the first time it’s asked.

Where a Lens May Point

Penal Law 250.45 makes recording in bathrooms, locker rooms, changing areas, or anywhere with a reasonable expectation of privacy a felony — schools included, no exceptions. Beyond those spaces the property is fair coverage: entries, corridors, stairs, cafeteria, gym, lots, fields, driveways, sheds, mechanical rooms, perimeter. Classroom interiors we treat as a governance call for your board and counsel; our default Suffolk drawing watches each classroom door from the corridor, so comings and goings log without a camera inside the room, and any deliberate exception is signed onto the plan. Where a head could catch a neighbor’s yard or a public road, we compose the shot onto school ground and note it in the survey record.

When Footage Becomes a Student Record

Federal FERPA rules can fold identifiable video into a student’s education record once it drives a disciplinary outcome — bringing parental review rights and disclosure limits with it — and New York’s Ed Law 2-d regulates any outside vendor holding student-identifiable data. The architecture that keeps all of this simple is the one we install by default: recorder inside your building, drives on your asset list, a written review-and-release procedure delivered at handover, and cloud copies only under a 2-d-compliant contract. The first angry-parent phone call then lands on a policy instead of a panic.

District projects draw on the state’s $2 billion Smart Schools bond, which covers camera systems as long as facial recognition stays out of the scope entirely. “Our school sits on 11 acres. How do you even camera that?”

Is facial recognition allowed at any Suffolk school?

No. New York’s State Education Department closed that door in September 2023 for every school in the state — district, charter, Catholic, independent, and religious equally. Conventional video was never part of the prohibition: cameras, recording, and motion analytics remain fully lawful. On our systems, face-matching features ship disabled at the firmware level and the disabled state is documented in your commissioning file.

Can cameras go inside classrooms?

Frequently lawful, never our default. We put the choice where it belongs — your board and counsel — and our standard Suffolk drawing covers each classroom doorway from the corridor instead, logging entries and exits with no lens inside the teaching space. If a board deliberately elects in-room coverage, that exception is initialed on the plan before a bracket goes up.

Which areas can never be recorded?

Bathrooms, locker rooms, changing areas, and any space where privacy is reasonably expected — Penal Law 250.45 classifies recording there as a felony and grants schools no exemption. The rest of the property is lawful coverage: entries, halls, stairs, cafeteria, gym, lots, fields, driveways, sheds, mechanical rooms, and perimeter, with any street- or neighbor-facing shot composed onto school ground and logged that way at survey.
