The Neighborhood

Why New York City Apartments Need Soundbar Installation

A soundbar is the easiest audio upgrade you can buy and one of the trickiest to put up well in a New York apartment. In a suburban house the job is a stud finder, two screws and a cable tucked behind a cabinet. Here the wall behind the TV might be hundred-year-old plaster on wood lath, a brick wall shared with the building next door, poured concrete in a post-war tower, or a metal-stud partition that a renovation added last year. The person downstairs is a few feet below your subwoofer. The building has a managing agent who wants paperwork before anyone drills, and the only way up may be five flights of stairs or a freight elevator that runs on its own timetable.

Those conditions are why so many soundbars in the city end up sitting on a console with a cable hanging down the wall, or bolted up with the plastic anchors from the box and slowly sagging out of a plaster wall. The bar itself is rarely the problem. Most current models — Sonos, Samsung, LG, Sony, Bose, Vizio and the rest — sound good when they are placed close to the screen, connected on the right port and set up for the room. What goes wrong is everything around the bar: an anchor that is wrong for the wall, a bracket that leaves the bar blocking the TV's infrared sensor, an HDMI cable in the wrong socket so the TV remote stops controlling the volume, or a subwoofer parked in a corner on a wooden floor where it shakes the ceiling of the apartment below.

Noise is the part New Yorkers worry about most, and with reason. Residents post to Reddit's r/AskNYC about bass "coming through the floor fairly regularly since we moved in", and the standard advice in those threads is to call 311. In a thread asking for neighbour-friendly soundbars, the practical answer was that bass frequencies travel easily, so either skip the subwoofer or choose one you can turn down. That matches what we see in practice: the soundbar's dialogue and mid-range stay mostly inside your walls, while low bass travels through floors, joists and shared structure. A good installation in this city is as much about where the subwoofer sits, what it sits on and how it is set as it is about the bracket.

The paperwork is the other city-specific layer. Co-op and condo buildings usually have house rules or an alteration agreement that covers contractors, and on r/AskNYC the plain advice to someone renovating a co-op was that it is standard for co-ops to insist on a certificate of insurance before any work is done. A soundbar on two screws is not a renovation, but the managing agent, the doorman and the superintendent still want to know who is coming, when, and whether they are insured. We issue that certificate free, name the building and its agent on it, and send it before the appointment so the front desk has it waiting.

Renters have a different set of limits. Many leases restrict holes in walls or require the apartment to be returned in its original condition, and nobody wants to lose a deposit over a cable channel. For tenants we lean toward console placement, brackets that hang the bar from the TV mount rather than the wall, and paintable cord covers instead of in-wall work, and we tell you plainly which of those choices leaves the fewest marks to fill when you move out.

Most New York soundbars are installed in older multi-family buildings, onto walls that need the right anchor, in apartments where sound carries to people who can and do complain. Getting it right means choosing the mounting method for the wall, keeping the bar close to the screen so dialogue lines up with the picture, and setting the bass for an apartment rather than a detached house.

Most New Yorkers who call about a soundbar are also mounting or re-mounting the TV above it, so the two are usually done in one visit. What We Install

Soundbar Installation Services in NYC

Every soundbar job we do in the five boroughs is one of the services below, or a combination of them. Each has a published price, and each finishes the same way: the bar level and centred, the cables dressed or hidden, the TV remote controlling the volume, and a quick listen with you at the couch before we pack up.

Wall Mount Under the TV

The most common request in New York apartments. The bar goes on its bracket a short distance below the screen, centred and level, anchored with fixings chosen for the wall we actually find — toggles in plaster, sleeve anchors in brick, concrete anchors in a slab-era tower.

Console or Stand Placement

For renters and anyone who doesn't want holes: we place the bar on the media console, connect it to the TV, set it so it doesn't block the TV's sensor, and dress the cables behind the furniture. Often the right answer in a rental.

TV-Attached Soundbar Brackets

A bracket that clamps the bar to the TV mount instead of the wall, so the bar moves with a full-motion arm and the wall gets no extra holes. Sanus makes models for this, including one designed for the Sonos Arc.

TV and Soundbar in One Visit

Mounting a new TV and the soundbar under it together, so spacing, height and cable route are planned once. Common when people move into a new apartment or upgrade both at the same time.

In-Wall Wire Concealment

Power and HDMI hidden inside the wall using a code-compliant in-wall power and cable kit, where the wall can be opened and the building allows it — usually owners in co-ops and condos rather than renters.

Paintable Cord Covers

A slim surface channel from the bar down to the outlet, painted to match the wall. The renter's answer to dangling wires, and the practical one on brick and concrete where in-wall work isn't possible.

HDMI eARC and Remote Setup

Connecting the bar to the TV's eARC or ARC port, switching on HDMI-CEC so one remote runs everything, fixing lip-sync, and updating the bar's firmware and app on your Wi-Fi.

Subwoofer and Rear Speaker Placement

Pairing wireless subwoofers and surrounds, finding the spot where the bass is even at the couch but not in the floor, and setting levels for an apartment rather than a house.

Moving a Soundbar to a New Apartment

Taking the bar and bracket down at the old place, filling nothing we didn't make, and reinstalling it at the new one on the right anchors for a wall that is probably completely different.

Building Specialists

Soundbar Installation for New York City's Architecture

The soundbar is the same in every building. The wall, the floor and the rules are not. These are the building types we install in most across the five boroughs and what each one asks of a soundbar job.

Pre-War Elevator Buildings

Plaster on wood or metal lath, often with masonry behind it, high ceilings and solid floors. The plaster cracks if it is drilled carelessly and the old lath doesn't hold a screw on its own, so fixings go through to something solid or spread the load behind the plaster. Doormen, superintendents and a managing agent are the norm.

Brownstones and Row Houses

Party walls of brick shared with the next house, plaster finishes and floors of wood joists. The TV wall is often the party wall. Brick holds well with the right sleeve anchors, but bass travels into the next house and the floors below more easily than people expect.

Walk-Ups and Tenements

Four to six storeys with no elevator, narrow stairs, small rooms and walls that have been patched many times. Planning the visit to carry everything in one trip matters, and so does a mounting method that tolerates uneven plaster.

Post-War Towers and Mitchell-Lama Complexes

Concrete floor slabs, block or concrete walls, and lower ceilings. Concrete makes a very strong fixing once drilled, but it needs a hammer drill, takes more time and creates dust we contain. Sound between floors is better than in wood-joist buildings but not immune to bass.

New Condos and Gut-Renovated Units

Metal-stud partitions with gypsum board, sometimes with resilient channel for sound. Metal studs need the right fasteners, not wood screws, and the manufacturer's in-box hardware often assumes wood. Owners here are the most likely to want in-wall wiring.

Garden Apartments and Low-Rise Co-ops

Common in Queens and parts of the Bronx: brick buildings around shared courtyards, plaster walls and a board with its own contractor rules. Jobs here are mostly wall mounts under an existing TV with surface cord covers.

How we mount a soundbar on each kind of New York wall

Plaster on wood lath. We find the studs or furring behind the plaster before drilling and use them where the bracket lines up. Where it doesn't, we use spring or snap toggles sized for the bracket so the load spreads behind the plaster rather than relying on the lath. Holes are started slowly with a sharp masonry bit and no hammer action, which is what keeps plaster from cracking around the hole. A soundbar is light compared with a TV, but a bar bumped while cleaning will still loosen a weak anchor over time.

Plaster over brick or block. Common on exterior and party walls in pre-war buildings. We drill through the plaster into the masonry and set sleeve or screw anchors in the brick, keeping holes in the brick itself rather than in the mortar joint where possible. This is one of the strongest fixings in the city once done.

Exposed brick. Loft and brownstone living rooms often have it. Fixings go into brick with anchors rated for masonry, and the cable route is surface-only because there is no cavity to hide wire in, so a painted or brick-toned cord cover is the usual finish.

Concrete and cinder block. Hammer drill, concrete anchors, and dust extraction or a vacuum held at the bit. Concrete walls take longer and carry a surcharge on our price list for that reason. They are also where in-wall concealment is generally impossible.

Metal studs and gypsum board. We locate the studs and use fasteners made for light-gauge steel, or toggles in the board where the bracket doesn't line up with a stud. Wood screws into metal studs strip; that is the most common failure we find on bars someone else installed in new buildings.

Tile and stone. Rare behind a soundbar but it happens in media walls and in some lobbies and offices. Tile is drilled with diamond or carbide tile bits at low speed, and there is always some risk of cracking a tile, which we explain before we start.

Many owners doing a soundbar in a renovated unit also have us run network cable to the TV wall at the same time, so the bar, the TV and the streaming box are wired rather than relying on Wi-Fi. Our structured cabling installation in NYC is often scoped on the same visit.

Commercial Soundbar Installation

Business Soundbar Installation in NYC

Soundbars are not only for living rooms. In the city they are the simplest way to make a conference-room display, a waiting-room TV or a bar's game-day screen intelligible without installing a full sound system. These are the commercial settings we work in most and what each tends to need.

Midtown and Flatiron Offices

Conference rooms where a large display handles video calls and the built-in TV speakers can't carry voices to the far end of the table. A soundbar under the display, on eARC, fixes that for a fraction of the cost of ceiling audio.

Downtown Brooklyn and Long Island City Workspaces

Smaller offices and co-working suites in converted and new buildings, often with metal-stud partitions and glass walls. Mounting into board and steel, with cables run through the wall or in tidy raceway.

Bars and Restaurants

From Bedford Avenue in Williamsburg to Main Street in Flushing and Arthur Avenue in the Bronx, screens over bars and dining rooms need audio people can hear at a table without filling the street. A soundbar per screen, or a bar feeding a small zone, is common.

Medical and Dental Waiting Rooms

A wall TV with captions and low-level sound. Bars here are set for clarity at modest volume, mounted clear of walkways, with cables fully concealed for a clean, cleanable finish.

Salons, Barbershops and Gyms

Small storefronts along every borough's commercial strips, where a TV and soundbar run all day. Durable mounting, tamper-resistant brackets and a remote that staff can't lose.

Building Lobbies and Amenity Rooms

Residential towers with lounges, screening rooms and gyms. The managing agent or the board decides, the work is scheduled around residents, and the audio is kept within the room's walls.

Compliance callouts for commercial soundbars in New York City

  • Noise Code limits for music. The city's Noise Code, as summarised by the Department of Environmental Protection, limits commercial establishments that play music to 42 decibels measured inside nearby residences, and to 7 decibels over ambient on the street 15 feet or more away between 10 PM and 7 AM, with a separate bass limit of 6 dB(C) over ambient. We set bars and subwoofers with those limits in mind and show staff where the volume ceiling sits.
  • Protruding objects in walkways. Under the 2010 ADA Standards, objects mounted on a wall between 27 and 80 inches above the floor in a circulation path should not stick out more than 4 inches. In corridors and waiting rooms we mount soundbars above that zone, in a recess, or out of the path of travel.
  • Low-voltage work and permits. Connecting a soundbar and running its low-voltage cables does not need its own Department of Buildings permit, but new or moved outlets are electrical work for a licensed electrician, and any work in a commercial building still follows the landlord's contractor rules, insurance requirements and hours.
  • Certificates of insurance. Commercial landlords routinely ask for a COI naming them and their managing agent. We issue it free, usually the same day it is requested.
  • Cable listing. HDMI and speaker cables run inside walls or above ceilings use in-wall or plenum-rated cable where the space requires it, not the patch cables that came in the box.

Many businesses that add soundbars to their screens also want the cameras and the network behind them upgraded. Our low-voltage installation services in NYC cover audio, data and security under one licence.

Your Questions Answered

Soundbar Installation in NYC: Questions Answered

These are the questions New Yorkers ask before booking a soundbar install, grouped by what they are really trying to work out: whether the building will allow it, whether the wall will hold it, whether the neighbours will hear it, and how it fits a rental or a small room. Price, warranty and scheduling questions are in the FAQ further down.

Building approvals and paperwork

Does a co-op or condo board have to approve a soundbar wall mount?

Usually not as a formal alteration, but check your house rules before booking. Alteration agreements in New York co-ops and condos are written for renovations — moving walls, plumbing, electrical — and a soundbar on a bracket with a few anchors normally falls below that line. What most buildings do require for any contractor visit, however small, is notice to the managing agent or the front desk, a certificate of insurance on file, and work inside set hours. Some boards also have rules about drilling into particular walls, especially exterior or party walls, or about running anything on the outside of a wall.

The quick way to find out is to ask the managing agent or the superintendent one question: "Do you need anything from a contractor who is mounting a soundbar and running cables?" If the answer involves an alteration agreement, in-wall wiring is usually what triggers it, and we can switch to a cord cover or a TV-attached bracket to keep the job below that threshold. If the work does need approval, we provide the scope, the insurance and the method so the board has what it needs.

What will the managing agent want on a certificate of insurance before you come up?

Most Manhattan and Brooklyn managing agents ask for a certificate showing general liability and workers' compensation, with the building's owner and the managing agent named as additional insureds, often with specific wording they supply. Many give you a sample certificate or a PDF of their contractor requirements. Some also want the building's exact legal name, which is not always the name on the awning, and limits that meet their minimums.

Send us whatever the building sends you, or just the managing agent's email, and we issue the certificate free, usually the same day. We send it to the agent directly and copy you, and we bring a copy to the appointment, because the most common reason a soundbar install in this city starts late is a doorman who hasn't seen the COI.

Our building only lets contractors use the freight elevator at set hours — does that change the appointment?

It changes when we book it, not what it costs. Many elevator buildings require contractors to use the service elevator and to arrive within a window, typically weekday business hours, and some don't allow contractor work at weekends at all. We ask about this when you book and pick a 2-hour arrival window that sits inside the building's hours, with enough time left to finish before the elevator or the work window closes.

A soundbar visit is short, so the building's hours rarely cause a problem. Where they would — a Saturday you wanted but the building forbids — we tell you before booking rather than turning up to be sent away. If we do arrive and can't start because the building refuses access, that is the situation our $150 return-trip fee covers, which is exactly why we check first.

Old walls and new walls

Can a soundbar bracket go into pre-war plaster and lath without cracking it?

Yes, if the plaster is drilled the right way and the fixing doesn't rely on the lath. Pre-war plaster cracks when it is hammer-drilled, when a dull bit wanders, or when a screw is driven into lath that splits. We locate the studs or masonry behind the plaster first, drill with a sharp bit and no hammer action, and either fix through to the stud or use toggles that spread the load behind the plaster. Soundbars are light, so the load itself is modest; the risk is in the drilling.

Plaster that is already loose, bulging or cracked behind the TV is a different case. We check it by tapping and pressing around the mounting area before drilling. If it is coming away from the lath, we tell you and suggest either a TV-attached bracket or a console placement until the wall is repaired, because no anchor can hold in plaster that isn't attached to anything. Plaster walls carry a $45 surcharge on our price list for the extra care involved.

The wall behind my TV is a brick party wall — is that a problem?

For holding the bracket, no; brick is one of the most secure walls to mount on once it is drilled with a masonry bit and fitted with sleeve or screw anchors. The considerations are different. A party wall is shared with the next building or apartment, so some buildings restrict drilling into it, and bass from a subwoofer against that wall travels into the neighbour's space more easily than from an interior partition.

On party walls we keep the subwoofer off the wall and away from the corner, mount the bar with masonry anchors, and run cables in a surface cover because there is no cavity behind brick. Brick and stone carry a $65 surcharge on our price list. If the building has a rule about party-wall drilling, a TV-attached bracket keeps the bar off the wall completely.

How do you anchor into the concrete walls of a post-war high-rise?

With a hammer drill, carbide bits and concrete anchors, and with a vacuum held at the hole to keep dust out of the apartment. Many post-war towers and Mitchell-Lama complexes in the city have poured-concrete or concrete-block walls, sometimes with a thin skim of plaster. They make an excellent fixing once drilled, but drilling takes longer and is louder, which is why concrete and cinder block carry a $75 surcharge and why we schedule within the building's quiet-work hours.

Concrete also decides the cable route. You can't fish a wire through a solid wall, so the choice is a surface cord cover painted to match, or routing the cable down behind the TV to the console. We show you both before drilling so you choose the finish, not us.

Neighbours, bass and quiet hours

Will a soundbar with a subwoofer bring noise complaints from the apartment below?

It can, and in New York it is the most common complaint a soundbar causes. The bar on its own mostly plays dialogue and mid-range sound that walls and floors block reasonably well. The subwoofer produces low frequencies that pass through floors, joists and shared structure, which is why the person below hears thumping long after the voices have disappeared. City residents filed hundreds of thousands of residential noise complaints with 311 last year, and loud music made up the largest share.

The answer is setup rather than doing without. We place the sub where it gives even bass at the couch at a lower level, set it on an isolation pad or a rug rather than bare wood, turn the sub level down against the bar during setup, and turn on the bar's night or dialogue mode so you can use it late at low volume. Many soundbars can also be used without the subwoofer altogether, and for some apartments that is the honest recommendation.

Where should the subwoofer sit when there are neighbours underneath?

Out of the corners, off bare floorboards and away from shared walls. Corners boost bass and push more of it into the structure; a floor of wood joists acts like a drum skin under a subwoofer sitting directly on it. In most New York living rooms the best spot is along the front wall near the TV, a little out from the corner, on a dense rug or an isolation platform, with the port or driver facing into the room.

We test a few positions with you on the day, sitting where you actually watch, and settle on the one that gives the most even bass at the lowest sub level. If the room has a wall shared with a neighbour, we keep the sub on the opposite side of the room where that's practical.

Does the city noise code reach a TV soundbar inside my own apartment?

Yes, in the sense that it covers sound from your apartment that reaches someone else's. The city's 311 guidance lists loud music, parties and television among the neighbour-noise complaints it takes, and the Noise Code sets limits between 10 PM and 7 AM measured inside the property receiving the sound. In practice, enforcement on residential neighbour noise is slow, and the real consequences in an apartment building are usually a letter from the managing agent or a lease or house-rule issue rather than a fine.

Many leases and co-op house rules add quiet hours of their own. The setup we do — sub off the floor and corners, sensible levels, night mode on the remote — is aimed at keeping you well within those rules without making movies sound thin. This is general information, not legal advice; your lease and house rules are what apply to you.

Renting and small rooms

I rent and can't open the walls — how do the soundbar wires get hidden?

Without cutting anything. The usual renter's setup is a paintable cord cover: a slim channel stuck or screwed to the wall that runs from the soundbar down to the outlet or console, painted the wall colour so it disappears at normal viewing distance. On our price list it is $59. Where the TV and bar sit on a console, cables go behind the furniture and are tied off so nothing shows from the room.

A third option is a TV-attached bracket for the soundbar, so the bar hangs from the TV mount and its cables follow the TV's cables behind the screen. That keeps the number of wall holes to whatever the TV needed. In-wall concealment, at $169 with a code-compliant in-wall power and cable kit, is for owners or tenants whose landlord has agreed in writing.

Will bracket holes cost me my security deposit?

That depends on your lease and your landlord, not on us, so read the lease's clause on alterations and wall holes before booking, or ask the landlord directly. Many New York leases treat small anchor holes as normal wear; others require walls to be restored. A soundbar bracket usually needs two to four holes, smaller than those a TV mount needs.

If holes are a concern, we steer the job toward console placement or a TV-attached bracket. Our price does not include patching or painting, so if you will need holes filled at move-out, a painter or handyman does that at the end of the lease. We are happy to tell you exactly where and how many holes a given option needs before you decide.

Is a full soundbar too much for a small studio or one-bedroom living room?

Usually not, but the size of the bar should match the room and the TV. In a small New York living room, where the couch might be eight or nine feet from the screen, a compact bar often sounds more even than a large one and is far easier on the neighbours. A bar roughly the width of the TV, or a little narrower, looks right and keeps sound centred on the picture.

The bigger risk in small rooms is the subwoofer and the surround effects. A large sub in a room of 150 square feet can overwhelm it, and up-firing height effects depend on a flat ceiling at a sensible height. We set up the bar for the room you have, and if you are still choosing, we will tell you plainly where a compact model will serve you as well as a flagship.

Why is TV dialogue hard to follow, and will a soundbar actually fix it?

Thin TVs have small speakers, often firing downwards or backwards, and modern film and TV mixes put a lot of dynamic range between quiet speech and loud effects. The result is the familiar routine of turning up the volume for voices and down again for explosions, which in an apartment means the neighbours hear the explosions. A soundbar with a dedicated centre channel and a dialogue or speech-enhancement mode is one of the most effective fixes.

Placement matters as much as the bar. A bar at roughly seated ear height, close beneath the screen and not tucked behind a console lip, sends speech straight at the couch. During setup we turn on speech enhancement and night mode where the bar has them, and test with a dialogue-heavy scene so you hear the difference before we leave.

How close should the soundbar sit to the TV when the couch is only a few feet away?

As close beneath the screen as the bar's height and the TV's sensors allow, usually a gap of an inch or two. Dolby's own guidance is that the bar and the TV should be as close together as practical and that below the screen is usually best, and Yamaha suggests aiming for roughly seated ear height. In a small room, where you sit close, a bar far below the TV makes voices seem to come from the floor.

We check that the bar doesn't cover the TV's infrared receiver or its stand, that the front of the bar isn't blocked by a console edge, and that any up-firing drivers have open space above them. Then we set the TV's height and the bar's together, so the picture is at eye level and the sound is just beneath it.

Getting in and getting parked

Will you carry the gear up a fifth-floor walk-up?

Yes. AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Soundbar Installation in NYC

A note on method first: Google's AI Overview wasn't viewable from the tools used to research this page, so none of the quotes below are presented as its words. Each heading is a claim that a real cost calculator, retailer menu, manufacturer guide or forum thread publishes, and that answer engines tend to pass on. Under each one is what changes once the soundbar is going into an apartment in one of the five boroughs.

"Installing a soundbar costs about $58 to $231"

That range comes from Homewyse's national calculator, which in September 2026 estimated the average cost to install a soundbar starting at $58.43 to $231 per bar. It is a reasonable national figure, and it is the kind of number a summary will quote as the answer.

A national average assumes an average wall. In New York the wall behind the TV is more often plaster, brick or concrete than wood-stud drywall, and each takes different tools and time. Add a fifth-floor walk-up, a building that wants a certificate of insurance, or a Manhattan parking garage, and the same job costs a different amount.

That is why we publish the parts instead of a range: $79 to place a bar, $129 to wall-mount it, $84.99 for a Sanus bracket, and a fixed surcharge per wall type. You can add up your own job before we arrive.

"A soundbar is just a small add-on to TV mounting"

Thumbtack's data puts the national average for TV wall mounting at $121 to $234, and cost guides often treat the soundbar as a minor extra on top. Big-box service menus also list soundbar setup and soundbar mounting as separate flat items, which reinforces the idea that it is a quick add-on.

Sometimes it is. But the soundbar is where most of the problems surface after the TV is up: the wrong HDMI port, CEC switched off, a bar that covers the TV's remote sensor, lip-sync drift, a sub that rattles the floor. Treating it as an afterthought is why so many people end up controlling the TV and the bar with two remotes.

We price the soundbar as its own line, and we don't leave until the TV remote turns the bar on and controls its volume, and dialogue sounds right from the couch.

"Just use the wall bracket and screws that come in the box"

Setup guides and some installers' blogs tell readers the in-box hardware will do, while others warn that it won't hold in city walls. Manufacturers' own manuals, such as Yamaha's and Denon's, carry precautions about checking that the wall can bear the bar and using fixings suited to it.

The bracket is usually fine; the anchors are the issue. Plastic plugs are made for solid material and grip poorly in old plaster; wood screws strip in metal studs; brick and concrete need masonry anchors. A bar that looks secure on day one can loosen within months from vibration and the occasional bump.

We use the manufacturer's bracket where it is suitable and our own fixings chosen for the wall, which is what the wall-type surcharges on our price list pay for.

"You need eARC to get Dolby Atmos"

A very common claim, and only partly true. Standard ARC can carry Dolby Atmos inside a compressed Dolby Digital Plus stream, which is how most streaming services deliver it. eARC is needed for lossless Atmos in Dolby TrueHD, which comes from 4K Blu-ray discs and some players and consoles.

The practical consequence is that people buy new TVs or cables they don't need, or assume their Atmos bar is faulty when the real problem is the TV's eARC setting, the wrong HDMI port or HDMI-CEC switched off. Usually only one port on the TV supports eARC, and it is not always the first one.

We check the TV's ports and settings, connect the bar to the right one, and confirm on screen which format is actually playing, so you know what you are hearing.

"Height doesn't matter much — put the bar wherever it fits"

Some quick-answer summaries reduce placement to "below the TV." Dolby's guidance is more precise: the bar and screen as close together as practical, the bar ideally near seated ear height, and placement off-centre, too high or too low making sound seem to come from somewhere other than the picture. Yamaha suggests aiming close to 42.5 inches off the floor.

In small New York living rooms, where you sit close and the TV is often mounted high to clear a radiator or a window, the gap between the screen and the bar becomes obvious. A bar left on a low console under a high TV puts voices at knee height.

We set the TV and bar heights together, around your actual seating, and if a radiator or a window forces the TV up, we explain the trade-off before drilling.

"A bigger subwoofer always sounds better"

Product reviews and forums often treat the subwoofer as the upgrade that makes a soundbar worthwhile, and in a house it often is. In an apartment the advice on r/AskNYC is the opposite: bass travels easily, so skip the sub or choose one you can turn down.

City rules treat bass as its own problem. The Noise Code guide sets a separate bass-weighted limit for commercial music, because people feel low frequencies through structure. A large sub on bare floorboards in a pre-war building can be heard three floors away while sounding only moderate in the room.

We set the sub for the room and the building: placement off the floor and out of corners, a lower crossover level, and night mode on the remote, so the bass is felt on the couch rather than in the ceiling below.

"It is a 20-minute job any handyman can do"

Gig-platform listings and DIY videos make soundbar installation look like a quick job, and in a house with a wood-stud wall and an outlet right behind the TV it can be. That is the scenario most short answers describe.

In New York the minutes go elsewhere: getting past the front desk with the right certificate of insurance, the freight elevator, the walk-up stairs, finding something solid behind old plaster, drilling concrete quietly, routing cable where there is no cavity, and sorting out HDMI and remote settings that the TV maker hides in sub-menus.

The question to ask any installer, us included, is what happens if the wall turns out to be plaster or brick and the building wants paperwork. A clear, priced answer before the visit is the sign of someone who has done this in the city before.

Neighbour-Friendly Setup

Keeping the Bass in Your Apartment

Most soundbar complaints in New York buildings are about bass, not volume. These are the steps we take on every apartment install to keep the low end in the room it belongs to.

  • Subwoofer off the bare floor. A dense isolation pad, platform or thick rug under the sub cuts the vibration it passes into floorboards and joists.
  • Out of the corner. Corners exaggerate bass and couple it into the structure. A position along the front wall, a little out from the corner, usually gives smoother bass at a lower level.
  • Away from shared walls. Where the TV wall is a party wall, the sub goes on another wall or near the room's centre line if the layout allows.
  • Sub level set against the bar. Many bars ship with the sub set high for showroom demos. We set it lower, so it fills in the low end without dominating.
  • Night mode within reach. We show you the remote button or app switch for night mode, which keeps voices clear at low volume after 10 PM.
  • The bar itself, correctly placed. A bar near ear height and close to the screen makes dialogue clear at lower volume, so the whole system can run quieter.
  • Option to skip the sub. Many bars play reasonable bass on their own. For some apartments that is the better choice, and we will say so.

None of this makes a soundbar silent to neighbours, and nothing will if it is played loudly late at night. It does mean that at normal listening levels, the people below you hear far less than they would from a sub on bare boards in a corner, which is where most complaints begin.

Field Notes

Field Notes on New York Soundbar Walls

How our installers approach a soundbar wall in an older building — working practice, not a single job

Before anything is marked on the wall, our installers knock on it. A hollow, papery sound with a stud every so often is drywall. A dense, slightly ringing sound is plaster, and a dull thud that doesn't change is usually masonry or concrete behind it. If the plaster sounds hollow in patches, it may have come away from the lath, and we press gently around the mounting area to find out before drilling.

The second check is what is already in the wall. Old buildings have pipes, abandoned wiring and gas lines in places no drawing shows, and radiators often have risers running up the same wall as the TV. We scan where we plan to drill and keep holes away from the line directly above and below outlets and switches.

The third is the floor. Before placing a subwoofer we look at what is underneath: bare boards, carpet, concrete. That tells us more about how far the bass will travel than the sub's specification sheet does.
