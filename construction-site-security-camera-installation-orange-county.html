Element 4 · Why Orange County Jobsites Get Hit

The Biggest Pipeline in the Valley, on the Emptiest Land

Orange County's economic development office has described a pipeline deeper than any other county in the Hudson Valley, and the shape of it is unusual: enormous single-building projects on former farmland. A national developer building a distribution facility of more than a million square feet on a 184-acre approved site. A 1.2-million-square-foot spec building proposed on 127 acres in the Town of Montgomery. A 500,000-square-foot industrial facility on an 84-acre site at the edge of Goshen village. Roughly a million square feet under discussion on the old Shapiro Farm along Route 6 in Wawayanda, with short-line rail access.

Those numbers describe security exposure better than they describe real estate. A 184-acre site has a perimeter measured in miles, two or three construction entrances, and months where the most valuable thing on it is a fuel tank and a row of machines parked at the far end of a field. Power tools remain the most commonly reported theft from American jobsites and recovery on any individual tool is poor. Copper is the more expensive loss because reaching it destroys finished work — federal estimates put national copper theft in the range of a billion dollars a year, and on a distribution building the wire runs are long enough to make a single night costly.

Access

Two interstates and a state route

I-84 crosses the county east to west, I-87 and Route 17 run the other way, and the Pennsylvania and New Jersey lines are minutes from Port Jervis and Warwick. Sites near an interchange are a different risk than a farm road in Minisink.

Scale

Perimeter, not square footage

Fencing a hundred acres does not secure it. On the county's large industrial parcels the fence is a line on a drawing, and the useful question is which entrances and which staging areas are actually covered.

Jurisdiction

Sheriff, city, town and federal

The Orange County Sheriff's Office, three city departments in Newburgh, Middletown and Port Jervis, more than a dozen town and village departments, State Police out of Troop F, and federal Military Police at West Point. Knowing whose report you are filing matters at six in the morning.

Element 5 · Systems & Configurations

Four Ways to Cover an Orange County Site

The configuration follows the site — what it has, what phase it is in, and how long coverage has to last.

Configuration 1

Battery + cellular unit

Self-contained camera with its own battery and LTE radio, mounted to fencing, a container corner or a temporary pole. Goes up before a temporary service exists, which on an Orange County greenfield site usually means the week earthwork starts.

Configuration 2

Solar-assisted unit

Same head with a panel sized to the site. Cleared farmland parcels in Wawayanda, Hamptonburgh and Montgomery carry solar about as well as any sites in the region. Wooded lots in Tuxedo, Highlands and Deerpark get larger battery banks instead.

Configuration 3

Hardwired PoE

Cameras on Cat6 back to a recorder in a locked field office once temporary power is set and the shell is closed. This is the distribution-center and multifamily answer, engineered from day one to become the building's permanent system.

Configuration 4

Mobile surveillance trailer

For cleared acreage, laydown yards and phased sites with nothing to mount on — which describes most of this county's industrial work for the first six months. See solar surveillance trailers for Orange County.

Element 5.5 · Terminology

What the Quote Actually Says

PoE

Power over Ethernet — one Cat6 run carries video and power, so a hardwired jobsite system needs a cable path rather than an outlet at every camera.

LTE / cellular

The unit has its own SIM and data plan, independent of any site network. Data budget is why clip length and alert sensitivity get tuned during commissioning.

AI detection

On-camera classification separating people and vehicles from branches, weather and wildlife — the difference between a useful alert and forty a night on a rural parcel.

Talk-down

A speaker that lets an operator or the account owner address someone on site live rather than reviewing a clip the next morning.

Retention

How long video is stored before overwrite, set per project. A two-year distribution build and a four-week renovation need different windows.

LPR

License plate capture, aimed along a construction entrance or truck lane at plate height and angle rather than at the wide scene.

Element 5.7 · System Selection

Four Questions That Settle the Spec

1. Is there power?

No temporary service means battery or solar. Once a service is energized and lockable, hardwired becomes available and gets cheaper per camera across a long build.

2. How long is the job?

Under six months usually rents. A distribution or multifamily project measured in years usually buys, because the equipment converts rather than going back on a truck.

3. How many ways in?

Entrance count drives head count on large parcels far more than acreage does. Two construction entrances is a two-camera minimum before anything else is covered.

4. Who needs access?

An owner watching one house is a different account than a GC, a national developer, a lender's inspector and a super all needing separate logins.

Element 5.8 · Placement Planning

Where the Cameras Actually Go

Placement comes off the drawings and the walk. On an Orange County site the priority order usually runs like this.

  1. Every construction entrance. On a large parcel that is two or three separate points, each needing a head at plate height and angle. A wide scene camera records that something happened, not who did it.
  2. The container row and gang boxes. Tools leave from where tools are stored. A dedicated head on conex doors with a tight after-hours zone is the highest-value camera on most sites.
  3. Material laydown and equipment parking. Fuel, machines, rebar, wire and rooftop units — the concentrations that sit still for weeks on a site this size.
  4. The building face under rough-in. Openings at grade and wherever the interior work has reached, which moves as the job progresses.
  5. Street or road frontage. On village lots in Goshen, Warwick and Walden this doubles as documentation of the sidewalk and neighboring property before your equipment arrived.
Element 6 · Equipment & NDAA

Equipment Specified for Orange County Conditions

Jobsite units are chosen for cold-weather battery behavior, cellular reliability across terrain that ranges from open farmland to the Highlands, and mounting hardware that holds through wind coming across an unobstructed hundred-acre field. Fixed systems use commercial IP cameras and recorders sized to the retention the project actually needs.

Private developers are not bound by that requirement, but compliant lines are specified by default so a project that later picks up public financing does not become a replacement job.

Element 22 · Build-Phase Security Map

What to Watch, Phase by Phase

Phase 1

Earthwork & site prep

On a hundred-acre parcel this phase runs for months with nothing but machines, fuel and a field. Coverage starts at the construction entrance and the machine parking area, and a trailer often makes more sense than fixed mounts.

Phase 2

Foundation & steel

Rebar, steel and the first real tool concentration arrive. Add the container-row head and tighten motion zones around storage so alerts stay meaningful.

Phase 3

Rough-in

Peak copper exposure — long wire runs, plumbing, rooftop mechanical staged for lift. On a distribution building the wire in the deck is the most expensive thing on the property.

Phase 4

Finish & closeout

Dock equipment, lighting packages, fixtures and a dozen subs moving through open doors. Access-driven losses overtake fence-line losses here, and the camera record becomes how disputes get settled.

Element 9 · Questions Orange County Builders Actually Ask

Fourteen Real Questions, Answered Straight

We're six months from a building on a 150-acre site. What do we even cover?

The entrances and the machine parking, in that order. During earthwork the exposure is equipment and fuel, not materials, and both live in predictable places. A trailer or a pair of battery units handles it until there is structure to mount to.

There's no power out there and won't be for a while.

That is the normal starting condition on a greenfield site here. Battery and cellular covers it now; power changes what is possible later, not whether you can be watched today.

Our site has three construction entrances. Do we need three cameras?

At minimum, one per entrance at plate height. Entrances are the only points every vehicle must pass, which makes them the highest-yield coverage on a large parcel.

How is solar out on open farmland?

About as good as it gets in this region — cleared parcels in Wawayanda, Montgomery and Hamptonburgh have the open southern sky that solar wants. Wooded sites in Tuxedo or the Highlands are a different specification.

Do we have to sign a year?

Rentals are month-to-month. Phases end early and schedules slip, and a twelve-month commitment on a seven-month job means paying for a camera watching a finished building.

Will every deer set off an alert out here?

On a rural Orange County parcel, wildlife is the leading cause of nuisance alerts. AI classification and zone drawing handle it, and the tuning happens at commissioning rather than as a later service call.

Who owns the video?

The account owner. Access is granted per user, retention is configurable, and clip export is available to the owner on request.

We're building in Goshen village. Does the board care about cameras?

Temporary units mount to site fencing, containers and poles rather than to protected structures, which generally keeps them outside architectural review. The permanent system is a separate conversation at turnover with the architect involved.

Does this do anything for our insurance?

Recorded footage may assist with incident and insurance documentation. Coverage requirements and any possible policy impacts must be confirmed directly with your insurer — we make no claims about your policy.

Can units move as the subdivision phases out?

Yes, and on subdivisions that is the normal pattern. Relocations are included on rental accounts, so the structure head follows the active lots while the entrance head stays put.

What if somebody takes the camera?

Units alert on tamper and removal, and footage lives in the cloud as well as on board, so pulling a unit does not erase what it already captured.

Our district project needs NDAA documentation.

Compliant equipment specified with documentation supplied in the submittal package. That is standard on district, city, town, village and county work in this county.

We're working near a federal installation. Any special rules?

Work on or adjacent to federal property brings its own access, equipment and coordination requirements, and those get confirmed with the facility rather than assumed. NDAA-compliant equipment is specified as a baseline for that reason.

What does removal cost at the end?

Nothing additional on a rental. Closeout removal is included, which matters when the last two weeks of a job are already a scramble.

Element 23 · Delivery-Week Protocol

The Weeks That Carry the Most Risk

On most Orange County builds the highest-value window is when wire, rooftop mechanical, dock equipment, appliances or millwork land and sit before installation. The protocol is mostly scheduling rather than hardware.

  1. Tell us the delivery date so coverage is re-aimed at the staging area before the truck arrives rather than after.
  2. Motion zones tighten around the material for the duration, so alerts mean something during the window that matters.
  3. Overnight monitoring can be added for the delivery week alone on rental accounts and dropped afterward.
  4. Signage goes up at each entrance and the frontage — visible equipment is part of the deterrent value.
  5. When the material is installed, zones return to normal so the site is not generating noise for the rest of the job.

Element 11 · DIY vs Professional

When a Consumer Camera Is Actually Fine

It stops being reasonable when any of the following is true — and on an Orange County industrial site every one of them is:

No power and no network for months · batteries that fade in a January freeze · alerts nobody triages at 2 a.m. · footage on a card that leaves with the camera · multiple entrances a quarter mile apart · nobody to call when it drops offline during the week it mattered.

Where professional pays

Licensed installation on record · equipment matched to power, sky and signal · mounts rated for open-field wind · cloud retention independent of the device · optional live operators · documented compliance for public work · a three-year warranty on AESS-supplied products.

Element 21 · Field Notes

Notes From Orange County Installs

Practical observations from working in this county, offered as installer guidance rather than as customer accounts:

On large greenfield parcels the construction entrance almost never stays where the site plan put it. Earthwork sequencing, haul routes and mud conditions move it, sometimes more than once, and every move invalidates the aim of the camera watching it. Planning for relocation as part of the account rather than as a change order is what keeps coverage honest across an eighteen-month industrial build.

Open-field wind loads mounts harder than most people expect. A pole that would be fine behind a building in a village center is exposed on a hundred-acre field with nothing upwind of it, so hardware and guying get specified for the exposure rather than for the convenience of the install.

Cellular coverage across this county is not uniform, and the pattern does not follow population. Sites along the I-84 and Route 17 corridors are usually straightforward; parcels tucked behind ridgelines in Deerpark, Tuxedo or the Highlands can drop off sharply. Checking signal at the actual mounting height during the walk, rather than from the truck at the gate, prevents the install that has to be redone.

Element 26 · Weather & Winter Operations

Open-Field Wind, Snow and December Sun

Three weather realities drive equipment choices here. Cold reduces usable battery capacity, so a unit sized in October can run short in a January freeze unless the bank was specified for winter. Short days cut solar harvest exactly when the site is dark for sixteen hours, which is why panel angle is set for the low winter sun rather than for June. And wind across an open hundred-acre parcel, or over the ridgelines in the western towns, loads a mast-mounted camera hard enough that mounting hardware gets specified for the exposure rather than for convenience.

Winter storms add the other problem — power and network outages across whole towns, and access roads that turn to mud. A cellular unit with on-board recording keeps working through an outage that takes a wired system down, and uploads what it captured once the link returns.
