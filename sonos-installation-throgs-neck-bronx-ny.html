The Neighborhood

Why Throgs Neck Homes Need Sonos Installation

Throgs Neck has been a place people come to make noise for a long time. Wikipedia, drawing on the WPA Guide to New York, describes the late-nineteenth-century peninsula as a fashionable summer resort with large German beer gardens, reached by steamboat from Yorkville up the East River. The resort became a neighbourhood in the 1920s, when the big estates were broken up into row homes and densely built bungalow lots, and the summer colonies of Silver Beach and Edgewater Park were later adapted for year-round living. What survived the change is the habit: long evenings outside, family parties in small yards, beach clubs, and a main street, East Tremont Avenue, lined with bars, restaurants and shops.

The geography makes that habit a shared one. Throgs Neck is a narrow spit of land between the East River and Long Island Sound, bounded on the west by Westchester Creek and cut through by the approaches to two bridges. In Edgewater Park, the Department of City Planning found lots only twenty to thirty feet wide, side yards "often between two and five feet," and places where "the eaves of one roof extend to abut those of an adjacent building." On the street grid the lots are larger, but detached houses still stand within a few strides of one another, and in the attached townhouse rows the neighbour is on the other side of a single wall. A speaker on a back porch here is, for practical purposes, a speaker in several back yards at once.

The city's own records show how often that becomes a problem. We queried NYC Open Data for 311 complaints in ZIP 10465, which covers Throgs Neck and its immediate neighbours, and found 1,801 complaints described as "Loud Music/Party" in 2025, after 2,259 in 2022, 1,775 in 2023 and 1,867 in 2024. Seventy percent of last year's arrived between May and September. Seventy-three percent were opened between 8 p.m. and 2 a.m. And seventy percent of the callers chose "Residential Building/House" as the source, against 62 calls naming a club, bar or restaurant. In other words, the music people complain about on this peninsula is mostly coming from homes, on summer nights.

Local news fills in the texture. In October 2021 News 12 reported Throggs Neck residents meeting police after weeks of complaints about late-night music. In May 2025 the same station filmed parties in Ferry Point Park where "music blared through the speakers"; nearby residents said they heard it "from miles away" and that "their houses shake." In September 2025 neighbours of a bar on East Tremont Avenue told News 12 that "pounding bass" had become almost nightly, and one longtime resident had installed soundproof windows to cope. The word that keeps recurring is not volume but bass, and the effect people describe is not hearing a song but feeling a house shake.

That is the design brief for this page. A good Sonos system in Throgs Neck is not a quiet one; families here want music in the kitchen, the game loud in the den and a playlist on the patio when the cousins come over. It is a system built so that the sound is loud where you are and fades fast at the property line. The tools for that are mostly free: where the speakers sit and which way they face, how many there are, what the Sub is allowed to do, which rooms join the outdoor group, and what the volume limits and timers say. They are just rarely set. We set them, and we explain them, because the people who will notice whether we did are the ones next door.

The 311 Record

What the Peninsula's Noise Complaints Say About Music at Home

We did not want to build a page about neighbour-friendly sound on anecdote alone, so we went to the city's open data. The 311 Service Requests dataset lists every complaint with its ZIP code, its category, the place the caller says the noise came from and the time it was opened. Filtering ZIP 10465 for 2025 and the descriptor "Loud Music/Party" gives 1,801 records. Three charts tell most of the story.

The timing is the first lesson. Barely a dozen complaints a month are opened in the late morning or early afternoon. The count climbs from about 6 p.m., passes 170 an hour-slot by 8 p.m. and peaks at 306 in the 11 p.m. hour, the hour after the city's general noise rule tightens. Under Administrative Code §24-218, as summarised in our Bronx research, sound that is measured in a receiving property is judged against the background level: 10 dB(A) above it during the day, only 7 dB(A) above it from 10 p.m. to 7 a.m. Night is also when the background drops, so the same playlist that disappears into lawnmowers and traffic at four in the afternoon stands out clearly at eleven. A system that is fine at dinner can be the problem at bedtime without anybody touching the volume.

The season is the second. April more than doubles March; August is the busiest month; October falls back to a third of September. That is window weather and yard weather. Closed windows and a closed back door are a household's biggest free sound barrier, and from May to September most of them are open. Any outdoor speaker plan, and any plan for a Sub in a room with windows toward the neighbours, has to be judged on a warm night with the windows up.

The source is the third, and the one that matters most for us. Bars and parks get the headlines, and they deserve them: 62 complaints named a club, bar or restaurant, 82 a park or playground. But 1,263 named a residential building or house. Some of those will be parties that no installer could make polite. Many, from what we see in houses like these, are ordinary evenings where a speaker was set up in five minutes, placed where it fit, and left at whatever level sounded good from the sofa. Those are exactly the cases where a careful installation changes the outcome.

Two cautions. First, ZIP 10465 is bigger than Throgs Neck proper; it also covers Country Club, Schuylerville and Edgewater Park, so the numbers describe the peninsula and its neighbours rather than one community. Second, a complaint records a caller's view, not a measured violation, and the location type is chosen by the caller. We use the figures to understand when and where music becomes a problem, not to point at anybody. If you want to look yourself, the dataset is public, and the query takes a few minutes.

Light-frame houses that began as 1920s cottages and bungalows and were winterized in the Depression, on lots 20 to 30 feet wide. DCP calls them "older, light-frame and combustible buildings." The other former summer colony, its streets named for flowers and trees from the old Hammond estate, where residents own their houses and lease the land. Frame and brick houses on the streets between East Tremont Avenue and the water, many with basements, finished attics and real back yards. Attached Townhouse Rows

The rows of attached houses that worried residents enough to prompt the 2004 rezoning. A shared wall carries bass directly to the neighbour, so the Sub never goes in a corner on that wall, in-wall speakers stay out of it, and ceiling speakers are kept a few feet in from it.

Bass and Distance

Why Bass Is the Part the Neighbours Hear

Every story in the local news about music on the peninsula mentions the same thing: pounding bass, houses shaking. That is not a coincidence or a matter of taste. Low frequencies get through buildings far more easily than the rest of a song, and once you understand why, most of the right installation decisions follow on their own.

Walls stop treble well and bass badly

Acoustic engineers describe single walls with the "mass law": a wall's ability to block sound rises by roughly 6 dB each time the frequency doubles, and by another 6 dB each time its weight doubles. A worked example published by the firm Commercial Acoustics puts numbers on a single layer of 5/8-inch drywall on studs: about 40 dB of blocking at 1,000 hertz, 34 dB at 500, 28 dB at 250 and only 22 dB at 100. The same article notes that a double wall with an air gap can add a further dip in performance somewhere between 50 and 100 hertz, which is where kick drums, bass guitars and a Sonos Sub do their work.

Put plainly: a voice on the TV or a guitar line loses most of its energy crossing a light wall, while the bass loses much less. The neighbour does not hear your song; they hear its bottom end, which is exactly what the residents near East Tremont Avenue described to News 12. Windows behave the same way. The resident who fitted soundproof windows will have cut the traffic hiss and the voices from the sidewalk far more than the thump. Low frequencies also pass through the structure itself, from a Sub resting on a floor into the joists and foundation, which is how a house can seem to shake without anything being loud in it.

Distance is the cheapest quiet there is

Outdoors, the rule that matters is the inverse square law. HyperPhysics at Georgia State University explains that from a small source, each doubling of distance drops the level by about 6 dB, and ten times the distance drops it by about 20 dB. In a yard, that means the listener sitting three feet from a small speaker hears it 12 dB louder than someone twelve feet away, and 24 dB louder than someone at forty-eight feet. Real yards add reflections from house walls and fences, so the drop is never quite that tidy, but the principle holds.

The installation lesson is counter-intuitive for anyone used to a single big Bluetooth box. Two large speakers on the back wall have to be turned up until the far end of the patio hears them, and that level reaches both fences and the houses behind. Four small speakers spread around the seating can each run far quieter, because every guest is close to one. The total level at the table is the same, the level at the property line is lower, and the sound is more even. On lots twenty-five feet wide, that difference decides whether the neighbours hear a pleasant background or a party.

Direction, height and the wall behind

Sonos's own guide to outdoor speakers says you get the best performance from surface-mount outdoor speakers "by directing them toward your desired listening area," and the Sonos community's standard advice is to mount under the eaves. On the peninsula we add two things. First, the house wall behind a speaker reflects sound forward, so a speaker on the back wall of your house facing out into your yard sends less sideways than one on a post in the middle of the lawn facing the house. Second, height matters: a speaker high under the eave aimed down at the seating throws less over the fence than one at head height aimed across it.

The Sub belongs indoors, and out of the party group

Sonos lets you group rooms so music plays everywhere at once, and lets you turn the Sub on or off, set Sub Level with a slider and flip its phase in the app. The trap in a small house is the party group: when the living room with its Sub joins the yard group, the bass in the living room rises to match the volume everyone set for the patio, and with the back door open it pours out of the house. We set yard speakers up as their own room, explain how to group them without dragging the Sub along, and where a household wants low end outside we discuss it honestly, because outdoor bass is the part that travels furthest.

Yards and Patios

Outdoor Sonos for Narrow Peninsula Yards

The yard is where Throgs Neck spends its summer, and where most of our outdoor work is. A typical brief is simple: music at the table, music by the grill, and no visit from the 45th Precinct. A typical yard is not: narrow, fenced on three sides, overlooked by the windows of two or three other houses, sometimes with the Sound or an inlet just beyond the fence.

Choosing the speakers

For permanent outdoor music we use Sonos's outdoor speaker pair, a passive speaker rated IP66 and driven by an Amp through speaker wire, as recorded in our silo research. The Amp lives inside; Sonos lists its operating range as 32 to 104 degrees Fahrenheit. Where the yard needs more than one pair, the Amp can drive additional pairs within Sonos's published limits, and we design the layout so several speakers share the work rather than two working hard. For people who want music only now and then, a Move 2 (IP56) or Roam 2 can join the system and come back indoors; the Move 2's charging base stays inside.

Mounting points on light-frame houses

On a cottage or frame house, the eave and the fascia above the back door are usually the best mounting points: protected from rain, high enough to aim down, and close to the seating. Where the eave is too shallow or the siding is vinyl or aluminum, we mount into framing rather than siding, and seal every penetration. Fence posts work for the far end of a deeper yard, as long as the speaker faces back toward the house, not out toward the next lot. On brick houses on the grid, the masonry lines on the price list apply.

Getting the cable out

The cable leaves the house on a slight downward slope with a drip loop and a sealed hole, or comes down through a porch ceiling from the room above. On narrow lots the run from a front-of-house Amp to the far end of the yard can still be long; Sonos's wire-gauge table moves from 16 gauge to 14 gauge past 80 feet, and we measure rather than guess.

Settings that do the neighbourly work

Sonos lets you limit the maximum volume of each product on a 0–100 scale, and the limit applies to alarms and line-in as well. We set the outdoor pair's limit with you on the day, standing at your table and then at your fence, rather than picking a number in the van. We name the yard as its own room, show you how to group the kitchen with it without bringing the living-room Sub, and if you like, set a sleep timer on the outdoor group for late evenings so the music ends on its own. None of this stops you having a party. It means that on an ordinary Tuesday, the system's default behaviour is the considerate one.

Compliance notes for business music on the peninsula

  • Three tests, not one. NYC Administrative Code §24-231 forbids commercial music that, measured inside a home that receives it, exceeds 42 dB(A); or exceeds 45 dB in any one-third-octave band centred between 63 and 500 hertz; or raises the C-weighted level 6 dB above an ambient over 62 dB(C). The second test looks only at bass and low mids.
  • After-hours limits. The 311 page for bar, club or restaurant noise says that between 10 p.m. and 7 a.m. music may not exceed 42 decibels inside nearby residences, nor 7 decibels over the surrounding level measured on a public street at least 15 feet away.
  • What the city accepts as a fix. For a first violation, §24-231(b) lets the commissioner recommend no civil penalty when the business certifies permanent improvements such as sound insulation, isolators, suspension mounting or mitigation devices, backed by sound measurements. We install and isolate; measurement for certification is a job for an acoustical consultant.
  • Where the music comes from. A personal streaming account is the wrong source for a shop floor: Spotify, for one, restricts its service to personal, non-commercial listening and names bars, restaurants, stores and salons as places it may not be played publicly. We connect a business music service to the commercial system instead.
  • Low-voltage wiring. Speaker and network cable under 50 volts does not need an electrical permit in New York City when installed by a qualified person, under Administrative Code §28-105.4.7 as recorded in our Bronx research; new outlets or circuits are an electrician's work.

A Tuesday room and a Saturday room

Most avenue businesses need two settings, not one. A weekday afternoon wants music a customer barely notices. A Saturday night with the game on and every table full wants more. We set the system up with both as saved groupings and limits, so staff choose between agreed levels rather than dragging a slider to the top. The Saturday setting is checked from the sidewalk and, where a resident upstairs is willing, from their living room.

Your Questions Answered

Sonos Installation in Throgs Neck: Questions Answered

These are the questions that come up on the peninsula and not, in the same form, anywhere else we work. Each is answered here in full; the shorter practical questions are in the FAQ further down.

Music that leaves the lot

When the yard is grouped with the living room for a party, does the living-room Sub's bass go outside too?

It can, and on a small lot it often does. A Sub plays as part of the room it is bonded to, so when the living room joins a group with the yard, the Sub plays along at a level tied to the living room's volume. If everyone turns the group up so the patio is loud enough, the Sub rises with it, and through an open back door or window it becomes the loudest thing the neighbours hear. We set the yard up as its own room, and show you how to group the kitchen or another room with it instead of the living room. If the living room has to join, you can switch the Sub off or lower Sub Level for the party in the app's Sub settings and put it back afterwards. We write the steps on the handover note.

Would four small speakers spread around our yard bother the house behind us less than two bigger ones on the back wall?

Usually, yes. Outdoors, sound from a small speaker falls by about 6 dB each time the distance doubles. With two speakers on the back wall, the far end of the yard is a long way from both, so they have to run loud, and that level carries to the fence. With four smaller speakers placed around the seating and aimed inward, each listener sits close to one, each speaker runs quieter, and the level at the property line drops for the same loudness at the table. The trade-off is cable and mounting work for the extra pair, which we quote per zone after measuring. On the narrow lots of Edgewater Park and Silver Beach it is often the only layout that suits the space.

Our cottage's side wall is three feet from the next house. Is a speaker on that side ever a good idea?

Rarely. A speaker on that wall, inside or out, puts its energy into a two-to-five-foot gap that ends at somebody's window, and the gap tends to channel sound rather than let it spread. Indoors, rears or wall speakers on a side wall that close to the neighbour also pass bass through the thin exterior wall. We would rather put rears on stands a little in from the wall, use ceiling speakers aimed down into the room, or move the outdoor speakers to the back eave where they face into your own yard. If a side passage is the only outdoor space, a portable speaker brought out when needed is the considerate answer.

We're on a corner lot with streets on two sides. Which way should the yard speakers face?

Toward the house, from the street side of the yard. On a corner lot the open sides are the street frontages, and a speaker aimed outward from the house wall sends music across the sidewalk and toward the houses opposite, where 311 records a separate street and sidewalk complaint type. Mounting speakers on fence posts or a pergola along the street edges and aiming them back toward the seating and the house uses your own walls as a backstop.

Inside light-frame and attached houses

While a wall is open for speaker wire, will stuffing it with insulation stop the bass reaching next door?

It helps less than people hope. Mineral wool or fibreglass in a stud cavity does reduce mid and high frequencies and damps the resonance of a hollow wall, which acoustic engineers say can otherwise weaken a wall between about 50 and 100 hertz. But bass is mainly stopped by mass and by decoupling, and a light-frame cottage wall has little of either. Insulation is worth adding while the wall is open anyway; it will not turn a thin wall into a party wall. The bigger wins are keeping the Sub away from that wall and setting Sub Level after Trueplay, not before.

Our neighbours say they hear the thump of our Sub but none of the music. Why only the bass?

Because the wall between you filters out almost everything else. Under the mass law, a typical light wall blocks far more at speech and melody frequencies than at the bottom of the bass range, so the guitars and voices fade while the low notes come through nearly intact. On top of that, a Sub sitting on a floor passes vibration into the structure, which travels through joists and foundations. The fix is not just turning down. Move the Sub off the shared or exterior wall, put it on isolation feet if the floor is light, lower Sub Level in the app and listen at your normal volume. Most rooms keep satisfying bass at the sofa with noticeably less arriving next door.

In our attached townhouse, should the Sub sit against the shared wall or the outside wall?

Not against the shared wall, and not in either of its corners. Corners and walls reinforce bass, which is why Sonos lists them among the Sub's placements; on a shared wall, that reinforcement goes straight to the neighbour. A corner on the outside wall gives you the same boost while sending less through the party wall, though it can carry more to the yard. Off the walls, beside the sofa, is usually the most neighbourly spot of all, and Trueplay can even out the room. Bars and shops on East Tremont Avenue

Our bar shows the game with sound and plays music in between. Can one Sonos system handle both?

Yes. A Sonos soundbar under the main screen, or a Port feeding an existing amplifier, carries the TV audio, and the room's ceiling speakers on Amps carry music. In the app the bar staff can switch the room between the TV and a playlist, or group the ceiling speakers with the TV room during a big game so the sound reaches the far end of the bar. We set separate volume limits on each product, so the game can be loud near the screen without the whole room following. Second screens usually run silent with captions; one soundbar per room keeps the sound from fighting itself.

The city's commercial-music rule has a separate limit for bass. How do we set the bar's system to stay under it?

You set the bass on purpose rather than by accident. Section 24-231 treats the one-third-octave bands from 63 to 500 hertz on their own, at 45 dB measured inside a receiving home, so a room can be acceptable in overall loudness and still fail on low end. In practice that means a modest Sub, placed on isolation feet away from the walls and ceiling shared with apartments, Sub Level and bass EQ set lower than the factory default, distributed ceiling speakers rather than a few loud ones, and volume limits on every product. Whether the result complies is a measurement question, inside the neighbour's home; we can arrange the system so it is easy to measure and adjust, and recommend a consultant for the measurement itself.

If our bar receives a commercial-music violation, what does the city want to see before it waives the penalty?

Section 24-231(b) lets the commissioner recommend no civil penalty for a first violation if, within 30 days, the business admits liability and certifies two things: that permanent improvements have been made, such as sound insulation, isolators, suspension mounting or other sound mitigation devices; and that sound measurements taken under the department's rules show full compliance. Extra time of up to 30 more days can be requested for hardship. Our part is the permanent improvements on the audio side: isolation for the Sub and speakers, a rebuilt layout, documented limits. The measurements and the filing are for an acoustical consultant and your attorney.

When someone calls 311

If a neighbour calls 311 about our music, what actually happens next?

For music from a home, 311 routes the complaint to the NYPD. The city's page says officers will respond within eight hours when they are not handling emergencies, and that they can take action only if the noise is still happening when they arrive. Building rules may also apply if you live in a co-op, condominium or rental building. For a business, the caller can also receive a Department of Environmental Protection survey that can lead to an inspection with readings taken inside their home. From our side, the useful response is technical: check what was playing, from which speakers, at what limit, with which doors open, and change the setup so it does not happen again.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Sonos Installation in Throgs Neck

Google's AI Overview was not viewable from the system we built this page on, so nothing here is presented as its wording. What follows are seven beliefs about music, neighbours and installation that turn up in news reports, product listings and conversations on the peninsula — the sort of statement a chatbot is likely to repeat — each checked against a source named at the bottom of the page.

Claim one: "Soundproof windows will sort out a neighbour noise problem"

A longtime resident near East Tremont Avenue told News 12 in 2025 that he installed soundproof windows to deal with a bar's nightly music. It is an understandable step, and better glazing does reduce traffic, voices and the upper half of a song very effectively.

The difficulty is the bass. Acoustic engineers' mass-law figures show any single barrier blocking far less at 100 hertz than at 1,000, and windows are the lightest part of most walls. The same report described "pounding bass" and homes shaking, which is low-frequency energy arriving through glass, walls and structure together.

For a Sonos owner, the lesson runs the other way. Your windows will not hold your Sub's output in, especially open on a summer evening. The controls that work are where the Sub sits, how high Sub Level is set and whether the Sub joins the party group at all.

Claim two: "Any weatherproof speaker is fine for a yard"

Product listings for outdoor speakers lead with ratings for rain, sun and salt, and those ratings matter on a peninsula. Sonos's outdoor pair is rated IP66. But weather resistance says nothing about where the sound goes.

Sonos's own outdoor-speaker guide says to direct surface-mount speakers "toward your desired listening area." On a lot twenty-five feet wide with neighbours on three sides, direction and height decide whether your music stays in your yard. A weatherproof speaker on a post aimed at the house behind is still a problem.

So the question we ask first is not "will it survive?" but "who is it pointed at?" The answer usually puts the speakers high under your eave, angled down at the table, with a volume limit set from the fence line.

Claim three: "Music is legal until 10 p.m."

The idea of quiet hours from 10 p.m. to 7 a.m. is repeated on noise-law summary sites and in Reddit answers, as our Bronx research recorded. It is half right: the city's general standard does get stricter at 10 p.m.

But the standard does not switch off before then. Under §24-218, sound measured in a receiving property can be unreasonable at 10 dB(A) above the ambient level during the day, and at 7 dB(A) above it at night. A Sub shaking the house next door at 7 p.m. is not protected by the clock, and the 311 data shows hundreds of complaints opened between 6 and 10 p.m. last year.

What changes at night is the background. The street quietens, the same playlist stands out more, and the limit tightens at the same time. That is why we suggest an evening setting, not just a curfew.

Claim four: "Bigger Sub, better system"

Cost guides and forums often treat the Sub as the upgrade that makes a system feel complete, and in a large room in a detached house it can be. Sonos offers the full-size Sub 4 and the smaller Sub Mini for that reason.

In a cottage living room with a thin exterior wall a few feet from the next house, the bigger Sub mostly delivers more energy to the structure. The Sub Mini, placed off the walls and set with a modest Sub Level after Trueplay, often gives the sofa all the weight it needs.

Sometimes the right answer is no Sub at all, and a Ray or Beam on its own.

Claim five: "Set a volume limit and the system is neighbour-safe"

Volume limits are a good feature. Sonos lets you cap each product on a 0–100 scale, and the cap applies to alarms and line-in too. We set them on every job here.

A limit caps overall level, though, not the balance within it. A Sub with its level turned up, or a bass EQ pushed high, can be the loudest thing next door while the volume slider sits well under its cap. The city's commercial rule recognises the same thing by testing the 63–500 hertz bands separately.

So the order matters: place the Sub, run Trueplay, set Sub Level and bass, then set the volume limit by listening at the property line.

Claim six: "A bar can play music as loud as it likes inside its own walls"

Neighbours of an East Tremont Avenue bar told News 12 that when they complained at the door they were told, in effect, that the business could play music whenever it wanted. Plenty of operators believe something similar.

The Administrative Code disagrees. Section 24-231 measures commercial music inside the homes that receive it: 42 dB(A), 45 dB in any one-third-octave band from 63 to 500 hertz, or a 6 dB(C) rise above a loud ambient. Where the music starts does not matter; where it arrives does. 311 adds a 42-decibel after-hours figure inside nearby homes.

For an operator, that is good news in disguise. A distributed, well-isolated system with sensible bass and limits is cheaper than violations, and the code itself lists isolation and mounting among the improvements it accepts.

Claim seven: "One powerful speaker covers the whole yard"

A single large wireless speaker is the default outdoor purchase, and it is marketed on how far its sound carries. That is exactly the problem on a narrow lot.

Sound outdoors falls about 6 dB per doubling of distance, so one speaker loud enough for the far corner is far louder than needed near it and still loud at the fence. Two or four smaller speakers close to the people listening do the same job at lower level.

Our outdoor quotes are written per zone for that reason. The cost is a little more cable; the return is a yard that sounds even and a neighbourhood that barely notices.

Yes, with the sellers' permission to visit. We identify what the existing speakers and wire are, whether the wire gauge suits the runs, where the cable goes, whether the speakers sit in shared or thin exterior walls, and whether the gear can work with a Sonos Amp. You get a short written summary of what can be reused and what we would change. It is billed as a setup-only visit, and it is not a home inspection or an electrical inspection.

Can a beach club or community hall have a summer system that is packed away for the winter?
+

Yes. We usually wire the hall for permanent indoor speakers on an Amp and give the deck or lawn either fixed outdoor speakers or portable Sonos speakers that live indoors off-season. The club's limits are agreed with its board and written down, and the outdoor group is set so it cannot include the hall's Sub. In autumn the portable units come in, the outdoor zone is muted, and in spring a short visit brings it back.

Our side passage is too narrow for an extension ladder. How do you reach the eave to mount speakers?
+

We bring the right access for the space rather than forcing a ladder in. In a two-to-five-foot passage that usually means a narrow step ladder or a short ladder with a stand-off for work under a low eave, or mounting the speakers from the yard side of the house where there is room to stand. If the only workable footing is on the neighbour's side, we ask you to arrange their permission first; we never set equipment on another property without it.
