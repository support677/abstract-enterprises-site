The Neighborhood

Why Hudson Valley Properties Need CCTV Installation Done for Distance

Most camera advice online is written for a house on a quarter-acre lot or a storefront with the recorder ten feet from the door. The Hudson Valley is not built that way. A horse property outside Millbrook has a barn 500 feet from the kitchen. A house on a ridge in Putnam Valley has a gated drive that curves out of sight before it meets the road. A farm stand in the black dirt fields near Pine Island sits beside a cooler shed with no network at all, and a winery on the Shawangunk Wine Trail has a tasting room, a crush pad and a parking field spread over several acres. Even in southern Westchester, where lots are smaller, a single property often has a detached garage, a pool house or a carriage house that the homeowner wants on the same recorder.

That is the real reason CCTV installation in this region is a different trade from city work. In a Bronx apartment building the problem is getting cable through masonry and up a stairwell. Here the problem is getting a clean signal and steady power across open ground, through winter frost, past lightning-prone hilltops and through outages that can last days. The camera on the wall is the easy part. The cable route, the surge protection, the recorder location and the backup power decide whether the system is still recording in February.

The numbers back that up. The Census Bureau's 2023 American Community Survey counts 896,080 housing units across the six counties, and 54.8% of them are single-family detached houses. In Putnam the share is 77.7%, in Ulster 68.6%, in Dutchess 62.6% and in Orange 62.4%. Detached houses mean four sides to watch, driveways instead of shared lobbies, and outbuildings instead of storage rooms. The same survey counts 18,380 homes held for seasonal, recreational or occasional use across the six counties, including 6,545 in Ulster alone, which is why so many of our calls are from owners who are not there most of the week and want to see the property from the city.

Property crime is lower here than in the five boroughs, but it is not rare. The New York State Division of Criminal Justice Services reports 1,808 burglaries, 18,704 larcenies and 1,278 motor vehicle thefts across the six counties in 2024. Larceny is the one that matters most for CCTV: package theft from long rural driveways, tools taken from open barns and construction trailers, catalytic converters cut from vehicles parked out of sight, and shoplifting along the retail corridors of Route 59, Route 9 and Central Park Avenue. Recorded video with a clear face or plate is what turns a report into a case.

What the region adds that the city never does

  • Distance. A copper Ethernet run is good for 100 meters (328 feet) including patch cords. Barns, gates, docks and far parking rows are often past it, so the run becomes fiber, a PoE extender or a point-to-point wireless bridge.
  • Internet that comes and goes. Ulster County's own figures counted nearly 1,300 homes, businesses and community buildings with no reliable broadband in 2025. A recorder that stores everything locally keeps working when the connection does not.
  • Outages. Central Hudson, Orange & Rockland, NYSEG and Con Edison in Westchester all lose lines to storms and falling trees. A recorder on a battery keeps recording through short outages and shuts down cleanly on long ones.
  • Lightning and long copper. A long outdoor cable to a hilltop barn behaves like an antenna in a summer storm. Surge protection at both ends, a proper ground, and fiber where the run is longest protect the recorder.
  • Wildlife. Deer, bears, raccoons and swaying branches set off simple motion detection all night. Smart person and vehicle detection is the difference between ten useful alerts and four hundred useless ones.
  • Winter. Ice and snow load on mounts, condensation inside cheap housings, and frozen ground that stops trenching from December to March. We pick weather-rated housings and plan buried runs around the season.

None of that stops a well-built CCTV system from doing its job. The cameras talk to the recorder over cable we install, the recorder writes to its own hard drive, and the internet is only needed when you want to watch from somewhere else. That design choice, local recording first and remote viewing second, runs through everything on this page.

County by county: how CCTV work changes across the six counties

Westchester. The most built-up of the six, with 391,861 housing units and the lowest share of detached houses at 43.5%. Southern Westchester, from Yonkers to New Rochelle and White Plains, looks more like the city: condos, garden apartments, mixed-use blocks and offices, where the job is clean cable through finished buildings and coverage of shared entrances and garages. North of I-287 the lots grow quickly, and Bedford, Pound Ridge and North Salem bring the long drives and horse barns that define the rest of this page. Westchester also reported the region's highest property crime counts in 2024, with 831 burglaries and 7,679 larcenies, and 812 motor vehicle thefts.

Rockland. Compact and suburban, with 57.1% detached homes, a dense commercial spine along Route 59 and the busiest retail traffic west of the river. Rockland reported 255 burglaries and 3,346 larcenies in 2024, roughly the same as the year before. Most of our Rockland work is colonials and split-levels in Clarkstown, Orangetown and Ramapo, plus businesses along Route 59, Route 303 and Route 9W, including the dealership on this page.

Orange. The biggest mix: suburban developments around Monroe and Chester, older city blocks in Newburgh and Middletown, warehouses along I-84 and Route 17K, horse farms near Goshen and the black dirt farms around Pine Island and Florida. Orange counted 148,460 housing units, 62.4% of them detached, and 3,430 larcenies in 2024. Orange County jobs are where we most often combine a house, a farm building and a long lane on one recorder.

Putnam. The most single-family county in the region at 77.7% detached, and the lowest crime counts, with 38 burglaries in 2024. Putnam's challenge is terrain rather than crime: rock ledge under thin soil, steep driveways, lakefront houses around Mahopac and Lake Carmel, and wooded lots where Wi-Fi gives up a few yards from the house. Fiber and bridges earn their keep here.

Dutchess. From Beacon and Fishkill in the south through Poughkeepsie to Rhinebeck, Millbrook and the farm towns near the Connecticut line. Dutchess has 122,636 housing units, 62.6% detached, and 2,759 homes held for seasonal use. It was also the county hit hardest by the July 2026 storms, with 27,816 Central Hudson customers still dark on the Sunday afternoon after, which is why we push battery backup on Dutchess jobs.

Ulster. The most rural county we serve, with 68.6% detached homes, the region's highest vacancy rate at 15.2%, and 6,545 homes held for seasonal or occasional use. Kingston and New Paltz have village and city work, but most Ulster calls come from weekend houses, farms and properties up the Rondout and Esopus valleys, many of them among the locations the county itself counted as lacking reliable broadband. Local recording and smart alerts matter more here than anywhere else on our map.

Around Lake Mahopac, Greenwood Lake, the Ashokan and the Catskill edge of Ulster. The owner watches from the city, so remote viewing, power backup and a camera on the propane tank or boat dock come first.

Materials and methods, by building type

Vinyl and fiber-cement siding. Most suburban homes here are sided. We mount on a proper mounting block or junction box so the camera sits flat and the siding can still move with the seasons, and we seal every penetration. Cable usually travels through the attic or the basement rim joist and comes out behind the camera base, which is the most common way to install CCTV cameras without visible wires on a sided house.

Fieldstone, brick and stucco. Older farmhouses and village buildings have stone foundations, brick fronts and stucco walls. Drilling into old stone takes the right bit and a careful anchor; in many cases the better answer is to mount on a wooden fascia or soffit and run the cable inside. On stucco we seal the hole and the anchor so water does not get behind the finish.

Timber, pole and steel barns. Barns move, flex and get wet. We fix cameras to framing rather than siding, run cable in conduit or inside the building where animals cannot reach it, keep the cameras above the reach of horses and machinery, and choose housings rated for dust and water. A barn needs its own surge protection where the cable enters, and if the barn has its own electric service, the run between buildings is the place where fiber pays for itself.

Log and post-and-beam homes. Few hollow walls to fish cable through. We plan routes along beams, inside closets and through the basement, and use surface raceway painted to match where there is no hidden route, so the job stays tidy without cutting into exposed timber.

Condo and HOA buildings. Common elements belong to the association, so the board approves camera locations and the recorder's home before we start. Cameras on parking areas, mailrooms, dumpster enclosures and entry doors usually connect over existing building cable or new runs through common ceilings to a recorder in a locked utility room. Owners can also ask for an apartment building security camera system for Hudson Valley multifamily properties, which goes into resident privacy and access rules in more depth.

Car dealership CCTV installation: what we did in Rockland County

The photos on this page come from a CCTV job at a car dealership in Rockland County. It is a good example of what a commercial system here looks like once it grows past a handful of cameras. The recorder's monitor shows 23 live cameras on a 32-channel layout, leaving nine channels open for future cameras. The cameras cover the main lobby and waiting area, the glass entrance from the inside, the guest services window, the front desk, the showroom floor from the mezzanine wall, a back hallway that ends at a grey door, and the office areas. Inside, the building is mostly drop ceilings and tall painted walls, so most cameras are ceiling or high-wall mounts, with cable carried above the grid back to the equipment area where the recorder, the monitor and the network rack live.

Three choices made the system work. First, mounting height: the showroom has a two-story ceiling, so the cameras on the mezzanine wall see the whole floor without being in anyone's reach. Second, camera type by job: domes where a camera is close to people, like the guest services window and the front desk, and bullets where a camera has to look a long way, like down the hallway or across the entrance. Third, stream settings: the bitrate table on the recorder screen shows each channel's live stream, and keeping each stream sized to its job is how a 23-camera system keeps weeks of footage on one recorder instead of days.

Privacy and compliance for business CCTV in New York

A few rules apply to every business camera system in New York State. They are listed here so you can plan; this is general information, not legal advice, and your attorney should confirm what applies to your situation.

  • No cameras in restrooms, locker rooms or changing rooms. New York Labor Law §203-c bars employers from video recording in restrooms, locker rooms and rooms designated for changing clothes, except under a court order.
  • Audio is different from video. New York's eavesdropping law (Penal Law §250.05, with definitions in §250.00) makes it a crime to record a conversation unless at least one party to it consents. We leave audio off by default at businesses and turn it on only where the owner decides it is appropriate and posts notice.
  • No cameras where people expect privacy. Penal Law §250.45 (unlawful surveillance) makes it a crime to secretly record people dressing or undressing in places where they expect privacy, such as bathrooms, bedrooms and changing rooms. We will not aim a camera into a neighbor's windows or yard.
  • Signs. Posted notice of video recording is good practice for businesses and landlords, and many insurers ask for it.
Your Questions Answered

Hudson Valley CCTV Installation: Questions Answered

These are the questions Hudson Valley owners actually ask us on the phone, most of them about distance, power, internet and weather rather than about cameras themselves.

How far from the recorder can a CCTV camera sit on a property with a barn or a long driveway?

On plain copper Ethernet, 100 meters, or 328 feet, is the limit for one run including the short patch cords at each end. That covers most houses, most garages and most stores. It does not cover a barn across a pasture, a gate at the end of a quarter-mile drive or a dock at the bottom of a hill. Past 100 meters a camera may still light up on a long run, but it drops frames, loses power at night when the infrared turns on, and fails first in the cold.

For those runs we use one of three methods. A PoE extender sits in a weatherproof box partway along the route and gives the signal and power a second 100 meters. Fiber optic cable, with a small media converter at each end, can cover hundreds or thousands of feet, carries no electricity and is immune to lightning on the cable itself. A point-to-point wireless bridge, two small dishes aimed at each other, works where trenching is impossible and there is a clear line of sight. Which one is right depends on whether the far building has power, whether we can trench or use existing conduit, and how many cameras sit at the far end.

Can CCTV cameras record without internet at a rural property that only has satellite or a hotspot?

Yes. In a wired system the cameras record to a recorder on site over cable we install, and the recorder writes to its own hard drive. The internet plays no part in recording. It is only needed to watch from somewhere else, to get alerts on your phone, or to update firmware. On a property with no internet at all, the system still records everything, and you watch it on the monitor in the house or the office.

Where the internet is slow or capped, as it often is with satellite, fixed wireless or a cellular hotspot, we set the recorder to send only small substreams and short event clips to the app, so watching a camera remotely does not eat a month of data in a weekend. Cloud-only cameras are the wrong choice for these properties: when the connection drops, they stop recording.

What keeps the cameras recording when Central Hudson, O&R or NYSEG power goes out?

A battery backup, called a UPS, under the recorder and the PoE switch. A camera system draws modest power, so a properly sized UPS can keep a typical home system recording through a short outage and then shut the recorder down cleanly before the battery is empty, which protects the hard drive. Without it, every flicker reboots the recorder and the minutes around a storm are the ones you lose.

Long outages are a real risk here. During the July 4, 2026 thunderstorms, about 50,000 Central Hudson customers lost power at the height of the storm, and the utility's senior vice president of engineering and operations, Eric Kiszkiel, said the storm “caused extensive tree-related damage across the central portion of our service territory, creating a large number of individual repair locations that crews must address before power can be restored” (Daily Freeman, July 5, 2026). If the house has a standby generator, we put the recorder circuit on it; the UPS then only has to bridge the seconds until the generator starts.

How do you protect cameras on a hilltop barn or gate from lightning?

Long outdoor copper cable picks up voltage from nearby strikes, and that surge travels straight back to the recorder and switch. We put surge protectors at both ends of any outdoor run, bond them to a proper ground, and keep camera cable away from the electric service where we can. Mounting brackets and poles are bonded too.

On the longest and most exposed runs, fiber is the real fix. Glass carries no electricity, so a strike near the barn cannot travel down the cable to the house. The camera at the barn still needs local protection, but the expensive recorder and everything else on the network are cut off from the surge.

How do you stop deer, bears and swaying branches from sending motion alerts all night?

Basic pixel-change motion detection reacts to anything that moves: headlights, shadows, snow, insects in the infrared beam and a lot of wildlife. On a wooded lot that means hundreds of alerts and people turning notifications off within a week. We use cameras and recorders with smart detection that classifies people and vehicles, then draw detection zones that cover the driveway, the doors and the barn entrance but not the tree line or the bird feeder.

We also tune sensitivity during the first week. The recorder still records everything that happens in view, so a deer at 3 a.m. is on the drive if you want it, but your phone only buzzes for a person at the door or a car in the drive. On farms we set zones so livestock in the paddock do not trigger alarms while a person at the tack room door does.

How many CCTV cameras does a country house with a long driveway and a detached garage need?

Usually six to ten. A practical layout is one camera on the driveway entrance or gate that can read a plate in daylight, one on the main approach to the house, one on each door people use, one covering the garage doors and one or two on the detached garage or barn. Four cameras cover the doors of a compact house well, but on a large lot four leaves the drive, the outbuildings and the back of the property blind.

We plan from what each camera must prove, not from a box size. A camera that should identify a face needs to be close and at a sensible height; a camera that should show a vehicle arriving can be wider and farther. That is why our 4-camera systems are a starting point and why the recorder we pick usually has room for more channels than you fill on day one.

Where should cameras go at a farm stand, orchard or winery that is busy only part of the year?

At the cash counter or register, at the entrance and exit of the parking field, on the cooler or storage barn door, and on any equipment parked overnight. Pick-your-own orchards and tasting rooms get crowds from late summer through the holidays and then sit quiet for months, so the system has to cover both: crowds and cash in season, an empty property out of season.

CCTV installation for a seasonal business like this usually means more storage than a house needs, because busy weekends produce a lot of footage, and good remote alerts in winter, because nobody is on site. We also cover the long farm lane or entrance road, since that is how anyone arrives after hours.

How much hard drive space does an 8-camera system need to keep two weeks of video?

As a rule of thumb, a 4MP camera recording all day in H.265 at about 2 megabits per second uses around 21.6 GB per day. Eight of them use about 173 GB a day, or about 2.4 TB for 14 days, so a 4 TB drive keeps roughly three weeks in normal conditions. A 4K (8MP) camera needs about twice that. Night scenes with rain, snow or moving trees use more; quiet indoor rooms use less.

Every system we sell includes two weeks of recording. If you need longer for insurance, for a business policy or because you are away for a month at a time, we size a larger drive or a second drive, and we can set some cameras to record continuously and others only on events. We check the real numbers after a few days of recording and adjust before we close the job.

Can I watch my weekend house's cameras from the city, and what happens when nobody visits for weeks?

Yes. We set up the app on your phone and anyone else you choose, test it from outside the property's network before we leave, and set alerts for people and vehicles. When nobody is there for weeks, the recorder keeps recording locally and the battery backup rides through short outages. We also turn on the recorder's health alerts so you hear if a camera goes offline or the hard drive reports a problem.

For second homes we like a camera on the driveway, one on each entry door, one on the propane tank or generator, and one inside at the main door. If the house is shut for winter with the heat turned down, we choose a recorder location that stays above freezing, such as a heated basement, rather than an unheated garage.

Does an HOA or condo board have to approve cameras on common areas before installation?

In almost every case, yes. Parking lots, mailrooms, pool gates and building exteriors in a condo or HOA community are common elements, so the board decides where cameras go, who can view them and how long footage is kept. We prepare a camera map and a simple written scope that the board can review at a meeting, and we install only after approval.

Unit owners who want cameras on their own doors or decks should check the association's rules first. A camera that looks out over a shared lawn or a neighbor's windows is a common cause of complaints, so we aim private cameras at the owner's own entry and driveway.

Can the old coax cameras on my barn stay when the recorder is replaced?

Often they can. Many older analog systems were wired with RG59 coax, sometimes with a power pair in the same jacket. If that coax is still in good shape, it can carry modern HD-analog video, the kind marketed as HD-TVI, HD-CVI or AHD, at much better quality than the old cameras. Coax also runs farther than Ethernet without help, which is a real advantage on a long barn run.

We test the old cable first. If the coax is cracked, waterlogged or chewed, or if you want smart detection and higher resolution on that camera, we replace it with outdoor-rated Cat6 or fiber. Many Hudson Valley upgrades end up hybrid: new IP cameras on the house, HD-analog on the reused coax to the barn, all on one recorder.

Is wireless CCTV good enough where we cannot trench across the property?

It depends on what you mean by wireless. Wi-Fi cameras that connect to the house router struggle at distance, through trees and through stone or metal barn walls, and battery models sleep until motion wakes them, so they miss the start of an event. A point-to-point wireless bridge is different: two small antennas aimed at each other carry a wired camera's signal across a field or a road with a clear line of sight, and the camera itself is wired and powered at the far end.

If the far building has power, a wireless bridge plus wired cameras is often the best answer where trenching is impossible. If there is no power at all, a solar-powered or cellular camera is a workable compromise, with the limits that come with it. We tell you which camera does what before you buy anything.

Can buried CCTV cable be installed in winter, or do we wait for spring?

Most of the job can be done any month. Cameras, the recorder, the house wiring and aerial runs on existing structures are not affected by frozen ground. What winter limits is trenching: once the ground freezes, digging a new trench to a barn or gate becomes slow and messy, and in deep cold it is not worth doing.

In winter we often install everything else, connect the far camera temporarily over a wireless bridge or a protected surface run, and come back in spring to bury the permanent cable or fiber in conduit. That way you are recording from the first visit, and the long run is done properly when the ground allows it.

Can one recorder handle the house, the barn and a guest cottage on the same property?

Yes, and on most country properties it should. One recorder means one app, one place to search footage and one hard drive to look after. The house usually holds the recorder, the main PoE switch and the battery backup; each outbuilding gets a small weatherproof switch fed by fiber, an extender or a bridge, and its cameras plug into that switch. On the recorder, every camera shows up on the same timeline, so you can follow a vehicle from the gate to the barn without changing screens.

The exception is a property where buildings belong to different people, such as a rented cottage or a shared barn. There we can give each person access to only their own cameras, or install a separate recorder so footage and passwords stay apart.

The install details that decide whether a system lasts

Cable. Inside, we use Cat6 for IP cameras; on longer or higher-power runs, Cat6A. Outside and underground, we use outdoor-rated cable, direct-burial or in conduit, because indoor cable cracks and wicks water. Analog upgrades reuse RG59 coax where it tests good. Between buildings, single-mode or multimode fiber, depending on distance and the gear at each end. Every cable is labeled at both ends.

PoE power budget. A standard 802.3af PoE port supplies up to 15.4 watts and an 802.3at (PoE+) port up to 30 watts. Most fixed cameras use well under that, but infrared at night, heaters in cold-weather housings, PTZ motors and active-deterrence lights all add load. We add up the worst-case draw of every camera and pick a switch or NVR with headroom, so cameras do not drop out on the coldest night when everything is drawing at once.

Compression. H.265 stores roughly the same picture in much less space than the older H.264, which is why we use it wherever the cameras and recorder support it. Combined with a smart-codec setting and sensible frame rates, it is how a system keeps two weeks or more on one drive.

Camera placement. Good CCTV system design starts at the property line and works inward. We ask what each camera must prove: that a vehicle came up the drive, who rang the bell, who opened the barn, which car left the lot. Then we choose a mounting height of roughly 8 to 10 feet for faces at doors, higher for lots and fields, keep the sun and headlights out of the lens, and check the field of view in daylight and again at night, because infrared changes what a camera sees.

DVR to NVR upgrades. Many Hudson Valley properties still run a DVR installed ten or fifteen years ago, with analog cameras that show grainy video and no smart detection. An analog to IP CCTV upgrade does not always mean replacing every cable. Where the coax is sound, an HD-analog DVR or a hybrid recorder lifts picture quality right away; where we are pulling new cable anyway, IP cameras on a PoE NVR give the best detection and the simplest future expansion. We document what we found and what we changed, so the next upgrade is easier.

Network. On businesses we keep the cameras on their own network segment or VLAN, change every default password, turn off features we do not use, and give remote access only through the manufacturer's secure app or a properly configured connection, never by opening the recorder straight to the internet.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About CCTV Installation in the Hudson Valley

Search summaries and chatbots give camera advice written for a suburban quarter-acre or a city storefront. Here is where that advice breaks on Hudson Valley properties, and what we do instead.

“Wireless cameras are the easiest option for large properties”

The claim sounds sensible: no cable, no trenching, no drilling. On a big lot it is usually the hardest option to live with. Home Wi-Fi fades quickly with distance, and it fades faster through stone foundations, metal barn siding and summer leaves. A camera that worked in the April test can drop off in July when the trees fill in.

Battery cameras add a second problem. To save power they sleep until motion wakes them, then take a moment to start recording. The first second or two, often the clearest look at a face, is gone. In cold weather the batteries drain faster, and somebody has to climb a ladder to charge them.

What we do instead is wire every camera we can, use a point-to-point bridge or fiber for the ones far away, and keep Wi-Fi cameras for the rare spot with no other option. The result costs a little more on day one and a lot less in visits, missed events and dead batteries.

“Ethernet can run as far as you need with good cable”

Better cable does not change the standard: a copper Ethernet channel is designed for 100 meters. Past that, signal and power both fall off. Some cameras will appear to work on a longer run in mild weather, which is exactly what makes the mistake expensive, because the failure shows up months later.

On Hudson Valley properties, runs past 100 meters are common: the gate at the end of a drive, the barn across the field, the far row of a dealer lot. Those runs need a planned answer, not a hopeful one.

That answer is a PoE extender at a powered midpoint, fiber with media converters, or a wireless bridge. We measure the route on the survey, not on a map, because a run that looks like 250 feet in a straight line can be 400 feet once it follows a fence or goes around a septic field.

“Cloud storage is always safer than a recorder on site”

Cloud storage protects footage if a burglar takes the recorder, which is real. But a cloud-only system depends on the internet connection to record anything at all. On a property with a satellite dish, a hotspot or fixed wireless that drops in storms, that dependency is a weak point.

Cloud plans also upload every camera's video continuously, which slow rural connections cannot carry, and they come with a monthly fee per camera. Our systems have $0 monthly fees because the recording lives on your recorder.

The better design for this region is local recording first, with the recorder hidden or locked away, on a battery, and sending alerts and short clips to the phone. If an owner wants an off-site copy of important events, we can add it on top of local recording, not instead of it.

“Four cameras are enough for a typical home”

Four cameras can cover the doors of a compact house. A detached Hudson Valley house with a long drive, a detached garage and a barn is not that house. Four cameras there leave the driveway entrance, the outbuildings and the back of the property unseen.

The count should follow the property: one camera for each place a person or vehicle arrives, one for each door, and one for each building or area you care about. On many country properties that is six to ten.

That is why we start with a 4-camera system price and size the recorder with spare channels. You can begin with the cameras that matter most and add the rest without replacing the recorder.

“More megapixels always mean better evidence”

Resolution helps, but it is not the whole story. A 4K camera aimed across a whole field still shows a person as a few pixels. A plate at the end of a 300-foot drive needs a narrow lens, not just more megapixels, and at night it needs the right shutter speed or the plate blurs.

Higher resolution also uses more storage and more network bandwidth, which matters on systems with many cameras or long fiber links.

We pick resolution and lens together for each camera's job: wide coverage where you want to see what happened, tighter views where you want to identify who did it.

“Motion detection will tell you when someone is on the property”

Plain motion detection reacts to any change in the picture. In the Hudson Valley that means deer, bears, foxes, raccoons, blowing snow, swaying branches and headlights on the road. Owners get so many alerts that they switch them off, and then the one alert that mattered never arrives.

Smart detection that recognizes people and vehicles, combined with zones drawn around the drive, the doors and the barn entrance, fixes most of that. It is now standard on our house-brand recorders and on the partner brands we install.

We tune it over the first week, because every property has its own problem spot: a flag that moves in the wind, a pond that reflects headlights, a tree line where deer cross at dusk.

“A CCTV installation takes a few hours”

A small system on a house with an open attic and a nearby recorder location can be a one-day job. A property with a barn run, a gate camera and buried conduit is not, and pretending otherwise leads to rushed cable work that fails in the first winter.

Long runs, trenching, fiber terminations, gate and pole mounts, and tuning detection on a large site all take time. Commercial systems like the dealership on this page are planned and staged around business hours.

We give you a realistic schedule in the written quote. We book within 2 hours of your request and schedule 7 days a week with early-day visits, first come first served once the deposit is paid, but we never promise same-day installation, because a good system is not built that way.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading the Recorder at a Rockland County Dealership

The most useful photo from the dealership job is not of a camera. It is the recorder's screen. The live view shows a 32-tile grid with 23 cameras running, labeled D1 to D23, and nine empty tiles. Under the grid, a table lists every channel and its current stream rate in kilobits per second. On the day the photo was taken, the busy channels were running several times the rate of the quiet ones, which is exactly what you expect: a camera watching a showroom with people walking through sends more data than a camera on an empty hallway.

That table is what we check before we leave any commercial job. If one channel is far above the others for no reason, the camera may be set to a frame rate or bitrate it does not need, and it will eat the hard drive. If a channel reads zero, the camera is offline. Above the monitor, the blue network cables run in bundles along the ceiling to the rack, which is where every camera on the job comes home.

Two more lessons from that building apply to most Hudson Valley businesses. Ceiling-mounted bullets on short brackets work well over work areas, because the camera clears light fixtures and looks down at desks and screens at an angle that shows hands and faces. A single camera at the end of a hallway is often the best value on a job: everyone who uses that door walks straight toward the lens.

How to Choose a CCTV Installer for a Hudson Valley Property

Whoever you hire, a few questions separate an installer who has done country properties from one who has only done townhouses. Use this as a checklist when you compare quotes.

  • How will you reach the farthest camera? If the answer for a barn 500 feet away is “a long Ethernet cable” or “Wi-Fi should reach”, keep looking. Ask for the method in writing: extender, fiber or bridge.
  • What happens when the power goes out? Ask whether a UPS is included or quoted, and how long the recorder runs on it.
  • What happens when the internet goes out? The right answer is that recording continues locally.
  • What surge protection goes on outdoor runs? Every long outdoor copper run should have protection at both ends and a ground.
  • How many days of recording will I get? Ask for the number at your camera count and resolution, not a generic “weeks”.
  • Who owns the passwords? You should get the admin login, not a reseller account you cannot control.
  • Are you licensed for low-voltage work in New York, and insured? Ask for the license number and check it.
  • What do I get at the end? A camera list, a cable map, the password sheet and a walk-through of playback and export.

Is it legal to record audio on security cameras in New York?
+

New York's eavesdropping law requires at least one party to a conversation to consent to recording it, so microphones that pick up conversations among other people raise legal risk, especially at businesses. We leave audio off by default at businesses and discuss it with you before enabling it anywhere. This is general information, not legal advice.
