No parking or travel charge applies anywhere in Rockland.

Rockland is the smallest county in New York outside the city, but it packs a lot of different walls into 174 square miles. A Nyack village house from the steamboat era, a 1950s cape off Central Avenue in Pearl River and a 1970s colonial on a New City cul-de-sac can sit twenty minutes apart, and each one hides something different behind the paint. That is why a single "from $99" number rarely survives the first test hole here, and why we post a separate add for each wall type instead of guessing. In professional TV mounting, and in TV mounting Rockland County homeowners can trust, the wall comes first.

Most of the county filled in after the Palisades Interstate Parkway and the Tappan Zee Bridge opened in the 1950s, when Rockland went from about 89,000 people to a full suburb. Those decades left raised ranches, splits and colonials with finished lower levels, many of them remodeled two or three times since. Walls get moved, paneling gets covered with drywall, and fireplaces get re-faced in stone veneer. On a TV job, the first ten minutes go to figuring out which version of the house we are actually standing in.

On r/Rockland, the posts that come up again and again are requests for a licensed contractor, warnings about home-improvement jobs that went sideways, and a homeowner asking whether to mount an antenna outside. Local handyman pages quote by the hour, and national cost guides for Nyack swing from about $200 to almost $1,000. People here want to know the number before someone drives over. A posted, flat labor price that changes only with the screen, the wall and the bracket answers that directly. That's what an insured TV mounting company and licensed TV mounting contractor should offer, rather than a TV hanging service found on an app.

The county also has its share of shared walls: two-family and multi-family homes in Spring Valley and Monsey, townhouse associations in Congers and Pomona, and tenants in Palisades Center and the Route 303 office buildings. Every one of those comes with someone else's rules about drilling, contractors and insurance, and television mounting or television installation in them takes paperwork as well as a drill. We sort those out before the appointment, issue certificates of insurance at no charge, and leave anything electrical to a licensed electrician.

Palisades Center tenants and Orangeburg–Blauvelt offices

Drywall over wood studs (most post-1950 Rockland homes)

Two studs are located and double-checked with a tiny probe hole, then 5/16″ lags go dead center into each so the wall plate hangs level. If the outlet or a window forces a one-stud spot, an $85 plywood backer spreads the load across three studs. No wall add: this is the base price.

Plaster on wood lath (Nyack, Piermont, Suffern village houses)

Stud finders read lath as one long stud, so we confirm framing with a magnet on the lath nails and test holes. Scoring the surface and drilling the plaster first keeps it from blowing out, and the lags then bite the lumber behind. The add is $45, and keyed plaster can chip at the edge; we don't patch.

Brick and fieldstone fireplaces

Sleeve or wedge anchors are set in the face of solid brick or stone, not in a mortar line by itself. Where the stone turns out to be a thin applied veneer over a framed chimney, we go through it and lag into the wood. Brick or stone adds $65, above-fireplace placement $130.

Cinder block lower levels

Block in a split-level or basement is hollow, so anchors have to land in the solid webs or use sleeve anchors rated for hollow block. We map the cores first with small pilot holes. Concrete or cinder block adds $75, and a $59 cord cover handles power on walls we can't open.

Metal studs (mall stores and office suites)

Steel studs are thin, so a heavy screen goes on toggle anchors through the stud flanges, or onto a plywood backer screwed across several studs. In mall and office spaces we confirm what the landlord allows first. Metal studs add $30.

Tile and marble (kitchens and baths)

We drill porcelain, ceramic and marble with diamond bits at low speed, hitting the grout lines when the layout allows, and then anchor into the framing behind. Even with care, tile or marble can fracture; booking this wall type means you accept that possibility. The add is $95.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Answers Get Wrong About TV Mounting in Rockland County

Ask a chatbot or a lead-generation site about TV mounting in Rockland and you'll get confident answers built from national averages and generic templates. Some of them are harmless. Some will cost you money or a cracked wall. Here are seven claims we've seen in AI summaries and cost-guide pages that cover Nyack, New City and the rest of the county, and what actually happens when a technician is standing in a Rockland living room.

1. AI says: ““Mounting a TV in Nyack typically requires a permit.””

One cost-guide page for Nyack says TV mounting usually needs a permit, with inspections before and after. That doesn't match how the work is done. A bracket on an interior wall is finish work, not the structural or electrical alteration that building permits are written for, and hanging one doesn't change your house.

What can need a permit is electrical work. Adding a new receptacle behind the TV, or running a new circuit to a deck, is an electrician's job and may involve the building department. That's exactly why we don't touch wiring and use a listed in-wall kit fed from your existing outlet instead.

If you live in a condo, a townhouse association or a rental, the permission you need comes from the board or the owner, not the village. Get that in writing before your appointment, keep it on your phone, and the technician can start the moment the wall check is done.

2. AI says: ““Expect to pay $230 to $500 to mount a TV in Rockland.””

National lead sites publish wide ranges for Nyack, such as a typical span of roughly $200 to almost $1,000, with labor alone listed at $150 to $250. Those numbers average together tiny bedroom TVs, 98-inch screens and stone fireplaces, so they don't tell you what your job costs.

Our Rockland list is specific. A 55″ on drywall is $219.99. A 65″ on plaster is $294.99. A 75″ on brick is $344.99. A 65″ above a stone fireplace is $444.99. You pick the size, wall and bracket, and that is the labor price.

Local handymen often bill by the hour, commonly with a one-hour minimum, and the clock keeps running if the wall fights back. Flat pricing moves that risk to us. If something unselected turns up, you get the new price before any drilling starts.

3. AI says: ““Split-levels and raised ranches all have studs every 16 inches.””

That's how they were framed, and it's where the advice stops being useful. Rockland's postwar splits have had sixty years of owners. Walls get moved to open the kitchen, lower levels get finished over block, and family rooms get re-faced with stone veneer or shiplap.

In one Pomona raised ranch the TV wall is original drywall on 16-inch centers. Two doors down, the same wall was furred out over knotty pine, or rebuilt around a closed-off doorway with a header in the middle. Readings from one house predict nothing about its neighbor.

So we test before we trust. A magnet, a small pilot hole and a look behind the outlet plate confirm the framing. If the wall turns out to be something other than what you booked, we price the difference before the first lag bolt goes in.

4. AI says: ““Any drywall anchor rated for 100 pounds will hold a TV.””

Packaging ratings assume a straight-down load in perfect drywall. A TV bracket pulls outward, especially a full-motion arm extended toward the kitchen. Half-inch gypsum crumbles around plastic or winged anchors under that leverage, and the result is the classic TV mount pulling away from the wall.

We mount into wood studs with lag bolts, into masonry with sleeve or wedge anchors, and into steel studs with toggles through the flange or a plywood backer. A drywall-only placement isn't something we'll do for a television of any real size.

If the studs don't line up with where you want the screen, an $85 plywood backer spanning three studs gives you the placement you want without gambling on the drywall. It's common behind fireplaces and beside windows in older Rockland rooms.

5. AI says: ““You can put your old TV out at the curb.””

Not in New York. Since January 1, 2015, state law has banned households from throwing televisions and other electronics in the trash. A hauler can refuse the set and leave it sitting at the end of your driveway for the neighbors to admire.

Rockland Green lists free recycling for TVs at participating retailers and at the county Household Hazardous Waste Facility in Pomona, which accepts electronics on weekday mornings. The county's drop-off runs weekdays from 8 AM to 1 PM, which is a good option if you have the time and a car big enough for a big panel.

If you don't, add the $79 haul-away when you book and we'll take the old TV with us for recycling. Need it off the wall first? That's another $85, and unscrewing the old plate is $45 more. Then the new set goes up in the same visit.

6. AI says: ““Stone veneer on a fireplace is as strong as solid stone.””

Plenty of Rockland family rooms got a stone face in a 1990s or 2000s remodel. Much of that is manufactured or thin natural veneer, about an inch or two thick, set in mortar over cement board and wood framing. It looks like a fieldstone chimney, but it isn't one.

Masonry anchors in a thin veneer have almost nothing to bite, and the stone can pop loose from its mortar bed under a TV's leverage. On veneer we drill through the stone and lag into the framing behind it, the same way we'd treat drywall over studs.

A real fieldstone or brick chimney is a different job: sleeve or wedge anchors set into solid units, never the joint alone. Both are priced at the $65 brick-or-stone add. A small test hole in an inconspicuous spot tells us which one you have.

7. AI says: ““Store and office walls are the same as house walls.””

A Palisades Center storefront or a Route 303 office suite looks like drywall, and it is, but behind it are light-gauge steel studs and a drop ceiling hiding the real structure. Lag bolts meant for wood have nothing to grip in thin steel.

We use toggle anchors through the stud flanges or a plywood backer spanning several studs, and ceiling mounts go to the deck or joists above the grid. The metal-stud add is $30, and ceiling brackets add $85 whether the store is in a mall or on a village street.

Commercial spaces also bring rules homes don't: the ADA 4-inch protrusion limit in walkways, landlord or mall contractor requirements, and a certificate of insurance. We handle the certificate free, so tell us the exact holder name and wording when you book.
