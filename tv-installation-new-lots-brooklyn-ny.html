The Neighborhood

Why New Lots Properties Need TV Installation

New Lots is older than the neighborhood that now surrounds it. The farm lots the Dutch laid out here in the seventeenth century gave the area its name, the Town of New Lots split from Flatbush in 1852, and the town governed itself from a hall on Bradford Street until Brooklyn absorbed it as the 26th Ward in 1886. Today most maps fold it into East New York, and the name lives on in New Lots Avenue, in the New Lots Reformed Church at Schenck Avenue, and in the New Lots Av station at the end of the 3 line. None of that history decides how a TV goes up, but the houses it left behind do. Wikipedia describes East New York's housing as mostly semi-detached homes, two- to four-family houses and apartment buildings, and the city's planning framework for the area describes its R5 blocks as two- and three-story row houses. Sales listings on the avenue show the same thing: a 2020 offering of 690–702 New Lots Avenue, two blocks from the station, was a run of six two-family buildings, and a listing the same year described a brick two-family on Berriman Street between New Lots Avenue and Hegeman Avenue. Short runs of brick two-families and attached row houses, with a few steps up to the door and a basement below, are what we expect on these blocks.

The basement is the first thing we ask about. When the Citizens Housing and Planning Council reviewed the city's basement pilot in 2023, Brownstoner and Brooklyn Paper reported that East New York has the most underground homes of any neighborhood in the city, an estimated 5,086 according to a 2021 Pratt Center count cited by the city comptroller. HPD ran its Basement Apartment Conversion Pilot Program in exactly this district, Community District 5, starting from building code changes passed in February 2019, and more than 8,000 homes were identified as possible candidates. Whatever the paperwork status of a given unit, the result for a TV installer is the same: a large share of the screens we hang in New Lots go into a garden-level apartment, a cellar den or a finished basement, where the TV wall is often the foundation itself. Poured concrete, concrete block and old brick each take a different anchor, and a foundation wall can be damp in a way no upstairs wall ever is.

The floors above have their own walls. Older New Lots houses usually have plaster, which may sit on wood lath or straight on brick, and an exterior wall of solid masonry is a common place for the living room TV because it is the long, unbroken wall facing the street. The houses rebuilt in the 1980s and after look different again. East Brooklyn Congregations began its Nehemiah homeownership program with a groundbreaking in Brownsville in October 1982, and the Gotham Center's history of the plan describes two- and three-bedroom attached houses of roughly 1,000 to 1,200 square feet with full basements; about 4,500 such homes went up across East Brooklyn over three decades. Inside those houses the partitions are usually drywall, which takes a stud mount, while the party walls and the basement are masonry. In one house, then, a TV can be a $179.99 job upstairs and a brick-and-block job in the cellar.

Then there are the people who own the walls. Many households in New Lots rent a floor of a two-family from an owner who lives downstairs, rent a basement unit from the family upstairs, or live in one of the eleven NYCHA developments that Wikipedia lists for East New York. Each of those arrangements puts someone between the tenant and the drill. NYCHA's 2024 Resident Handbook says residents may not add fixtures or fittings without prior written consent from their property management office, and an ordinary lease often has an alterations clause that says much the same. We do not treat that as an obstacle. We treat it as the first step of the job, and we explain further down exactly what to ask for and what we can send to help. The same is true on the commercial side: the city's 2024–2025 study of the New Lots shopping corridors found that 72 percent of the merchants surveyed rent their space, so a shop owner who wants a screen above the counter often needs a landlord's yes before we drill.

Those shopping corridors are the other half of our New Lots work. The NYC Department of Small Business Services and the Cypress Hills Local Development Corporation counted 367 storefronts along New Lots, Livonia and Sutter Avenues between Linwood Street and Van Sinderen Avenue and along Van Sinderen itself, and the mix reads like a list of places that run a TV all day: 48 salons and barbershops, 41 delis, bodegas and smoke shops, 32 restaurants, 22 medical offices, 10 laundromats and 33 houses of worship and nonprofits. The same study found a median of two full-time employees per business, which tells us something practical: the person who books the TV is usually the person who opens the gate, works the counter and closes up, and the install has to fit around that day rather than the other way round.

Same-Day Repair in New Lots

Need Repair in New Lots? A Screen Pulling Off a Basement Wall, Anchors Spinning in Old Plaster, or an Old TV That Has to Come Down Today

Some calls are not new installs at all. A bracket that someone fixed into a block wall with the wrong anchors starts to tilt forward, and the gap at the top grows every week. A full-motion arm in a plaster wall loosens because the screws found lath and nothing behind it. A screen in a cellar den sits above a power strip that got wet. Or an old TV simply has to come off the wall before a new one arrives. If the TV is leaning, sagging or the bracket is visibly pulling away, unplug it, keep children and pets away from the wall, and do not try to push it back flat; the load on a failing anchor is highest when someone leans on the screen.

Send a photo of the bracket from the side, where the gap shows, and one of the wall around the fixings. That is usually enough for us to tell whether the anchors failed, the wall failed or the bracket itself is the problem. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our list. If the bracket can be refixed properly on the same wall, the work is priced from the same Brooklyn list as a new mount, with the wall add-on that fits; if the wall itself is crumbling or wet, we will tell you that before quoting and suggest another wall. We give same-day estimates on most requests and book the visit as soon as it is paid, inside a two-hour arrival window.

  • Leaning TV on a basement wall: usually plastic plugs or drywall anchors in block. The fix is the right masonry anchor in solid material, which sometimes means moving the bracket a few inches to find it.
  • Loose arm on a plaster wall: usually screws in lath instead of studs, or toggles in plaster too weak to hold. We locate the studs or the brick behind and refix.
  • Power strip or box near damp: we move it up off the floor and away from the wet wall, and if an outlet itself looks damaged we tell you to have an electrician check it before the TV goes back on.

Building Specialists

TV Installation for New Lots's Architecture

New Lots has a handful of building types, and most TV questions start with which one you live or work in. These are the six we see most, and the details below them explain what changes from one to the next.

The brick two-family

Two to three stories, often with the owner on one floor and a tenant on the other. Plaster or drywall partitions inside, solid brick outside, and a basement below. Most of our New Lots quotes start here.

The garden-level or basement unit

A home below the first floor, sometimes legal, often not yet. Foundation walls of block or concrete, low ceilings with pipes and ducts, and the occasional damp corner.

The attached row house

Narrow, with party walls shared with the neighbors on both sides and a long living room. The question is usually which wall is the party wall and which is safe to drill.

The rebuilt home

Nehemiah houses and other homes built from the 1980s on. Drywall inside, masonry party walls and full basements, and a TV job that is often simpler than people expect.

The NYCHA apartment

Public housing with its own house rules. Written consent from the property management office comes first; the walls are usually masonry or plaster, and cable boxes sit wherever the jack is.

The storefront with rooms above

A shop at street level, often with apartments above. Block or brick side walls, a dropped ceiling, a rolling gate out front and a landlord who may need to approve anything on the walls.

The brick two-family: two households, one set of walls

In a New Lots two-family, the TV wall on the upper floor often sits directly above the TV wall downstairs, because both apartments were laid out the same way. That matters for two reasons. First, the wall may carry the building's plumbing or a chimney flue that serves both units, so we scan before drilling and avoid the line where pipes usually run. Second, the owner downstairs is often the landlord, and an owner who can see exactly what will be fixed to his wall says yes faster. On the upper floors we usually find plaster partitions on wood studs or lath, priced at +$45; the exterior wall facing the street is solid brick, priced at +$65. If you want the TV on the front wall between two windows, plan for brick.

The garden-level or basement unit: foundation walls, low ceilings, damp corners

The basement is where New Lots differs most from the rest of Brooklyn. The foundation walls in these houses are typically concrete block or poured concrete, sometimes covered with paneling or a layer of drywall on furring strips, and that cover is not strong enough to hold a TV on its own. We locate the masonry behind it and anchor through the cover into solid material. The ceilings are low; the basement pilot program required at least 7 feet 6 inches of headroom, and the CHPC review found many homes could not meet that without digging out the floor. A low ceiling with a duct or a beam crossing the room limits where the screen can sit and how large it should be. And foundation walls can be damp. We use a moisture meter on the wall before drilling, keep the power strip and any box off the floor, and steer you away from a wall that is visibly wet.

The attached row house: finding the party wall before we drill

An attached row house shares its side walls with the houses on either side. Those party walls are masonry and usually make an excellent TV wall, but they are also where the neighbor's TV, shelves or kitchen may already be fixed, and drilling right through is the last thing anyone wants. We drill only as deep as the anchor needs, never through. In older row houses the party wall may be plastered directly onto the brick, so a hollow-sounding knock can mean the plaster has separated from the masonry behind it. In that case the anchor has to pass through the loose plaster into the brick, and we tell you before drilling if the plaster around the bracket is likely to crack.

The rebuilt home: drywall partitions and masonry party walls

Many New Lots blocks were rebuilt from the 1980s on, including homes built under the Nehemiah program that East Brooklyn Congregations started in 1982. Inside, the walls between rooms are usually drywall on wood studs, which is the simplest wall on our list: $179.99 for a TV up to 55 inches, bracket extra. If you want to hide the cables, a hollow drywall partition is also the wall where the $169 in-wall power and cable kit fits best, because the kit's two plates can be installed in the same stud bay with a clear path between them. The party walls and the basement are masonry, though, so a TV on the long side wall or in the cellar is priced as brick or block. We ask which wall you have in mind before quoting.

The NYCHA apartment: consent first, then the wall

NYCHA's Resident Handbook says residents may not alter their apartments or add fixtures or fittings without prior written consent from the property management office, and that residents are responsible for any damage. It also says cable TV installation is the resident's responsibility, and that satellite dishes are allowed only inside the apartment, never on window frames or the roof. So the first step in a NYCHA apartment is the consent letter, not the drill. Once you have it, the job itself is like any masonry or plaster wall on our list. If your development is enrolled in Big Apple Connect, the free program that supplies a cable box and remote along with internet, the box goes where the jack is, and we plan the TV position and the cable run around it.

The storefront with rooms above: block walls, dropped ceilings and a landlord

Storefronts on New Lots, Livonia and Sutter Avenues usually have block or brick side walls and a dropped ceiling with a void above it. A TV on the side wall is a masonry job. A TV hung high near the ceiling for customers in line may call for a different bracket, and any cable run above the dropped ceiling has to be supported properly rather than left lying on the tiles. Because most merchants on these corridors rent their space, we ask whether the landlord has approved the work, and we can write a short description of what will be fixed to which wall for you to send. Shop owners also tend to want the work done before opening or after the gate comes down, and we plan for that when we book.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Order We Check Things in a New Lots Cellar Den

This is how our technicians work through a basement TV wall, written the way they would tell it. It describes our method, not one particular job.

"Before the drill comes out of the case, I put my hand flat on the wall I've been asked to use. A foundation wall that's been wet recently feels cold and a little clammy, and you can't fake that. Then I look at the bottom of the wall: a white crust or bubbling paint along the base tells me water has come through at some point. If there's paneling or drywall over the foundation, I knock along it and find where it's fixed to furring, because that cover won't hold a TV and I need to know how deep the masonry sits behind it. Next the moisture meter goes on the spot where the bracket will go, and again at the floor. Then I look up. In these cellars there's nearly always a duct, a gas line or a beam crossing the ceiling, and that decides how high the screen can sit and whether a tilt bracket will hit something when it moves. Last, I find where the outlet and the cable jack are and how far they are from the TV. Only after all that do I tell the customer which wall I'd use. Sometimes it's a different wall from the one they picked, and I'd rather say so in the first ten minutes than after drilling."

Wall by Wall

Block, Concrete, Brick, Plaster or Drywall: What Holds a TV in a New Lots Wall and What Each One Costs

Every wall price on our Brooklyn list starts from the same base, $179.99 for a TV up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs, with the bracket sold separately. The add-on depends on what the wall is made of. Here is how the walls we meet in New Lots compare.

Drywall on wood studs. Common in homes built since the 1980s and in renovated rooms. We find two studs and fix the bracket with lag screws into them. No add-on. A drywall wall with no stud where the TV needs to go can still take a light TV on heavy toggles, but for most modern TVs we would rather shift the bracket to the studs and use the bracket's side-to-side adjustment to center the screen.

Plaster on lath or on brick. Usual on the living floors of older New Lots houses. Plaster is hard and brittle, so it is drilled with a masonry bit at low speed and never with a hammer setting, and we look for the stud or the brick behind it rather than trusting the plaster to hold the weight. Plaster is +$45 on our list. If the plaster has come away from the lath or the brick, which you can sometimes hear as a hollow patch in an otherwise solid wall, we tell you and pick fixing points where it is still bonded.

Brick. The exterior wall of a brick two-family, many party walls and some basements. Solid brick is excellent for a TV once the anchor is in the brick itself rather than in the mortar joint. Brick or stone is +$65.

Concrete block. The wall we meet most in New Lots basements and in storefront side walls. Block can be hollow inside, so the anchor has to grip the block's face shell or reach a filled cell, and we choose sleeve anchors sized for that. Block does not have its own line on the price list we publish for this area, so we confirm the masonry add-on for your wall from your photos and tell you the total before you book.

Poured concrete. Some foundation walls and the structural walls of larger buildings. Concrete is the strongest of all once drilled, but it takes the most effort, and we check for damp before drilling into a foundation. As with block, the masonry add-on for concrete is confirmed from your photos before you book, never decided on the day.

Paneling or drywall over masonry. Very common in finished New Lots basements. The cover is decoration, not structure. We go through it into the masonry behind, with spacers so the bracket does not crush the paneling, and the price follows the masonry underneath.

Hiding the wires without breaking the electrical code

The most common mistake we are called to fix is a TV power cord pushed into a hole behind the screen and fished out at the outlet below. It looks tidy and it is not allowed. As Gizmodo's summary of the National Electrical Code puts it, flexible cords may not be used "where run through holes in walls" or "where concealed by walls, floors, or ceilings," because "running power cords through the walls is not a substitute for permanent wiring." There are two legal ways to get a clean look. In a hollow wall, a code-compliant in-wall power and cable kit puts a proper power inlet behind the TV and a recessed plate at the bottom, connected by rated cable inside the wall, with room for HDMI and other signal cables to pass through. That is $169 on our list. On brick, block, concrete or plaster that should not be opened, a paintable cord cover runs from the TV to the outlet on the surface of the wall for $59. In a basement with a foundation wall, the cord cover is almost always the right choice, because there is no cavity to run anything through.

We do not do electrical work. If you want a new outlet installed behind the TV, a circuit added, or an ungrounded two-prong outlet replaced, that is a job for a licensed electrician, and we are happy to tell you exactly where the TV will sit so the electrician puts the outlet in the right place before we come.

Renters, Owners and NYCHA

Who Has to Say Yes: Mounting a TV in a New Lots Rental, a Two-Family or a NYCHA Apartment

In New Lots the person watching the TV and the person who owns the wall are often different people. That does not stop the job, but it does decide the order of things. Here is what we have learned to check first in each situation, with the rules quoted where they are written down.

Private rentals. Brick Underground looked at this question for New York City renters in November 2022. A broker told the site that landlords are within their rights to ban hanging things on walls, though it is rare, and Steven Kirkpatrick of Romer Debbas pointed to the lease: "If it's not included in the lease, then this wouldn't be enforceable." The same article quoted Arik Lifshitz of DSA Property Group on what counts as damage at move-out: "If the hole is larger than the size of a nail, then it's considered damage," and holes left by a removed TV mount are larger than a nail. The article's advice was to ask before installing a TV bracket even when the lease is silent, and it noted that landlords must give an itemized list of any deposit deductions and return the rest within two weeks of move-out. Our advice is the same: read the alterations clause, ask in writing, and keep the reply.

Two-families with the owner downstairs. This is the most common New Lots arrangement and usually the easiest. Most owners say yes quickly once they know what wall, how many holes and whether anything goes inside the wall. We can send you a short description to forward: the wall, the bracket, the anchor type, the number of fixing points and whether the cables run in a cord cover or an in-wall kit. If the owner would rather the TV stay on a stand, $119.99 on our list, that is a proper job too.

NYCHA developments. The 2024 Resident Handbook says residents are not allowed "to alter their apartments or add any fixtures or fittings in their apartments" without prior written consent from their property management office, and that residents are responsible for any damage that results. Ask the management office for that consent in writing before booking, and mention that the TV will be fixed with masonry anchors and that the cables will run in a surface cord cover, not inside the wall. The handbook also notes that every NYCHA employee or vendor must carry ID; our technicians carry company identification and are happy to show it, and the management office is welcome to call us before the visit.

Basement and garden-level units. Whether a basement apartment has been legalized or not, the person who owns the house owns the foundation walls. A hole in a foundation wall is also a different matter from a hole in a partition, because a badly placed anchor in a damp wall can give water a path. Cellar dens and garden-level rooms

Our basement apartment off New Lots Avenue has one wall that looks like poured concrete and another that is clearly block. Which one should carry the TV?

Either can hold a TV well, but they are drilled and anchored differently, so the choice usually comes down to the room. Poured concrete is solid all the way through and holds an anchor anywhere on its face, though it takes more drilling. Block may be hollow inside, so the anchors have to grip the face of the block or land in a filled cell, and we space the bracket to suit. In practice we pick the wall that is drier, faces the seating and has an outlet within reach of a cord cover. We confirm the material and the masonry add-on from your photos before you book.

A duct and two pipes run along the ceiling of our cellar den, so the room feels low. How do you keep a wall-mounted screen from crowding it?

We measure from the underside of the duct or pipe, not from the ceiling, because that is the real limit. A fixed low-profile bracket keeps the screen close to the wall and avoids the clearance a tilting or swinging bracket needs. We also look at the seating: in a low room the TV usually works best a little lower than it would upstairs, which is better for the neck anyway. If the only open wall puts the screen right under a pipe that could drip, we tell you and suggest another spot.

The foundation wall in our garden-level unit gets damp after heavy rain. Will anchors hold in it, and could drilling let water in?

Masonry anchors hold in damp concrete or block, but we do not like drilling a wall that shows active water, and a hole in a foundation wall is something to place carefully. We check the spot with a moisture meter, keep fixings well above the floor line where water usually shows first, and keep the power strip and cable box off the floor. If the wall is wet to the touch, efflorescence is spreading or paint is bubbling, the better answer is a different wall or a TV on its stand until the owner deals with the water.

What AI Gets Wrong About TV Installation in New Lots

Search for TV mounting and an AI summary will usually give you a tidy national answer. Most of it is reasonable for a drywall room in a suburban house. Here is where those summaries tend to miss what a New Lots house, basement or storefront actually needs. The claims below are paraphrased from the kind of general advice these summaries give, not quoted from any one tool.

"Expect to pay about $100 to $300 to wall-mount a TV"

That range is real. Fixr's TV mount installation page, updated in January 2025, gives a national average of $200 and a typical range of $100 to $300, and Thumbtack quotes a national average of $100 to $130 for mounting services. Angi's Brooklyn page is higher, with an average of $280 and a typical range of $172 to $376.

The problem is what the low end assumes. Fixr's own page shows how the price moves with the wall: $100 to $300 for drywall but $200 to $800 for brick, concrete or stone. In New Lots, where a large share of TVs go on basement block or brick, the drywall figure is often not the one that applies.

Our answer is a published Brooklyn list rather than a range: $179.99 for a TV up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs, bracket extra, then +$45 for plaster or +$65 for brick or stone, with block and concrete confirmed from your photos. You see every line before you book.

"For a clean look, feed the power cord through the wall behind the TV"

Plenty of how-to videos show exactly this, and AI summaries sometimes repeat it. It is not allowed. The National Electrical Code bars flexible cords "where run through holes in walls" or "where concealed by walls, floors, or ceilings," as Gizmodo's summary of the rule spells out.

The legal way to hide power in a hollow wall is a rated in-wall power kit, which puts a proper inlet behind the TV and a recessed plate near the outlet, connected by cable rated for use inside walls. That is $169 on our list.

In a New Lots basement the question often does not arise, because a foundation wall has no cavity at all. There, a paintable cord cover on the surface, $59, is the clean and legal answer, and we run it straight and tight to the outlet.

"Renters need the landlord's permission for any hole in the wall"

Some summaries say renters can never mount a TV; others say a few holes are always fine. Neither is right in New York City. What matters is the lease. As Steven Kirkpatrick of Romer Debbas told Brick Underground, a ban on hanging things that is not in the lease "wouldn't be enforceable."

The same article quoted Arik Lifshitz of DSA Property Group saying that a hole "larger than the size of a nail" counts as damage, which covers the holes a TV bracket leaves. So even where the lease is silent, asking first protects the deposit.

NYCHA is clearer still: its Resident Handbook requires prior written consent from the property management office before residents add fixtures. In New Lots, where many people rent from an owner downstairs or live in public housing, getting the yes in writing is step one of our booking.

"A basement TV is mounted the same way as one upstairs"

General advice treats every room alike. A New Lots basement is not like the living room above it. East New York has the most underground homes of any neighborhood in the city, an estimated 5,086 in the 2021 Pratt Center count, and the TV wall in those homes is usually the foundation.

Foundation walls are block or poured concrete, sometimes hidden behind paneling or drywall on furring strips that cannot hold a TV on their own. They can be damp after heavy rain. And the ceilings are low; the city's basement pilot required at least 7 feet 6 inches, and many homes could not reach it.

So we check for damp before drilling, anchor through any cover into the masonry, measure clearance from the lowest pipe or duct rather than the ceiling, and keep power strips and boxes off the floor.

"Use any anchor rated for more than the TV's weight"

The weight printed on an anchor's package assumes the anchor is in the material it was designed for. A drywall anchor in a block wall, or a masonry plug in a mortar joint, can fail well below its rating, and that is the most common cause of the leaning TVs we are called to fix.

In a hollow concrete block, the anchor has to grip the block's face shell or reach a filled cell. In brick it belongs in the brick, not the mortar. In old plaster the plaster itself should carry nothing; the load goes to the stud or the brick behind it.

We confirm the material with a pilot hole that the bracket plate will cover, then choose the fastener for that wall. The rating on the package only means something once the anchor is in the right material.

"In a shop or office, the screen can go wherever there is wall space"

For a living room, that is mostly true. For a clinic, a church corridor or any public walkway it is not. Section 307.2 of the 2010 ADA Standards limits objects between 27 and 80 inches above the floor to four inches of projection into a circulation path.

A TV on a full-motion arm in a hallway breaks that limit easily. So does a TV on a standard bracket on a narrow wall where people walk past. The fix is a low-profile bracket, a position above 80 inches, or a wall out of the path.

With 22 medical offices and 33 houses of worship and nonprofits counted on the New Lots corridors, this comes up more often here than people expect, and we plan for it before quoting.

"The lowest bid on a booking app gets you the same job"

Booking apps make it easy to compare prices, and for a drywall wall with studs the cheapest bid may be fine. But the price on an app is usually for the easy wall, and the person who shows up may not know until they arrive that the wall is block, plaster on brick or a damp foundation.

What you are really comparing is who carries the risk when the wall is not what was expected, and who can hand your landlord or management office the paperwork they ask for. We work under NYS license #12000287431, carry insurance, and can send a certificate of insurance before the visit.

We also tell you the wall add-on before you book, from your photos, so the price is not a surprise on the day. That matters more in New Lots, with its mix of plaster, brick and block, than in a neighborhood of new drywall.
