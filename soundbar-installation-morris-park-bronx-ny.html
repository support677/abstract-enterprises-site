The Neighborhood

Why Morris Park Homes Need Soundbar Installation

Morris Park looks quiet from the street: two-storey houses, driveways, a pitched roof over each attic. Inside, many of those houses are busy around the clock. The neighbourhood runs from Neill Avenue and Pelham Parkway in the north to the Amtrak tracks and Sackett Avenue in the south, and from Bronxdale Avenue and White Plains Road across to Eastchester Road. Along that eastern edge stand the Albert Einstein College of Medicine, Jacobi Medical Center, Montefiore's Jack D. Weiler Hospital, Calvary Hospital and the Bronx Psychiatric Center. One resident on r/bronx summed up the result in a single line: a hub near Montefiore, Jacobi and Einstein, “surrounded by working-class families juggling shift work, childcare, and elder care.” That sentence is a better brief for a soundbar than any spec sheet.

The houses came after the horses. John Albert Morris's racecourse occupied this land from 1889, staged both the Preakness and the Belmont Stakes in 1890, and burned in 1910, after which the property was cut into building lots. City Planning's account of the 2005 rezoning describes what followed: homebuilding speeded up once a streetcar ran along Morris Park Avenue in 1910 and carried on well after the Second World War, leaving a neighbourhood made mostly of single- and two-family detached houses and semi-detached houses, with small and medium apartment buildings along Morris Park Avenue and Williamsbridge Road. The new R4A district was drawn to match “the area's two-story homes with attics beneath pitched roofs.” That is the shell almost every Morris Park soundbar goes into: a front room in a brick or sided house, a staircase in the middle, bedrooms above, and very often a second household on another floor.

That second household matters. In the 2020 census 65.8 percent of Morris Park's occupied homes were rented, which in a district of houses means floors and basements let by owners living in the same building; one r/bronx reply describes “multi family homes that might have their ground floor apartment to rent.” Bass ignores the deed and goes through the joists. So our first question is not which bar you bought, but who sleeps where, and when.

Care at home is the other thread. Calvary runs its hospice programme, Calvary@Home, from 1740 Eastchester Road, with nurses, aides and social workers working inside family homes. Hospital staff move in for jobs on the campus, and older residents are often looked after by family on the floor above. So a TV and soundbar may need to be heard from a bed, run from one remote, kept clear of a walker or set up beside someone with a pacemaker. That takes planning before the first hole, not special equipment.

Then there is Morris Park Avenue itself: Italian-American bakeries, pizzerias and restaurants, a large Albanian-American community, and a Columbus Day parade every year since 1977. When Italy won the 2006 World Cup, Wikipedia records, over 30,000 people poured in for an all-day party. Big gatherings around one screen are part of life here, with somebody upstairs often trying to sleep through them.

What is usually behind the TV wall in a Morris Park house

Brick and block. A semi-detached house's party wall and the outside walls of many brick houses are solid masonry, sometimes with plaster laid straight onto it and sometimes with furring strips and board in front. We find out which before drilling: the scan, a knock, and one small pilot hole inside the bracket's footprint. Solid brick takes a hammer drill and sleeve or screw anchors set into the face of the brick rather than the mortar joint. Brick or stone is a $65 line on our wall table, concrete or block $75, and there is no cavity to fish a cable through, so the wiring goes in a painted channel or along to an interior wall.

Plaster on lath. Houses from before the war often kept their plaster, over wood lath or a later gypsum lath. It is hard, sometimes cracked, and easy to chip at the edge of a hole. We drill slowly without hammer action, aim for the studs behind it, and use a toggle sized for the bracket where there is no stud where the bar needs to be. Plaster is a $45 line.

Renovated drywall. Plenty of two-families have been updated floor by floor, so the upstairs living room may be fresh drywall over wood studs while the ground floor still has its plaster. Drywall on wood studs carries no surcharge, and it is the one wall where hidden in-wall wiring is usually straightforward, as long as the stud bay is clear of a stair, a duct or a plumbing stack.

Basement walls. A finished basement may be drywall on furring strips in front of the poured or block foundation. Furring is a thin wooden strip, not a stud, so a bracket goes through it into the masonry behind, or onto a backer board ($85) spanning two strips. If the basement is a separate apartment, the ceiling above it is not ours to cut into; more on that further down.

Attic rooms. Under a pitched roof the TV often lands on a short knee wall or the gable end. Up there a compact bar on the console usually beats a long bar on a bracket, and a streaming bar needs a Wi-Fi signal that has made it up two flights. If the router is in the basement, structured cabling installation in Morris Park can run a wired drop to the TV wall on the same visit; for a cable already in the walls that has stopped working, see AESS network cable repair in the Bronx.

What Morris Park businesses should check before a soundbar goes up

  • Flats above the shop set the limit. DEP's summary of the noise code caps a business's music at 42 decibels inside a neighbouring home, and overnight (10 PM to 7 AM) at 7 decibels over street ambient, with its own test for pervasive bass. Wherever people live above or beside the business, as they do in the avenue's apartment buildings, a commercial bar here is tuned for voices with little or no sub.
  • Keep screens off the walking route. Section 307.2 of the 2010 ADA Standards limits wall-mounted objects between 27 and 80 inches above the floor to a 4-inch projection where people walk. A TV with a bar under it sticks out further, so in a narrow waiting room it goes behind the desk or higher on the wall.

Your Questions Answered

Soundbar Installation in Morris Park: Questions Answered

Three themes come up again and again in this neighbourhood: the hospitals next door, two households in one house, and the avenue's restaurants. The answers below follow those themes; costed combinations sit in the FAQ near the end. We are installers, not doctors or lawyers, so a clinician, a lease or the building code always overrides us.

Living next to the hospitals

Mom is coming home on hospice and the hospital bed is going in our front room — where should the TV and soundbar go so she can see and hear from the bed?

Start from her pillow, not from the wall. A hospital bed raises the head and changes where her eyes and ears are, often well above the 42 inches or so that placement guides assume for someone on a sofa, and the bed usually sits where the nurses and aides can reach both sides of it. Once the bed's position is settled with whoever is coordinating her care, we put the screen where she can see it without turning her head: on a stand or an arm at the foot of the bed, or on the wall opposite, with the soundbar a short gap below the screen and aimed toward the head of the bed rather than at the empty sofa.

Then we make the room easy for the people working in it. Cables run along the skirting or up to a shelf, never across the floor where a wheeled bed, a commode chair or an oxygen line will go. The subwoofer, if there is one, stays low in level and out of the bed's corner. Dialogue enhancement goes on, because a concentrator or a fan in the room competes with speech, and the TV remote is set to run the bar so one handset does everything. If the TV is already on an articulating arm, a TV-attached bracket for the bar is $129 to fit plus $84.99 for the bracket, and the two swing together toward the bed. A screen on a stand at the foot of the bed with the bar in front of it needs no drilling at all.

Calvary@Home's nurses and aides will be in and out of that room, so ask them where they need clear space and tell us when they visit; we keep our visit short and around their schedule.

My father has a pacemaker — is it safe for him to sit close to the subwoofer or to handle the soundbar?

For ordinary use, the published guidance is reassuring. The American Heart Association lists “TV and stereo speakers” among household electronics that pose little or no risk to pacemakers and implanted defibrillators. What it does flag is magnets held close to the device: it advises keeping magnets at least 6 inches from where the device is implanted, keeping headphones at least 6 inches away, and not draping headphones around the neck so they hang on the chest. The FDA gives the same six-inch distance for consumer electronics with magnets, such as phones and smartwatches.

A bar on the wall and a sub across the room are far outside that distance for someone on the sofa. The guidance is about close contact: a small speaker or sub on the lap or chest, leaning on the sub, or headphones hung round the neck. So we put the sub where nobody leans on it, keep TV headphones on a hook, and teach him the TV remote so he never reaches behind the bar.

His cardiologist or device clinic has the final say; if they give a different distance, we place the equipment to match.

I'm on call for the hospital some nights — how do I make sure I still hear my phone ring over the soundbar?

A soundbar generally has no idea your phone is ringing, so it comes down to setup. First, don't pair the on-call phone to the bar by Bluetooth: a paired phone can route its alerts and ringtone to the speaker, where the bar's volume decides whether you hear them. Keep the TV on HDMI and let the phone ring on its own speaker.

Second, take the edge off the loud moments. Night mode or dynamic-range compression narrows the gap between quiet speech and loud action, which means you can watch at a lower overall level and a ringtone still cuts through. We set the subwoofer level down a notch for evenings when you are on call, because bass masks a phone buzzing on a table more than anything else in the room does.

Third, wear the backup: a watch that vibrates on your wrist will catch the call even if the room is loud.

Two households under one roof

My father downstairs keeps his TV very loud because he can't make out the words — would a soundbar let him hear clearly at a volume that doesn't fill our floor too?

Often, yes, and this is one of the most useful things a soundbar does in a two-family house. Turning up a TV's built-in speakers raises everything at once: the music, the crowd noise, the bass that travels through floors, and the speech he actually wants. A bar with a dedicated centre channel, and a dialogue or speech setting switched on, lifts the voices in particular, so he can usually understand them at a lower overall volume than before. Some bars go further: Sonos says its Speech Enhancement on the Arc Ultra has four levels, the highest designed with the hearing-loss charity RNID for viewers with hearing loss.

Placement helps too. We mount the bar at the height of his ears when he is sitting in his chair rather than high on the wall, keep the subwoofer low or leave it out, and take it off the ceiling you share; a sub on a joist floor sends bass straight up into your living room. Then we sit in his chair, set a level he is happy with, and go upstairs to listen on your side before we leave.

We're legalizing our basement apartment under the city's pilot program — can the TV cables still go through the ceiling between the basement and our floor?

Not by drilling a quick hole, and not without checking with whoever is designing the legalization. Morris Park sits in Bronx Community District 11, one of the districts in the basement and cellar pilot set up by Local Law 126 of 2024. Under that law an owner who receives the city's temporary authorization has a year to show that the unit meets the fire separation standards in Appendix U of the building code, and two years to install sprinklers. The ceiling between the basement and the floor above is exactly the kind of construction that separation is about.

So each floor's equipment stays on that floor: the basement bar on the basement's own walls with cables in a channel, your system wired inside your own walls. If a cable truly must cross between floors, the architect or engineer behind the legalization specifies how the opening is sealed, and their contractor makes it before the ceiling closes. We won't punch through a rated ceiling and leave it.

On Morris Park Avenue

In our restaurant's party room, families bring a laptop or a phone with a slideshow for christenings and communions — can they play it through the TV and soundbar without our Wi-Fi password?

Yes, and it is better that they don't need it. The simplest route is a cable: we fit an HDMI input at a sensible height near the table where the family sets up, run it to the TV, and the bar plays whatever comes in through the TV's eARC or ARC socket. Most laptops plug straight in, and phones need an adapter; we can leave one in a drawer behind the counter. Nothing joins your network, and staff switch back to the restaurant's own source with the TV remote.

Wireless is possible — a streaming stick on a separate guest network for casting, or Bluetooth for music — but casting depends on each guest's phone and Bluetooth can lag behind the picture. For a recorded message, the cable wins.

We also cap the maximum volume and leave a one-page input guide at the host stand.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Soundbar Installation in Morris Park

Google's AI answers weren't available to us during research, so none are quoted here. Instead, every heading is a line printed by a price guide, a maker or a support site, which is the raw material those answers are assembled from, followed by how it plays out in a Morris Park home.

“Surround sound installation costs $241 to $1,350”

Angi publishes that national range for surround sound installation, with an average near $794, and it is a fair picture of wiring speakers into a room. Summaries tend to apply it to any soundbar with a subwoofer and rears.

A bar with wireless rears is a different job.

On our Bronx menu the bar is $129 on the wall or $79 on the console, the bracket $84.99 if needed, and pairing a whole multi-component system is the $350 full home theater line. What changes the price in Morris Park is the wall — $45 for plaster, $65 for brick — and whether the leads are hidden, not how many speakers came in the box.

“Place it anywhere in the room — even on its side and under your sofa”

That is how the Sonos guide describes its Sub 4, and it is true in the sense that bass is hard to locate by ear: you won't hear it coming from the corner. Shortened in a summary, it becomes “the subwoofer can go anywhere,” which is where Morris Park houses push back.

Under a sofa on a timber floor, a sub couples straight into the joists, and the people below hear it as a hum through the ceiling. In a semi-detached house, a sub against the shared wall plays into the neighbours' front room. “Anywhere in the room” is fine acoustically; it isn't fine for whoever lives on the other side.

So we treat the sub's spot as a decision for the whole house. Off the shared wall, off the floor over a bedroom, on an isolating pad if it must sit on joists, and with the level agreed with both households when that's possible.

“Four levels of control, with the highest level designed for viewers with hearing loss”

Sonos and RNID describe the Arc Ultra's Speech Enhancement that way, and it reflects a real improvement in how bars separate voices from background. Summaries can stretch it into “a soundbar solves hearing loss.”

It doesn't, and nobody serious claims it does.

What a good speech setting can do is let someone with mild or moderate difficulty follow dialogue at a lower overall volume, which in a two-family house is a benefit for everyone on both floors. It works best when the bar sits near the listener's ear height and the room isn't fighting it with a fan or an open window onto Williamsbridge Road.

“The Sound bar will turn off automatically if there is no audio signal for 20 minutes”

Samsung's support page says exactly that about its bars, and LG's notes that a bar can also shut off because of a faulty or loose cable or a volume set too low. Summaries usually present auto-off as a fault to be fixed.

Field Notes: Reading a Two-Family House Before Choosing the Wall

A general checklist we work from in Morris Park two-families, not a story about a specific job.

In a Morris Park two-family, look at the house as a stack before you look at the wall. Count the doorbells and the meters by the side entrance; two of each usually means two households.
