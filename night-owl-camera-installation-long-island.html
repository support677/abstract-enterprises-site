Why Long Island Properties Need Night Owl Camera Installation

Long Island is an island of houses, and most of them were built before anyone planned for a camera cable. The U.S. Census Bureau's 2023 American Community Survey counts 477,860 housing units in Nassau County, 364,529 of them one-unit detached houses, with a median year built of 1955. Suffolk County has 579,790 units, 463,095 of them detached, with a median year built of 1970. Put plainly, the typical Nassau house is a postwar cape or ranch, and the typical Suffolk house is a generation younger. This is our Long Island hub page, so it covers both counties: the Nassau Hub around the Coliseum in Uniondale and East Garden City, the Levittown blocks, the South Shore, the North Shore, the Route 110 corridor, Hauppauge, Ronkonkoma, Stony Brook and out to the East End. The two counties have different building ages, different rates on our price list and, in places, different rules, and the page says which is which.

Night Owl is a brand many Long Island homeowners meet on a store shelf. The company describes itself on its own site as the "#1 Wired Security Brand*" in the United States and says its systems need no mandatory monthly subscription or long-term contract. What it does not do is install them. Answering a customer on a Best Buy product page, a Night Owl representative wrote that Night Owl does not offer installation services. That is why a Long Island installer, Total Security, openly asks whether people have bought a camera system from Costco, BJ's, PC Richards or another retailer and need it put up. The box lands in the hallway, and then someone has to decide where the cameras go, how each cable gets from the eave to the recorder, and how the recorder reaches the router. We do that work, and we do it on whatever house the kit happens to have come home to.

The houses are the reason it is harder than the box suggests. Keri Murray Architecture records that 17,447 homes were built in Levittown between 1947 and 1951, ranch homes on concrete slabs, with an expandable attic, no garage, and hot-water radiant heating pipes in the slab. A slab house has no basement to drop cables into, and a floor with heating pipes inside it is a floor you never drill. In Uniondale, NeighborhoodScout reports that single-family detached homes make up 69.74 percent of housing and that 55.45 percent of the housing dates from the 1940s through the 1960s. Homes.com describes the residential side of Uniondale, south of the Hempstead-Bethpage Turnpike, as mostly Cape Cods and minimal traditionals, often with detached garages and private driveways. A camera on that detached garage is a camera at the end of a long cable run, sometimes across a driveway. One buyer reviewing a Night Owl kit on Adorama summed it up: easy to install, apart from the effort of routing the wires through the attic and walls.

Then there is what Long Islanders actually want to watch. In January 2024, ABC7 New York reported on a multi-agency burglary and stolen-car task force, with the Nassau and Suffolk police departments and district attorneys, the NYPD, State Police and the FBI. Police said the burglars were attracted by cars in people's driveways, and that break-ins while people were home had gone up. In November 2025, CBS New York reported that the Suffolk district attorney had charged 14 men in a package-theft ring that operated across 31 communities, including Brentwood, Central Islip, Shirley and Deer Park, from October 2023 to February 2025, and allegedly used hacked systems to learn when Verizon and AT&T devices were being delivered, taking more than 200 phones and tablets. Patch's Garden City crime report for the week ending September 15, 2026 included packages taken from a front porch. News 12 Long Island showed a doorbell camera recording a thief taking a package mid-delivery in Massapequa Park. And in May 2026, Daily Voice reported that a Roslyn Heights homeowner spotted would-be burglars on his surveillance system and called police, and that arrests followed. Driveways, porches and side doors are the views that matter here, and a camera only helps if it is aimed at them, recording, and reachable from your phone.

Weather is the fourth reason. When Tropical Storm Isaias came through in August 2020, ABC7 reported almost half of PSEG's 1.1 million customers in the dark at the peak, with trees and power lines down. A Night Owl recorder is a plug-in box; when the power goes, so does the recording, unless it is on a battery backup. In August 2024, the New York State Department of Financial Services described the first-ever flash flood emergency for Suffolk County, a rain event it called one in a thousand years for the area, the failure of the Mill Pond dam in Stony Brook, and more than 2,000 residents and business owners with flood damage. Hurricane Sandy, according to Wikipedia's account, left the city of Long Beach with 150 million dollars in damage and brought voluntary evacuations of Nassau's South Shore surge area south of Sunrise Highway. A recorder on a basement floor, or a power splitter lying on a garage slab, is in the wrong place on this island. Where the equipment sits is part of the installation, not an afterthought.

Suffolk is divided into 10 towns across 2,373 square miles. Licensing for home improvement work differs between the two counties, police camera programs differ between departments, and the mailing address on a house does not always match the hamlet it sits in. City-Data forum posters, for instance, note that the hamlet of Uniondale and the Uniondale 11553 ZIP code cover different areas. 1950 in Nassau, 1970 in Suffolk: How a Long Island House's Age Decides Where the Night Owl Cables Go

A Night Owl camera does not care what year the house was built. Its cable does. The route from the eave to the recorder depends on whether there is a basement, how the attic is shaped, what the siding and sheathing are made of, and whether the floor hides heating pipes. Across Long Island those features follow the building periods closely enough that the year on the deed is our first clue. The slab ranch: everything goes up, nothing goes down

A Levittown-style ranch is the most common house where a do-it-yourself Night Owl install stalls. The kit's instructions assume you can drop a cable somewhere, and in a house on a slab there is nowhere to drop it. Worse, the original Levitt houses were heated by hot-water pipes laid in that slab, so drilling the floor to bring a cable up behind a TV stand is the one move that can turn a camera job into a plumbing emergency. Many of those houses have since been renovated, extended and converted to other heating, and we never assume the pipes are gone. Our route in a slab ranch goes the other way: from each camera on the eave, into the soffit, across the attic, and down inside a single interior wall or a closet to where the recorder will live. The recorder ends up near the router, often in a hall closet or a home office, and the attic carries every cable. Expandable attics that were later finished into bedrooms complicate it; then we look for the unfinished eave spaces behind the knee walls and work from those.

The cape with the detached garage

The postwar cape is where the detached garage problem lives. Homes.com describes exactly this in Uniondale: Cape Cods and minimal traditionals with detached garages and private driveways. The house cameras are straightforward, with cables running through the knee-wall spaces and the basement where there is one. The garage camera is the long run. A Night Owl wired kit ships each camera with a cable of a fixed length; on older coax kits Night Owl told a Best Buy customer that each camera comes with a 60-foot BNC cable and that a run can go up to 160 feet per camera with extra cabling. A garage at the end of a long driveway can be past what came in the box, and the cable has to cross open ground. We either run it underground in conduit rated for burial, or overhead on a support wire where that is cleaner and allowed, and we bring it into both buildings through sealed entries. Some customers decide to put the recorder in the house and the garage camera on its own run; some prefer a small switch or a second cable path. We talk through the choice before we dig anything.

The split-level and the hi-ranch

Split-levels and hi-ranches, which one local source describes around Hofstra and the Coliseum, stack their rooms half a floor apart. That is good news and bad news. The good news is that the garage, usually tucked under the upper level, has an open ceiling that makes a natural cable highway to the front and side of the house. The bad news is that the walls between levels are offset, so a cable that goes straight down from the attic may land in the middle of a living room ceiling instead of inside a wall. We trace the stacked walls first, usually at the stair or a closet, and use them as the vertical path. The recorder in these houses often fits well on a garage shelf near where the internet comes in, provided it is raised off the floor, ventilated, and on a surge-protected outlet.

The 1970s Suffolk colonial

The Suffolk median house, built around 1970, is often the easiest Night Owl install of all, and it is easy because it was built with a basement and an attic that line up. Cables run from the soffits, through the attic, down a chase or plumbing wall to the basement, and across the basement ceiling to wherever the router is. The attached garage gives another route to the front corners. Two things still need care. Basements in parts of Suffolk took water in August 2024, so the recorder goes on a shelf or wall, not on the slab. And colonial attics are often insulated more heavily than postwar capes, so we keep cables on top of the framing rather than buried in loose insulation where the next person cannot find them.

Apartments, condos and townhouses

NeighborhoodScout puts large apartment complexes at 19.83 percent of Uniondale's housing and attached homes at 5.93 percent, and the same pattern of garden apartments and condos shows up across the island. In those buildings the exterior walls, hallways and grounds usually belong to the landlord, the board or the association. A tenant or owner can often put indoor cameras on their own furniture or walls without touching common property, but a camera on the outside wall or in a hallway needs written approval. We ask for that approval before the visit, and if it does not exist yet we design an indoor-only setup that needs none.

Decide what each camera is for

The most useful thing you can do before we arrive is to write down, for each camera, what you want it to see. "The driveway" is a start; "a face at the side door" and "the plate of a car stopping at the curb" are better, because they decide how close the camera has to be and how wide its view should be. Police in the 2024 task force report said burglars were drawn to cars in driveways, so many Long Island owners want one camera looking down the driveway toward the street and another looking at the cars themselves. A porch camera for packages wants to see the step and the face of whoever bends down to it. A back-yard camera wants the gate and the back door. A short list like that, one line per camera, lets us place each one with a purpose instead of spreading them evenly around the roof line.

Check the outlet and the internet

A Night Owl recorder needs a power outlet and an Ethernet connection to the router. If the router is in a bedroom and the best place for the recorder is a hall closet, we either run an Ethernet cable between them or move the recorder next to the router and bring the camera cables there instead. SafeWise, reviewing a Night Owl DVR, noted that it had no built-in Wi-Fi, had to sit near the router for its Ethernet connection, and that long cable runs add installation time. Tell us which room the internet comes into and whether there is an outlet near it that is not shared with a space heater or an appliance, and we can design around it before the visit.

Building Specialists

Night Owl Camera Installation for Long Island's Architecture

Long Island's housing is more varied than its reputation for "all capes and ranches" suggests. Each type changes three things about a Night Owl install: where the cables can travel, where the recorder can safely sit, and who has to say yes before we drill. These are the types the public record and local sources describe, and how we handle each one.

Levitt-Era Slab Ranches

Concrete slabs with radiant heating pipes in the original houses, expandable attics, no basement. All cable goes up and over; the slab stays untouched. Many have been extended, so we check what each addition was built on.

Postwar Capes

One-and-a-half stories with knee-wall attic spaces, finished upstairs rooms and, often, a detached garage. The knee-wall spaces are the cable route; the garage is the long run.

Split-Levels and Hi-Ranches

Rooms half a floor apart, a tuck-under garage with an open ceiling, and offset walls between levels. The garage ceiling and the stair walls carry the cables.

Suffolk Colonials

Two stories, basement, attic and attached garage, often built around 1970 and after. The friendliest cable routes on the island, as long as the recorder stays off the basement floor.

Waterfront and South Shore Homes

Salt air, wind and flood history south of Sunrise Highway. Sealed cable entries, stainless fixings where they matter, connections kept out of the weather, recorders up high.

East End and Seasonal Homes

Houses that sit empty for months, where remote viewing is the whole point. Recorder placement, power backup and a reliable internet connection matter more than the camera count.

Condos, Townhouses and Garden Apartments

Shared walls and common grounds. Indoor cameras inside the unit, exterior cameras only with written approval from the board, association or landlord.

Mitchel Field Officers' Housing

The Mitchel Air Base and Flight Line historic district, added to the National Register in 2018, includes two rows of former officers' housing on Ellington Avenue. We found no local rules on exterior hardware there and would check before mounting.

On attics in capes and ranches. An attic is a fine place for a camera cable and a poor place for anything else. We keep cables off the attic floor where people step, fasten them to the side of framing, and stay well clear of recessed light housings, bathroom fan ducts and any wiring we find. We do not bury cables in loose insulation, because the next person who has to trace one will not find it. In knee-wall spaces of capes, the space is tight and often hot in summer; we schedule those runs early in the day when we can. Where an expandable attic has been finished into rooms, the eaves behind the knee walls usually still connect from one end of the house to the other, and we use them.

On older houses and what is inside their walls. A restoration contractor working in Uniondale warns that capes and ranches from the postwar building boom frequently contain asbestos materials. That source is a business, not an independent study, and we have no survey of Long Island houses. But it is a sensible caution for any house of that age: we do not cut into old ceiling textures, pipe insulation, siding or flooring we cannot identify. Camera cables can almost always be routed through open attic and basement space and through small holes in framing and trim, without disturbing old materials. If a route would require cutting into something suspicious, we stop and choose another route, and we tell you why.

On the outside wall. Night Owl's own setup guidance is to mount outdoor cameras at least seven feet above the ground, angled slightly down, and it strongly recommends placing outdoor cameras under an eave or awning. On Long Island houses that usually means the soffit or the fascia board just under it, which has the added benefit of letting the cable go straight up into the attic with no exposed wire on the siding. Where a camera has to go on a gable end with no overhang, we pick a fixing point in solid framing, seal the cable entry, and give the connection a weatherproof box so rain driven off the water does not run into it.

What a business Night Owl system has to do differently

A home system records the driveway for the family's own peace of mind. A business system records evidence that someone else, a police officer, an insurer or a lawyer, may need to rely on. That changes several things. The recorder must be somewhere a thief cannot simply unplug or carry out, which usually means a locked office, a closet with a lock, or a wall-mounted lock box, not the shelf under the register. The camera at the door wants to capture a face at the moment someone enters, so it goes inside, looking at the door, at head height plus a little, rather than outside on the wall looking down at the tops of heads. The register camera wants hands and the cash drawer. The lot camera wants the entrance and exit lanes, where every vehicle has to pass. And the recording time should be long enough to cover a long weekend plus the time it takes for someone to notice a problem, which in a small business can be several days. When police released footage from the Jerusalem Avenue robbery, it was useful because the cameras were where they needed to be; our planning on a business job is about getting each camera into that kind of position.

Flex buildings and warehouses on Route 110 and in Hauppauge

The warehouse, flex and R&D buildings that fill the Route 110 corridor and the Hauppauge park have high ceilings, steel framing, block walls and loading doors. A Night Owl system can serve a small unit in one of them well, provided the cable routes are planned for the building rather than for a house. That means supporting cables properly overhead instead of laying them across a drop ceiling, using conduit where the cable is exposed to forklifts or damage, and keeping every run within the length the camera and recorder support. Where a building is large enough that distance becomes the limit, we say so, and the quote shows the extra switch or cable path that the layout needs. Some larger buildings are better served by a commercial-grade system, and we will say that too rather than stretch a consumer kit past what it was designed for.

Rules and practical limits for business cameras

  • Installer licensing. The New York State Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers under General Business Law Article 6-D. A law-firm summary of the regulations (19 NYCRR 195.2) says that license covers installing or servicing CCTV used to detect or monitor intrusion, break-in, theft, movement, sound or fire, and an industry association says unlicensed installers can face fines that reach ten thousand dollars. We work under NYS license #12000287431.
  • County home improvement licensing. Nassau County's Office of Consumer Affairs says it is against the law to operate a home improvement business in Nassau without a license, and its FAQ says contractors who work only on commercial buildings do not need that license. Suffolk's Code, Chapter 563, sets its own licensing for regulated trades, with exceptions for towns and villages that license separately.
  • Audio is not the same as video. New York's eavesdropping law is a one-party consent law, according to the Digital Media Law Project. A business recording conversations it is not part of, such as customers talking at a counter, is a different matter from recording video, and many owners choose to leave camera microphones off.
  • Private spaces are off-limits. A camera in a restroom, changing room or similar private space without consent can be a class E felony under Penal Law section 250.45, according to RecordingLaw. We do not install cameras that view those spaces.
  • Insurance papers. Many commercial landlords and property managers, including those in office towers and shopping centers, want a certificate of insurance before contractors start. Tell us who needs it and how they want it named.
  • Working hours. In occupied stores and offices we schedule drilling and ceiling work for hours when customers and staff are not underneath it.
Field Notes

Field Notes: The First Walk-Around on a Long Island Night Owl Job

We do not publish stories about a particular customer's house unless we can document them, so this is not a tale about one address. It is the routine our technicians follow before a single camera comes out of its packaging, on the kind of house that fills Nassau and Suffolk: a postwar cape or ranch, a router somewhere inside, a driveway down one side and a garage at the end of it.

We read the box label first and confirm whether the kit is coax to a DVR or Ethernet to a PoE NVR. We walk the outside of the house with you, one corner at a time, and ask what that camera is for. We look at the soffits and note which corners have an overhang and which are bare gable. We find the router and the outlet beside it. Then we go up the attic hatch and look across: where the eaves connect, where the insulation is deep, whether there are recessed lights or a fan duct in the way. If there is a basement, we go down and look for a chase or a plumbing wall that lines up with the attic. If the house is on a slab, we look for the one interior wall that can carry the cables down to the recorder. We measure out to the detached garage and look at what lies between. Only when every cable has a planned route, and you have agreed where each camera goes, does the first hole get drilled. On houses this age, that half hour is what separates a clean install from a weekend of loose wires.

Recording, the router and your phone

Do I really need an Ethernet cable from the DVR to the router to see the cameras on my phone, or will Wi-Fi do?

For Night Owl's wired DVR kits, you need the cable. Night Owl representatives have answered on Best Buy that wired Night Owl DVRs must be connected to your router with an Ethernet cable for remote viewing and mobile alerts, and SafeWise noted that the DVR it tested had no built-in Wi-Fi. That is why recorder placement matters so much on Long Island houses: either the recorder sits beside the router, or we run a proper Ethernet cable from the recorder to the router. We do not rely on a wireless bridge as the main link for the system unless there is no alternative, and if we have to use one, we tell you it is the weakest point in the setup.

If the internet goes down, does the Night Owl recorder keep recording?

Yes. Night Owl's support article on wired DVR setup says the DVR does not need the internet for basic operation; it needs the router only for remote viewing on a computer or phone. If your Optimum or Verizon service drops, the recorder keeps writing video to its hard drive, and you simply cannot see it from outside the house until the connection is back. What stops recording is a power cut, not an internet outage, which is why we suggest a battery backup on the recorder and router for Long Island houses that lose power in storms.

Does the recorder have to stay plugged into a TV or monitor?

Not for recording, and not for viewing on your phone. A monitor or TV is useful at setup and for watching live footage at home, and Night Owl's support pages cover connecting one. Many Long Island owners keep the recorder in a closet with no screen at all and use the app. Others want a small monitor in the kitchen or the office. Either works. If you want a monitor, tell us where; if it is not next to the recorder, the video cable has length limits and we plan for it.

Cables, coax and long runs

Our house has coax from an old camera system. Can it be reused for a new Night Owl kit?

It depends on which Night Owl kit you bought. For older DVR-style kits, Night Owl told a Best Buy customer that existing BNC cabling can be used and that you can simply swap the cameras and the DVR. For Night Owl's PoE NVR kits, the company said older cables work only if they are Ethernet (Cat5e or Cat6), and that BNC or coax will not work with a PoE NVR. So the first step is the box label. If it is a DVR kit, we trace the old coax, test each run, and reuse the ones that are sound and reach the corners you want. If it is an NVR kit, the old coax can sometimes serve as a pull string for new Ethernet through the same holes.

Our ranch sits on a slab with no basement. Where do the camera cables actually go?

Up. In a slab house, every camera cable goes from the eave into the attic, travels across the attic to a point above the recorder, and comes down inside one interior wall or a closet. We never drill through the slab: the original Levittown ranches had hot-water heating pipes laid in it, and even in renovated houses we do not assume they are gone. The recorder ends up near the router, often in a hall closet or office, and the attic does all the work. If the attic has been finished into rooms, we use the eave spaces behind the knee walls.

The garage is detached, at the end of a long driveway. How does a camera out there get back to the recorder?

With a dedicated run across the gap, either buried in conduit rated for the ground or overhead on a support wire where that is cleaner and allowed, and sealed where it enters each building. On older coax kits, Night Owl says each camera comes with a 60-foot cable and that runs can go up to 160 feet per camera with extra cabling; PoE runs have their own distance limit. If your garage is farther than the cable allows, we add a small switch or plan a different path, and the quote shows it. We never leave a cable lying loose across a driveway or stapled along a fence where the next mower or snow shovel will cut it.

Placement for Long Island's real problems

How high should the cameras go on a cape or ranch, and why does Night Owl want them under the eave?

Night Owl's setup guidance is at least seven feet above the ground, angled slightly down, and it strongly recommends placing outdoor cameras under an eave or awning. On a typical Long Island cape or ranch, the soffit is a little above that height, and mounting there keeps rain and snow off the lens, makes the camera harder to reach and knock, and lets the cable go straight up into the attic. Too high, and every face becomes the top of a head. We pick a height per camera based on what it needs to see: lower and closer for the side door, higher and wider for the driveway.

Which camera spots matter most for driveway car theft and porch package theft?

Police told ABC7 in 2024 that burglars were drawn to cars in driveways, and Suffolk prosecutors described a ring that targeted delivered phones and tablets across 31 communities. So on most Long Island houses we start with three views: one looking down the driveway toward the street, to catch a car pulling in and the time it did; one looking at the parked cars and the side door; and one at the front step, close enough to show the face of someone bending down to a package. Back yards, gates and the detached garage come next. A system with eight evenly spaced cameras that misses the step is less useful than four placed on purpose.

Hard drives, power and the weather

Can the hard drive in our Night Owl recorder be replaced with a bigger one?

Often, yes, but it depends on the model. Night Owl kits ship with drives of different sizes, and recorders have a maximum they support. We check your recorder's limit, fit a suitable surveillance-rated drive, format it in the recorder, and confirm that it is detected and recording before we leave. A larger drive means more days of footage before the oldest is overwritten, which matters for East End and seasonal homes and for businesses that might not notice a problem for days.

What happens to the cameras when a storm like Isaias knocks out the power?

Without backup, everything stops: the recorder, the cameras it powers and the router. Isaias left almost half of PSEG's 1.1 million customers in the dark at its peak in August 2020. A battery backup sized for the recorder and router keeps recording through short outages and lets the system shut down cleanly instead of crashing, which protects the hard drive. For longer outages, a home generator is the real answer, and the recorder should be on a circuit it feeds. We place the recorder on a surge-protected outlet in every install, because storms also bring power surges.

We live south of Sunrise Highway near the water. What changes for salt air and flooding?

Two things: the outside hardware and the inside equipment. Outside, salt air works on exposed connectors and cheap fixings, so we keep every connection inside a weatherproof box or inside the eave, use corrosion-resistant fixings where they are exposed, and seal each cable entry. Inside, the recorder and any power supplies go high. Sandy brought voluntary evacuations of the Nassau surge area south of Sunrise Highway, and the 2024 Suffolk flood damaged more than 2,000 homes and businesses; a recorder on a basement floor is the first thing lost. We put it on an upper floor or a high wall shelf, near the router.

Rules, permits and the police

For the camera system itself, we have not found a source saying a standard Night Owl install needs a building permit, though towns and villages set their own rules and we check by address. The Nassau County police alarm permit applies, according to a local alarm company, to alarm systems tied into a central monitoring station; a self-monitored Night Owl system is not monitored that way, but we could not confirm in writing how the county treats cameras, so we say so plainly. The Nassau Sheriff's office describes its alarm registration as not mandatory. What does apply is installer licensing: New York requires a state license for installing CCTV used to detect intrusion or theft, and Nassau requires a home improvement license for residential work.

Should we register our Night Owl cameras with the police?

It is your choice, and the programs are voluntary. The Suffolk County Police Department runs a Camera Registry Program in which owners register the location of their systems; the department may ask a participant to check video for a particular date and time, with retrieval at the owner's convenience. The Garden City Police run a private camera registration program and state that it does not give police access to privately owned systems. We found no confirmed county-wide registry run by the Nassau County Police Department. Registration does not change how we install, but if you plan to register, it helps to have the recorder's clock set correctly and a simple way to export clips, which we set up at handover.

FAQ

Long Island Night Owl Camera Installation FAQ

Could I install Night Owl wired cameras on my own Long Island house, and what tools would the job take?
+

Many people do, and Night Owl designs its kits for it. The tools are a drill with long bits, a ladder, a fish tape or glow rods, a stud finder, cable clips and weatherproof sealant, plus patience in the attic. The hard part on Long Island is not the connection but the route: through knee-wall spaces in a cape, across the attic of a slab ranch, or out to a detached garage. If the route stops you, we can finish the job or take over from wherever you got to.

My Night Owl recorder says "no HDD detected." Is that something you fix?
+

Yes. That message can come from a loose or failed drive cable, a drive that was never formatted in the recorder, a drive that has failed, or a recorder fault; a Trustpilot reviewer reported a BTN8-series NVR that would not detect its drive. We check the connection, test the drive, format or replace it, and confirm that recording has restarted. If the recorder itself is at fault and still under Night Owl's warranty, we tell you so you can claim it.

The Night Owl app stopped connecting after we got a new router. What changed?
+

Usually the recorder is no longer on the network the app expects. A new router from your provider often means the recorder's Ethernet cable was never moved across, or its network settings no longer match. We reconnect the recorder to the new router by cable, confirm it is online, and re-add the system in the app on each phone. Changing internet equipment after an install is one of the most common reasons a working system suddenly shows offline.

One camera's night vision stays dark after sunset. Could the power splitter be the problem?
+

It could. Night Owl's own troubleshooting advice is that if a camera's night vision lights do not come on, plugging the camera directly into its own power adapter, bypassing the splitter, may fix it, because infrared lights draw extra power at night. We check the adapter rating, the splitter and the cable length, and give that camera dedicated power if it needs it.

Is Night Owl a good choice for a house on Long Island?
+

It can be a sensible choice for owners who want a wired system with no mandatory subscription and are happy to own the recorder. Its online reviews are mixed, so we test every camera and the hard drive on install day. For most houses here, where the cameras point and how well the cables and recorder are installed matter more to the result than the brand on the box.

We're torn between a Lorex kit and a Night Owl kit. Which would you put on a Nassau cape?
+

We install both, and on a cape the installation work is almost identical: the same attic routes, the same soffit mounts, the same recorder placement. Compare the specific kits you are considering on camera resolution, recorder channels, drive size and app, and buy the one whose app you find easier to use, because that is what you will live with. Send us both model numbers and we will tell you if either has a wiring difference that matters for your house.

Is there a monthly fee to keep a Night Owl system recording?
+

Not to record. Night Owl says its systems have no mandatory monthly subscription or contract; video records to the hard drive in the recorder. Night Owl offers optional cloud backup for a monthly charge, which some owners of seasonal homes like as a second copy. We do not charge any monthly fee for a system we install.

Is it legal to record audio on our outdoor cameras in New York?
+

New York is a one-party consent state for recording conversations, according to the Digital Media Law Project, but recording conversations you are not part of, such as passers-by on the sidewalk, is the risky case. Video in private spaces like bathrooms or bedrooms without consent can be a class E felony. Many owners leave outdoor microphones off; we explain the settings and let you decide.

I rent an apartment in a Uniondale complex. Can I have Night Owl cameras put up?
+

Inside your own unit, usually yes, with indoor cameras that need no exterior drilling. On outside walls, hallways or grounds, the landlord or management owns the space, so you need written permission first. Send it with your booking. If there is none, we design an indoor-only setup that watches your own door and windows without touching common property.

Will cameras from our older Night Owl kit work with the new recorder we just bought?
+

Not always. Some Night Owl series are not backward compatible with other Night Owl wired products, and coax cameras will not run on a PoE NVR. Send us the model numbers of the old cameras and the new recorder before the visit, and we will tell you which cameras can stay, which have to go, and whether the old cables can be reused.

Do you install Night Owl systems on the North Fork, the South Fork and other East End homes?
+

Yes, at the Suffolk rate. For houses that sit empty for part of the year, we focus on what matters when nobody is there: a reliable wired link from recorder to router, a battery backup, a hard drive large enough to hold weeks of footage, and app alerts set so you hear about a car in the driveway, not every passing deer.

Wired or wireless Night Owl cameras for a house in Nassau?
+

For outdoor cameras on a house you own, wired is usually the better choice: no batteries to charge, no dependence on Wi-Fi reaching the far corner, and a recorder that keeps working when the internet drops. Wireless indoor cameras make sense for renters and condo owners who cannot run cable. Many Nassau owners mix the two, wired outside and a wireless camera or two inside.

How long does a typical Night Owl installation day take on Long Island?
+

It depends almost entirely on the cable routes, which is why we will not promise hours before seeing the house. A four-camera kit on a colonial with an open basement and attic goes much faster than an eight-camera kit on a slab ranch with a detached garage. After the walk-around, the technician tells you how the day will run, and the price is set by the list, not by the clock.

Our house was built in the 1950s. Is drilling for camera cables safe with older building materials?
+

We plan for it. A restoration contractor working locally warns that many postwar capes and ranches contain asbestos materials, so we route cables through open attic and basement space and small holes in framing and trim, and we do not cut into old ceiling textures, pipe wrap, siding or flooring we cannot identify. If the only route would disturb a suspicious material, we stop and choose another.
