The Neighborhood

Why Bedford-Stuyvesant Properties Need Smart Lock Installation That Records Who Holds the House

Most smart-lock pages sell convenience. In Bed-Stuy the stronger reason is custody. A brownstone on Macon, Decatur or MacDonough Street may have been bought by a grandparent, raised two generations, and now belong — on paper or not yet on paper — to several grown children who live in three different places. Over those decades keys were cut for tenants, carers, neighbours, a nephew who stayed for a year, the roofer, the oil man. Nobody can say how many are still out there. That uncertainty used to be a nuisance. Today, in this neighbourhood, it is a risk, because the houses are worth a great deal and a lot of people know it.

The numbers explain why. The NYU Furman Center’s neighbourhood profile, last updated in May 2026, puts Bedford-Stuyvesant’s homeownership rate at 19.7 percent in 2024, against a population of 181,949. Only about one household in five owns, so the owners there are concentrated in exactly the rowhouses that families held on to through the lean decades. Those same houses are what outsiders now chase. NPR’s Code Switch, in a July 2026 episode featuring Councilmember Chi Ossé and WNYC/Gothamist reporter David Brand, described deed theft as rampant in gentrifying areas “like Bed Stuy, where brownstones that redlining once made cheap now sell for millions,” and reported that complaints to the state Attorney General rose 240 percent between 2023 and 2025, with more than 500 in 2025 alone.

Deed theft is a paperwork crime, and no lock stops a forged signature. But the way it plays out on the ground often runs through the front door. Scammers, NPR reported, knock, call, leave leaflets and approach through distant relatives; the Attorney General’s office lists empty houses and homes “where the homeowner is deceased and the heirs do not legally transfer title” among the properties thieves target, and tells owners plainly: “Do not abandon your property. Take care of it yourself or ask someone else to look after it.” In December 2025 News 12 reported a Bed-Stuy woman whose family had owned their paid-off brownstone for 60 years answering the doorbell to a man who said he was there to evict her. A house whose doors are visibly controlled, whose every entry is logged and whose owners are alerted when it opens is a harder house to walk into — and a better-documented one if somebody later claims they were let in.

The second thing that sets Bed-Stuy apart is how much of it is landmarked. The Stuyvesant Heights Historic District was designated on September 14, 1971. The Bedford-Stuyvesant/Expanded Stuyvesant Heights Historic District, designated in 2013, added roughly 825 buildings around it. On December 8, 2015 the Landmarks Preservation Commission unanimously designated the Bedford Historic District — about 800 buildings, mostly rowhouses and small apartment houses built between 1870 and 1900, roughly between Bedford, Nostrand, Putnam, Tompkins and Marcy Avenues and Monroe, Fulton and Halsey Streets. On blocks inside those lines, replacing a lock is generally light work in the commission’s eyes, but anything mounted beside the door is governed by specific rules, and we plan keypads and readers around them rather than discover them afterwards.

Third, the neighbourhood is not only brownstones. Bed-Stuy holds some of the largest public-housing campuses in Brooklyn — the Furman Center counts 9,802 public-housing units, 12 percent of the area’s rentals — plus a wave of new construction: 11,644 units added between 2010 and 2025, almost all in buildings of four or more units. And it has busy shopping streets, led by the mile-long Fulton Street corridor that the Bed-Stuy Gateway Business Improvement District says holds around 400 businesses. Each of those has a different answer to “who decides what goes on this door,” and we start every job by working out which answer applies.

Building Specialists

Smart Lock Installation for Bedford-Stuyvesant’s Architecture

Bed-Stuy was built quickly and then left largely alone, which is why so much of it survives. The Bedford district’s designation report, as summarised by the Historic Districts Council, calls its concentration of row houses “a rare showcase for the rapid changes in row house fashion” across a short span of years. For a lock installer that means variety: neighbouring houses built a decade apart can have completely different doors.

Brownstone and Brick Rowhouses, 1870–1900

The core of the historic districts: high stoops, parlour-floor entrances, double outer doors or a single heavy leaf, a vestibule and an inner door. Many are still owner-occupied by the same family, with the garden floor let or used by relatives.

Limestone and Romanesque Revival Rows

Later houses with rock-faced stone, arched entrances and deep reveals. The arch often shelters the doors well, but the stone surround leaves little flat surface for anything mounted beside them.

Small Apartment Houses and French Flats

Three- to six-storey buildings from the same decades, with a street door, a hall and several units. These are multiple dwellings, so the street door, unit-door and tenant rules all apply, and a shared foyer is where parcels tend to go missing.

Grand Apartment Buildings

Landmarks such as Montrose W. Morris’s Alhambra Apartments of 1889–90 on Nostrand Avenue. Unit-door work runs through management and the building’s own entry arrangements.

Public Housing Campuses

Marcy Houses (27 buildings, 1,705 apartments, completed 1949), Sumner, Tompkins, Brevoort, Roosevelt, Armstrong and Stuyvesant Gardens. Apartment doors and building entries belong to the housing authority; residents should not have locks drilled without its say-so.

Post-2010 Mid-Rises

Concrete or steel-stud rental and condo buildings with metal unit doors, developer-chosen lobby systems and house rules. Signal in the corridor and compatibility with the building’s platform matter more than period detail.

Converted Two- and Three-Family Houses

Rowhouses split into floor-through apartments decades ago, often with a hall door at the top of the stoop that every household shares. Crossing from two families to three changes which housing rules apply.

Houses Mid-Renovation by New Owners

Gut renovations where the house is a building site for months. Access needs to be granted by the week to trades, then withdrawn completely on the day the owners move in.

How we work on Bed-Stuy’s older doors

Reading the bore history. A door that has carried a family for a century usually carries the evidence: a filled hole from a rim lock, a mortise pocket from the original lock, a round bore from a 1970s deadbolt, a second one from a 1990s replacement. Before we recommend a lock we take the old hardware off and measure what is left. If the wood around the bore is split or packed with filler, the smart lock goes on a reinforced plate or a different door, not into the damage.

Keeping the face of the door. On landmarked houses we prefer locks whose outside face is small and dark, or interior-only retrofit units that leave the street side exactly as it was. Where the original lock must stay for appearance, the keyless function moves to the vestibule door. Every piece of original hardware we remove is labelled and returned to the family, not thrown away.

Stone, not wood, around the door. Brownstone and limestone surrounds crumble when drilled carelessly. Where a keypad, reader or bell has to sit beside an opening we fix into mortar joints or the door frame rather than the carved face, and we route any cable inside the house, never across the facade.

Empty-house power and signal. A battery smart lock keeps working when the electricity at an empty house is cut, but notifications and remote unlocks need the bridge or router to stay powered and online. On estate houses we confirm with the family who is paying for the electricity and internet while the house is empty, because the monitoring is only as good as that bill.

Exit without a key. Whether it is a parlour door, a garden door or a cellar door, every door we touch opens from inside in one motion without a key, code or phone. On apartment doors in multiple dwellings, New York City’s housing agency, HPD, states that double-cylinder locks are illegal; we apply the same principle to every house door we work on.

Compliance points for Bed-Stuy business doors

  • Egress first. Doors on an exit route must open from inside without a key, code or special knowledge. A keypad outside is fine; anything that needs a credential to get out is not. Magnetic locks and strikes on exit doors are paired with an inside release that works without power.
  • Storefronts inside historic districts. Several Fulton Street and Nostrand Avenue blocks fall within or beside the historic districts. Replacing a lock is light work; a new reader or box on the facade is governed by the commission’s rules on what may be fixed beside a door opening.
  • Homes upstairs. Where a shop shares a building with apartments, the residential street door keeps its own credentials and its own housing rules. No staff code opens the residents’ door, and the residents’ exit route is never locked to suit the shop.
  • Accessible hardware. Under the 2010 ADA Standards, door hardware should be usable without tight grasping, pinching or twisting of the wrist, and mounted between 34 and 48 inches above the floor. Levers and push-button keypads meet that more easily than knobs.
  • Congregations and community rooms. Churches and community groups that share a building need schedules by group and day rather than a key passed between volunteers; we set those up and leave a written list of who holds which code.

Many Fulton Street businesses add cameras after a lock upgrade so that each door event can be checked against video; our commercial security camera installation in Brooklyn handles that work.

Your Questions Answered

Smart Lock Installation in Bedford-Stuyvesant: Questions Answered

These are the questions Bed-Stuy families, owners and shopkeepers raise before a keyless lock goes on, grouped by the situation behind them: houses passing between generations, empty houses, landmarked rows, shared foyers and back doors. Booking and visit details sit in the FAQ further down. Nothing here is legal advice; where a law, a city office or a court process matters, we name it so you can follow it up with your own lawyer.

Houses passing between generations

Our late mother’s brownstone is empty while the estate goes through Surrogate’s Court — what should happen to the keys and codes right now?

Take stock first, then close every route you cannot account for. Most family houses in Bed-Stuy have keys in more hands than anyone remembers: past tenants, home attendants, neighbours who watered plants, cousins who stayed for a summer, contractors from years ago. The safest assumption on the day the house becomes empty is that some of those keys still work. So we start by asking the family to list everyone they know held a key or code, then we change or retire every mechanical cylinder on every exterior door — parlour door, vestibule door, garden door, rear door, cellar — so that none of those old keys open anything.

Then the house needs one clearly named custodian. That is usually the person the family expects to be appointed executor or administrator, or the relative who lives nearest and has agreed to look after it. That person holds the admin account on the smart lock, a second family member is added as a backup admin, and everyone else who genuinely needs access — a sibling who checks the mail, a cleaner, a surveyor — receives a personal code that can be removed without affecting anyone else. The Attorney General’s deed-theft guidance tells owners not to abandon a property and to “ask someone else to look after it”; a custodian with a working, logged lock is how that looks in practice.

Two things we do not do. We do not decide who in the family is entitled to the house — that is for the court and your lawyer. And we do not change locks on a house where someone is currently living without that person’s agreement or a court order, even if the family believes they have no right to be there. If you are unsure whether anyone is staying in the house, tell us; we will look before we work.

Several of us inherited the house and none of us lives there — whose phone should hold the admin account?

Never just one phone. An admin account is the power to add and delete everyone else, so in a house owned by several heirs it should behave like a joint bank account: two named administrators, ideally from different branches of the family, each able to act if the other is unreachable, and neither able to quietly lock the other out without it showing in the history. Most current smart-lock apps let the owner invite at least one further person with full or near-full rights; we set both up, test both, and write down which email address each account sits on.

Below the administrators, every heir gets an individual credential rather than a shared family code. That matters more than it sounds. When an inherited house becomes contested — and the Center for NYC Neighborhoods counts roughly 350 lawsuits a year across the city over homes in inherited-ownership disputes — the first argument is often about who went in, when, and what they took. A shared code answers none of that. Personal codes, with an entry log that shows which one opened the door, answer most of it.

Finally, agree in writing, before the lock is fitted, what the administrators may and may not do: add a contractor for a fixed week, yes; remove another heir, only with notice to all of them. We leave a one-page access register with the names, credentials and dates so that the agreement and the lock’s settings match.

How can a lock’s entry history help show that nobody was let into an empty house without the family’s say-so?

An entry history is a timeline: this door, this credential, this date and time, locked or unlocked. On an empty house it does two jobs. While nothing happens, it is a quiet record that the house has been secured and visited only by the people the family authorised. When something does happen — a door found unlocked, a stranger claiming they were given permission to stay — it shows which credential opened the door, or that none did and the entry was forced.

To make that history worth something, set the lock up properly from the start. Each person has their own code; no code is ever shared. The lock is connected, so events reach the cloud and the family’s phones rather than sitting only in the lock’s memory. Notifications go to at least two people. And someone exports or screenshots the history regularly, because app histories are not kept forever and a record you can produce months later is better than one that has rolled off the end.

Be realistic about its limits. A lock log does not prove who owns the house, and it will not stop a fraudulent deed being recorded at the City Register. For that, the city’s Mayor’s Office of Deed Theft Prevention offers free property and deed alerts through ACRIS, so the owner of record is told when documents are filed against the property. The lock covers the door; the alert covers the paperwork. In Bed-Stuy we recommend both.

We found someone living in our relative’s vacant house — can we just change the code or the lock?

Do not try, and do not ask us to. The law here moved in 2024: the state budget signed that April changed the definition so that a tenant does not include a squatter, and further bills have been introduced since. But how a particular occupant is treated still depends on facts a court or the police will weigh — how they got in, how long they have been there, what paperwork they hold. Fox 5 New York reported a Queens case in which the homeowner herself was arrested when she tried to change the locks on people occupying her house. New York City’s Administrative Code makes it unlawful to change the lock on an occupant of 30 or more days without supplying them a key, and those rules are enforced first and argued about later.

So the order is: call 911 if anyone is in danger or you believe a break-in is in progress; call the local precinct — Bed-Stuy is covered by the 79th and the 81st — to report the occupation; contact a lawyer, the Attorney General’s Homeowner Protection Program or the city’s deed-theft hotline; and gather any record you have of the house being secured and empty, including a lock’s entry history if one was fitted. If the occupant is in housing court claiming the house through a transfer you believe was fraudulent, the Attorney General notes you can ask the judge to pause the case for 90 days while ownership is challenged in the right court.

Our part comes afterwards, once the house is lawfully back in the family’s hands: re-securing every door the same day, fitting a monitored lock so the next attempt is noticed in minutes rather than months, and leaving you with a written register of who can get in. This is the strongest argument we know for fitting a connected lock the week a family house becomes empty, not after something goes wrong.

People keep knocking with offers to “help” with the house — how should an older owner decide who gets the door opened?

Start from the habit that protects most: do not open the door to talk business. NPR’s reporting on deed theft in Bed-Stuy described scammers who knock, phone, leave leaflets or approach through distant relatives, offering loans, repairs or a way out of a mortgage, and then slip a transfer of the house into the paperwork. The Attorney General’s advice is just as direct: never transfer a deed without your own attorney, never sign anything you do not understand, and be wary of anyone who says you can “buy back” your home.

Hardware can make that habit easy for an older owner to keep. A video doorbell or small camera by the door lets them see and speak to a caller from the kitchen or their phone without unlocking anything. A smart lock means the people who should come in — family, the home attendant, the doctor’s nurse — have their own codes and never need the owner to come to the door at all, so a knock from a stranger can simply go unanswered. And because every family visitor has a code, the family can see from the history that the attendant came on Tuesday and nobody else did.

We set these up with the owner, at their pace, and leave large-print instructions. We also suggest the family sign the owner up for the city’s free deed alerts and keep the number for the city’s deed-theft hotline, 1-855-HOME-456, by the phone. A lock keeps people out of the hall; only those steps keep them out of the deed.

Landmarked rows

Our row is in the Bedford or Stuyvesant Heights historic district — where may a keypad or reader be mounted beside the front doors?

Within or right next to the door opening, and modestly. The Landmarks Preservation Commission’s permit guidebook does not have a section called “keypads”, but it does set out rules for the closest thing: light fixtures, intercom panels and doorbells “must be installed within or adjacent to a door opening,” and on a given opening there may be no more than two light fixtures and one intercom panel. We apply the same logic to a wall-mounted keypad or reader. Practically, that means inside the door reveal or on the frame, on the vestibule wall behind the outer doors, or on the outer leaf itself as part of the lock — not out on the carved face of the stoop surround.

The simplest answer is usually to avoid a separate wall device altogether. A smart deadbolt carries its own keypad on the door, which counts as hardware rather than an attachment to the facade; the guidebook lists repairing or replacing hardware such as hinges, knobs, locks and handles among work that may not need a permit at all. Where a separate reader is unavoidable — say, for a three-family house whose street door needs fob access — we fix it in the reveal or into mortar joints, keep it small and dark, and run its cable inside the house.

If your project goes beyond hardware — a new door, new ironwork, cutting stone, exposed conduit — talk to LPC staff before work starts. The commission’s staff can tell you whether a proposal fits its rules or needs review. We are glad to send them photographs and the lock’s drawings so the conversation is concrete.

There is already an intercom panel at our door opening — can a separate keypad still be added on a landmarked facade?

Often the better route is to replace the panel rather than add beside it. Because the guidebook allows one intercom panel per door opening, a second box next to an existing buzzer panel on a landmarked house is the kind of addition that invites questions. Many current entry panels combine call buttons and a keypad or fob reader in one plate, so a three-family house can swap its tired buzzer panel for a single combined unit in the same position and gain keyless entry without adding anything to the facade.

Where the existing panel must stay — it still works and the tenants rely on it — the keypad can go on the door itself as part of a smart deadbolt, or on the inside of the vestibule if only residents need it. Couriers and visitors keep using the bell; residents use the lock. That keeps the outside of the house exactly as it is.

If the buzzer system itself is failing, we would rather fix or replace it properly than build a lock plan around a fault. Our intercom installation in Bedford-Stuyvesant covers panel replacements on rowhouse fronts, and the two jobs can be done at the same visit.

Can the keypad for our front-yard gate go on the iron fence at the sidewalk instead of on the house?

In principle, yes. The same guidebook passage that limits intercoms to the door opening adds that “an intercom can also be installed on a fence or wall in front of the entranceway,” and a gate keypad sits in the same place for the same reason. On many Bed-Stuy rows the low iron fence and gate are original or carefully restored, though, so we fix to a post or a plate rather than drilling decorative ironwork, and we keep the device as small as the job allows.

The harder question is whether a locked gate is what you want. Gates on these rows usually open onto a small front yard and the areaway below the stoop — which is where parcels get left and where one r/BedStuy poster near Lafayette and Throop described a man who “politely opens my gate, steals my package & politely closes gate” at seven in the morning. A locked gate stops that, but it also stops couriers, meter readers and anyone who needs to reach the garden-floor door, so it needs a delivery code, a bell and, if the garden floor is someone’s home, an inside release that works in one motion without a key.

Power and cable are the practical limits. A battery keypad gate lock needs no wiring at all; a powered reader and gate strike needs a cable from the house, which we bring under the yard or along the areaway wall rather than across the stoop. We price both and let you choose.

Shared foyers and back doors

Parcels go missing from our building’s shared foyer even though the street door locks — how are people getting in, and what fixes it?

Almost always by a door that does not finish closing, a code too many people know, or a neighbour buzzing in whoever rings. One r/BedStuy thread from Lexington Avenue described someone getting in repeatedly over months and taking parcels left in the foyer — three times for one resident. In small walk-ups that pattern is common: the street door is heavy, its closer is tired, and it stops a quarter-inch short of latching, so anyone who pushes walks straight in.

The fix comes in layers. First the door: closer adjusted or replaced, strike aligned, latch checked so the door locks every single time it swings shut. Second the credentials: one personal code or fob per resident on the street door instead of one number shared since the last tenant left, so a leaked code can be removed in a minute. Third, if the building has space, a lockable parcel cupboard or room with its own keypad — one r/Brooklyn poster in a non-doorman building said adding a keypad deadbolt to the package room door “stopped most thieves.”

A building with three or more households is a multiple dwelling, so the city’s housing rules on entrance doors and self-closing doors apply, and under the Tenant Data Privacy Act an owner may not make a smart access system the only way into a tenant’s apartment. For buildings whose old buzzer is part of the problem, our Bed-Stuy door buzzer repair and the lock plan usually go together.

A house on our block was entered through the back — what should the rear garden door of a rowhouse get?

A lock that is as strong from the yard as the front door is from the street, and an inside release that a child or an elderly parent can use in the dark. Rear doors on Bed-Stuy rowhouses are often the original garden-floor door or a cheap replacement from decades ago, with glass in the top half, a worn frame and a single spring latch. Because the yard is enclosed by neighbours’ walls, people assume it is private; in practice it is the side of the house nobody watches.

We start with the frame and hinges, because a smart deadbolt in a soft or split frame fails at the first kick. Longer screws into the framing, a reinforced strike and, where needed, a new frame section come first. Then a keyless deadbolt with auto-lock, so the door relocks after someone takes out the rubbish, and a plain thumb-turn inside. If there is glass within reach of the thumb-turn we look at security film or a lock position further from the glass rather than a key-both-sides deadbolt, which would trap people inside in a fire.

The 79th Precinct has itself posted crime-pattern notices asking Bed-Stuy residents to lock doors and windows after rises in residential and commercial burglaries. A connected lock on the rear door lets you check from your phone at night that it really is locked, and alerts you if it opens while the house is empty. Many owners add a camera covering the yard at the same time.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Smart Lock Installation in Bedford-Stuyvesant

Our research tools could not display Google’s AI Overview for this neighbourhood, so the headings below are not quotations from it. They are claims that cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, retailers and general smart-home articles commonly make, and that answer engines tend to repeat. Underneath each is what changes on a Bed-Stuy block.

“Installing a smart lock costs about $300”

Fixr’s national guide, last updated in January 2025, gives $250 to $400 for having a smart lock put in, averaging $300, and that is a fair figure for one modern door in a suburban house. Angi and HomeAdvisor pages tend to land in a similar band.

An estate house in Bed-Stuy is rarely one door. It is a parlour door, a vestibule door, a garden door, a rear door and a cellar door, every one of which may still open to keys nobody can account for. The smart lock may go on only one of them; the cylinders on the rest still have to change on the same day.

So we quote house doors flat after a free walkthrough, and the quote covers every door that needs closing off — not just the one with the keypad. Building-door items carry our published Brooklyn figures.

“A smart lock lets you see who comes and goes”

True only if each person has their own credential. The log records which code or fingerprint opened the door, not who was standing there. If the whole family uses one code, the history says only that “the family code” was used.

In an inherited house where relatives may later disagree, that difference is the whole point. A shared code produces a log nobody can rely on; individual codes produce a timeline that can be read back months later.

We set one credential per person, two administrators, and notifications to at least two phones, and we tell families to save the history regularly because apps do not keep it indefinitely.

“Change the locks and the problem is solved”

Generic advice for a new homeowner or a bad break-up is to change the locks. In Bed-Stuy that advice can be dangerous when the problem is a person living in the house. Changing a lock on an occupant is a legal act with consequences, and Fox 5 reported a Queens homeowner arrested when she tried it.

And when the problem is deed theft, changing a lock solves nothing: the thief’s weapon is a recorded document, not a key. The Attorney General’s list of protections is about lawyers, wills, records and complaints, not hardware.

Locks are the right tool before anyone moves in and after the house is lawfully back in the family’s hands. In between, the right tools are the precinct, a lawyer and the courts.

“Historic districts make smart locks complicated”

Renovation articles often warn that anything in a landmark district needs approval. For a lock, the opposite is closer to the truth: the commission’s permit guidebook lists repairing or replacing hardware such as hinges, knobs, locks and handles among work that may not need a permit.

Where care is needed is beside the door. The guidebook confines intercom panels and doorbells to the door opening and to one panel per opening, and allows an intercom on a fence or wall in front of the entrance. A wall keypad or reader is planned with that in mind.

In Stuyvesant Heights, the Expanded Stuyvesant Heights district and the Bedford district, that means locks on doors, readers in reveals, cables inside the house and original hardware returned to the owner.

“Smart locks need Wi-Fi to work”

Many setup guides imply the lock is useless without internet. In fact a battery smart lock opens with its keypad or fingerprint reader with no connection at all; it is the remote features — notifications, remote unlocks, a live log — that need a bridge, Wi-Fi or Thread.

That matters at an empty estate house where the family has cancelled broadband. The door still works; the monitoring does not. August’s own support pages ask for its Wi-Fi bridge to sit within 10 to 15 feet of the lock with a strong signal where the bridge is placed, which assumes there is a router to talk to.

We ask the family which bills will be kept up while the house is empty, and design the connection around the answer — including mobile-data hubs where broadband is off.

“Keypad codes are more secure than keys”

Smart-home marketing often presents a keypad as automatically safer than a key. A code is safer only if fewer people know it. A code written on the inside of a kitchen cupboard, texted to a cousin and given to a contractor is a key that can be copied without a locksmith.

The real advantage of a code is that it can be cancelled in seconds, individually, without touching anyone else’s. That only helps if you actually cancel codes when people stop needing them.

We set expiry dates on every temporary code at the time it is created — contractors by the job, estate agents by the listing, cleaners by the schedule — so the clean-up happens whether or not anyone remembers.

“Anyone with a drill can install one”

Bob Vila’s testers and many retailers say a smart lock can be fitted with a screwdriver in about half an hour, and on a new door that is fair. On a Bed-Stuy door from the 1880s, with a filled rim-lock hole, a mortise pocket and two later bores, it is not.

A motorised bolt that rubs in a misaligned strike drains its batteries and stalls; a strike screwed into old, dry wood pulls out at the first shove. On an empty house nobody notices until the lock is dead and the door is unlocked.

We check the door, the frame and the bolt throw before fitting, align and reinforce as needed, and test every credential from both sides before we leave.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Counting the Keys on a Family House

How our technicians approach an inherited or empty house — a working method, not the story of any single customer

Before we touch a screwdriver we sit down with whoever is in charge and make a list on paper: every person who might hold a key or a code, and when they last needed it. Families are often surprised by how long the list gets once they start — the tenant from years ago, the aide, the neighbour, the handyman. Then we walk the house from the cellar up, trying every door from both sides and noting which lock and which key belongs to each.

On the doors themselves we look for the history: filled holes, a second deadbolt somebody added, a strike that has been moved, a glass pane replaced with plywood. Those tell us where the house has been forced or patched before, and where a new lock needs reinforcing rather than simply swapping.

Last, we ask the question families find hardest: who will hold the admin, and who will be the second? We do not leave until both are set up, tested from two phones, and written on the register alongside everyone else’s name. A lock without an agreed owner just moves the argument from the keys to the app.

Contact the city’s Mayor’s Office of Deed Theft Prevention on 1-855-HOME-456 and sign up for its free property and deed alerts through ACRIS, contact the New York Attorney General’s office or the Brooklyn District Attorney, and speak to your own lawyer before signing anything. A lock protects the door; those steps protect the title.

Can relatives who live out of state join the planning meeting by video?
+

Yes. Many inherited Bed-Stuy houses belong to families spread across several states. We can hold the walkthrough with whoever is in Brooklyn while other relatives join by video call, so everyone hears the same plan for administrators and codes before a single cylinder is changed.

Can a keypad lock be removed later without leaving new holes in an original door?
+

Usually. Wherever the door’s existing bore or mortise is sound we fit the smart lock to that preparation, and interior retrofit units leave the outside untouched. When the lock comes off, the original or a matching mechanical lock can go back into the same holes, and we keep any original hardware labelled for exactly that purpose.
