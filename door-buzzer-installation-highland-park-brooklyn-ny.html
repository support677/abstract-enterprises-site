The Neighborhood

Why Highland Park Properties Need Door Buzzer Installation

Highland Park takes its name from the 141-acre park on the Brooklyn and Queens line, and the houses that climb toward it from Jamaica Avenue are usually described as a section of Cypress Hills rather than a neighborhood of their own. On this page we mean the hill between Jamaica Avenue and Highland Boulevard: Sunnyside Avenue, Highland Place, Miller Avenue, Barbey and Hendrix Streets, Vermont Street, Arlington Avenue near Barbey and Schenck, and the short courts off the side streets. Brownstoner's architecture column has written up runs of brick porch houses from 1900 to 1920 on Sunnyside Avenue, brick singles with Dutch gambrel roofs from 1908 to 1912 on Arlington Avenue, and an Arts and Crafts house from before 1915 on Highland Boulevard. That is the stock most buzzer calls from the hill come from.

Those houses were mostly built for one family or two. Over a century, many of them have come to hold more. A listing for 211 Sunnyside Avenue describes a semi-detached two-family from 1910 with separate electric and gas meters for every apartment and "a full finished basement with outside separate entrance." A listing for 133 Sunnyside Avenue describes two upper units over a garden level. A rental listing for 68 Sunnyside Avenue shows four apartments on two stories in a building from 1910, behind a gate. In each of those houses the front door that once opened for one family now has to answer for two, three or four, and the bell that used to ring in the front hall now has to reach a kitchen two floors up.

That is the most common reason we are called to install a buzzer here. An owner rents out a floor, or an adult child moves into the basement, and suddenly someone is walking down the stairs every time the bell rings to find out who it is for. Or a tenant asks why the front door depends on whoever left last remembering to lock it. A landlord on a real-estate forum described the problem in one sentence about a three-family with no intercom: "the front door relies on tenants remembering to lock it behind them as they leave." A buzzer fixes both halves of that. Each household gets its own button and its own way to answer, and the front door locks every time it shuts, because the people inside can let a visitor in without leaving it on the latch.

The second reason is building. Highland Park is not a neighborhood of big new projects, but it does have empty lots and old houses being replaced. A lot at 25 Sunnyside Avenue has been listed for sale as the site of a planned two-family residence. Marketproof reported new building permits filed in 2025 for a three-story, three-unit building at 488 Jamaica Avenue, replacing a two-story building from 1915, and for a three-story, two-unit building at 494 Jamaica Avenue, replacing a low-rise from 1899. A rental listing for 215 Highland Boulevard describes a two-apartment house built in 2004 with two entrances and a voice intercom. A new house is the cheapest place there is to install a buzzer properly, because the cable can go in while the walls are open, and the most expensive place to get it wrong, because a missing pipe or box is found only after the plaster is painted.

The third reason is the basement. Highland Park sits in Brooklyn Community District 5, where the city ran a pilot from 2019 to help owners of one- to three-unit homes in East New York and Cypress Hills turn basements and cellars into legal apartments. HPD's page for that program now says it is no longer accepting applications, and the state law that opened a new path for basement apartments in 2025 covers 15 community districts, not including Brooklyn's District 5. Owners on the hill still ask us about basement doors constantly, because so many of these houses already have a finished basement with its own way out. What a basement door should and should not have on the buzzer is a question with a clear answer, and we cover it below.

Six facts that shape a Highland Park buzzer installation

  • The houses here are old and small. The Sunnyside Avenue porch houses date from about 1900 to 1920, the Arlington Avenue gambrels from 1908 to 1912, and the listings we read for 211 and 68 Sunnyside Avenue both give 1910 as the year built.
  • Two families is the common case, four is not rare. 211 Sunnyside Avenue is listed as a two-family, 133 Sunnyside Avenue as two upper units over a garden level, 215 Highland Boulevard as two apartments, and 68 Sunnyside Avenue as four units on two stories.
  • Three households changes the legal category. New York's Multiple Dwelling Law treats a building occupied by "three or more families living independently of each other" as a multiple dwelling; a house for "not more than two families" is a private dwelling.
  • The city's intercom rule starts at eight. 1 RCNY §42-01 requires self-closing, self-locking entrance doors and an intercommunication system at the main entrance of class A multiple dwellings with eight or more apartments, so almost every house on the hill installs one because the owner wants it, not because the rule demands it.
  • The basement pilot is closed and the new law skips this district. HPD's Basement Apartment Conversion Pilot Program for East New York and Cypress Hills is no longer taking applications, and the 2025 basement law's Brooklyn districts are 4, 10, 11 and 17.
  • There is new construction at the edges. Permits were filed in 2025 for a three-unit building at 488 Jamaica Avenue and a two-unit building at 494 Jamaica Avenue, and a lot at 25 Sunnyside Avenue is marketed for a two-family.

Install or Repair in Highland Park

Need Repair in Highland Park? How to Tell a Fix From a First Real System

Some calls that reach us as installation calls are repairs, and the other way round. A two-family on Sunnyside Avenue that has had a working buzzer for twenty years and stopped ringing last week usually needs a repair: a transformer in the cellar, a button that corroded, a strike that worked its screws loose in a soft frame. Those are priced as repairs, starting with the $250 service call booked online, which is applied toward the work.

The quickest way to tell which you have is to count. If the number of buttons at the door matches the number of households that get visitors, and each household has a station that rings and releases, it is probably a repair. If the count does not match, or the release works from only one place, you are looking at an installation, and our Brooklyn door buzzer installation team prices it from the installation list.

Six-story apartment houses at the crest

Divided two-family houses: one door, two households and a third door below

The commonest house we see on Sunnyside Avenue, Highland Place and the side streets is a brick two-family, often semi-detached, with the owner's apartment on one floor and a tenant on the other. The front door opens into a small hall with the stair going straight up. In many of them there is also a basement that has been finished at some point, and in some, like the listing for 211 Sunnyside Avenue, that basement has its own outside door.

For the buzzer, that means two buttons at the front door, one station on each floor and a release in the front door's frame. It also means a decision about the third door. If no one lives in the basement, that door needs a good lock and a closer, not a button. If someone does, they need their own button somewhere a visitor will find it, and their door needs to lock as reliably as the front. We go through that with the owner at the walkthrough rather than guessing from the outside of the house.

Separate meters are worth noticing too. When each apartment pays for its own electricity, the power supply for a shared front door should not be plugged into either apartment's outlet. It belongs on the house's own circuit, in the cellar or the hall, where the owner can reach it and no tenant's unpaid bill or tripped breaker turns off the front door for everyone.

Small buildings of three or four apartments: the busiest small door on the hill

A two-story building with four apartments behind one door, like the 1910 building at 68 Sunnyside Avenue described in its rental listing, has a front door that opens and closes more often than any house door and less often than a lobby. Four households means four sets of visitors, four sets of deliveries and four sets of people coming home late. It is also, under the Multiple Dwelling Law, a multiple dwelling: three or more families living independently.

These buildings need a small button panel rather than a single doorbell, a station in every apartment and a release that is built for a busy door. The door itself matters more here than anywhere else on the hill. A closer that slams or drifts, a latch that misses its keeper one time in ten, or a lock with a hold-back button someone has pressed will leave the door open for all four households, and no buzzer can fix that by itself.

Where there is a locked gate in front, as the listing for 68 Sunnyside Avenue notes, the visitor's first stop is the gate, not the door. Then the question is whether the release should open the gate, the door or both, and whether the panel belongs at the gate. We answer that on the sidewalk, standing where a courier would stand, rather than from a drawing.

Large single houses being split: the buzzer is part of the plans

Some of the bigger old houses on and near Highland Boulevard have been apartments for decades. Others are being planned into apartments now, with an architect filing with the Buildings Department. When a house goes from one or two families to three, it becomes a multiple dwelling, and the drawings for the conversion are the right place to decide where the panel, the stations and the power supply go.

We work from the architect's floor plans, mark a cable route to each new apartment, and agree with the contractor when the cable goes in. That is usually after framing and before insulation and drywall, with a box at each station location and a protected path from the cellar. The panel and stations themselves go in at the end, once the front door is hung and the apartments have walls to mount on. Doing it in that order means nothing has to be chased into a finished wall later.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Counting Doors, Leaves and Households Before Anything Is Ordered

At a Highland Park house our technician spends the first half hour of a walkthrough on three counts, before any equipment is discussed. Those counts decide most of the price, and most of the mistakes we see in older installations come from getting one of them wrong.

The first count is households. The technician asks who lives where and who receives visitors, not how many apartments the deed shows. A two-family may have an adult son in the finished basement, a top floor shared by two roommates who each want their own calls, or a grandparent on the ground floor who answers the door for everyone. Each household that gets its own visitors gets its own button.

The second count is doors. Front door, basement walk-out, side door to the yard, a gate at the sidewalk, a door from the garage. The technician opens each one, lets it go and watches whether it closes and latches by itself. A door that people use but nobody locks is the door that undoes the buzzer, so every one goes on the list, even if most only need a closer and a lock.

The third count is leaves. Many of the older front entries on the hill are wide, and some are a pair. The technician checks which leaf people actually open, which one is held shut by bolts, whether those bolts are ever used, and what kind of lock the everyday leaf has. That decides where the release goes, and it is the step most often skipped.

Then, and only then, the cellar: where the old bell transformer hangs, what wire runs where, whether the cellar is reached from the hall or only through an apartment, and where a battery-backed power supply can be mounted on the house's own circuit. Every old pair gets metered. Sound pairs are reused, failed ones are replaced, and the quote says which.

Basements and New Households

A Basement With Its Own Door: What a Highland Park Buzzer Should and Should Not Do Below the Stoop

Basement doors come up on almost every walkthrough on the hill. Many houses here have a finished basement, and some, like the 1910 two-family at 211 Sunnyside Avenue described in its listing, have one with walk-out access and its own outside entrance. Owners want to know whether that door belongs on the buzzer, whether it should have a button, and whether they should wire for a basement apartment they might add one day. The answers depend on one question the buzzer cannot answer for you: does a separate household live down there legally?

What the city's basement programs say about this district

Highland Park is part of Brooklyn Community District 5, East New York and Cypress Hills. That district was the site of the city's Basement Apartment Conversion Pilot Program, which HPD ran with the Cypress Hills Local Development Corporation. According to HPD, the pilot offered owner-occupants of one-, two- and three-unit homes low- or no-interest loans of up to $120,000 to convert a basement or cellar into a legal apartment, under building code changes passed in February 2019. HPD's page now says it is no longer accepting applications.

The pilot was small. Brooklyn Paper reported that, as of May 2023, more than 8,000 homes had been identified as possible candidates, just over 100 had passed the city's screening, and five were enrolled, with one under construction. Howard Slatkin of the Citizens Housing and Planning Council, which studied the pilot, told the paper: "Often these property owners are immigrant families renting to immigrant families." The cost of digging out a cellar to the required ceiling height was one of the main reasons so few homes went ahead.

In 2025 a state law opened a new path to legalizing existing basement apartments in New York City, effective June 16, 2025, with applications allowed until April 20, 2029. Epicenter-NYC's guide lists the 15 community districts it covers. In Brooklyn they are Districts 4, 10, 11 and 17. District 5 is not on the list. Among the conditions the guide describes are a ceiling of at least seven feet and "at least one door leading outside."

What that means at a Highland Park front door

We install buzzers; we do not decide whether a basement is a legal apartment, and a button on a door does not make it one. What we can say is how each situation is handled at the door.

  • The basement is storage, a laundry, a family room or part of an apartment upstairs. The basement door gets a lock that works from inside without a key and, if people use it, a closer. It gets no button of its own. If the basement is part of the ground-floor apartment, that apartment's station can have a second station or chime downstairs so nobody misses the front door while they are below.
  • A relative lives down there as part of the household upstairs. Same as above. They share the household's button, and a second station downstairs lets them answer and release without climbing the stairs.
  • A legal apartment exists in the basement. It gets its own button, labeled the way the owner chooses, and its own station. Visitors should be sent to whichever door the occupant actually uses, and that door must lock as reliably as the front.
  • An apartment is planned through a filing with the Buildings Department. We price the new button, station and cable with the rest of the system, run the cable while the walls are open, and connect it when the unit is approved. Until then the button is left blank.

Wiring for the future is cheap when the system is being installed anyway. Most button panels come in sizes, and choosing one with a spare button, plus a spare pair in the cable to the basement, costs little at installation and saves a second visit if a third household ever comes. We do that on request; we do not label or connect a button for a unit that does not exist.

The Front Door

Wide Entries, Pairs of Doors and Hold-Back Buttons: Getting the Release Onto the Right Leaf in Highland Park

A buzzer is only as good as the door it opens. On the hill, the front doors of the older houses are often wide, heavy and original, and a fair number are pairs: two narrower leaves that meet in the middle, one opened every day and one that stays shut. They are handsome doors. They are also where most first-time installations go wrong, because the release ends up on the wrong side or the lock on the everyday leaf was never meant to latch into anything electric.

A question Brooklyn owners have been asking for years

In a 2007 Brownstoner forum thread, a Brooklyn owner asked whether "a door buzzer (electric strike door) can be installed on double doors or not." One reply came from an owner who had done it on two sets of double doors during a renovation and warned, "You'll need to open up the walls, probably quite a bit," and that "the wiring needs to run down the doors." Another asked, half joking, "You don't want to just toss the key out the window?" That is still how many two-families on the hill let a visitor in.

The answer is that a pair of doors can take a buzzer, and the walls usually need far less opening than that owner found, if the release goes in the right place. Allegion's knowledge center, the lock maker's own reference, explains that on a pair the electric strike goes on the inactive leaf, the one held shut by bolts, and that where that leaf is "held closed with flush or surface bolts" a strike made for a pair is used. The everyday leaf keeps its own lock and latch. When someone presses release, the strike in the edge of the inactive leaf lets the active leaf's latch go, and the active leaf swings open.

What that means for an old pair on Highland Boulevard or Arlington Avenue

First, the inactive leaf has to really be inactive. On many old pairs the top and bottom bolts have not been thrown in years, or one of them is missing, and the second leaf is held shut by nothing more than habit. A strike in its edge will not hold anything if the leaf behind it can be pushed open. So the bolts are checked and, where needed, repaired before the strike is fitted, and that work is named on the quote.

Second, the cable has to reach the strike. With the strike in the inactive leaf, the wire runs from the frame into that leaf, either through a hinge made to carry power or a small protected loop near the top hinge, and down inside the leaf's edge to the strike. Allegion also notes that some combinations, such as an electric strike with a vertical rod device, are "not for use in fire rated openings." House front doors are rarely rated, but if a door between a hall and a stair is, we say so and choose hardware that fits it.

Third, the lock on the active leaf has to have a latch that will release cleanly into a strike. Old mortise locks with a worn or short latch bolt sometimes do not. When that is the case, the lock body is replaced with one that matches the old trim where possible, and the keys change with it.

Hold-back buttons and the door that never locks

Many locks have a small button or slide on the edge or the inside that holds the latch back so the door can be pushed open without a key. It is useful when moving furniture. In a house with several households, it is also the most common reason a front door stays unlocked for days: someone presses it, forgets, and the door swings shut without latching. A buzzer cannot fix that, because the door was never locked in the first place.

When we install a release, we look for that button, show it to the owner and, if the owner agrees, disable it or replace the lock with one that does not have it. Tenants then get in with a key and visitors with the buzzer, and nobody needs to leave the door on the latch. If the owner wants a way to keep the door open on moving day, the right answer is a release that can be held for a set time from the owner's station or phone, not a button on the lock that anyone can press.

Rules and Responsibilities

Which Highland Park Buildings Must Have a Buzzer, and What the Rules Ask of the Ones That Choose One

  • A one- or two-family house is a private dwelling. The Multiple Dwelling Law's definition covers buildings for "not more than two families." No city rule requires a buzzer there, and most two-families on the hill install one by choice.
  • Three households make a multiple dwelling. A building occupied by "three or more families living independently of each other" is a multiple dwelling, and one used for permanent residence is class A, a class the law says includes "tenements, flat houses, maisonette apartments, apartment houses." Three- and four-apartment buildings on Sunnyside Avenue fall here.
  • The intercom requirement starts at eight apartments. 1 RCNY §42-01 requires automatic self-closing and self-locking doors at every entrance from the street, court, yard or cellar, and an intercommunication system at the main entrance, for class A multiple dwellings with eight or more apartments.
  • A bell and a return buzzer can meet that rule. The minimum the rule accepts is a bell or buzzer system, or a speaking and listening device, for each apartment, plus a return buzzer that unlocks the door. Talk-back and video go beyond it.
  • The vestibule can carry the lock. Under the same rule, when the door from a vestibule into the hall self-closes and self-locks, the street door into the vestibule does not have to. That decides which door gets the release in buildings with a vestibule.
  • Self-locking doors open from inside without a key. The rule requires it, and so do we, on every door we fit, whatever its size.
  • Older large buildings have their own history. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a ties the intercom requirement for buildings put up before 1968 to a written request from tenants in a majority of apartments; for the six-story buildings at the crest, ask HPD or your lawyer which provisions apply before relying on either.

None of this is legal advice for a particular building. If you are not sure which rules apply to yours, HPD, the Buildings Department or your own lawyer can tell you. What we do is install a system that meets those rules where they apply and leave a written record of what was installed and tested.

Rules that apply to business doors with a buzzer in Highland Park

  • Anyone inside can always get out. A business door with a buzzer still opens from inside with one motion and no key. With an electric strike, the lever or push bar on the inside works whatever the release is doing.
  • Fail-secure strikes on most doors. Allegion's guide explains that "fail safe products are unlocked when power is removed" and "fail secure products are locked when power is removed," and that with an electric strike a person inside can exit by turning the lever either way. A fail-secure strike keeps a shop locked in a power cut without trapping anyone.
  • Magnetic locks need the full package. Allegion notes that electromagnetic locks are only made fail-safe. Where a door cannot take a strike, a magnetic lock needs a sensor on the egress side, a manual release marked "PUSH TO EXIT," release on loss of power and a fire alarm connection, as the building code describes. We name all of that in the quote before anyone chooses it.
  • Controls within reach. The 2010 ADA Standards set the high forward reach at 48 inches maximum and the low at 15 inches minimum for operable parts, so the button a customer presses and the release staff use are mounted inside that range where a business serves the public.
  • Tenants need the owner's written approval. A shop that rents its space orders a buzz-in door only with the building owner's written permission, and its cable stays clear of the residents' system.
Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Installation in Highland Park: Questions Answered

Basements, splits and the households a house gains

Our semi-detached two-family on Sunnyside Avenue has a finished basement with its own walk-out door, but nobody lives down there. Should that door be part of the new system?

It should be part of the plan, but probably not as a button. A basement walk-out is a second way into the house, and a front door with a buzzer protects nothing if the basement door is left on the latch. So it gets looked at on the walkthrough like any other door: does it close on its own, does it lock, can it be opened from inside without a key, and does anyone use it day to day.

If it is a storage or laundry door, it usually needs a sound lock and, if people carry bins or laundry through it, a closer. It does not need a station or a button, and putting one there would send visitors to a door nobody answers. If the basement is part of the ground-floor apartment, a second chime or station downstairs lets that household hear the front door while they are below. We price those on the same quote, so the whole house locks together.

The city's 2025 basement law has been in the news. Does it reach Highland Park, and should our new buzzer be built around a basement apartment we might add?

According to Epicenter-NYC's guide to the law, it covers 15 community districts, and in Brooklyn those are Districts 4, 10, 11 and 17. Highland Park is in District 5, East New York and Cypress Hills, which is not on the list. The district's own earlier program, HPD's Basement Apartment Conversion Pilot, is no longer accepting applications. Whether a basement in your house can become a legal apartment is a question for an architect and the Buildings Department, not for us.

What we can do cheaply is leave room. A door panel with one spare button and a spare pair in the cable to the basement costs little when the system goes in, and turns a future third household into an afternoon's work rather than a new installation. We leave the spare button blank and unconnected until there is a legal unit to connect it to.

We are dividing our big old house near Highland Boulevard into three apartments with an architect. Which buzzer decisions come with going from two families to three?

Three are worth deciding on the drawings. The first is the panel: three buttons, or a code panel if the household count may change again. The second is the cable route: one run per apartment from a single point in the cellar or hall, planned so it goes in after framing and before drywall. The third is the power supply: on the house's own circuit, not inside any apartment, where the owner can reach it. Going from two families to three also moves the house into the Multiple Dwelling Law's definition of a multiple dwelling, and HPD's description of the basement pilot shows the same line, with two-to-three conversions handled under that law.

The city's intercom rule itself starts at eight apartments, so a three-apartment house is not required to have one. But it will have three households sharing one front door, and a conversion is the cheapest moment there will ever be to give each of them a button, a station and a door that locks. Ask your architect what the conversion requires of the doors themselves; we build the entry system to match.

New and newer houses on the hill

We own one of the few empty lots on Sunnyside Avenue and plan to build a two-family. Should the buzzer come from our builder's electrician or from a separate installer?

Either can work if the job is split properly. The parts that belong with the builder are the ones that happen early: a 120-volt outlet where the power supply will live, a front door and frame ordered so they can take an electric strike, and boxes in the walls where the stations will go. The parts that belong with a low-voltage installer are the cable plan, the release, the door station, the stations and the programming. We do not do electrical work, so the outlet is always the electrician's.

What goes wrong is a builder's kit chosen in the last week, after the door is hung and the walls closed, with the strike cut into a frame that was never prepared for one. If you call us while the framing is up, we mark the station locations with you, run the cable and leave it coiled and labeled, then come back to finish once the door and walls are done. On our list a two-family video system usually lands in the $700 to $1,800 range, and the quote shows the prewire visit and the finishing visit as separate lines.

Our two-apartment house on Highland Boulevard went up in 2004 and came with a plain voice intercom. Can a camera be added at the door without replacing everything?

Often, yes. Houses of that age usually have modern multi-pair or network cable behind the stations, and there is a reasonable chance it can carry a video system. The technician meters every run and checks what kind of cable it is. If it is suitable, the old door station and apartment stations come off, and a video door station and video stations go onto the same wires.

If the builder used one thin pair that will not carry video, the old cable often makes a good pull string for the new one along the same route, so the walls still stay closed. Either way the quote says which it is before anything is ordered. If the house has two entrances, as many of these newer two-apartment houses do, each one gets its own station on the same system, and each apartment sees whichever door the visitor is at.

Doors that will not stay locked

Our tenants keep pressing the little button on the edge of the front-door lock that holds the latch back, and the door never locks. Will an electric strike fix that?

Not by itself. An electric strike only matters when the latch is out and the door is locked; if the latch is held back, the door just swings open and the strike never comes into play. So the fix is at the lock. The hold-back button is either disabled or the lock is replaced with one that does not have it, and the owner decides which.

Then the buzzer takes away the reason people used the button in the first place. Tenants come and go with their keys, visitors are buzzed in from upstairs, and deliveries are answered at the station instead of by leaving the door open for the afternoon. If the owner wants a way to keep the door open for a move, we set that up as a timed release from the owner's station or phone, not as a button anyone can press.

Our Sunnyside Avenue porch was enclosed years ago with its own outer door, so visitors can't get to the house door at all. Which door gets the buttons and which gets the release?

Usually the outer porch door gets both. The visitor's first stop is the porch door, so that is where the station belongs, and that is the door the release has to open, because otherwise the visitor is buzzed into a porch and left facing a second locked door. The house door then works like an apartment door: locked with a key, or with its own release if the owner wants one.

Choosing what goes in

A landlord forum told us a no-wire system is fine for a small multi-family. For our four apartments on two floors, is wireless really good enough?

For the stations, sometimes; for the door, no. Owners on landlord forums often ask for a buzzer "that doesn't require hardwiring," and one owner with a three-family noted that big brands like Ring and Nest seemed designed for single-family homes. That is the problem in a nutshell. A store-bought doorbell rings the phones of whoever set it up, has one button and does not release a strike unless someone wires it to one.

The release in the frame is wired whatever the stations are, and it needs a power supply that will not drop out. Wired stations on cable through the cellar keep working when the internet does not, and if you want to compare, we price a wired system and a wireless one side by side. Where a single run truly cannot be made, we use wireless for that one station and say so on the quote.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Door Buzzer Installation in Highland Park

Ask an AI assistant or a search summary about installing a buzzer and you get tidy answers drawn from cost guides, vendor blogs and news about city programs. Some are right somewhere. Here are seven we see repeated, each traced to where it comes from and set against what we find at the two-families, small buildings and new houses of Highland Park.

"You can legalize your basement apartment under the new city program"

This comes from the wave of coverage in 2025 when a state law opened a path to legalizing existing basement apartments in New York City, effective June 16, 2025, with applications until April 20, 2029. Summaries often present it as citywide.

It is not. Epicenter-NYC's guide lists 15 community districts, and in Brooklyn only Districts 4, 10, 11 and 17. Highland Park is in District 5. The district's own pilot, run by HPD with the Cypress Hills Local Development Corporation, is no longer accepting applications, and Brooklyn Paper's report on it counted five enrolled homes as of May 2023 out of more than 8,000 identified candidates.

For a buzzer, that means we never assume a basement is an apartment because a summary said it could be. A basement door on the hill gets a lock and a closer; a button goes on it only when a legal household lives there.

"Installing an intercom costs an average of $2,500"

HomeAdvisor's intercom cost guide gives that average, with a spread from $1,500 to $15,000, wireless systems from $150 to $1,500 and hard-wired systems from $1,000 to $15,000. Its numbers blend whole-house room intercoms in suburban homes with entry systems, which are different products.

For Highland Park the average lands in the wrong place for nearly everyone. A two-family here usually pays well under it for a first system: $400 to $900 for audio and $700 to $1,800 for video on our Brooklyn list. A six-story building at the top of the boulevard pays well over it.

On our list, $2,500 is roughly where a multi-unit entry system for a large building starts, not where a typical job on the hill ends. The useful figure is the range for your house, which is why we price from photos and a count of households rather than a national average.

"Intercoms cost $500 to $1,500 per audio unit and $1,000 to $3,000 per video unit"

CountBricks' 2026 guide to New York intercom rates gives per-unit figures of "approximately $500–$1,500 per audio-only unit" and "$1,000–$3,000 per video intercom unit," and attributes them to other installers' sites rather than its own data.

Multiplied out, those figures would put a two-family's video system at $2,000 to $6,000 and a four-apartment building's at $4,000 to $12,000. That is not what we see on the hill. The door station, the release and the power supply are shared by every household; each extra household adds a button, a station and a cable run, not another whole system.

"Buy a Ring Intercom to make your building's buzzer smart"

Reviews and announcements of Ring Intercom, a small box that connects an existing apartment intercom to a phone app, circulate widely, and summaries sometimes recommend it to anyone with an old buzzer.

Ring's own support page says the information applies only to customers in the United Kingdom, the European Union and Australia, and states: "This device is not available in the US." It also notes that a residence "might have rules limiting the installation and use of Ring Intercom."

In a Highland Park two-family the owner can add phone answering legitimately, either with a door station that rings phones as well as stations or with an adapter made for the specific system already installed. We price that as part of the installation, and the quote shows any monthly fee separately.

"A smart lock and an app can replace a buzzer in a small building"

The idea shows up in landlord forums. One owner on BiggerPockets with a three-family and no intercom asked for a system that would not need hardwiring and ideally offered keyless entry; another with a four-unit building wanted "an intercom/buzzer system that also has a key fob entry for the tenants." Neither thread got a working answer.

A smart lock solves the key problem and the forgotten-lock problem for residents. It does not give each household a way to talk to a visitor and let them in from upstairs, which is the whole point of a buzzer. Visitors still end up texting someone to come down.

The two fit together. A door station with a keypad, a button per household and a release in the frame gives residents codes instead of keys and gives visitors a way to reach the right apartment. That is what we recommend for the three- and four-apartment buildings on Sunnyside Avenue.

"Phone-based entry saves you running any cable"

Summaries often say a panel that rings phones avoids wiring the apartments. Habitat Magazine reported back in 2017 that a ButterflyMX installation cost about $5,000 to $7,000 plus $2.50 per unit per month, and that telephone-entry systems need a dedicated phone line and offer no video. Swiftlane's cost guide puts software subscriptions at "$5 to $15 per unit per month, or $30 to $100 per door per month."

The panel at the door still needs power, a release wired into the frame and a data connection, and the release still needs cable from the power supply. In a two-family, the cable to the apartments is a short run up an interior wall, often on an old bell pair that already exists.

Phone answering has a place on the hill, especially for owners who manage a house from another neighborhood. We usually add it alongside stations in the apartments rather than instead of them, so older residents and anyone without a smartphone can still answer.

"Any doorbell installer can put a release on your front door"

Cost guides treat a buzzer as an electronic product and the door as a given. HomeAdvisor's guide, for example, puts installation at one to two hours per station and describes wireless systems as the best option for doing it yourself.

On the hill, the door is where the work is. Allegion's reference shows that on a pair of doors the strike belongs on the inactive leaf, held closed with flush or surface bolts, and that some strike and exit-device combinations are not for fire-rated openings. A Brooklyn owner who put strikes on two pairs of double doors in 2007 found the wiring had to "run down the doors."

In most cases, yes. An electric strike replaces the keeper in the frame or in the inactive leaf, not the lock in the door, so the cylinder and keys stay the same. +

No. Each button is wired to a floor, not to a family, so when households swap floors the wiring stays exactly as it is.
