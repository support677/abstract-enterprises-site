The Neighborhood

Why Sea Gate Properties Need Door Buzzer Installation

Sea Gate is the private, gated community at the western tip of Coney Island, west of West 37th Street, with the Atlantic, the Lower Bay and Gravesend Bay around it. The Sea Gate Association was formed in 1899, its website puts the full-time population at about 8,000, and its residents pay for their own security, street lights, beach and street cleaning. Wikipedia describes it as mostly single-family, with Queen Anne and Mediterranean-style houses, and the Village Voice's 2005 profile described "stand-alone three-story houses" along Surf Avenue and the numbered Beach streets that run off it. That picture is true as far as it goes, and it is why so many owners assume a door buzzer is something for apartment buildings somewhere else.

Building records tell a fuller story, and it is the story that matters for buzzer work. 3764 Surf Avenue is a two-story house built in 1920 that is recorded as three residential units. 4505 Surf Avenue, built in 1928, has fifteen units over three floors. 4725 Beach 47th Street is a two-story building from 1930 with thirteen units. 3811 Surf Avenue, also known as 3702 Sea Gate Avenue and 3801 Surf Avenue, was built in 1952 with 41 units. 3813 Neptune Avenue, a 1930 building, was converted into four condominiums in 2018. Openigloo's list of Seagate buildings runs to several pages, some of them rent-stabilized. A gated community of houses, in other words, also holds a good number of households that share a front door, and every shared front door needs a way for a visitor to reach the right household.

The gate does one job and only one. It decides who gets onto Sea Gate's streets. It says nothing about who gets through your front door, which household a visitor wants, or whether a tenant on the top floor of a converted frame house can let a friend in without walking down three flights. That is the buzzer's job. In a house that became two or three apartments, a single bell rings the owner for every tenant's guest. In a building of thirteen or fifteen apartments with no working entry system, the street door ends up propped or left unlatched so that visitors and couriers can reach people at all. And because the association's own rules list "broken windows and/or doors" among the property conditions that bring a warning or a fine, a street door that no longer latches is also a problem with the association, not only with the tenants.

Water is the other reason a Sea Gate installation is planned differently. City Limits reported in December 2012 that "everything flows to Sea Gate," with water thigh-high in the streets for a day after Sandy, and 6sqft described the Atlantic and Coney Island Creek both flowing through the streets. The cellar wall, where Brooklyn buzzer transformers and power supplies have always been mounted, is exactly where a Sea Gate system should not keep its electronics. The city's own flood-resistant construction rules say the same thing, and we cover them below.

Six facts that shape a buzzer installation behind the Sea Gate entrance

  • Many houses are no longer one household. 3764 Surf Avenue, built in 1920, is recorded as three units in two stories, the pattern that turns a single bell into a three-button station with separate releases.
  • The apartment buildings are older than the intercom law's 1968 line. 4505 Surf Avenue (1928), 4725 Beach 47th Street (1930) and 3811 Surf Avenue (1952) all predate the date in Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a, which shapes when an intercom becomes mandatory.
  • Some buildings carry long violation records. Openigloo lists 42 open HPD violations at 3862 Neptune Avenue and 23 at 3817 Nautilus Avenue; Augrented records 20 HPD violations at 4725 Beach 47th Street. A new entry system is often installed while an owner is clearing other repairs.
  • The association polices doors as well as streets. Its 2024 Rules list broken doors among the conditions that draw a warning, a fine or an ECB summons, and make the homeowner liable for what a "service person" does on the property.
  • The city wants equipment above the flood line. Appendix G of the NYC Building Code requires utilities and attendant equipment in flood zones to sit at or above the design flood elevation, or be built to keep water out.
  • Every street ends at the water. Sea Gate has water on three sides, so salt spray reaches porches and door stations that face the Atlantic, the bay or the Narrows, and fasteners and housings have to be chosen for it.

Repair or Replace in Sea Gate

Need Repair in Sea Gate? Decide Whether the Old System Is Worth Saving Before You Buy a New One

A good share of the calls we get about "putting in a buzzer" here come from owners who already have something at the door: a bell button wired generations ago to a chime in a back hall, an audio panel a previous owner fitted when the house was divided, or a building system in which some buttons ring and others have been dead for years. Before any new equipment is chosen, we sort the job into one of three paths.

The first is a targeted repair, when one part really is the whole problem: a transformer that failed after it got wet, a strike that has rusted in its frame, a closer that no longer pulls the street door shut. The second is a partial replacement, with a new door station and power supply on old cable that still tests sound. The third is a new installation with new cable, for houses where the old pairs corroded, were cut during a post-storm rebuild or never existed beyond a single bell. If a repair is all you need, we say so and price it as a repair; our Brooklyn door buzzer team handles both kinds of work.

In Sea Gate the deciding checks are usually about water rather than electronics: whether the old cable spent time submerged, whether the supply sits below the height water reached, and whether the door station's screws and terminals have started to corrode in the salt air. A system that passes those checks is often worth keeping. One that fails them is usually cheaper to replace than to keep repairing.

One-family frame houses: three floors, a porch and a bell that stops at the first landing

The frame houses that gave Sea Gate its reputation were built for large households, and many still have one. They are also built of wood, with porches, deep halls and stairs that turn, so the sound of a mechanical bell at the front door dies somewhere around the first landing. An owner on the third floor or in a sunroom at the back simply does not hear it. Couriers who cannot raise anyone leave parcels on the porch or carry them away, and a visitor arriving for a family dinner stands outside knocking.

The fix here is an indoor station on each floor the household lives on, or phone answering, or both. The cable route is usually generous in a frame house, because closets stack, baseboards come off cleanly and the cellar ceiling is open. What takes time is finding a route that stays clear of the cellar's low zone, which we cover below.

On our list a house like this lands in the $700 to $1,800 video range. The low end is a video station on an old bell pair that tests sound with one or two indoor stations. The high end is three or four indoor stations, phone answering and a new hidden run from the porch to the top floor. The release goes into the front door frame, sized for a heavy old door, with a closer that pulls it shut.

Houses divided into two or three apartments: one street door, one bell and three households

The most common Sea Gate buzzer job is the one the gate hides: a house that was built for one family and now holds two or three. 3764 Surf Avenue is recorded as a two-story house with three units, built in 1920. Some houses were divided long ago; others were set up as a rental floor above an owner's duplex. Whatever the history, the front door usually still has one button, and it usually rings in the owner's hall. The owner answers for everyone, the tenants walk down to let their own guests in, and the street door gets left on the latch because that is easier than either.

The installation gives each household its own button at the street door, its own indoor station and its own ability to release the street door, and nothing more. The tenant cannot open the owner's apartment door, and the owner is not rung for the tenant's visitors. Where the upstairs apartment has its own door at the top of a shared stair, that door stays a keyed door with its own lock, as the Housing Maintenance Code expects for a dwelling unit. A house with three or more independent households is a class A multiple dwelling under city law, and §27-2043 of the Housing Maintenance Code requires each apartment's entrance door to have a heavy-duty latch set and a heavy-duty dead bolt, so we look at those doors while we are there and tell you if they fall short.

A two-button audio system on sound cable can land in the $400 to $900 audio range. Most owners of three-unit houses choose video, which puts the job in the $700 to $1,800 range: a three-button video station, one indoor station per apartment, a strike and a closer.

Prewar apartment buildings: thirteen to fifteen apartments and a street door that has been propped for years

Sea Gate's older apartment buildings are small by Brooklyn standards. 4725 Beach 47th Street has two stories and thirteen apartments; 4505 Surf Avenue has three floors and fifteen. Both are older than 1968, which matters for the law (see the next section), and both are the kind of building where an entry system is either missing, partly dead or a patchwork of replacement parts. In buildings like these we usually find one of three things at the street door: an audio panel in which some buttons work, a lock with no electric release at all, or a door that does not latch and has not for some time.

The first decision is whether the old riser cable can be reused. In a 1920s or 1930s building, apartment pairs run from the street door to each apartment, and ButterflyMX's guide puts it plainly: older analog systems "often use dedicated conductors between the entrance and each unit," and reuse "still depends on conductor condition, topology, available wires, voltage, controls, and lock compatibility." We meter every pair. If most test well, the building can take a new panel and new apartment stations on old cable. If they do not, the options are new cable up the stairwell or phone answering for every apartment with no riser cable at all.

These buildings start at about $2,500 on our list and rise with apartment count and cable work. A thirteen-unit building with a sound riser lands near the bottom of the multi-unit range; the same building with a failed riser and two entrances moves up.

The 1952 building on Surf Avenue: 41 apartments, four addresses and one entry plan

3811 Surf Avenue was built in 1952 with 41 apartments, and city records also know it as 3702 Sea Gate Avenue, 3801 Surf Avenue and 3802 Oceanic Avenue. A building of that size with several street addresses raises questions that a house never does: how many entrances need a panel, whether visitors at each entrance should see the whole directory or only the apartments served by that door, where the controller lives, and how residents will get in while the old system comes out and the new one goes in.

We plan a building like this around the entrances people actually use, not the addresses on the deed. A main entrance usually gets a directory or code panel that reaches every apartment; secondary doors get a smaller panel or a keypad, or stay as egress-only doors that open freely from inside. The cutover is planned so the old system keeps working until the new panel is tested, and residents are told in writing when their stations change.

A 41-unit building sits well into our multi-unit range, from about $2,500 for a straightforward panel on reusable cable toward $10,000 or more for video, phone answering for every apartment and several entrances.

Condo conversions: four owners, one door and nobody in charge of it

CityRealty lists 3813 Neptune Avenue as a 1930 three-floor building converted to four condominiums in 2018, with a common front yard, a large cellar storage room and a "virtual doorman" mentioned in its description. Conversions like this leave the entry system in an awkward place: the developer chose it, the unit owners now share it, and no managing agent may be looking after it. When a station fails or an owner sells, nobody is sure who holds the admin login or which parts belong to whom.

When we install or replace a system in a small condo, the written quote separates the shared parts, meaning the street door station, the strike, the supply and the riser, from each owner's own station, so the cost can be split the way the condo's documents say. We hand the admin access to whoever the owners name, in writing, and leave a wiring record with the condo's papers.

Four or five units usually land in the upper part of the $700 to $1,800 video range or the bottom of the multi-unit range, depending on whether every owner wants video and phone answering.

Rebuilt and raised houses: new walls, new stairs and a ground floor kept clear

6sqft wrote in 2022 that Sea Gate was "still in the process of rebuilding" a decade after Sandy, and the houses that were repaired or rebuilt since 2012 are the easiest in the neighborhood to wire. The walls are new, the framing is open during the work, and a cable can be pulled to every floor before the drywall goes up. The catch is that the same rebuilding often moved the living space up and kept the ground floor or cellar for parking, storage or nothing at all, which changes where the front door is and where equipment can live.

If you are rebuilding now, call us before the walls close. Pulling cable while the framing is open costs far less than fishing it later, and the controller can be given a spot on a living floor from the start. A house whose front door now sits at the top of a stair may also need a second call station at the bottom, which we price as part of the same job.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Count the Mailboxes, Find the Waterline, Then Ring From the Farthest Room

This is how our technician spends the first part of a visit to a Sea Gate property, before anything is priced.

First, the households. Before looking at any wire, the technician counts the mailboxes, the meters and the doors inside the front hall, because a house that looks like one family from the street often is not. The number of households, not the size of the house, decides how many buttons the station needs and how the releases are split.

Second, the waterline. In the cellar or on the ground floor, the technician looks for the height water reached in past storms: a tide line on the foundation, newer drywall above old block, replaced outlets, or simply what the owner remembers. Wherever the old bell transformer or intercom supply is mounted, we note whether it sits below that line, because that alone decides whether the old supply can stay.

Third, the far room. The technician rings the existing bell from the porch while someone stands in the room farthest from the front door, usually a top-floor bedroom or a back kitchen, and asks whether they heard it. Most of the time the answer is no, and that is the whole case for indoor stations.

Fourth, the water side. The technician walks around to the side of the house that faces the ocean or the bay and looks at the back door, the screws on anything already mounted outside, and the paint on the frames. Rust streaks and blistered paint tell us how hard the salt air works on that side of the house and which hardware will last there.

Thirteen Units in 1930, Forty-One in 1952: When the Law Asks a Sea Gate Building for an Intercom

New York's Multiple Dwelling Law has had an intercom rule since 1968, and Sea Gate's apartment buildings sit on both sides of it in a way that surprises many owners. Section 50-a says that every class A multiple dwelling erected or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments shall be equipped with an automatic self-locking door at the main entrance and an intercommunication system that lets residents speak to a visitor at that door and release it from their apartments. Buildings older than that must install the doors and the intercom if tenants occupying a majority of the apartments ask for it in writing, with the work begun within 90 days and finished within six months.

Run Sea Gate's buildings through that rule and the answers differ. 4505 Surf Avenue (1928, fifteen units), 4725 Beach 47th Street (1930, thirteen units) and 3811 Surf Avenue (1952, 41 units) were all built before 1968 and all have more than eight apartments, so none was required to have an intercom when it went up, and each must install one once a written majority request arrives. A three-unit house such as 3764 Surf Avenue is below the eight-apartment line altogether, so the state rule does not reach it, although the city's Housing Maintenance Code still governs its apartment doors and its entrance lighting. A building converted after 1968 into eight or more apartments falls under the first half of the rule.

Owners of older buildings sometimes read that as permission to wait. In practice it is a timetable that tenants control. Once a majority signs, the owner has 90 days to start, and a six-month clock that is hard to meet if the riser cable turns out to be corroded and the equipment has to be ordered. We would rather plan the system before the letter arrives, so the owner chooses the equipment and the timing.

Two other city rules usually come up during the same visit. Section 27-2040 of the Housing Maintenance Code covers lighting near entranceways of multiple dwellings, so a dark front walk gets mentioned on the quote even though we do not install the light fixture. And §51-c of the Multiple Dwelling Law gives tenants the right to add their own lock to their apartment door, up to three inches in circumference, which is why the apartment doors are never part of the buzzer system: the system opens the street door, and each household keeps control of its own.

None of this is legal advice, and a building with open HPD violations should check its own record. Openigloo's Seagate list shows how varied those records are, from a single open violation at several addresses to 42 at 3862 Neptune Avenue. When an owner is clearing a list like that, we put the entry system and the door hardware on the same plan so the street door is fixed once.

Above the Flood Line: Where the Supply, the Controller and the Splices Go in a Sea Gate Building

Every Brooklyn buzzer installer has a habit: the transformer or power supply goes on the cellar wall, beside the electrical panel, because that is where the 120-volt circuit is and where the riser starts. In Sea Gate that habit is how systems get ruined. The Brooklyn Eagle reported in 2015 that Sandy damaged or destroyed most of the bulkhead and pushed sand and debris into streets, homes and sewers, and City Limits described water standing thigh-high for a day. A supply or controller that sat under that water is finished, and the cable splices beside it corrode from the inside for months afterward.

The city's flood-resistant construction rules point the same way. Appendix G of the 2022 NYC Building Code says that in flood zones, "utilities and attendant equipment shall be located at or above the design flood elevation" specified in ASCE 24, or be built to keep water from entering or collecting in them. The same appendix allows wiring itself to run below that height if it meets the electrical code. For a door buzzer that translates into a simple layout: the cable can pass through the cellar, but the supply, the controller, the battery backup and any splice box live at or above the flood line, on a ground-floor closet wall or higher.

How we apply it on site:

  • The supply moves up. We mount the power supply and controller in a ventilated enclosure on a living floor or high on the cellar stair wall, wherever the owner's information and the building's marks put it clear of past water. If the nearest outlet is in the cellar, a licensed electrician adds one higher up, and that is on the quote before you approve.
  • The cable passes through, it does not stop. Where a run must cross the cellar ceiling, it crosses in one continuous length, with no splice below the line. Splices and terminal strips go in the enclosure upstairs.
  • The door station gets a drip loop and a sealed entry. The cable enters the back of the station from below with a loop so water runs off before it reaches the terminals, and the entry hole is sealed.
  • The battery backup is sized for an outage. A battery in the supply keeps the station and the release working through a short power cut; we tell you how long the chosen battery is rated for and what the door does when it runs out.
  • The release is chosen for the outage, too. A fail-secure strike keeps the street door locked when power is lost while the inside lever still opens it, which is why it is our default here. A magnetic lock, which releases when power is lost, is used only with the sensor and push-to-exit arrangement the building code requires.

The same thinking applies to a one-family house with a modest system. A small supply costs little to put on the first-floor closet wall instead of the cellar, and it is the difference between a system that comes back on after the next storm and one that has to be replaced.

What the Association's Rules Mean for Your Installation Day

The Sea Gate Association's 2024 Rules and Regulations say almost nothing about door buzzers by name, but several of them reach an installation anyway. We plan around them so the job leaves nothing for the association to write you up for.

  • The homeowner answers for the crew. Article IV makes the homeowner "ultimately liable" for any violation committed by "a resident, guest, tenant, service person, or other person" associated with the property. We treat that as our rule too: the crew parks legally, keeps materials on your property and leaves the street and sidewalk as clean as we found them.
  • Broken doors draw notices. Article I lists "broken windows and/or doors" among the conditions that can bring a warning, a fine or an ECB summons. A new strike and closer that leave the street door shutting and latching properly is part of every job, and we do not leave a door unlatched overnight on a two-day installation.

Rules that apply to institutional and building entry installations in Sea Gate

  • Free exit is not optional. Whatever releases a door from outside, the door must open from inside with one motion, without a key or special knowledge. Electric strikes allow that by design.
  • Magnetic locks need the full arrangement. The building code allows electrically locked egress doors with a sensor on the inside, a push-to-exit button between 40 and 48 inches above the floor within five feet of the door, unlocking on loss of power and on fire alarm or sprinkler activation, and UL 294 listed parts.
  • Fire-rated doors take fail-secure strikes. Allegion's guide notes that fire doors need fail-secure strikes so they stay latched in a fire, which is another reason fail-secure is our default.
  • Reach ranges matter. At a house of worship or community building open to the public, the 2010 ADA Standards set the high forward reach at 48 inches maximum, so the station's buttons go no higher.
  • Tenant data has rules of its own. In a rental building, the city's tenant data privacy law covers smart access systems that collect data about residents, so we explain what the chosen system records before the owner orders it.
Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Installation in Sea Gate: Questions Answered

Houses that hold more than one household

Besides the new buttons, what else at the entrance of a three-apartment house should be put right while the buzzer goes in?

Three things, all at the street door. The door itself has to close and latch on its own, so the closer is checked and replaced if it is tired, and the frame is reinforced where the strike goes if the wood has softened. The apartment doors on the inside need the heavy-duty latch and dead bolt that §27-2043 of the Housing Maintenance Code expects in a class A multiple dwelling, and we tell you if any fall short. And the walk and the door should be lit at night, which the Housing Maintenance Code also addresses for multiple dwellings.

We price the strike, the closer and any frame work on the same quote as the station. Lighting and apartment locks are listed as notes, because a locksmith or an electrician usually does that part.

Our tenants come and go by the beach-side back door as much as by the front. Does that door need a station of its own?

Not always a full station, but rarely nothing. If the tenants' visitors arrive at the front and only the household itself uses the back, a keypad at the back door does the job: no key to lose on the beach, and the door relocks behind them. If guests regularly come up from the beach to the back, a small call station there, ringing the same indoor stations as the front, saves them walking around the block of the house.

Either way the back door gets the same strike, closer and sealed hardware as the front, and the keypad codes are given per household so one tenant's code can be changed without changing everyone's.

We rent out the whole house and live elsewhere. Can the system tell us when the street door is released without letting us watch the tenants?

Many systems can send the owner a simple notification that the door was released, or log the event, without showing video of the tenants. Whether that is appropriate depends on the lease and, in a building, on the city's tenant data rules, which limit what smart entry systems may collect about residents and how long it may be kept. We set the system to the least the owner actually needs and tell the tenants what it records.

Salt air, winter and empty houses

We close the house from late fall until spring and keep the heat low. Will a door station and indoor stations come through a cold, empty winter?

The outdoor station is built for cold, so the station itself is rarely the problem. What suffers in an empty house is the indoor equipment and the battery: indoor stations are rated for a heated room, and a backup battery in an unheated space loses capacity. We check the operating temperature range of the chosen indoor stations against how cold you keep the house, and we put the supply and battery in the warmest place that is also above the flood line.

If nobody will be there for months, phone answering lets you see who comes to the door, and a power outage notice from the system tells you if the house has lost power while you are away.

Spray off the water reaches our porch on the ocean side. What should we ask for in the station, its box and its screws?

Ask for three things. A station whose maker states an ingress-protection rating for outdoor use, so rain and spray are kept out of the electronics. Stainless steel screws and mounting hardware, because plated steel screws rust and streak within a season or two near the water. And a sealed cable entry with a drip loop, so water that does get behind the station runs off before it reaches the terminals.

Where a porch faces the open water and takes the full force of the weather, we sometimes mount the station on the side of the door that is more sheltered, or under the porch roof, rather than on the most exposed face.

Our walk is dark at night once the porch light is off. Will a video station still show who is ringing?

Most video door stations have infrared or low-light capability and will show a recognizable picture of someone standing at the door in the dark. What they show less well is a person standing back on the walk, or a face lit from behind by a streetlight. A porch light on a timer or a motion sensor gives a much better picture and helps visitors find the button, and in a multiple dwelling it also meets the city's entrance lighting expectations.

We place the station so the camera faces where the visitor stands, not into a streetlight, and test the picture after dark before we leave if the job runs late.

Association rules and the job itself

The association's rules list broken doors among the conditions that bring a warning or a fine. Could a new buzzer leave our door in that state?

Not if it is installed properly, and avoiding that is part of the job. A badly fitted strike can stop a door from latching, and a release left energized can leave it unlocked. We fit the strike so the door latches with the closer alone, test that it relatches after every release, and check it again at the end of each working day.

The rules make the homeowner liable for anything a service person does on the property. What does your crew do to keep that off our record?

We work as if every rule applied to us directly. The crew works weekdays inside the association's hours, parks legally and takes the van out at night, keeps tools and materials on your property rather than the sidewalk, and leaves no sign, sticker or flyer anywhere. If the work involves drilling through an exterior wall, we tell you before we start and patch the hole around the cable.

If the association office ever asks about our visit, you can give them our name and license number, NYS license #12000287431, and we will answer any questions directly.

Our installation will take two days. Can we leave the old bell working overnight in between?

Usually yes. On a two-day job we keep the old bell or the old system working until the new one is ready to test, or we wire a temporary button so visitors can still ring. The street door is never left unlatched overnight; if the new strike is not finished by the end of day one, the old latch goes back in.

For larger buildings we tell residents in writing which day their stations change, so nobody comes home to a door that will not let them in.

The larger buildings

Our building has fifteen apartments on three floors and the riser cable is original. How do you decide between new cable and phone answering for every apartment?

We meter every apartment pair first. If most pairs test sound, the cheapest good answer is a new panel and new apartment stations on the old cable, with the few failed pairs replaced. If many fail, new cable up the stairwell is one option and phone answering for every apartment is another, which needs no riser at all but relies on residents' phones and the building's internet at the panel.

Many owners choose a mix: stations in apartments where the pair is good and phone answering where it is not. We price each option before you choose, with any software charge per apartment stated.

Our building is listed under four street addresses. Which address goes on the panel?

The panel shows what a visitor needs at that door: the number on the building face and the apartments that door serves. Where a building has several addresses on one lot, as 3811 Surf Avenue does, we label each entrance with the address painted or fixed beside it, which also helps the association's requirement that a numbered address sign be visible from the street.

If every entrance leads to every apartment, each panel can show the whole directory; if each entrance serves only one wing, each panel shows only its own apartments.

Can residents keep using the old panel while the new one goes in, so 41 households are never locked out?

Yes, and in a building that size it is how we plan the work. The new panel is mounted and wired alongside the old one where possible, apartment stations are changed floor by floor, and the old panel is disconnected only after every new station has been tested from the street. If the new panel must go in the old panel's place, the swap is scheduled for one morning and residents get a written notice beforehand.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Door Buzzer Installation in Sea Gate

AI summaries of door buzzer installation draw mostly on national cost guides and vendor blogs. Below are seven claims those sources make, each checked against what applies behind the Sea Gate entrance.

"Installing an intercom system costs an average of $2,500"

HomeAdvisor's guide says exactly that, with a range from $1,500 to $15,000, wireless systems from $150 to $1,500 and hard-wired systems from $1,000 to $15,000. The figure is an average across every kind of home in the country, which is why it fits almost nobody in particular.

In Sea Gate the spread is wider than that average suggests. A one-family frame house that needs an audio station and a strike sits in our $400 to $900 range, and most two- and three-family houses land in the $700 to $1,800 video range. A $2,500 figure is closer to where a thirteen-unit building starts than to what a house costs.

What actually moves a Sea Gate price is the number of households, the state of the old cable after years near salt water, and whether the supply has to move up out of the cellar. None of those shows up in a national average.

"Modern IP and video intercoms cost $1,000 to $5,000 or more per entry door"

Swiftlane's cost guide gives that per-door figure and notes that software subscriptions "commonly add $5 to $15 per unit per month, or $30 to $100 per door per month." Those numbers come from cloud systems sold to managed apartment buildings, where the vendor's pricing model includes the software.

Most Sea Gate properties do not need that model. A house with three apartments can have a wired three-button video station with no subscription at all, and even the larger buildings can choose a wired panel with phone answering only where it is wanted.

If an owner does want cloud management, for example a company running many buildings, we state the subscription per apartment or per door in writing before the system is chosen, so it is compared with the no-fee option on equal terms.

"New York law requires every apartment building to have an intercom"

Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires an intercom in class A multiple dwellings with eight or more apartments erected or converted after January 1, 1968. Older buildings must install one when tenants occupying a majority of the apartments ask in writing.

Sea Gate's apartment buildings are mostly on the older side of that line: 4505 Surf Avenue from 1928, 4725 Beach 47th Street from 1930 and 3811 Surf Avenue from 1952. None was required to have an intercom when built, and each becomes subject to the rule once the tenants' written request arrives. A three-unit house is under the eight-apartment line entirely.

The practical point is that an older building's obligation can start on short notice, with 90 days to begin the work and six months to finish. Planning before the request arrives is cheaper than meeting the deadline after it.

"Mount the power supply next to your electrical panel"

Generic installation advice puts the transformer or power supply beside the electrical panel, which in most houses means the basement, because that is where the power is. For most of Brooklyn that is sensible.

In Sea Gate it is the advice that gets systems destroyed. The Brooklyn Eagle reported that Sandy pushed sand and debris into homes and sewers, City Limits described water thigh-high in the streets for a day afterward, and the city's own Appendix G requires utilities and attendant equipment in flood zones to sit at or above the design flood elevation or be built to keep water out.

We run the cable through the cellar if we must, but the supply, the controller and the splices go higher. It costs a little more cable and sometimes a new outlet, and it is the difference between a system that comes back after a storm and one that does not.

"A wireless buzzer avoids running any cable"

Wireless doorbells and plug-in chimes avoid a cable between the button and the chime, and HomeAdvisor notes that wireless systems are the best option for a do-it-yourself job. ButterflyMX's apartment buzzer guide puts the limit plainly: "a wireless system isn't free of wiring."

The part a wireless button cannot replace is the release. To open a street door from upstairs, an electric strike in the frame needs power and a control signal, which means cable from a supply to the door. In a Sea Gate house with three households, three wireless buttons would still leave everyone walking down to open the door.

Wireless and app-based answering do have a place here, for owners who winter elsewhere and for apartments in a building where the riser cable has failed. They work alongside a wired release, not instead of it.

"Your old doorbell wire can carry a new video buzzer"

Sometimes it can. Aiphone's upgrade guidance says that "if the existing wires are in good condition, they can be reused," and its GT series is sold for retrofit projects using existing wire. In many Brooklyn houses an old bell pair carries a new video station perfectly well.

The phrase that matters is "in good condition." In a Sea Gate cellar that flooded in 2012, a pair that still rings a bell can be corroded inside its jacket, and a video signal is far less forgiving than a bell. That is why we meter every pair rather than assume, and why the quote names the cost of a new run if the old one fails its test.

Daniel Vasquez of Paragon Security and Locksmith told Brick Underground that with intercoms "it's all in the wire connections." Near the water, the connections are where salt does its damage first.

"A gated community doesn't need door buzzers"

Summaries of gated communities tend to describe the gate as the security system: guards at the entrance, guest passes, a private police force. Sea Gate has all of those: the Association's website calls it "a private, gated community," and overnight guest passes come from the Sea Gate police precinct in the chapel at the Surf Avenue entrance.

The gate decides who gets onto the streets. It does not decide which of three households in a converted house a visitor wants, whether a tenant on the top floor can let a friend in, or whether a courier can reach anyone inside a large house. Those are the jobs of the front door, and they are the same inside the gate as outside it.

If anything, the gate makes a working buzzer more important, because visitors who were let through the gate expect to be able to reach the house they came for.

A door station's camera is aimed at the person ringing, a few feet in front of the door, so it shows your own porch and steps rather than the street. Where a neighbor's walk is right beside yours, we angle the station or choose a narrower lens so it does not look onto their property. If you want wider coverage of the street, that is a separate security camera decision.

Can the system be added to later if we divide the house or add an apartment?
+

Usually yes, if we plan for it. We can install a station with a spare button, leave a spare pair in the cable to each floor, and size the supply for one more household. Adding the apartment later is then a matter of an indoor station and a short visit, not a new system. Tell us at the walkthrough if a change is likely.
