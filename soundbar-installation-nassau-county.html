The Neighborhood

Why Nassau County Homes Need Soundbar Installation

Nassau had built-in TV before most of America had TV at all. The 1950 Levitt houses came with a twelve-and-a-half-inch Admiral television built into the staircase, according to the New York Heritage archive's catalogue of the 1950 House of Levittown. Seventy-five years on, the people living in those houses, and in the hundreds of thousands of houses built around them, are trying to hang a 65- or 75-inch screen and a soundbar on walls that were never planned for either. That mismatch, a modern home-theatre product meeting a mid-century house that has been altered many times, is the real story of soundbar work in this county.

The numbers explain why. Census-based figures compiled by Point2Homes put the median construction year of Nassau's homes at 1955. A third of the county's housing, about 162,000 homes, went up in the 1950s alone, and fewer than one home in thirteen has been built since 2000. Nassau didn't replace its houses; it kept them and rebuilt them from the inside. Capes got dormers and second floors. Ranches lost a wall to open the kitchen. Back porches became family rooms. Basements became playrooms, offices and, in the Town of Hempstead, legal apartments. Each change moved studs, buried outlets and left at least one wall that is now hollow in some places and solid in others.

So the TV wall in a Nassau house is rarely what it looks like. It may be the old exterior wall, with sheathing and insulation inside, now facing a family room. It may be a knee wall in a dormered attic bedroom. It may be a closed-up doorway with no stud where the bracket wants to go, or a cinder-block foundation wall in a basement unit, or a paneled library wall in an Old Westbury house that nobody wants to cut. Getting a soundbar centred under the screen, level with it, and connected by a cable nobody can see depends on knowing which of those walls you are standing in front of.

Then there is who lives where. Nassau is not only single-family houses. Great Neck has more than 100 co-ops and condos in 1.4 square miles, according to CooperatorNews. Long Beach has oceanfront buildings and whole streets of houses that were lifted after Sandy, when FEMA found 860 Long Beach homes substantially damaged. Mineola and Hicksville are adding apartments beside their LIRR stations, and two-family houses in Hempstead, Elmont and Valley Stream hold two households under one roof. A soundbar's bass travels through all of those, and in a two-family or a co-op the people below are neighbours you will see in the driveway or the lobby every day.

And the county itself is a patchwork. Nassau has two cities, three towns and 64 incorporated villages, each village with its own building department and its own way of handling paperwork. A soundbar and its low-voltage cabling don't usually need a permit, but the moment a plan involves a new outlet, it becomes an electrician's job with village or town paperwork attached. Knowing where that line sits is part of doing the job right here.

That is what we bring to a Nassau soundbar job: an installer who expects the wall to be complicated, carries the anchors, backer boards, cord covers and in-wall kits to deal with it, stops at the edge of electrical work, and prices every step before the drill comes out.

Knee walls and sloped ceilings. Dormered attic rooms in Capes have short walls and sloped ceilings. Behind a knee wall is often unfinished attic space, which can make cable routing easier than anywhere else in the house.

Patched openings. Closed-up doorways and windows are common where houses have been rearranged. The patch may have a header but no studs, or studs at odd spacing. We map it before drilling, and where the framing doesn't line up with the bracket, a plywood backer board ($85) fixed to the studs on either side carries the load across.

Concrete in apartment buildings. Oceanfront and mid-rise buildings in Long Beach and Great Neck Plaza often have concrete or block walls between units. Masonry anchors and the $75 concrete rate apply, cables go in a cover, and we keep the bar and any sub off the shared wall where we can.

A Little Local History

From a Built-In Admiral to a Soundbar Under the Screen

The 1950 Levitt house treated the television as part of the building. The New York Heritage catalogue of the 1950 House of Levittown records a twelve-and-a-half-inch Admiral set built into the staircase, and the Hofstra University Library's Levittown exhibit describes how the 1950 and 1951 models kept the 1949 design but added new features, including that built-in television. It was a selling point: buy the house, and the TV came with it, already in the wall.

Very few of those sets survive in place, but the idea has come back in a different form. Today's Nassau homeowner wants the screen flat on the wall, the sound under it, and no wires showing, which is the same goal the Levitts had, achieved with very different equipment. What has changed is the wall. The original houses sat on slabs with heating pipes inside them, and the walls were simple. Decades of additions, dormers, new kitchens and opened-up floor plans have made them anything but.

That history is a useful way to think about any Nassau install. The question is never only "where does the soundbar go?" It is "what has happened to this wall since it was built?" A family room addition from the 1970s, a kitchen opened up in the 1990s, a basement finished in the 2000s: each leaves a trace that a scanner, a small inspection opening and some experience can read. Reading it first is what keeps a one-visit job from becoming two.

Your Questions Answered

Soundbar Installation in Nassau County: Questions Answered

These are the questions Nassau households ask once they are standing in front of their own wall. They are grouped by the situation behind them; short questions about prices, policies and coverage are in the FAQ further down.

Walls that have been rebuilt more than once

The TV wall in our expanded Cape used to be the outside of the house — what changes when we mount on it?

Mostly what's inside it. When an addition went on the back of a Cape or ranch, the old exterior wall was usually left standing and covered with new drywall on the family-room side. Inside the stud bays there may still be the original board sheathing, old insulation and, at the floor and ceiling, fire blocking or a doubled top plate from the old roof line. None of that stops a soundbar going up, because the bracket screws into the studs, which are still there and usually at regular spacing.

What it changes is the hidden-wire plan. A code-compliant in-wall kit needs to drop through the bay from the TV position to the outlet below, and sheathing or blocking can be in the way. We open a small inspection hole behind where the TV will hang, look, and then tell you whether the $169 in-wall kit will go or whether a $59 paintable cover along the wall is the realistic route. You decide before we cut anything further.

A forced-air register or a baseboard radiator sits right under the TV — is that a problem for the soundbar?

It can be, so it gets checked before anything goes up. A lot of Nassau houses heat with hot-water baseboard or have a supply register low on the living-room wall, and the TV wall is often the only wall long enough to hold it. Warm air rising from a radiator or register washes straight over a soundbar mounted just above it, and a bar sitting on a console in front of a register can block the heat to the room as well.

Usually the fix is placement. Mounting the bar under the screen but a little higher, with the TV raised slightly to keep the spacing even, keeps it out of the warmest air; a deflector on a register sends the airflow out into the room rather than up the wall. We take a quick temperature reading at the bar's position with the heat running, and we never rest a bar or a sub directly on a radiator cover.

A doorway on the TV wall was closed up years ago — can the bracket go over that patch?

It can, but not by trusting the patch. A closed-up opening often has a header across the top and a few short studs, or sometimes only drywall screwed to whatever was handy. A soundbar is light, but it sits directly under a TV, and the bracket needs real framing. We scan the area, find where the framing actually runs, and mark it before drilling.

If the studs fall where the bracket needs them, nothing extra is required. If they don't, a plywood backer board ($85) fixed to the solid studs on either side of the old opening spans the gap and gives both the TV mount and the soundbar bracket something solid to hold. Painted to match, it reads as part of the wall.

The wall behind the TV backs onto the attached garage — can the cables go through it?

Sometimes, and carefully. Many Nassau ranches and high ranches have an attached garage, and the wall between garage and house is a fire separation. Any hole through it has to be kept small and sealed afterwards with fire-rated sealant so the separation still works. We don't run cables through that wall casually, and we never leave an open hole between a garage and a living space.

Often there is a better path anyway: along the garage side of the wall to a point where the cable can enter an interior stud bay, or up into the attic and down. A cable run of up to 25 feet is $99. If the only sensible route would weaken the fire separation, we tell you and use a paintable cover inside the room instead.

Our ranch faces a long picture window across from the TV — why does the sound seem harsh, and what helps?

Glass is one of the most reflective surfaces in a room. In the classic Nassau ranch, the living room has a wide picture window on the street side and the TV on the wall facing it, so everything the soundbar plays bounces straight off the glass and comes back to the couch a fraction of a second later. That reflection blurs speech and makes treble sound sharp, especially in a room with hard floors.

The cheapest fixes are soft ones: lined curtains or heavy blinds that can close over the window at night, and a rug between the TV and the seating. On the equipment side, the soundbar's room calibration and dialogue setting help, and a bar aimed squarely at the seats rather than angled toward the glass does better. We run the calibration with the curtains in the position you normally keep them.

Two-families, basement units and apartment buildings

I rent a basement apartment in a house — can a soundbar be mounted on the foundation wall?

Physically, yes: block and poured-concrete foundation walls hold masonry anchors very well, at a $75 surcharge for concrete or cinder block. The real question comes first: it's the owner's wall, so we ask for the owner's written OK before drilling. That protects your deposit and keeps the job within your lease. Basement units are permitted, with permits, in the Town of Hempstead, and an owner who has gone through that process usually has clear rules about alterations.

If drilling is off the table, a soundbar on the TV stand needs no holes and costs $79 to set up. If it is allowed, cables on a foundation wall run in a painted cover, because block can't be fished. And since the household upstairs is directly above, we set the sub low and show you the night mode before we leave.

Our Long Beach oceanfront apartment has concrete walls and ceilings — how does the cable get from the TV to the cabinet?

Not inside the structure. Concrete and block in oceanfront and mid-rise buildings can't be fished the way a stud wall can, and coring through slabs or bearing walls isn't something a building permits for a TV. So the route is on the surface: a slim paintable cord cover from the TV down to the cabinet, painted to the wall, or along the top of the baseboard where it hardly shows. The cover is $59, and a run up to 25 feet is $99.

Often the simplest answer is to bring the equipment to the TV. If the cable box and console sit on a console directly below the screen, the only visible cable is a short drop, and the soundbar connects with a single HDMI lead. Anchors go into the concrete at the $75 rate, and we keep the bar off any wall shared with the neighbouring unit.

Raised houses on the South Shore

Our Long Beach house was lifted after Sandy — does the extra flight of stairs change the job?

A little, mainly in logistics. In a raised house the living room may be a full storey or more above the driveway, reached by an exterior stair. Large TVs and soundbar packages go up by hand, and a big screen may need two people, which we plan for when we book. Inside, the work is the same as any other house: find the framing, mount, route, connect.

Two details are specific to raised houses. Their framing is often newer and more regular than the rest of Nassau's stock, which makes mounting straightforward. And their wiring often runs differently from an older house, so we check where outlets and data cables actually come up before planning any hidden run. Hardware on decks and porches facing the ocean or the bay gets stainless fasteners.

Can a TV and soundbar go in the enclosed area under a raised house?

We advise against it. Under National Flood Insurance Program rules, an enclosure below the lowest floor of an elevated building is meant primarily for parking, building access or limited storage, and it has flood openings so water can pass through. It is exactly the space designed to flood, and a TV, a soundbar and their power strips have no place in it.

If you want a screen outdoors, a covered deck at living level is a much better spot, with an outdoor-rated bar under the roof and power from an existing protected outlet. If you are unsure what your elevation certificate or local floodplain rules allow below the house, check with the city or village building department before you put anything there.

Estates and larger North Shore homes

How do you add a soundbar in a paneled library or a room with original millwork?

By drilling as little as possible and in places nobody will see. In a paneled room the first step is to look for a route that already exists: the back of a built-in bookcase, a closet or service space behind the TV wall, or the floor of a room above with access to the bay. Cable can often reach the TV position without a single new hole in the face of the paneling.

The soundbar itself can attach to the TV mount with a bracket rather than to the wall, which avoids a second set of holes, or sit on a console in front. Where something must be fixed to old woodwork or plaster, we drill slowly, use the framing behind, and agree the exact position with you before starting. Plaster carries the $45 surcharge; there is no extra charge for patience.

Can soundbars in four different rooms play the same music at once when we have people over?

Usually, if they come from the same family. Systems designed for multiroom use, Sonos being the best-known example, let you group bars and speakers in the app so the family room, kitchen, basement and deck play the same playlist, then split them again when the party ends. Mixing brands makes it harder: some combinations can share music through a common streaming feature, others can't group at all.

So it is worth deciding before buying the second and third bars. Tell us which rooms you want to link and what you already own, and we will tell you what will group cleanly. Each room is priced as its own line, and we check that the Wi-Fi in every room is strong enough for grouped playback before we leave. Where it isn't, our Nassau County network installation team can add coverage.

Can a household member's hearing aids get the TV sound while everyone else hears the soundbar?

Often, but it depends on the TV and the hearing aids. Many modern hearing aids can stream audio by Bluetooth, and some TVs can send sound to a Bluetooth device and to the soundbar at the same time; others switch one off when the other connects. We check what your TV's audio menu actually supports rather than promising a feature that isn't there.

Where simultaneous output isn't possible, a manufacturer's TV streamer accessory for the hearing aids, connected to the TV separately, is the usual answer. Meanwhile the soundbar's speech or dialogue enhancement setting helps everyone in the room. We set it up and show the household how to switch it on.

Converted rooms and inherited wiring

Our back porch was closed in and became the TV room — what's different about a soundbar there?

Closed-in porches and sunrooms are common in Nassau houses, and they make popular TV rooms. They differ from the rest of the house in three ways. Walls are often mostly glass, with short framed sections between windows, so the TV and bar may have to go on the one solid wall or on a narrow pier. Insulation is often lighter, so the room gets hotter in summer and colder in winter than the living room. And the original porch floor may slope slightly toward the yard.

We find the framing in the solid sections first, because a converted porch wall may have been built with less framing than an original interior wall; a backer board ($85) solves it where it is thin. A bar mounted out of direct afternoon sun lasts longer, and the TV is levelled to itself rather than to a sloping floor or ceiling. If the room has no outlet where the TV will go, an electrician adds one before our visit.

The previous owners left speaker wire coming out of the walls and ceiling — can we use it with a new soundbar system?

Possibly. Many Nassau basements and family rooms were pre-wired for surround sound when a receiver and five speakers were the standard home theatre. A typical soundbar with wireless rear speakers doesn't use speaker wire at all, but the same wire works perfectly if you choose a receiver and passive speakers instead, or a soundbar that accepts wired surrounds.

The catch is knowing where each wire goes and whether it is still intact. We trace each run, label it and test it before anyone buys speakers for it. If the wire is good, connecting a receiver and speakers is $129. If it's damaged or leads nowhere, we tell you and work with what you have.

"A TV wall mount with wires hidden inside the wall costs $139"

The gap between those numbers is the method. Running power for a TV inside a wall has to be done with a code-compliant in-wall power kit or by an electrician; an extension cord stuffed through the drywall is a fire risk, whatever it costs. Our in-wall concealment is a single line at $169 using such a kit, fed from the outlet already on the wall.

When comparing quotes, ask how the power gets behind the TV. If the answer is vague, so is the safety.

"Drywall anchors are fine because a soundbar is light"

Some bars are light. Others aren't: Crutchfield lists Samsung's HW-Q990D bar at 17 lb, and Sanus rates its universal soundbar mount for most bars up to 20 lb. A bar that weighs as much as a small TV, sitting below a TV that may be bumped by children, a vacuum or a cleaning cloth, deserves the same care.

Hollow-wall anchors also behave differently in Nassau's mixed walls. In plaster they can crumble the keys behind; in an old patch they may sit in drywall with nothing behind it at all. Studs, backer boards and masonry anchors in solid material are what keep a bar level for years.

We anchor every soundbar bracket into framing or masonry, and where neither lines up, we add an $85 plywood backer rather than gamble on anchors.

"Mount the soundbar tight against the bottom of the TV"

It looks tidy and it's a common recommendation, but the manufacturers are more specific. Sonos's own Arc Ultra guide says to keep at least 4 inches between the soundbar and the TV, and to keep both ends at least a foot from a wall or obstruction.

The reasons are acoustic and practical: drivers that fire upward or sideways need room, and a bar jammed under the screen can block the TV's infrared sensor so the remote stops working. In narrow Nassau living rooms where the TV sits between two windows or beside a built-in, those end clearances decide which bar fits.

We follow the manufacturer's spacing for your model, measured from the screen, and test the remote before tightening anything.

"You need eARC to get Dolby Atmos"

Not quite. Technical explainers such as AWOL Vision's distinguish the two: older ARC carries Dolby Atmos inside compressed Dolby Digital Plus, which is how most streaming apps deliver it, while eARC is needed for the lossless formats — Dolby TrueHD Atmos and DTS-HD Master Audio — that come from discs and some players.

That matters because many Nassau households have a TV that is a few years old and a brand-new soundbar. If you stream, ARC may already give you Atmos; if you collect 4K discs, you'll want eARC. The most common failure we see isn't the standard at all: it's the bar plugged into an ordinary HDMI port instead of the one labelled ARC or eARC.

We check the TV's ports and audio settings before recommending anything, and only suggest new equipment when the existing pair genuinely can't do what you want.

"Putting up a soundbar never involves a permit"

For the soundbar and its low-voltage cables, that is generally right. The trouble is the advice that often comes with it: "just add an outlet behind the TV." A new outlet is electrical work, and in Nassau that means a licensed electrician and whatever paperwork your town, city or one of the county's 64 villages requires.

Village forms make the split clear. Garden City's building permit application lists permit applications from licensed electricians among its requirements, separate from the village's own minor permits for fences, driveways and similar work.

Our rule is simple: we don't add or move outlets. The in-wall kit carries power from the outlet already there, and when the room genuinely needs new power, an electrician does it first.

Shared Walls and Shared Floors

Soundbars in Two-Families, Co-ops and Apartments

Basement units are permitted, with permits, in the Town of Hempstead. A soundbar in any of these homes has an audience you didn't invite.

The physics is simple. Mid and high frequencies are easily stopped by a wall or a floor. Bass isn't: it travels through structure, especially when a subwoofer sits on a hard floor or a bar's bracket is screwed into a shared wall. So the planning starts with location. A TV wall that is internal to your unit is better than a party wall. A sub on a rug, away from walls and on isolation feet, is better than one in a corner on a tiled floor. In a two-family, the upstairs household's sub sits directly over the downstairs living room unless it's placed with care.

Then the settings. Most soundbars have a night mode that compresses loud passages and a dialogue setting that lifts speech, which together let the volume come down without losing the words. Many also let you turn the sub's level down independently. We set these up during the hand-over and show everyone in the household where they are.

Finally, permission. In a rented unit the walls are the owner's, so we ask for the owner's written OK before drilling. In a co-op or condo, the board or managing agent may need to approve the work and see a certificate of insurance. If approval is slow, the no-drill starter setup gets the sound working now, and the bar can go on the wall later.

Barrier Island and Bay Shore

A Checklist for Soundbars in Raised South Shore Houses

Sandy reshaped the South Shore's housing. The Long Beach Herald reported that FEMA carried out 2,755 post-storm inspections in Long Beach and found 860 homes substantially damaged, which had to be raised or demolished, with the city's building commissioner estimating that raising a house cost homeowners around $75,000 on average. Many of those houses now have their living space well above the street.

  • Keep electronics on the living floor. The enclosure under an elevated house is meant for parking, access and limited storage, with flood openings so water can pass through. It is not a place for a TV, a soundbar or a power strip.
  • Plan the carry. Exterior stairs and landings decide whether a large TV needs two people. Tell us the screen size and the stair layout when booking.
  • Use stainless outside. Brackets and screws on an ocean- or bay-facing deck rust quickly in salt air. Outdoor hardware gets stainless fasteners.
  • Choose outdoor-rated gear for decks. An indoor soundbar on an open deck won't last. Outdoor-rated bars still go under a roof and away from strong afternoon sun.
  • Power from a protected outlet only. Outdoor TVs and bars plug into an existing weather-protected outlet. If there isn't one, an electrician adds it first.
  • Check where wiring comes up. Raised and rebuilt houses often have newer, differently routed wiring than older Nassau houses; we confirm outlet and data locations before planning a hidden run.
  • Unplug before a storm. When a coastal storm is forecast, unplugging the TV, bar and sub is the cheapest protection there is.
Before You Call

Six Things to Find Out About Your Wall First

Answering these before you phone, and snapping one picture, lets us give a firm number in a single conversation.

  • Was the room ever added on or opened up? If the TV wall used to be an outside wall, or a wall was removed nearby, say so. It changes the hidden-wire plan, not the price of the bar.
  • Is there a closed-up door or window in the wall? Look for a slightly uneven patch, or ask the previous owner's paperwork. That is where a backer board may be needed.
  • What material are you looking at? A light tap tells a lot. Hollow between solid spots means drywall on studs; a flat, dense thud suggests plaster; a hard click is block or concrete.
  • Which receptacle will feed it? One sitting low on the TV wall is ideal for a hidden kit; if the closest is across the room, or still the old two-slot type, an electrician may need to visit first.
  • What's on the other side? A garage, a neighbour's apartment, a bedroom, an attic or a closet each change the route and the settings.
  • Who needs to say yes? Your board, HOA or landlord. Get the approval and the certificate wording before booking.

Fixing Soundbar Installs That Didn't Quite Work

A fair number of calls come from households whose bar is already on the wall but doesn't behave. Here is what usually turns out to be wrong, and the remedy.

  • The bar is in the wrong HDMI port. Sound drops when inputs change, or the TV remote can't set the volume. Moving one cable to the ARC or eARC port and changing two settings fixes it.
  • The bracket is in drywall anchors over a patch. The bar sags at one end. We re-anchor into framing, adding a backer board if the framing doesn't line up.
  • The bar blocks the TV's remote sensor. Mounted too close beneath the screen, it stops the remote working. Respacing it to the manufacturer's clearance solves it.
  • An extension cord was pushed through the wall. That isn't a safe way to power a TV. We replace it with a code-compliant in-wall kit, or a cover if the wall can't take one.
  • The sub is booming into the apartment below. Moving it off the floor's hard spot, onto isolation feet, and lowering its level with night mode set usually ends the complaints.
  • Surrounds that go silent mid-film. Nine times in ten the culprit is either poor Wi-Fi where the speaker sits or a receptacle that someone turns off at the light switch by the door.

Settings and pairing faults fall under the Nassau setup-only rate, $225 for the first hour. Taking a bar down and hanging it again properly is charged as a new wall mount, $129.

Field Notes From Nassau Living Rooms

Reading a renovated wall: the routine on every Nassau visit (describes our usual practice, not one particular household)

The first question on a Nassau job is "what happened here?" We look at the room the way an inspector would: a ceiling line that steps down where an addition starts, a strip of floor that changes direction, a window that sits oddly high, a light switch on the wrong side of a door. Each is a clue that the wall behind the TV may not be the wall in the original plans.

Then the scanner, run the full width of the TV position, not just where the bracket will land. Studs that stop part-way down, a run of solid readings where there should be a cavity, or nothing at all over a stretch where there should be framing tell us where the old openings, the blocking and the sheathing are. If the reading is unclear, a small hole behind where the TV will hang settles it, and nobody will ever see it.

Two habits round it off. We photograph the inside of any wall we open before the kit or plate closes it, so the household has a record of what is behind the TV for the next renovation. And we note the soundbar model, bracket and connection settings on the job record, which makes a later repair visit or a second room quicker for everyone. Neither costs anything, and both reflect the same idea: in a county where most houses have been changed many times, the next person to open the wall should know what they will find.

Only then do we decide the route for the cables and confirm the price with the household. If the in-wall kit won't drop through, we say so before cutting, and offer the cover. Reading the wall first is the difference between one visit and two.

We're moving within Nassau — can you take the soundbar down at the old house and put it up at the new one?
+

Yes, as two jobs. At the old house we take the bar and its bracket down, and removing a bracket is $45; at the new house the bar goes back up at the $129 wall-mount price, with the wall surcharge for whatever the new wall turns out to be. Moving the gear between houses is up to you, and we pack nothing ourselves.

Can you come the same day an electrician adds the outlet behind the TV?
+

Yes, as long as the electrician has finished before our two-hour window starts. Book the electrician first, confirm their finish time, then book us for a later window that day. Our TV is mounted across a corner — where does the soundbar go?
+

Directly under the screen, on the TV mount rather than the wall, because a corner leaves no flat wall below the TV for a bracket. A bracket that attaches to the TV mount keeps the bar centred as the screen angles out. If the TV sits on a corner cabinet instead, the bar goes on the cabinet's front edge for $79.

Do you work in the incorporated villages as well as the unincorporated hamlets?
+

Yes, everywhere in the county: both cities, all three towns, all 64 villages and every hamlet. Prices don't change between them. What can change is the paperwork around electrical work and building rules, which is why we stay on the low-voltage side and leave new outlets to licensed electricians.

Will you take the old TV away when the new one goes up?
+

Yes. Taking down the old TV is $85, removing its old bracket is $45, and hauling the set away for recycling is $79. Taking away the new TV and soundbar boxes and foam is $25. Tell us when booking so we bring the right vehicle space.

Can a turntable or an old stereo be connected to a new soundbar?
+

Often. It depends on the inputs the soundbar has: some take an analogue or optical input, many accept Bluetooth, and a turntable needs a phono preamp unless it has one built in. If the bar has no suitable input, an A/V receiver is the better hub, and connecting one with its speakers is $129. We check the model before you buy any adapters.

Does the certificate of insurance our co-op or HOA wants cost extra?
+

No. Certificates of insurance are free. Send us the exact wording the board, managing agent or HOA asks for, including any additional insured names, and we issue it before the appointment so nothing holds up the job.

How high must a TV and soundbar go in a waiting room to stay clear of the ADA projection rule?
+

Under section 307.2 of the 2010 ADA Standards, a wall-mounted object whose leading edge is between 27 and 80 inches above the floor may stick out no more than 4 inches into a circulation path. A TV with a soundbar under it usually projects more than that, so in a waiting room we put the bottom of the bar above 80 inches or place the pair outside the walkway.

Does doing several rooms in one visit cost less per room?
+

Each room is priced from the same published lines, so the per-room price doesn't change. What you save is a second appointment: there is no visit charge on top of the lines, and doing two or three rooms together means one arrival window instead of several.
