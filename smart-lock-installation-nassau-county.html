The Neighborhood

Why Nassau County Properties Need Smart Lock Installation

In Nassau, the door decides the lock. Drive from Inwood to Bayville and you pass through nearly every era of American house-building, packed tightly together. A Garden City street laid out after Alexander Turney Stewart bought part of the Hempstead Plains in 1869 gives way to post-war split-levels and ranches; a block of Levitt Capes and 1949 ranches sits a few minutes from a Gold Coast estate in Old Westbury; raised houses on the canals of Freeport, Baldwin and Island Park sit across the bay from the barrier-island city of Long Beach. Each era built doors its own way, and a smart lock that drops straight into one of them can fight the next one for an afternoon.

That is the real reason people call us rather than just buying a box at the hardware store. The keypad deadbolt on the shelf assumes a modern door: a 2-1/8-inch cross bore, a 2-3/8 or 2-3/4-inch backset, a door between about 1-3/8 and 1-3/4 inches thick and a frame that lines up with the strike. Plenty of Nassau doors meet that. Plenty don't. Original doors on houses from the 1920s often had a mortise lockset and no deadbolt bore at all. Levitt-era doors have been re-hung and re-bored by several owners. Split-level entries open onto a half-flight of stairs where the inside of the lock can foul a railing. Colonial front doors carry thumb-latch handlesets that a new deadbolt has to sit above and match. Canal houses raised after Superstorm Sandy have front doors at the top of an exterior stair, open to wind-driven rain, and sometimes a ground-level door that the next surge could put under water.

The security case is the same as anywhere on Long Island, and Nassau police keep making it. In June 2023 the Nassau County Police Department described two masked men walking a Syosset driveway before dawn, trying car doors, then testing a rear sliding door and a basement window until the window broke and woke the homeowner; the department's advice was for residents to lock their vehicles and their residence doors, because "locked doors can help to prevent crimes." Years earlier, Nassau police warned about distraction burglars posing as contractors or utility workers while a partner slipped in through an unsecured entry, and told residents to keep doors locked even when they are home. A deadbolt that relocks itself after the door closes does exactly that, without anyone remembering to turn a key.

Then there is daily life. A Nassau household commonly has a working parent who leaves before seven, children who get home before anyone else, a dog walker, a cleaner, a contractor in the middle of a kitchen job, and an older parent who visits on Sundays. Each of those people currently has a key, or knows where one is hidden. A smart lock replaces that with a code for each person, switched on and off when needed, with a record of when each one was used. For Main Street businesses — a boutique in Rockville Centre, a dental office off Mineola Boulevard, a seafood restaurant on the Freeport Nautical Mile — the same idea runs staff codes and early delivery access, and ends the ritual of re-keying every time someone leaves.

So our Nassau work starts at the door, not at the catalogue. We measure, look at the frame and the hinge side, check what the door is exposed to, then choose between a retrofit that keeps your outside cylinder, a full keypad or touchscreen deadbolt, a smart handleset, or — for a commercial door — a keypad or reader on an electric strike. Then we pair it to the panel, hub or app already in the house and teach everyone how to use it.

Methods that change with the era of the door

Pre-war wood doors. The question is how much of the door's original construction we can keep. A mortise lock lives in a deep pocket cut into the door edge, and a standard deadbolt bore driven through that pocket can leave very little wood behind. Where the existing lock still works, a retrofit unit that mounts on the inside and turns the existing thumb-turn is often the kindest option. Where a new bore is safe, we drill with a jig so the hole is square to the face and the latch hole meets it cleanly, then mortise the strike into the frame rather than surface-mounting it.

Post-war doors with a history. A Levitt-era or 1950s door may have carried a knob, then a knob plus deadbolt, then a replacement deadbolt at a slightly different height. We fill abandoned holes with hardwood plugs or a fill kit, fit a reinforcing wrap or edge plate where the wood around the bore is thin, and use long screws through the strike into the stud behind the jamb. The goal is a bolt that slides home with a fingertip before any motor is asked to do it.

Factory-bored modern doors. Fiberglass and steel replacement doors installed in the last few decades are usually the quickest: the bore and backset match what smart deadbolts expect. The time goes into alignment — a strike plate that the bolt actually hits on a muggy day and a dry one — and into pairing, scheduling and setting up users.

Exposed and coastal doors. On a stair-top entry facing the bay, we look for a lock rated for exterior temperature range and moisture, use stainless fasteners, and pay attention to how much shelter the keypad has from driving rain. A keypad that faces the weather with no overhang will see more water than its maker imagined. We would rather suggest a small canopy or a different model than fit something that corrodes in two seasons.

Commercial frames. Narrow-stile aluminium storefront doors rarely have room for a residential deadbolt. Instead, a keypad or reader on the frame or wall releases an electric strike in the jamb, or a maglock holds the door at the header, with a request-to-exit device and free egress on the inside. Where the landlord controls the frame, we propose options that need the least cutting and document them for approval.

Cross-sell worth considering: most Nassau Colonials and split-levels that get two or three smart locks also get a video doorbell on the front entry, and Main Street shops that start with one keypad often move on to access control installation for Nassau County businesses once they have more than a couple of doors and want schedules and audit reports.

Compliance callouts for Nassau doors

Free egress first. Model building and residential codes require exit doors to be readily openable from the inside without a key or special knowledge or effort, and electrified locks on commercial doors must release toward the exit side. New York's statewide 2020 codes, in force from May 12, 2020, are built on the 2018 International Code Council books, and your village or town building department applies the edition in force. Every lock we fit leaves an inside thumb-turn, lever or exit device that works with no code and no power.

The county's own list of covered trades includes “doors, windows, awnings” and carpentry; it does not name locks, locksmiths or low-voltage work. Its licensing FAQ says contractors who work only on commercial buildings do not need the licence, and that licensed contractors must display it and put the licence number on contracts. Door Reading Guide

How to Tell What Kind of Door You Have Before You Buy

You can learn a lot about your door in five minutes with a tape measure and a phone camera, and it will save you from buying a lock that does not fit. Here is the routine we follow on every Nassau walkthrough, in the order we do it.

  1. Measure the thickness of the door slab at the lock edge. Most smart deadbolts list a range of roughly 1-3/8 to 1-3/4 inches, a few go thicker. A heavy pre-war or estate door may be outside that range.
  2. Measure the backset — from the edge of the door to the centre of the existing deadbolt or knob hole. It should be close to 2-3/8 or 2-3/4 inches. Anything else usually means an older lockset or a non-standard door.
  3. Look at the edge of the door. A small round latch hole with a rectangular faceplate means a bored lock. A tall rectangular faceplate with the latch and bolt in one body means a mortise lock.
  4. Count the holes. Filled or abandoned holes on the face or edge tell you the door has been re-bored. Photograph them; we will plan the fill.
  5. Close the door and turn the existing deadbolt slowly. If you feel it rub or have to push or pull the door to make it throw, the strike is out of line. A smart lock will struggle with the same door until that is fixed.
  6. Check what is in front of and behind the door. A storm door, a railing on a split-level landing, a wall return or a glass sidelight all affect which interior and exterior units fit.
  7. Note the exposure. Is there a canopy? Does the door face the bay, the ocean or the prevailing wind? Is it one step above grade or at the top of a raised-house stair?
  8. Find the router and any hub or alarm panel. Note the alarm keypad brand and the hub model if there is one. That decides whether the lock should speak Wi-Fi, Z-Wave, Thread or Bluetooth.

Typical preparation that most retail smart deadbolts list. Always check the exact figures for the model you are considering; heavier and older Nassau doors often fall outside them.

Your Questions Answered

Smart Lock Installation in Nassau County: Questions Answered

These are the questions Nassau homeowners and shop owners actually put to us at the door, grouped by the kind of property they come from. Each answer is the one we would give standing on your front step.

Doors from different housing eras

Can a keypad deadbolt be added above the thumb-latch handleset on our Colonial or Tudor front door without replacing the whole set?

Usually, yes. Many Nassau Colonials and Tudors have an exterior handleset — a grip with a thumb latch — with a separate deadbolt above it, and a smart deadbolt can replace just that upper deadbolt. Three things decide whether it looks and works right. First, the spacing: on many handlesets the deadbolt sits a fixed distance above the latch, and the new deadbolt's exterior unit has to fit between the handleset's upper post and the door's top rail or glass. Second, the finish: a satin-nickel keypad above an oil-rubbed bronze handleset looks like a mistake, so we bring the finish options to the walkthrough. Third, some handlesets are one-piece units where the deadbolt and grip share a backplate; on those, a matched smart handleset that replaces the whole assembly is the clean answer. We tell you which you have before quoting.

What if our front door has never been bored for a deadbolt at all?

That happens on some original 1920s and 1930s doors in older villages, on doors that rely on a mortise lock, and occasionally on interior-grade doors someone hung on an exterior opening. If the door is solid and thick enough, we bore a new deadbolt hole with a jig — square to the face, at the right backset, clear of any rails or panels — and mortise a reinforced strike into the frame. If the door is a mortise-lock door, we look at whether a retrofit unit can drive the existing lock or whether boring a separate deadbolt would weaken the stile. And if the door is hollow or thin, we say so: the honest answer may be a better door first, which is carpentry outside our work.

The front door on our split-level opens onto the landing between two half-flights — will the inside of a smart lock hit the railing?

It can, and it is one of the most common snags we find in East Meadow, Merrick and Plainview. Smart deadbolts carry their batteries and motor in the interior unit, and some interior units are tall and deep. On a split-level or high-ranch landing the door may swing back toward a railing, a newel post or the wall of the upper flight. We measure the clearance with the door fully open, pick a lock with a slimmer interior unit if needed, and check that the thumb-turn is still easy to reach from the landing without leaning over the stairs. Where a door stop is missing, we add one so the lock body never meets the railing.

Why we open a split-level door all the way before choosing a lock: the interior unit travels with the door and must stop short of the railing.

Our center-hall Colonial has glass sidelights beside the front door — how do we stop someone reaching through and turning the lock?

This is a fair worry: a thumb-turn within an arm's reach of a sidelight can be turned by someone who breaks the glass. Fitting a lock that needs a key on the inside creates a different problem, because doors used to leave the house must open from inside without a key. The practical answers are to make the glass harder to get through — laminated glass or a security film — and to make breaking it noisy and reported: a glass-break detector or door contact on the alarm, and a lock that sends an alert whenever it is unlocked. Some smart locks also let you disable the outside keypad remotely while you are away, which doesn't stop a reach-through but does stop a code from being guessed. We go through the options for your door before choosing.

How do we stop the door from being kicked in at the frame once the smart deadbolt is on?

The lock is rarely what fails in a forced entry; the frame is. A strike plate held by two short screws in a thin pine jamb splits away long before the bolt bends. On every exterior door we fit, we replace the stock strike screws with long screws that reach the framing behind the jamb, and on doors that need it we fit a reinforced strike box or a full-length jamb reinforcement. We also look at the hinge side — short hinge screws are just as weak — and at whether the deadbolt throws its full length. A smart lock that reports “locked” but only throws half its bolt is not doing its job.

Mudrooms, families and everyday use

The mudroom door is where everyone kicks off their boots — what happens if it relocks while the door is still ajar?

An auto-lock that fires on an open door throws the bolt into thin air, and the next push leaves the door wedged against the bolt or marks the frame. On busy mudroom and kitchen doors we use one of two approaches: a lock with a door-position sensor that only locks once the door is actually closed, or a relock delay long enough to cover the time it takes to get a stroller or a load of groceries through. We also set an alert for a door that has been left open for more than a few minutes, which is often more useful on a mudroom than the auto-lock itself.

Can the lock tell us which of our children got home and at what time?

Yes, if each child has their own code or their own phone credential. The app then records which code opened the door and when, and most systems can send a notification for a specific user — “Sam unlocked the side door at 3:12” — rather than for every unlock. We set up named users at the handover, show you how to switch notifications on for particular people, and talk through what happens if a child shares a code with a friend: you change that one code, not everyone's.

Which smart locks are easiest for an older parent with arthritis or weak eyesight to use?

Look for three things: large, backlit buttons with a clear click or a touchscreen with high-contrast digits; a lever-style or large thumb-turn on the inside rather than a small knob; and an auto-lock so nobody has to remember to lock up. Fingerprint models can help some people and frustrate others, so we prefer to let your parent try one before committing. We also set a longer code-entry timeout where the lock allows it, and make sure the person has a key or an override they can actually manage on the day the batteries run down.

We have a kitchen renovation starting — can the lock show when the contractor's crew actually arrives and leaves?

Yes, within limits. Give the contractor a code of their own, valid only on working days and hours, and the lock's history records every time that code opens the door. It shows when the door was unlocked with that code, not who walked through or when they left, so treat it as a check rather than a timesheet. Put the code on the side or mudroom door if you can, so the front door stays family-only, and delete it the day the job is signed off. If the crew needs to come and go without anyone home, a camera at that door fills in what the log cannot.

Will auto-lock shut me out when I step into the yard to let the dog out?

Not if it is set up sensibly. On a back or kitchen door you use constantly, we either lengthen the relock delay, turn auto-lock off for that door during daytime hours where the lock supports schedules, or rely on the keypad so being outside without a phone is never a problem — you simply enter your code to get back in. The one combination we avoid is a keyless lock with auto-lock on and no keypad, where a phone left on the kitchen counter really does lock you out.

Flood zones, canals and the South Shore

Should a smart lock go on a ground-level door that sits below the flood line in a canal-front house?

Generally we advise against putting the only working lock on a door that could be under water. Smart locks are built for rain and humidity, not for being submerged in salt water, and a surge that floods the ground level will usually ruin the electronics. For storage, garage and utility doors below the living floor of a raised house, a good mechanical deadbolt is the dependable choice; put the smart lock on the stair-top entry you use every day. If a lower door must be keyless, choose a model that keeps a mechanical key override and expect to replace the lock after a flood.

We would like to let our Lido Beach or Point Lookout house to weekend visitors with keypad check-in — does the Town of Hempstead allow that?

Not for stays that short, as the law was reported. In July 2017 the Town of Hempstead board voted to set a 28-day minimum on residential rentals and to require a rental permit and registry with inspections. A keypad lock makes it easy to give a monthly tenant or a family member their own code and to cancel it when they leave, but it does not change what the town allows. Rules differ in Long Beach, Glen Cove and each incorporated village, and codes change, so check with your own municipality before advertising.

Apartments, co-ops and shared entrances

Our garden-apartment unit door opens straight to the outdoors rather than onto a hallway — does that change which lock suits it?

It does. Many garden-apartment complexes near Nassau's LIRR stations have unit doors that face a courtyard or a parking area, so the lock sees rain, sun and cold like a house door, not like a corridor door in a city high-rise. That means an exterior-rated lock, a keypad with some shelter, and batteries that cope with winter. It also means the building's rules still apply: many co-op and rental buildings require that management keeps a working key or code for emergencies, so we fit locks that allow that and leave a key or arrange a management code before we go.

When Optimum or Verizon swaps our router, do the smart locks stop working?

The locks keep locking and unlocking with codes and keys, but Wi-Fi locks and Wi-Fi bridges lose their connection if the new router has a different network name or password, so the app and remote access stop until they are rejoined. Z-Wave and Thread locks connect to a hub or border router rather than to the internet router directly, so they are usually unaffected unless the hub itself was moved or reset. The easy fix is to give the new router the same network name and password as the old one; otherwise each Wi-Fi lock or bridge has to be re-added. We can do that on a setup-only visit if you would rather not.

Shops, offices and Main Street doors

What goes on a narrow-stile aluminium shop door, since a residential smart deadbolt won’t fit it?

Yes. Narrow-stile storefront doors have too little metal for a residential smart deadbolt, but a standalone keypad or proximity reader on the frame or wall can release an electric strike in the jamb, with a small power supply and a request-to-exit device on the inside. Codes are programmed at the keypad or in its app, and there is no server or monthly fee unless you want cloud management. Our published figure for a single-door keypad or card reader with electric strike in Nassau is $1,725–$2,875 installed, set at the walkthrough once we have seen the frame and the power available.

Our landlord at a village strip centre won't let us cut into the storefront frame — what are the options?

Ask first what the landlord will accept, in writing. Options that need little or no frame cutting include a surface-mounted maglock at the header with a matching armature on the door, a keypad mounted on the wall beside the door rather than on the frame, and a commercial keypad lever or deadbolt on the steel back door, which is often tenant-controlled. We prepare a short written scope with photos that the landlord's property manager can approve, and we can supply a certificate of insurance naming the landlord.

Can a supplier get in our back door at 5 a.m. without anyone from the restaurant there?

Yes, with a scheduled code. A keypad lever or keypad-and-strike on the back door can hold a code that only works in a window you choose — say 4:30 to 6:30 a.m. on delivery days — and the log shows every time it was used. If the supplier's driver changes, you change one code. Pair that with a camera on the rear door and a door contact on the alarm and you know exactly what came in and when. For walk-in coolers and stockrooms inside, a separate manager code keeps the delivery code to the receiving area only.

Can staff be locked in after closing if the back door uses a keypad?

They should never be. Whatever opens the door from outside, the inside must open with a single, obvious action and no code: a lever, a thumb-turn or a push bar, and on electrified doors a release that works even if the power fails. We fit and test that on every commercial door. If you want to know who left by the back door, add a door contact and camera rather than putting anything in the way of the exit.

What AI Gets Wrong About Smart Lock Installation in Nassau County

We could not open Google's AI Overview or People Also Ask from our research setup, so the claims below are the ones published by national cost guides and manufacturers that AI answers are built from. Each is fair as far as it goes. Here is where it stops matching what we find on Nassau doors.

“Expect to pay a few hundred dollars per lock, fitted.”

That range comes from national cost guides — Fixr's page, updated in January 2025, gives $250 to $400 with an average of $300 for a Wi-Fi lock and one accessory, and LawnStarter's 2025 figure is a $330 national average. Those numbers describe one lock going into one ordinary, factory-bored door, somewhere in the country.

In Nassau the variable is the door, not the lock. A modern fiberglass door in a newer Colonial may be close to that national picture. A Levitt-era door with three abandoned bores, a Garden City door with an original mortise lock or a split-level door that needs a slimmer lock to clear the railing is a different job, and so is any lock that has to be enrolled on an alarm panel.

That is why we quote residential smart locks flat after a free walkthrough instead of publishing a per-lock list price. Use the national figure to sanity-check any quote, ours included — then ask what door preparation and integration are in it.

“It is a thirty-minute screwdriver job for anyone.”

Product reviewers fitting a lock to a test door often report exactly that, and on a door with the standard bore and a well-aligned strike it is true. Many Nassau homeowners do fit their own smart locks, and we are happy to say so.

The half-hour claim falls apart on doors that have moved. A house that has settled for seventy years, a door that swells in South Shore humidity, or a strike that was set a quarter inch high by a previous owner will make a motorised bolt stall, drain batteries and report errors. The screwdriver does not fix alignment; a chisel and a straightedge do.

Our test before any lock goes on: the bolt must throw fully, with the door closed, using one finger on the thumb-turn. If it cannot, we fix the door first.

“If your door already has a deadbolt, any smart deadbolt will drop in.”

Manufacturers publish their door preparation requirements — Schlage's, for example, call for a door between 1-3/8 and 2 inches thick on some models, a 2-1/8-inch cross bore and a 2-3/8 or 2-3/4-inch backset. “Standard” means a door that matches those numbers.

Nassau's housing stock includes plenty of doors that do not: pre-war doors with mortise locks, estate doors thicker than the listed range, sidelight doors where the exterior unit would sit over glass, and narrow-stile shop doors with no room for a residential deadbolt at all.

The fix is to measure before buying. Our door-reading routine above takes five minutes and tells you which lock families can work.

“Buy a Matter lock and the app question goes away.”

Matter is a genuine step forward: a Matter lock can join more than one ecosystem. But the manufacturers' own help pages are careful. Yale's says a Matter-over-Thread lock needs a Thread border router set up first, that feature support varies by app, and that its own app and the Matter app cannot run the lock at the same time.

In practice, a Nassau household with an older alarm panel that speaks Z-Wave, an Amazon speaker in the kitchen and an iPhone in every pocket has to choose which system owns the lock. Matter does not make that choice for you.

We ask what you already run before recommending a protocol, and we tell you plainly which features you will and will not get in each app.

“Hackers make a plain keyed deadbolt the safer bet.”

Any connected device can have vulnerabilities, and keeping firmware up to date matters. But the burglaries Nassau police describe are not sophisticated: masked men testing car doors, sliding doors and basement windows before dawn in Syosset; distraction burglars walking in through an unsecured door while a partner keeps the homeowner talking.

Those break-ins exploit doors that were left unlocked. A lock that relocks itself and tells you when it was opened closes that gap better than a key that depends on someone remembering to turn it.

The real weak points on most Nassau doors are the frame and the glass beside the lock, which is why our installs include long strike screws, reinforcement where needed and an honest look at sidelights.

“Who fits it doesn’t matter, it is just a lock.”

For a single battery deadbolt on a modern door, a careful handyman can. The question is what else the job touches. A keypad and electric strike on a shop door involves low-voltage wiring, a power supply, a request-to-exit device and free-egress rules. A lock enrolled on an alarm panel changes how the alarm behaves.

Nassau County also licenses home improvement contractors working on homes of up to three families, and its covered-trades list includes doors and carpentry. If your project includes replacing a door, that part of the work falls in a licensed trade.

Ask any installer what licence and insurance they carry and for a certificate of insurance. Ours is NYS Low-Voltage licence #12000287431, and certificates are free.

“Outages don’t affect a battery lock at all.”

Mostly true: battery locks keep locking and unlocking by code, key and nearby phone when the house loses power. What stops is remote access and notifications, because the router and any bridge or hub are down.

Nassau knows long outages. After Sandy, Long Beach was without power and running water for two weeks. A lock that is fine on batteries for that long still needs fresh batteries going in, and someone who knows where the mechanical key or override is.

Every lock we fit keeps a key or override, and at the handover we show you exactly where it is and how the lock behaves with the internet down.

Moving In

Taking Over a House That Already Has Smart Locks

Nassau houses change hands constantly, and more and more of them come with a smart lock already on the door. The previous owner may have deleted their codes and removed the lock from their account — or may not have. Until you know, assume that someone else could still have a code or app access.

  • Find the brand and model. It is usually printed on the inside unit or under the battery cover. Find the manual or the maker's support page.
  • Check whether it is still on someone's account. If the app will not let you add the lock, it is probably still registered to the seller. The seller can remove it, or the lock's factory reset will clear it.
  • Factory-reset and start fresh. A reset removes stored codes and pairings. Then set it up in your own account with your own codes.
  • Change or re-key the mechanical cylinder. Previous owners, their families and their contractors may still have keys. A new cylinder or a re-key closes that off.
  • Check what it is paired to. If it was enrolled on an alarm panel that stayed with the house, your alarm company may need to re-enrol it on your account.

Finish & Style

Choosing a Lock Finish That Suits a Nassau Front Door

A smart lock is the most handled object on the front of a house, and on many Nassau streets the front door is also the most photographed. Getting the finish right matters more than most people expect, and it is easy to get wrong when you are choosing from a phone screen.

  • Match what stays, not what goes. If the handleset, knocker, house numbers or kick plate are staying, the lock should match them. If everything is being replaced, choose the finish first and the lock family second.
  • Dark finishes on dark doors, light on light, is not a rule. A black or bronze keypad on a red or navy Colonial door often looks deliberate; satin nickel on a white fiberglass door on a high ranch looks clean. Hold samples against the door in daylight.
  • Think about exposure. Bay- and ocean-facing doors are hard on plated finishes. Ask about the maker's exterior and coastal ratings and expect to spend a little more for them on the South Shore.
  • Touchscreen or buttons. Touchscreens look tidy but show fingerprints and can be harder to use with gloves in January. Push buttons wear but are easier by feel. On a side or mudroom door, practicality usually wins.

Field Notes: Reading Doors Across Nassau

These notes describe how we work rather than any single job; we have not attached a customer story we cannot document. The pattern repeats across the county. On a re-bored post-war door, the first thing we do is close it and turn the old deadbolt slowly; nine times in ten the hand on the thumb-turn tells you more than the tape measure does. If it drags, the strike comes out, the mortise gets deepened or moved, and long screws go into the stud before the new lock leaves its box.

On a split-level, we open the door all the way before choosing a lock and look at what it swings toward. On a Colonial, we hold the finish samples against the handleset in daylight, because a keypad that looks right under the kitchen lights can look wrong at the front step. On a raised canal house, we stand at the top of the stair and look at where the rain comes from, and we ask which lower doors flooded last time.

On a Main Street door, we ask the landlord question before the lock question. And everywhere, before we leave, every person in the house tries their code on every door while we watch. That last step catches more problems than any other.

Door Hardware Glossary

Door and Lock Words You Will Hear on a Nassau Walkthrough

Half the confusion around smart locks comes from words that installers use without explaining. These are the ones that come up most on Nassau doors, in plain English.

TermWhat it means at your door
BacksetThe distance from the edge of the door to the centre of the lock hole. Most smart deadbolts expect 2-3/8 or 2-3/4 inches; older doors sometimes differ.
Cross boreThe big round hole through the face of the door that the lock body passes through, usually 2-1/8 inches on modern doors.
Edge boreThe smaller hole in the edge of the door where the bolt slides out, usually 1 inch.
StrikeThe metal plate and pocket in the door frame that the bolt slides into. If it is out of line, a smart lock strains and stalls.
Reinforced strikeA heavier strike or strike box fixed with long screws into the wall framing, so a kick splits nothing.
Mortise lockA lock whose whole body sits inside a deep rectangular pocket cut into the door edge, common on older and heavier doors. Standard smart deadbolts do not drop into it.
HandlesetThe exterior grip with a thumb latch found on many Colonial and Tudor front doors, often with a separate deadbolt above it.
Retrofit lockA smart unit that mounts on the inside over your existing deadbolt and turns it, leaving the outside and your keys as they are.
HandingWhich way the door swings and which side the hinges are on. Smart locks have to be told, or they turn the bolt the wrong way.
Door-position sensorA small sensor that tells the lock whether the door is actually shut, so auto-lock never throws the bolt into an open doorway.
Electric strikeA strike for commercial doors that releases electrically when a keypad or reader grants access, while the door's own lock stays latched.
MaglockAn electromagnet at the top of a door that holds it shut while powered. Needs a request-to-exit device and a fail-safe release.
Request-to-exit (REX)A button, bar or motion sensor on the inside of a commercial door that releases the lock for people leaving.
Z-WaveA low-power radio used by many alarm panels and smart home hubs. A Z-Wave lock is enrolled on the panel or hub, not on Wi-Fi.
Thread and MatterThread is a low-power mesh network; Matter is a standard that lets one device join several apps. A Matter-over-Thread lock needs a Thread border router.

If a word on your quote is not on this list, ask. A good installer can explain every line in one sentence.

Our front door has a mail slot and a knocker near the lock — will they get in the way?

They can. A keypad's exterior unit is taller and wider than a plain deadbolt, and on some older Nassau doors the mail slot or knocker sits right where it needs to go. We measure the space at the walkthrough and choose a slimmer model, or suggest moving the knocker. Moving or closing up a mail slot is carpentry, so we flag it before quoting rather than on the day.

Do you need to touch our electrical panel or turn off power for a smart lock?
+

Not for battery smart locks, which need no wiring at all. Wi-Fi bridges plug into an ordinary outlet. Electric strikes on shop doors use a low-voltage power supply; if a new outlet or circuit is needed for it, that part is done by a licensed electrician.

We are in the middle of a renovation — when should the smart locks go on?
+

After the doors are hung, painted and adjusted, and after any flooring that changes the door's clearance. Until then, give the contractors a temporary keyed lock or a basic keypad. If you want new doors pre-bored for a particular lock, tell your contractor the model so the bore and backset match.

Is there a smart option for the sliding glass door out to the deck?
+

Far fewer smart locks are made for sliders than for hinged doors, and most sliding doors lock with a latch in the handle rather than a deadbolt. For most Nassau decks and patios we recommend a sturdy secondary lock such as a bar or foot lock, plus a contact sensor on the alarm so you are told whenever the slider opens. If you want a motorised slider lock, we check your door's frame and handle before suggesting one.

Will you protect our floors and painted trim while you bore and chisel the door?
+

Yes. We lay a drop cloth at the door, tape the face of painted or stained doors where the boring jig sits, and catch shavings as we go. Dust is vacuumed before we leave, and boxes and packaging go with us.

Will the quote list each door separately so we can phase the work?
