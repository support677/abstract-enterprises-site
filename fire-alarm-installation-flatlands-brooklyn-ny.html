Six kinds of property account for nearly all of our Flatlands fire alarm work. What separates them is less the brick or the siding than who lives on which floor, who owns what, and where the fuel-burning equipment sits in relation to the bedrooms.

Detached frame and brick houses

One- and two-family homes from the 1920s to the 1950s on the East streets between the lettered avenues, usually with a cellar, a pitched roof and bedrooms on the second floor.

Postwar bungalows

Low houses from the years after World War II, often with every bedroom on one level and a finished cellar or attic room added later.

Semi-detached brick pairs

Two houses sharing one wall, each with its own stair, cellar and boiler, often with a driveway along the open side and a garage at the back.

Two-family houses with a tenant

An owner on one floor and a tenant or relative on the other, where the city treats the rented floor differently from the floor the owner lives on.

Apartment buildings on the avenues

Multiple dwellings along Kings Highway, Nostrand Avenue and the edges of the neighborhood, where smoke detectors are owed in every apartment and notices go up in the entrance.

Stores, shops and yards

Detached frame and brick houses: the cellar is the floor that decides the plan

A typical detached Flatlands house has three or four levels: a cellar with the boiler, the water heater, the laundry and often the gas meter; a first floor with the kitchen and the living room; a second floor with two or three bedrooms off a short hall; and an attic reached through a hatch or a narrow stair. The bedrooms are where the rules start, because HPD measures smoke and CO placement from the entrance of each room used for sleeping. But the cellar is where most of the risk and most of the cable routing problems live. It holds the equipment that burns fuel, it holds the oldest wiring in the house, and it is the floor the family is least likely to be on at 3 a.m. The December 2024 basement fire on East 37th Street near Avenue I happened at about that hour.

So our plan for a detached house usually begins at the bottom of the cellar stair and works up. A carbon monoxide alarm goes near the burner and the water heater, where the code and the manufacturer allow, and a smoke alarm goes at the top of the cellar stair, on the first-floor ceiling, so smoke rising out of the cellar reaches it before it reaches the bedrooms. If the dryer is down there, a heat detector beside it is often a better choice than a smoke alarm, because lint and steam make smoke alarms nuisance-trip. Then the first floor, then the upstairs hall, then each bedroom where the family wants one. The question we ask every owner is whether the cellar alarm will be heard upstairs with every bedroom door shut. In most two-story houses with a cellar, it will not be unless the alarms are linked, which is why so many of our Flatlands jobs end up as linked systems.

Frame houses and brick houses route cable differently. In a frame house, wire can often travel up inside an interior wall from the cellar to the attic, and a hardwired linked system goes in with a few small openings. In a brick house with plaster on masonry, the outside walls are solid, and cable has to use a closet, a chase beside the chimney, or the space beside an old pipe. Where that becomes too disruptive, sealed wireless interconnected alarms are the honest alternative for a household system, and we test the links between the cellar and the attic before committing to them.

Postwar bungalows: one level of bedrooms, and the rooms added later

The bungalows that went up on the last open lots after the war were built for one family on one floor. Bedrooms often open off the living room or a short passage, and the kitchen is a few steps away. That layout makes the basic placement simple: one alarm can often sit within fifteen feet of two bedroom doors at once, and the whole sleeping area is on the same level as the main living space. Many owners are surprised by how few devices a plain bungalow needs.

The complication is what families have added since. A finished cellar with a bedroom for a teenager or a grandparent, an attic room built under the roof slope, a sun room on the back, a garage turned into a den. Each new sleeping room brings its own fifteen-foot circle, and a room under a sloped ceiling needs its alarm placed by the manufacturer's rules for slopes rather than in the middle of the ceiling. A cellar bedroom also changes the CO picture, because the person sleeping there is now on the same floor as the boiler. We draw the house as it is used today, not as it was built, and the device list follows that drawing.

Semi-detached brick pairs: two houses, one wall, separate everything else

A semi-detached pair shares a party wall but nothing else that matters for fire detection: each half has its own stair, its own cellar, its own boiler and its own owner. Our work stays entirely on your side. Alarms in your half are not linked to your neighbor's, and we do not run cable through the shared wall. What we do look at is the shared wall itself, because the cellar and attic of each half sit right beside the other's. A detector at the top of your cellar stair and one at the attic hatch are the two devices most likely to notice a fire that begins in your own house near that wall.

Semi-detached pairs in Flatlands often have a driveway along the open side and a garage at the back of the lot. The garage matters for detection only where it is attached or where someone sleeps beside it; a car's exhaust is the reason smoke alarms do not belong inside a garage, and a heat detector on the house side of the connecting door is the usual answer when the garage is attached. If the garage is a separate building, it stays outside the household plan unless you ask us to add it.

The brick on these pairs is usually solid masonry, so a hardwired retrofit means drilling through brick in the cellar and finding a chase upward. On most semi-detached jobs we price both a hardwired linked option and a sealed wireless linked option, and let the owner choose after seeing how much wall would need to be opened for each.

Two-family houses with a tenant: the owner's floor and the rented floor are not the same in law

The two-family house is the most common Flatlands job we see, and the one where owners most often misunderstand their duties. HPD's rule for carbon monoxide detectors covers one- and two-family homes the owner does not occupy when they have fuel-burning equipment. Its gas detector rule from January 1, 2027 covers the same kinds of buildings where there is gas piping. In practice, an owner who rents one floor of a two-family is responsible for the detectors the rules require in that rental, and HPD lets the owner ask the tenant to reimburse $25 for each CO or natural gas detector, with a year to pay. The tenant's side of the bargain is to test the detectors monthly, replace batteries in the models that have them, never paint over them, and replace a detector that goes missing or is broken while they live there.

The floor the owner lives on is a different matter: the household chooses what it wants, but the fire does not care which floor is rented. A fire in the owner's kitchen downstairs threatens the tenant upstairs, and a tenant's space heater threatens the owner's children. That is why most of the two-family plans we write link the alarms in the common stair or hall so that a fire on either floor is heard on both, while each apartment keeps its own alarms inside. Where a cellar is shared, with both boilers or both meters down there, we treat it as part of both households' protection.

Many Flatlands two-families are occupied by relatives rather than rent-paying strangers: a married child upstairs, a parent downstairs. Whether that counts as owner-occupied for HPD's purposes depends on the facts, and we do not give legal opinions. What we can do is write the device list so that it satisfies the stricter reading, which costs very little extra in a house this size and removes the question.

Apartment buildings on the avenues: every apartment, every notice, one list

Flatlands has fewer apartment buildings than many Brooklyn neighborhoods, but the ones it has are substantial: postwar buildings along Kings Highway and Nostrand Avenue, buildings on the neighborhood's edges, and NYCHA's Glenwood Houses, which went up in the early 1950s and are managed by NYCHA, not by private contractors like us. For a privately owned multiple dwelling, HPD's rules are clear. The owner installs smoke detectors in each apartment within fifteen feet of every room used for sleeping, CO detectors under the same placement where there is fuel-burning equipment, and natural gas detectors where there is gas piping by January 1, 2027. The owner posts a notice in the entrance, gives an adult in each apartment a notice when devices are installed, and replaces devices at the end of their life or when they go missing before a new tenant moves in.

The job in a building like that is mostly organization. We work from a unit list, schedule access floor by floor, record what we found in each apartment, replace what needs replacing, and give the owner a single dated record with every device by apartment and room. HPD lets Class A owners charge a tenant $25 per smoke, CO or gas detector, $50 for a two-type combination unit and $75 for a three-type unit, with a year to pay. In June 2026 News 12 Brooklyn reported on tenants at 1155 East 35th Street in Flatlands who described ceiling leaks and other problems and multiple open HPD complaints; water coming through a ceiling near a detector is one of the conditions we check and note in every apartment, because a wet alarm cannot be trusted.

If the building has a fire alarm system of its own, with a panel and devices in the halls, that system belongs to the FDNY's side of the rules, with filed designs, approvals and inspections. We test and document what is there before proposing anything, and we say clearly which work is a like-for-like repair and which would need new plans filed.

Stores, shops and yards: the work in the room decides the detector

The commercial side of Flatlands splits in two. The shopping streets, Utica Avenue, Flatbush Avenue toward Kings Plaza, Avenue N, Flatlands Avenue and Ralph Avenue, are lined with ground-floor stores, many with apartments above. There the question is how a fire in a closed store at night reaches the families upstairs, and how a fire upstairs reaches the staff downstairs during the day. The Flatlands/Fairfield industrial zone is different again: auto dismantlers, salvage and scrap yards, vehicle and materials storage, carting companies and concrete plants, according to NYC Planning's survey, many of them open-air lots with a small building or two.

In a yard business, the ordinary smoke alarm is usually the wrong tool. Exhaust from trucks and forklifts, dust from cement, fumes from cutting torches and solvents from parts washers will trip it constantly, and an alarm that trips constantly gets disabled. Heat detectors with the right rating, CO detection where engines run indoors, and smoke detection only in offices and storage rooms that stay clean are the usual mix. The other question in a yard is who hears the alarm at night, when nobody is there; that is where central station monitoring earns its monthly fee.

Whether a business needs a full fire alarm system filed with the FDNY depends on its occupancy, its size and its certificate of occupancy, which is a question for a registered design professional. Many small stores and shops need only well-chosen detection and, if they want it, monitoring. Where a filed system is needed, the FDNY's own guide puts the average time from plan submission to final approval at about 20 to 24 weeks, so the sooner the question is asked, the better.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Start at the Bottom of the Cellar Stair, Then Count the Bedroom Doors

On a Flatlands house survey, the first stop is not the alarm that is chirping. It is the bottom of the cellar stair. We look at what burns fuel down there, the oil burner or gas boiler, the water heater, sometimes a second range in a summer kitchen, and where the gas meter and piping run. Then we stand at the top of the cellar stair and ask whether someone asleep on the second floor, door shut, would hear an alarm mounted right there. Usually the honest answer is no.

Then we walk up and count bedroom doors, not bedrooms on the deed. A den with a pull-out where a grandmother sleeps all winter counts. An attic room counts. So does a cellar room where a nephew stays. Each door gets a fifteen-foot circle on our sketch, and the alarms go where the circles overlap. Only after that do we open the old alarms, read the dates, and decide which stay and which go. The device list we leave behind follows the same order: cellar, first floor, upstairs, attic, with every date written on it.

After Dark

Hallway, Attic, Basement, Bedroom: What Four Flatlands Night Fires Say About Where Detection Goes

We do not know what alarms any of these houses had, and none of the reports we read say. We use them because they are local, recent and specific about where and when each fire began, and because those two facts, the room and the hour, are exactly what a detection plan is built around.

East 55th Street, June 2026: the fire between the bedrooms

The fire on East 55th Street between Avenues K and L began on the second floor of a two-story house, and the homeowner found flames in the upstairs hallway after her son woke her. An upstairs hall is the place HPD's fifteen-foot rule usually puts the alarm in a small house, because it sits outside every bedroom door at once. That is the right place for a smoke alarm. The lesson is what happens when the fire starts in that same hall: the alarm there should be the first device to see it, so it has to be working, inside its date, and not disabled after a cooking false alarm. A second alarm inside each bedroom, linked to the hall, gives the people behind closed doors the same warning at the same moment.

The timeline in that report is also worth reading closely. The FDNY's call came at 4:02 a.m. and the fire was under control by 4:37 a.m. Thirty-five minutes is fast work by the fire department, and the crews kept the fire from spreading through the house. But every minute before 4:02 belonged to the family. A detection plan exists to make the call come earlier and the waking up happen sooner.

January 2025: the fire above the ceiling

News 12 Brooklyn reported that the January 22, 2025 Flatlands house fire began in the attic, just before 1 a.m., and that close to 150 firefighters needed about three hours to put it out, with at least five people hurt. Attics in Flatlands houses range from open storage under the rafters to finished bedrooms. An unheated attic is a poor place for an ordinary smoke alarm, because cold, heat and dust push it outside its listed operating range; a heat detector rated for the space is the usual choice there, linked to the alarms below. A finished attic bedroom needs a smoke alarm placed by the manufacturer's rules for sloped ceilings. Either way, the alarm at the attic hatch or stair door matters, because a fire up there spreads above the bedrooms before anyone below can see it.

East 37th Street, December 2024: the fire in the basement at 3 a.m.

The basement fire near Avenue I and Brooklyn Avenue happened at about 3 a.m. and left one person critically injured, according to News 12. We do not know whether anyone was sleeping in that basement. In many Flatlands houses someone is, in a finished room or a rented apartment, and in others the basement holds only the boiler and the laundry. The plan differs for each. A basement bedroom gets its own smoke and CO alarms within fifteen feet of its door and, in our view, a link to the floors above. A basement with nobody in it still gets a smoke alarm at the top of its stair and CO near the fuel-burning equipment, because a fire in the cellar is a fire under every bedroom in the house.

East 49th Street, July 2018: smoke, not flame

Bklyner reported that the owner of 1666 East 49th Street died of smoke inhalation after the fire there in the early hours of July 2, 2018. Smoke is what kills in most house fires, and it moves faster through a stairwell than flame does. That is the strongest argument for photoelectric smoke alarms in halls and bedrooms, for at least one alarm on every level, and for linking them so smoke that reaches the first alarm sounds every alarm. The FDNY's own guidance puts it plainly: having a working smoke alarm "may cut the chances of dying in half, in most fires."

January 1, 2027

The Natural Gas Detector Deadline in a Flatlands Two-Family: Which Rooms, Which Devices, Who Pays

Local Law 157 was passed in 2016 and its deadline has moved more than once. The Real Deal reported in June 2025 that the City Council unanimously postponed it from May 1, 2025 to at least January 1, 2027, citing backlogs at vendors, little competition among manufacturers, and a recall of 26,000 devices from the one battery model that met the placement rules at the time. HPD's detector page now lists the gas requirement as starting January 1, 2027. The Real Deal described the law as covering residential buildings except owner-occupied single-family homes. For a Flatlands owner, that usually means: a one-family you live in alone is outside it; a two-family with a rented floor, or any house you rent out, is inside it.

The Department of Buildings' FAQ answers the placement question. Where a fuel-gas appliance is in a dwelling, the alarm goes in the same room, at least three feet but not more than ten feet from the appliance, measured horizontally. Where an apartment sits directly above a gas appliance or gas piping, the alarm goes on the ceiling, or within twelve inches of it, in the room directly above. Devices must be listed to UL 1484 and installed to NFPA 715. In a Flatlands two-family with a gas range on each floor and a gas boiler in the cellar, that can mean a detector in each kitchen, one in the cellar near the boiler, and one in the first-floor room directly above the boiler if it is part of an apartment.

On power, the DOB says buildings with a certificate of occupancy issued before January 1, 2027 may use a monitored battery as the primary power source, while buildings certified after that date need AC power with a secondary source. Hardwired alarms must be installed by a New York City licensed electrical contractor with permits, while battery or plug-in alarms may be installed by others, including the owner. On cost, HPD lets the owner of a one- or two-family home ask the tenant to reimburse $25 per gas detector, with a year to pay. On the Brownstoner forum, a landlord preparing to rent out a home asked which detector to buy for the law; the replies stressed checking for UL 1484 on the box and noted that smoke and CO alarms do not count as gas detectors. Both points match the DOB's guidance, and both are the first things we check on a device someone has already bought.

Our advice for Flatlands owners is to combine visits. If the smoke and CO alarms in a rented two-family are near the end of their life, replacing them on the same visit as the gas detectors means one round of access to the tenant's apartment, one dated list and one set of notices instead of two.

Rules that apply to Flatlands business fire alarm work

  • Filed systems start with a design professional. The FDNY's fire alarm guide says the system is designed and filed by a New York State registered professional engineer or architect, with a TM-1 application and a valid certificate of occupancy or approved Schedule A, or a Department of Buildings Letter of No Objection where neither exists.
  • Who may install a filed system. The same guide says installers must be a New York City licensed electrician or a fire alarm vendor holding an FDNY Fire Alarm Company Certificate. Ask any company, us included, to show the credentials that apply to your job in writing before you sign.
  • Acceptance testing and monitoring come before the FDNY inspection. Systems are acceptance-tested under NFPA 72 as modified by Appendix Q of the Building Code, and the central station connection must be active before the inspection is scheduled.
  • Plan on months, not weeks. The FDNY guide puts the average from plan submission to final inspection at about 20 to 24 weeks, with the first plan review taking up to eight weeks.
  • Repairs and changes are different. Replacing detectors or panels with identical parts and no design change is treated as a repair. Moving or replacing a control panel, or changing the design, generally means new plans filed with the FDNY.
  • Wireless needs approval too. Wireless devices on a filed fire alarm system must be approved by the FDNY before installation, and unlicensed radio frequencies are not allowed, according to a New York engineering firm's summary of the rules.
  • Apartments above stores keep their own detector duties. Whatever the store has, HPD's smoke, CO and gas detector rules still apply to the apartments above it, and the building owner is responsible for them.
Your Questions Answered

Fire Alarm Installation in Flatlands: Questions Answered

These are the questions Flatlands owners and landlords bring us that turn on a specific rule or a specific kind of house. Each answer starts with the short version.

Old alarms, sign-offs and turnovers

The battery alarms in the 1940s house we just bought off Avenue L have no install date printed anywhere. Is there a deadline for alarms whose age nobody can prove?

Yes. When the city required sealed ten-year battery alarms, the Department of Buildings' bulletin on the change said that existing battery smoke alarms with unknown end-of-life dates had to be replaced within seven years. Any alarm with no readable date is long past that window today, so treat it as due. The same bulletin says alarms installed under the new rule must meet UL 217 and use a non-removable, non-replaceable battery that powers the alarm for at least ten years. Those rules apply to battery-powered alarms, not to alarms that run on the house wiring.

In practice, when we find undated alarms in a newly bought Flatlands house, we replace them all in one visit, write the install date and the replacement year on our device list, and in most cases add the alarms the house was missing: the top of the cellar stair, the upstairs hall, the attic hatch. If you are not sure whether an alarm is battery or wired, a photo of it with the cover twisted off tells us.

Our contractor's DOB job for finishing the cellar cannot close until someone signs a smoke and CO alarm affidavit. Who is allowed to sign it?

The Department of Buildings' affidavit of installation and testing for smoke and carbon monoxide alarms is signed by the owner, a registered design professional or a licensed electrician, who swears that the required alarms were installed and tested in accordance with the applicable codes. The form says it is completed at the time the project's required alarms are tested. For hard-wired installations it also needs the seal of a professional engineer, a registered architect or a licensed electrician.

So if the cellar job used sealed battery alarms, the owner can sign, as long as the alarms really were installed and tested to the code. If the drawings call for hardwired alarms, a P.E., R.A. or licensed electrician has to be involved. Either way, what we hand the signer is a device list with every alarm, its location, its type, its power source and the date and result of its test, so the person signing knows exactly what they are swearing to.

The CO alarm we put in our tenant's apartment stopped working after five months, and it was not the tenant's fault. How fast does it have to be replaced?

Within 30 days. HPD's detector page says that if a detector becomes inoperable within one year of installation because of a defect, not because of the occupant, the owner replaces it within 30 days. After the first year, or when a detector is stolen, removed or broken by the occupant during the tenancy, the duties shift: the tenant replaces what goes missing or gets broken while they live there, and the owner replaces devices at the end of their useful life.

If we installed it, a defective device in its first year is a warranty matter, and we replace it. If someone else installed it, we can replace and date it on a service visit. Either way, keep the dead unit until it is replaced and write down the date the tenant reported it.

The new tenant on our second floor moves in on the first of the month. What has to be checked or replaced between the old tenant leaving and the new one arriving?

HPD says the owner must replace detectors that are stolen, removed, missing or inoperable before a new tenant moves in, if the previous tenant did not replace them. So the turnover is the moment to check every smoke, CO and, from 2027, gas detector in the apartment: present, in date, not painted over, and working when tested. Anything missing or dead is the owner's job before the keys change hands.

It is also the cheapest moment to fix placement, because the apartment is empty. A turnover visit on our list is usually a short service call or part of the per-unit landlord rate, and it ends with a dated list for that apartment and a fresh HPD notice for the new adult occupant.

Where should we keep our alarm records so they survive if the house itself burns?

Outside the house. The device list, the install dates, the affidavit if there was one, and any warranty papers should live somewhere a fire cannot reach: a photo on your phone, an email to yourself, a copy with a relative. After a fire, an insurer, a fire marshal or a buyer years later may ask what was installed and when, and the copy taped inside the cellar door will be gone with everything else.

We send the record by email as well as on paper for exactly that reason.

Fuel, gas and the cellar

We are converting our oil burner to gas this winter. When does the house pick up the gas detector duty, and does the CO duty change?

The CO duty does not change, because an oil burner already counts as fuel-burning equipment and so does a gas boiler. The gas detector duty is new. HPD applies its natural gas detector rule where there is gas piping, and the Department of Buildings puts the alarm in the same room as the fuel-gas appliance, three to ten feet away horizontally. Once the gas line to the new boiler is in, a rented Flatlands two-family needs a gas detector near that boiler under the January 1, 2027 requirement, in addition to any already needed near the ranges.

The conversion is a good moment to plan. The cellar is already being worked on, the electrician is already there, and a hardwired gas alarm, which has to be installed by a licensed electrical contractor with permits, costs less to add now than later. If you prefer a battery unit, we can fit it once the gas work is finished and tested.

Yards and shops in the industrial zone

Cement dust settles on everything in the office at our concrete yard in the Flatlands/Fairfield zone. Which detector survives that?

Not an ordinary smoke alarm in the dustiest rooms. Fine dust settles inside a smoke chamber, makes it more sensitive over time, and ends in false alarms until someone pulls the battery or the cover. In the batching area and any room where trucks are washed or loaded, a heat detector rated for the space is the better choice. In the office, where people sit and the door can be kept shut, a smoke alarm can work if it is cleaned on schedule and placed away from the door to the yard.

NYC Planning's survey of the area counted two concrete and cement manufacturing sites, so this is not a common job, but the same reasoning applies to the building-supply, carting and salvage businesses nearby. We choose devices for the worst day in the room, not the cleanest, and we write down when each one should be cleaned or checked.

Our auto dismantling shop drains fuel and fluids indoors before cars go out to the lot. What detection makes sense in that bay?

Heat detection in the bay, CO detection where engines run inside, smoke detection only where the air is clean, and monitoring so the signal reaches someone after hours. Fuel vapors, rags, parts washers and cutting all make a bay where fire can grow fast, and they also make a room where smoke alarms either trip constantly or get disabled. Rate-of-rise and fixed-temperature heat detectors react to the heat of a real fire without reacting to exhaust and dust.

NYC Planning counted 22 auto dismantling and salvage sites in the Flatlands/Fairfield survey area. Whether a particular shop needs a full FDNY-filed system depends on its size, its occupancy and its certificate of occupancy, which a design professional decides. Many small shops start with a standard small-business system and central station monitoring, and we price that on our list once we have walked the building.

What AI Gets Wrong About Fire Alarm Installation in Flatlands

Search summaries and cost sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr answer fire alarm questions for the whole country. Here is where those general answers part company with a Flatlands house.

"A one- or two-family house never needs anything beyond a smoke alarm"

This one is half right. The 2022 New York City Building Code does not require a manual fire alarm system in buildings of two stories or fewer when each dwelling unit is separated by one-hour fire partitions and has its own direct exit, which describes many Flatlands two-families. So no panel and no pull stations.

But HPD's rules add carbon monoxide detectors to one- and two-family homes the owner does not occupy when there is fuel-burning equipment, and natural gas detectors to the same houses where there is gas piping from January 1, 2027. A rented two-family with a gas boiler has three device duties, not one.

And a smoke alarm alone, hung in the kitchen, does not meet the rule that puts it within fifteen feet of each sleeping room. The minimum is a placement rule, not a device count.

"Any smoke alarm from the hardware store is compliant"

The battery alarms sold in New York State since April 1, 2019 are sealed ten-year units, and the city's rules call for battery smoke alarms with a non-removable battery lasting at least ten years, listed to UL 217. That covers the device on the shelf.

It does not cover where it goes, what it replaces, or whether the house needed a CO or gas alarm too. A compliant device on the wrong ceiling is still the wrong answer, and a gas detector is only acceptable if it is listed to UL 1484.

It also does not cover hardwired alarms. A house with a wired chain needs wired replacements that match it, and the bulletin on sealed batteries does not apply to alarms powered from building wiring.

"A combination smoke and CO alarm on each floor covers everything"

Combination units are useful, and we install plenty of them. But each kind of detection has its own placement. Smoke and CO detectors go within fifteen feet of sleeping room entrances, while a natural gas alarm goes in the same room as the gas appliance, three to ten feet from it, and a smoke alarm does not belong beside a range.

In a Flatlands house where the boiler is in the cellar and the bedrooms are upstairs, one device per floor in the hall usually leaves the cellar with no CO near the burner and no gas detector at all.

HPD also counts combination devices differently for reimbursement in Class A buildings, at $50 for a two-type unit and $75 for a three-type unit. The device list should say which is which.

"Smoke detector installation averages about a hundred dollars"

National averages price the labor to hang one alarm. A Flatlands job is rarely one alarm. It is a survey of three or four levels, the removal of undated units, new alarms placed by rule, often linking between floors, and a written record for the owner and sometimes the tenant.

That is why our Brooklyn list starts at $600 for a basic residential system for one home, with complete protection for a house from cellar to top floor at $2,500 and custom hardwired systems at $2,000 to $6,000. The price covers the plan, the devices, the installation, the testing and the record.

"Wireless alarms are always the easy answer"

Sealed wireless interconnected alarms are often the right choice for a finished brick house where running cable would mean opening plaster. But radio has to get from the cellar to the attic through floors, masonry and sometimes a boiler room wall, and that is a thing to test, not assume.

We test every wireless link from the cellar alarm to the top-floor alarm before calling the job finished, and if a link is weak we say so and price a different route.

On a filed commercial system, wireless devices need FDNY approval before installation, and the rules on radio frequencies and battery supervision are strict. Wireless is a tool for the right building, not a shortcut.

"The FDNY has to approve any fire alarm work"

Not in a typical Flatlands house. Household smoke, CO and gas alarms are covered by HPD's and the Department of Buildings' rules, and a DOB affidavit may be needed when they are part of a DOB job. The FDNY's plan review and inspection process is for fire alarm systems that are required and designed by a registered design professional.

For a store or yard that does need a filed system, the FDNY's own guide puts the average from plan submission to final approval at about 20 to 24 weeks, and the central station connection has to be live before the inspection.

FAQ

Flatlands Fire Alarm Installation FAQ

If the monitored alarm in our Flatlands store goes off at night, does the signal go straight to the firehouse on East 48th Street?
+

No. A monitored alarm reports to a central station, and the central station's operators contact the fire department's dispatch, which sends the units assigned to your address. Engine 309 and Ladder 159 on East 48th Street are often among them for Flatlands addresses, but the firehouse does not receive your signal directly. That is why the central station's file needs the right address, the right entrance and a current contact list.

Our engineer's paperwork for a new store system mentions an S-87. What is that?
+

It is an FDNY Certificate of Fitness for the professional certification of fire alarm system installations. The FDNY lists New York State licensed engineers and architects, NICET Level III or IV holders, and New York City Master or Special Electricians among those who can hold it. It belongs to the individual, not to a company, and it is used anywhere in the city. It has nothing to do with household smoke alarms in a house.

What is the difference between a smoke alarm and a smoke detector?
+

A smoke alarm senses smoke and sounds by itself, which is what most Flatlands houses have. A smoke detector is a sensor connected to a building's fire alarm system, and the FDNY notes that such detectors are not stand-alone units and may not sound on their own. Turning a system detector into a single-station alarm without that connection can lead to fire department enforcement, so the two should never be swapped casually.

Is a heat detector better than a smoke alarm next to the kitchen in our two-family?
+

Near the stove, often yes. Smoke alarms placed close to a kitchen trip on ordinary cooking, and an alarm that trips often gets its battery pulled. A heat detector beside the kitchen ignores frying smoke but reacts to the heat of a real fire. It does not replace the smoke alarm the rules require within fifteen feet of each bedroom, which still goes in the hall outside the sleeping rooms.

Is Flatlands on the same price list as the rest of Brooklyn, or is there a travel charge?
+

The same list. Every Flatlands block, from Nostrand Avenue to Ralph Avenue, is billed on our standard Brooklyn rate with no travel charge. The price changes with the house, the number of devices and how cable has to run, not with the street. Our Brooklyn office is at 1282 Troy Ave.

The FDNY checklist for our new shop's fire alarm filing mentions a TB-60. Where does that fit?
+

It belongs to the monitoring side of the filing. The FDNY's fire alarm guide asks for a copy of the monitoring agreement and an executed TB-60 application with the filing, and it requires the central station connection to be active before the inspection is scheduled. Your design professional assembles the filing; we make sure the monitoring account is set up in time.

If the power goes out on our block, do hardwired smoke alarms still work?
+

Hardwired household alarms carry a backup battery, sealed or replaceable depending on the model, so they keep sensing and sounding during an outage. Wireless linked sealed alarms run on their own batteries anyway. What can stop during an outage is a monitoring communicator without its own backup, which is why we check the battery on any panel or communicator we install.

We rent the upstairs of our two-family. Do we have to post a carbon monoxide notice even though the house has no lobby?
+

HPD's detector page says Class A multiple dwellings and one- and two-family homes must post the CO and natural gas notices, while only Class A buildings must post the smoke notice, and a combined notice may be used. In a two-family without a lobby, the notice goes in a visible spot by the entrance the tenant uses. We leave the notice with the installation when the rules call for it.

The panel in our Flatbush Avenue store still works but its parts are hard to find. What does replacing it on the existing wiring cost?
+

On our Brooklyn list, an alarm panel replacement on existing wiring is $800 to $1,500, provided the cable tests good. If the store's system was filed with the FDNY, replacing or moving the control panel generally means new plans, which a design professional handles and which is priced separately. We test the wiring and check the system's paperwork before quoting.

Our smoke alarms are due for replacement next year. Should the gas detectors go in on the same visit?
+

Usually yes, if the house needs gas detectors. One visit means one round of access to the tenant's apartment, one dated list and one set of notices. Replacing smoke alarms a little early to line them up with the gas detectors also means every device in the house reaches its replacement year together, which makes the next round simpler.

Store fire alarm paperwork keeps mentioning NFPA 72 and Appendix Q. What are they?
+

NFPA 72 is the national fire alarm and signaling code, and Appendix Q of the New York City Building Code is the city's set of changes to it. The FDNY requires filed systems to be inspected and acceptance-tested under NFPA 72 as modified by Appendix Q. For a household with sealed alarms they do not come up; for a filed store system they set the testing standard.
