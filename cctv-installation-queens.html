The Neighborhood

Why Queens Properties Need CCTV Installation

Queens is the borough of driveways. Walk almost any side street in Bayside, Middle Village, Hollis, Howard Beach or Whitestone and you see what Manhattan almost never has: a curb cut, a strip of concrete, a car parked a few feet from the front door, and a detached garage or a fenced yard behind it. That changes what a CCTV system is for. In a borough of walk-ups the camera mostly watches a hallway. In most of Queens it watches a car, a side gate, a back door that opens onto a yard, and the sidewalk where a stranger decides whether the house is worth a second look.

The housing numbers back that up. The U.S. Census Bureau's American Community Survey 2019–2023 estimates for Queens County count 903,598 housing units. Of those, 19.1 percent are one-family detached houses (172,141 homes), 9.3 percent are one-family attached houses (83,924), and another 19.4 percent sit in two-unit buildings. Put together, almost half of all Queens homes are in one- and two-family houses, and 44.9 percent of occupied homes are owner-occupied. Those are owners who decide for themselves where a camera goes, who pay for it themselves, and who usually want it to cover the driveway first.

The age of those houses matters on the install. The same survey shows 30.3 percent of Queens housing was built in 1939 or earlier, 14.1 percent in the 1940s and 19.7 percent in the 1950s, which puts close to two-thirds of the borough's homes before 1960. That is the era of brick-faced attached rows, stucco and frame two-families, Tudor-style detached houses and the post-war garden apartment complexes. Under the siding you find plaster on wood lath, balloon-framed walls with open bays from cellar to attic, and cellars with low joists where the boiler, the meters and now the recorder all share space. A good CCTV installer reads those walls before deciding where a cable can run.

The theft picture in Queens has its own shape too. The NYPD reports CompStat for two patrol boroughs here, Queens North and Queens South. Through November 8, 2026, Queens North recorded 1,099 burglaries, down 33.1 percent from 1,642 a year earlier, but 2,471 grand larcenies of autos, up 12.2 percent from 2,203. Queens South logged 1,465 vehicle thefts, down 12.7 percent, alongside 819 burglaries. Across both patrol boroughs that is 3,936 stolen vehicles in ten months, compared with 1,918 burglaries. In Queens the car in the driveway is a bigger target than the house behind it, and a CCTV plan that ignores the driveway is solving the wrong problem.

Prosecutors here have said the same thing in plain words. When the Queens District Attorney's office announced the Operation Hellcat indictments in May 2025, a ring charged with stealing 126 cars, 52 of them in Queens, District Attorney Melinda Katz said: “With these indictments, we have dismantled a criminal enterprise that stole at least $4.6 million worth of vehicles from our streets and driveways.” (Queens District Attorney, May 8, 2025). The release describes thieves breaking a door lock with a screwdriver and programming a new key fob through the car's diagnostic port. A camera does not stop that by itself. What it does is put a face, a time, a direction of travel and often a plate on the recorder, and that is what turns a report into a case.

North Queens, South Queens and the waterfront

The borough is too big for one pattern. In the northwest, Astoria, Long Island City, Sunnyside and Woodside mix attached brick rows, six-story walk-ups, new glass towers along the East River and the industrial blocks between the Sunnyside rail yards and Newtown Creek. Jobs there look like city jobs: shared walls, narrow lots, cellars, and owners who need a camera on the front door, the areaway and the back yard. Across northeast Queens, Bayside, Douglaston, Little Neck, Whitestone and Fresh Meadows lean toward detached houses on wider lots, many with a garage at the end of a long driveway, and garden co-ops with shared courtyards and parking bays.

Central Queens, from Forest Hills and Rego Park to Kew Gardens and Briarwood, adds tall pre-war and post-war co-ops along Queens Boulevard, where cameras are a board decision and the job looks more like a Manhattan elevator building. Southeast Queens, in Jamaica, St. Albans, Hollis, Cambria Heights, Laurelton and Springfield Gardens, is mostly detached and semi-detached houses with driveways, plus the warehouse and freight-forwarding blocks around JFK. And the Rockaways are their own world: beach bungalows, newer town houses, ocean wind, salt air and sand, which decide which cameras and housings survive a winter.

Closed circuit television, explained for a Queens homeowner

Closed circuit television installation means the video never leaves your property unless you ask it to. Each camera sends its picture down its own cable to the recorder, and the recorder writes it to a hard drive in your cellar or closet. That is different from the plug-in cameras sold in big-box stores, which usually send clips over Wi-Fi to a company server and charge a monthly fee to keep them. A CCTV setup can still send alerts to your phone and let you watch live from work or from a trip to see family overseas, but the recording itself does not depend on your internet staying up.

There are two families of CCTV systems we put in. HD analog systems use HD-TVI, HD-CVI or AHD cameras on coax cable to a digital video recorder. They are a good fit when a house or store already has coax in the walls from an older system. IP systems use network cameras on Cat6 cable to a network video recorder, with power delivered over the same cable (PoE). They are our default for new work because one cable does everything, resolution goes up to 4K and beyond, and adding a camera later is simple. Wireless CCTV installation is possible too, but every wireless camera still needs power, and Queens brick and stucco cut Wi-Fi signal hard.

What the job includes, start to finish

CCTV installation and setup on our crews always covers the same ground. We plan each view on the survey. We run and label the cable, choosing the path that keeps it out of sight on the front of the house. We mount the camera on the right base for the surface, whether that is aluminum siding, stucco, face brick or a soffit. Then comes the part most cheap installs skip: CCTV installation and configuration of the recorder, which covers recording schedules, motion and smart detection zones that ignore the street, night settings, the correct time zone and daylight saving, drive formatting, user accounts and strong passwords. We finish with remote viewing on your phone and a short lesson on finding and exporting a clip.

After the install, CCTV installation and maintenance go together. Lenses near a busy avenue collect grime, spiders love an infrared camera under an eave, and a hard drive in a warm boiler room wears out sooner than one in a cool closet. We offer maintenance visits, repairs and replacements, and we take service calls on systems we did not install once we know what equipment is on the wall.

The moments Queens owners call us

Most calls arrive at a few predictable points. The morning after a car disappears from the driveway, when the owner learns the old camera faced the street and caught only a blur. After a package theft from the porch or the vestibule of a two-family. When a house is bought and the new owner inherits a recorder nobody has the password for. Before moving in, when the walls are open for painting and running cable costs the least. When a store is getting ready for the holiday shopping season and the Black Friday rush. Each one leads to a different first camera, which is why we ask what happened before we talk about equipment.

Queens has more kinds of houses than any other borough, and each one hides the cable in a different place. These are the building types our crews see every week, and what each one does to a CCTV plan.

Detached houses

Frame or brick houses with a driveway down one side and a garage behind. The attic and cellar give clean cable paths, so most cameras end up under the eaves or soffits with no wire showing on the facade.

Middle Village, Ridgewood, Glendale and Jackson Heights rows share side walls and often a common driveway behind the houses. Cameras go on the front and the rear walls only, and the rear view has to respect the neighbors' half of the drive.

🏘️

Two-family houses

Two meters, two front doors, often a separate basement unit. The recorder belongs to the owner, and we split viewing rights so each household sees only the shared entrances, the driveway and its own door.

🌳

Garden co-ops

Two- and three-story brick buildings around lawns and courtyards, with parking bays and laundry rooms spread across a campus. Cable crosses between buildings underground, in conduit on the exterior, or by fiber, and the board approves every camera.

🏢

Elevator co-ops and rentals

Six- to twenty-story buildings along Queens Boulevard, in Kew Gardens, Rego Park, Forest Hills and Jackson Heights, with a lobby, a package room, a garage ramp and a super who holds the keys.

🏖️

Rockaway bungalows and beach houses

Small frame houses and newer town houses near the boardwalk, where salt air corrodes cheap mounts and sand gets into every connector. Weatherproof housings and sealed junction boxes are not optional here.

For larger multi-unit properties there is more on our apartment building security camera installation in Queens page, which covers lobbies, package rooms and management access in detail. The notes below are the parts of a CCTV job that change with the building type.

Balloon frames, plaster and lath

Many Queens frame houses from the 1920s and 1930s were built with balloon framing: wall studs that run from the sill in the cellar to the roof plate with no break at each floor. For a CCTV contractor that is good news. A cable can often be dropped from the attic straight down an exterior wall bay and pulled out in the cellar, which lets us put a camera under the front eave and land the cable at the recorder with no visible wire. Platform-framed houses from the post-war years have a top plate at every floor, so the same run means drilling through plates from the attic or using a closet as a chase. Plaster on wood lath cracks if it is cut carelessly, so we use small access holes, patch them, and tell you before we start where any patching will be.

Brick fronts, stucco and aluminum siding

The facade decides the mount. On face brick we fasten into solid brick or mortar joints with sleeve anchors and a junction box, so the cable connection sits inside a sealed box rather than hanging in the weather. On stucco we seal every penetration, because water behind stucco is a slow, expensive problem. On aluminum and vinyl siding we use a mounting block so the camera sits on a flat, solid base and the siding is not crushed. And on houses with a brick first floor and siding above, which is common in Flushing, Fresh Meadows and Bayside, we usually mount on the soffit, where the cable comes straight down from the attic.

Keeping the recorder above the high-water line

In a Queens house the recorder almost always ends up in the cellar. It is central to the cable paths, it is out of sight, and a burglar who grabs the box by the TV upstairs leaves the evidence behind. Cellars here also flood. Southeast Queens and the low blocks near Jamaica Bay have had real basement flooding in heavy rain, and plenty of houses in every neighborhood see water at the foundation in a bad storm. So we mount the recorder on a shelf or a wall bracket well above the floor, away from the boiler and the water heater, with a battery backup under it, and we keep the drive's location out of the path of any pipe that has ever dripped. In a two-family, the recorder sits in the owner's part of the cellar, behind a lock if the tenant shares the space.

Shared driveways and common walls

A lot of attached and semi-detached houses in Queens share a driveway down the middle, each owner holding half of it or an easement over the other's half. A camera on your wall that looks down the shared drive is common and usually reasonable, but it records your neighbor coming and going too. We frame driveway cameras on your car, your door and the approach from the street, rather than on the neighbor's side door or yard, and we can mask parts of the image in the recorder so a window or a neighbor's back door is blacked out. The same applies to cameras that look over a low fence into the next yard. Talking to the neighbor before installation solves most of these issues before they start.

Garden co-ops: campuses, not buildings

Post-war garden co-ops are spread across many low buildings with lawns, paths and parking courts between them. A CCTV plan for one of these complexes looks more like a small college campus than an apartment house. We usually put a recorder in a central management office or boiler room, run PoE switches in the basements of outlying buildings, and connect the buildings with fiber or with Cat6 in conduit where the run is short enough. Exterior cameras cover building entrances, walkways, the parking courts and the laundry rooms, where most incidents and complaints happen. The board approves the camera list and the policy for who can view and export footage before we pull any cable.

Historic districts in Queens

Thirteen Queens neighborhoods or blocks are designated historic districts by the Landmarks Preservation Commission, among them Jackson Heights, Sunnyside Gardens, Addisleigh Park, the two Cambria Heights districts, Central Ridgewood, Ridgewood North and South, Douglaston, Douglaston Hill, Fort Totten, Hunters Point and Stockholm Street. Work on the street-facing parts of buildings in those districts generally needs an LPC permit. For cameras, the commission's permit guidance favors small, discreet cameras placed at mortar joints or plain surfaces, with conduit kept out of sight. We check whether an address is inside a district during the survey, design the front-facade cameras to the commission's guidance from the start, and supply the camera cut sheets for the application. Rear and side cameras are usually simpler, and that is often where the driveway view is anyway.

Basement apartments and the tenant question

Many Queens homeowners rent a basement or ground-floor unit. A camera on the shared side door or the driveway is a normal part of protecting the house, but it records the tenant's comings and goings as well. We keep cameras out of the tenant's living space, point them at shared approaches rather than into a unit's windows, and suggest the owner tell the tenant in writing which areas are recorded. Giving the tenant a view-only login to the shared door camera is an easy way to make the system feel like it protects both households, which in practice it does. This is general information, not legal advice; your lease and local rules control.

High-rise co-ops along Queens Boulevard

Building CCTV installation in an elevator co-op is mostly about the risers and the board. These buildings usually have a telephone or cable-TV riser, a lobby desk or a super's office, a garage ramp and a package room that did not exist when the building opened. Where the board allows it, we pull Cat6 up an existing riser to a PoE switch every few floors and bring the whole building onto one NVR, with separate accounts for the managing agent, the super and the lobby staff. Elevator cab cameras need the elevator maintenance company, because the signal has to travel in the cab's traveling cable or over a wireless bridge the elevator company approves.

Commercial CCTV

Business CCTV Installation in Queens

Commercial CCTV installation in Queens splits into two very different worlds. On the shopping streets it is small-business work: bakeries, pharmacies, phone stores, restaurants, money transfer counters, beauty supply shops and family groceries, many run by owners who work the register themselves. In the industrial belts it is freight, trucks and fences: warehouses, distribution buildings, air cargo forwarders, contractors' yards and auto shops. Commercial CCTV for stores, restaurants, offices and multi-story buildings in Queens runs $1,500–$5,000+, depending on the camera count, the recorder, how much cable has to be hidden and how many sites share one app. Our commercial security camera installation in Queens page goes deeper on retail and office layouts.

Corridors where we install business CCTV

  • Main Street and Roosevelt Avenue in downtown Flushing: dense blocks of restaurants, bakeries, malls-within-buildings and upstairs offices, with signs in Chinese and Korean and very heavy foot traffic.
  • Roosevelt Avenue under the 7 train, Jackson Heights to Corona: jewelry stores, money transfer shops, restaurants and phone stores under the elevated tracks, where the steel structure and passing trains shape every camera angle.
  • Jamaica Avenue and 165th Street: apparel, sneaker and discount stores, plus the busy blocks around the Jamaica Center and Parsons/Archer transit hubs.
  • Steinway Street and 30th Avenue in Astoria: cafes, restaurants, hookah lounges, salons and small groceries, many in two- and three-story buildings with apartments above.
  • Liberty Avenue in Richmond Hill and Ozone Park: groceries, sari and jewelry shops, roti shops and money transfer counters under the elevated A train.
  • Northern Boulevard: car dealerships, auto repair and tire shops, gas stations and strip-front stores, with lots where plate-readable entrance views matter as much as the showroom.

Multilingual storefronts and family-run shops

Business CCTV installation on the shopping streets of Queens usually means talking to an owner, a spouse and an adult child, often in more than one language, and setting up a system all three can use. We keep the design simple: a camera over the door at face height, one on the register from above and behind the counter, domes down each aisle, one at the back door or delivery door, and one or two on the sidewalk from under the awning. The recorder goes in the back office or the cellar, not under the counter. Each family member gets a separate login on the app, with only the owner allowed to delete footage or change settings. When the staff changes, the owner removes one account instead of changing the password everyone knows.

Restaurants, bakeries and supermarkets

Restaurant CCTV installation in Queens has to deal with grease, steam and long hours. Kitchen cameras go in sealed housings away from the fryers, cable in the kitchen runs in conduit, and the cameras we point at the dining room avoid shining into the eyes of customers at window tables. The views that matter most are the register, the host stand or counter, the back door where deliveries and garbage go out, and the walk-in or storage room. Supermarket and grocery store CCTV installation adds aisle coverage, the produce racks out front, the beer cooler, and the loading area where pallets arrive in the early morning. A bakery that opens at 5 a.m. usually wants the door and the sidewalk covered before anything else, because that is when the street is emptiest.

Auto repair shops, dealerships and gas stations

Auto repair shop CCTV installation is one of the most common commercial jobs in the borough. Shops along Northern Boulevard, in the blocks of Long Island City and Maspeth, and on Rockaway Boulevard keep customers' cars overnight, sometimes on the street or in an open lot. The priorities are the lot and the gate at night, the bay doors, the key board or key cabinet, and the counter where customers drop off and pay. Car dealership CCTV installation adds the showroom floor, the service drive and long rows of inventory, where vehicle detection keeps the recorder from flagging every swaying tree. Gas station CCTV installation covers the pumps, the kiosk window and the register, with a plate view at each driveway in and out of the station.

Warehouses, freight forwarders and the JFK cargo belt

Warehouse CCTV installation in Queens is shaped by two industrial zones. In the west, Long Island City, Maspeth and Ridgewood's industrial edge hold distribution buildings, food wholesalers, printers, contractors' yards and storage businesses along Newtown Creek and the rail lines. In the south, the blocks around JFK Airport in Springfield Gardens, Jamaica and Rosedale hold air cargo forwarders and logistics facilities where trucks queue for pickups around the clock. Our warehouse security camera installation in Queens page covers high-ceiling and dock layouts in more depth.

Air cargo is a reminder of why the paperwork side of a pickup matters as much as the door. In October 2020, Queens District Attorney Melinda Katz and the Port Authority Police announced charges against a crew accused of using forged documents to collect about $6 million in designer goods from an air cargo importer at JFK. “The safety and security of this county's airports are a top priority,” Katz said (WABC-TV, October 29, 2020). According to the charges, one trailer from the heist was later found abandoned on 56th Road in Maspeth, and surveillance video showed defendants going in and out of a closed storefront in Jamaica used to store the goods. For a freight business, that is the case for a camera at the release counter that records the driver's face and the paperwork, a plate camera at the yard gate, and dock cameras that show which pallets left in which truck.

Distribution center CCTV installation follows a set pattern. Every dock door gets a camera inside, looking out at the trailer and the dock plate, so a loading clip shows the pallet and the trailer number in the same frame. The truck yard gets bullet or varifocal cameras with long infrared range, often on light poles or the building corners, with a plate camera at the gate. The perimeter fence gets cameras with smart detection so a person along the fence at 3 a.m. triggers an alert and a raccoon does not. Long runs across a yard go over fiber or with PoE extenders, because Cat6 tops out around 100 meters, and the recorder sits in a locked office on a battery backup.

Self storage, construction sites and parking lots

Self storage CCTV installation covers the gate keypad, the office, every corridor of units and the loading area, with cameras that read the plate and the face at the keypad. Construction site CCTV installation is temporary by design: a recorder in a locked job box, cameras on poles or on the hoist, temporary power, and a plan to move the cameras as the building goes up. Parking lot CCTV installation and parking garage CCTV installation start at the entrance lane, with one tight camera on the plate and one wider camera on the car and the driver, then overview cameras along the rows where break-ins happen.

Field Notes

What our crew checks first on a Queens driveway survey. We stand where the car is parked at night and look back at the house. The camera has to see the driver's door and the face of anyone standing at it, so it goes on the wall or soffit closest to that door at roughly eight to ten feet, not on the far corner of the garage. We check where the streetlight sits, because a light behind the car turns a face into a silhouette, and we note the neighbor's motion floodlight if it points across the drive.

Then we follow the cable path back to the cellar: attic or wall bay, soffit or brick, and whether the run passes a gutter that overflows in a storm. We find the spot for the recorder above the high-water line and near an outlet for the battery backup. Last, we ask the owner how they want alerts. Most say "only when someone walks up the driveway," so the detection zone stops at the curb cut and the street stays out of the alerts. All of that goes into the written scope before anyone drills.

Offices, medical suites, pharmacies and banks

Office CCTV installation in the borough runs from a two-room accounting office above a Flushing storefront to a full floor in a Long Island City building. Cameras cover the suite entrance, reception, the server or IT closet and sometimes a supply room. Medical office and dental office CCTV installation keeps cameras out of exam and treatment rooms and away from screens showing patient information, and points them at the entrance, waiting room and front desk. Compliance callouts for Queens businesses

  • Restrooms, locker rooms and changing rooms are off limits. NY Labor Law §203-c prohibits employers from video recording employees in those rooms. A camera aimed at the hallway door is fine; one inside is not.
  • Microphones need a decision, not a default. NY Penal Law §250.05 makes recording a conversation without at least one participant's consent a crime. We set audio-capable cameras with audio switched off unless the owner chooses otherwise after getting advice.
  • Face matching triggers a posting duty. A shop, restaurant or venue in the city that turns on face-matching or any other biometric collection has to post a notice where customers enter, and cannot sell or trade the data, under section 22-1202 of the NYC Administrative Code. Our installs leave those features off.
  • Private spaces stay private. NY Penal Law §250.45 covers recording people where they have a reasonable expectation of privacy. Upstairs apartments, changing areas and neighbors' windows are not part of any view we set.

This list is general information, not legal advice. It is what we go over with every Queens business owner before the cameras go up, so the system protects the business without creating a new problem.

Which camera angle actually catches a face or a plate in a Queens driveway at night?

Can one recorder cover both units of a two-family house, with a separate login for the tenant?

Yes, and it is the setup we recommend. One NVR in the owner's part of the cellar records every camera, so there is one drive, one battery backup and one system to maintain. Who decides where cameras go on a driveway shared by two attached houses?

Each owner controls cameras on their own wall, but a shared driveway is a shared space, and a camera that records the whole drive records the neighbor too. The practical answer is to frame your camera on your car, your door and your half of the approach, and to use privacy masking in the recorder to black out the neighbor's door or windows. Can a garden co-op put cameras on courtyards, paths and parking bays between its buildings?

Yes. Recording storage, compression and how long footage lasts

CCTV recording storage requirements come from three numbers: how many cameras, how much data each one sends, and how many days you want to keep. A 4MP camera on H.265 compression typically sends a few megabits per second; an 8MP camera roughly twice that. H.265 stores about the same picture in roughly half the space of the older H.264, so we set it on every camera that supports it. Our Queens packages include two weeks of recording, and many homeowners ask for 30 days so they can find a clip after coming back from a long trip. We work out the drive size from your camera list, then test it on the last day.

Continuous recording suits stores and warehouses, where the important moment often looks ordinary when it happens. Motion or smart-event recording stretches a drive much further on a quiet house, where hours pass with nothing but parked cars. Most of our residential systems record continuously on the driveway and front door cameras and on events only in the back yard, which balances storage with evidence.

Cable runs, PoE budget and the detached garage

The cable is the part of the system you will least want to replace. We use Cat6 for IP cameras because it carries video and PoE power up to about 100 meters and is easy to terminate and test. Detached garages and outbuildings get outdoor-rated, direct-burial cable in conduit, or fiber when the run is long or the garage has its own electric service, which avoids ground problems between buildings. On the recorder side, every PoE switch has a total power budget; infrared cameras draw more at night and active-deterrence or PTZ cameras much more, so we add up the load and leave headroom.

PTZ camera installation suits a large lot, a garden co-op parking court or a warehouse yard, where an operator or a preset tour can follow a car across a big space. What AI Gets Wrong About CCTV Installation in Queens

"Queens is suburban, so a video doorbell covers the house"

AI summaries tend to sort Queens with the suburbs and suggest that a doorbell camera and maybe one floodlight camera is enough for a house. A doorbell sees the person ringing the bell. It does not see the car at the end of the driveway, the side gate, the alley between houses or the back door, which is where most break-ins and car thefts in the borough actually happen.

Queens houses are close together, with narrow side yards, shared driveways and rear yards that open onto alleys or other yards. A thief can reach the back door of many attached and semi-detached houses without ever passing the front steps.

A doorbell is a fine piece of a system. The rest of the plan is a driveway camera framed on the car, one on the side gate or alley, and one on the back door and yard, all recording to a recorder in the cellar.

"Crime is down, so cameras matter less than they used to"

Some AI answers point to falling burglary numbers and suggest home cameras are less urgent. The burglary part is true: Queens North burglaries are down 33.1 percent this year in the NYPD's CompStat report through November 8, 2026.

The same report shows grand larceny of autos in Queens North up 12.2 percent, to 2,471 vehicles, and the two Queens patrol boroughs together reporting 3,936 stolen vehicles in ten months, about twice their burglary count. The risk has moved from the house to the driveway, not away.

So the CCTV question in Queens has shifted. It is less about the front door and more about what happens around the car at 3 a.m., which is why we lead with driveway and curb-cut views.

"Any 4K camera can read license plates"

This is one of the most common claims in AI answers about cameras. Resolution helps, but plates are small, reflective and usually moving. A wide camera on a garage corner sees the whole driveway in 4K and still turns a moving plate at night into a white smear.

Plate capture needs a tight field of view on the spot where the car slows, a fast shutter, the right angle (not too steep, not too far off to the side) and infrared that does not flare off the plate's reflective coating. That usually means a second, dedicated camera.

On Queens driveways we mount that camera low and close to the curb cut. At auto shops, lots and warehouse gates, we use LPR cameras set up for the lane after we watch how cars actually come in.

"Battery cameras are easiest for a garden co-op's grounds"

AI suggestions often favor battery and solar cameras for large outdoor areas because there is no wiring. On a garden co-op campus that sounds attractive, with dozens of entrances and paths across several buildings.

In practice, batteries in a Queens winter drain quickly, Wi-Fi signal does not cross brick buildings and courtyards reliably, and battery cameras usually record short clips on motion rather than continuous video. A board that needs footage of a car break-in in the parking court often finds the clip started too late or never uploaded.

Wired cameras on PoE switches in each building, linked by fiber or Cat6 to one recorder, cost more on day one and far less over the life of the system. The board also gets continuous recording and one place to pull footage.

"Power outages are rare in the city, so battery backup is optional"

AI answers often treat a UPS as an extra for rural homes. Queens has its own history here. In July 2006, failed feeder cables in Con Edison's Long Island City network left parts of Astoria, Long Island City, Sunnyside and Woodside without power, affecting about 174,000 people, with many still in the dark a week later during a heat wave.

Short outages and brownouts are far more common than a week-long blackout, and they do their own damage: a recorder that loses power mid-write can corrupt its drive, and cameras that reboot leave gaps in exactly the minutes when a dark street invites trouble.

We put a battery backup on the recorder and the PoE switch on almost every job. It keeps recording through short outages and lets the system shut down cleanly in a long one.

"Warehouse cameras only need to cover the doors"

Generic advice for warehouses focuses on entrances. In the freight blocks around JFK and in Maspeth, losses often happen in the yard and at the paperwork counter rather than through a forced door.

The JFK case described above, where prosecutors say forged release papers were used to drive away with designer goods, is the kind of loss a door camera alone cannot explain. Trailers parked overnight in the yard, pallets staged outside a dock door and a fence along a dark side street are just as exposed.

So a warehouse plan covers the release window, every dock door from the inside, the yard, the gate and the fence line, and records it all continuously.

"Any handyman can mount a few cameras in an afternoon"

AI cost guides often suggest CCTV is a quick handyman job. Mounting a camera is the easy part. Getting a cable from a soffit to a cellar through a balloon-framed wall, or across a garden co-op courtyard, or into a historic-district facade without breaking the rules, is where the work and the skill are.

Recorder setup takes just as much care. Detection zones that ignore the street, retention that actually reaches two weeks, the correct time for police reports, separate logins for a tenant, and a recorder that will not be reachable by strangers online are all part of the job.

That is why it pays to hire a licensed CCTV installation contractor: insured, licensed in New York State, and willing to put the camera list, cable paths and price in writing before drilling.

FAQ

Queens CCTV Installation FAQ

Can the camera over my store door also count how many customers come in?
+

Yes. People counting cameras, or counting features on some AI cameras, tally the people crossing a line drawn across the doorway and report totals by hour or day. They work best mounted directly above or just inside the door, looking down. We confirm which cameras in your system support counting before quoting it.

Can you mount a camera on the utility pole or the street tree in front of my house?
+

No. Utility poles belong to the utility companies and street trees and lamp posts belong to the city, so we mount cameras only on your own building, fence, garage or a pole set on your property. A camera under your soffit or porch roof usually sees the curb and the street just as well.

Will my CCTV system work with Verizon Fios or Spectrum internet in Queens?
+

Yes. The recorder connects to your router with a network cable and works with either provider. Recording does not use the internet at all; only remote viewing does. A faster upload speed makes live view smoother when several people watch at once, and we check your speed during setup.

Do you install license plate cameras for Queens auto shops, lots and warehouse gates?
+

Yes. Plate cameras need a narrow lane, the right angle and a fast shutter, so we quote them after we see how vehicles approach the gate or curb cut. Most installs pair one plate camera with a wider camera that shows the vehicle and driver, so the clip shows both the plate and who was behind the wheel.

Can you put cameras inside the elevators of a Queens co-op or rental building?
+

Yes, working with the building's elevator maintenance company. The camera's signal has to travel down the cab's traveling cable or over a wireless bridge, and that work is coordinated with the elevator contractor. We handle the camera, the recorder and the rest of the building's CCTV, and schedule the cab work with the elevator company and the managing agent.

I mounted my own cameras. Can you set up only the recorder and the phone app?
+

Yes. Recorder and app setup on customer equipment is a labor-only job quoted by phone or text from your equipment list and photos, then booked with the deposit. We set recording, detection zones, time, users and remote access. If a camera you mounted turns out to be aimed or wired poorly, we will tell you and quote fixing it.

What paperwork do I get when the CCTV job is finished?
+

You get a camera map showing each camera's location, name and cable label, the recorder's model and drive size, the retention we set in days, the admin account details handed to you in person, and the service agreement with the warranty terms. Keep the map with the recorder; it saves time on any future service call.

Can a tenant in a Queens house or building ask to see footage from the landlord's cameras?
+

The landlord owns the system and decides who views it, but many owners give tenants a view-only login to shared doors and driveways, or export a clip when a tenant reports a theft or a package problem. We can set up limited tenant accounts that cannot delete or export anything. This is general information, not legal advice.

Do you install CCTV for churches, mosques, temples and community centers in Queens?
+

Yes. Most houses of worship want entrances, the parking area, the office and the donation box covered, with cameras kept out of private counseling and prayer rooms. We can schedule installation around services, and set up separate logins for the clergy, the office and volunteer security.

Do I need to stay home for the whole CCTV installation?
+

No. Someone needs to let the crew in, show us the attic, cellar and garage, and be reachable during the day. We ask that the owner or manager be there for the last half hour, when we put the app on your phone and show you playback.
