The Neighborhood

Why Greenwood Heights Properties Need Intercom Installation

Start with the houses most people picture when they think of Greenwood Heights. Brownstoner's architecture writer put it simply in 2014: the neighborhood "is better known for its wood framed houses, both restored and covered with aluminum siding." The city's own records agree. PropertyShark lists 347 20th Street as a two-story, two-family frame house built in 1899 with aluminum or vinyl walls, 310 22nd Street as a two-family frame house from 1901, and 203 20th Street and 204 22nd Street as two-family frame houses from 1910. The Department of City Planning, writing about the 2005 rezoning of the blocks between 15th and 24th Streets, described rowhouses of two to four stories built in the late 1800s and early 1900s as housing for workers. In practice that means a large share of Greenwood Heights front doors are shared by exactly two households, often an owner and a tenant, sometimes two generations of one family, entering through a single street door into a narrow hall.

A plain doorbell cannot serve that arrangement. It rings one chime in one place, it does not tell a visitor which household is which, and it cannot open the street door from upstairs, so the person on the second floor walks down every time somebody rings. That is the first reason Greenwood Heights owners call us: to give each household its own button, its own station and its own way to let a visitor in, while the street door still closes and locks behind everyone. The second reason is age. A house built in 1899 or 1910 may still carry bell wire from an early electrification, or wire added in pieces by three or four owners since. Some of it is fine. Some of it is the reason the upstairs station whispers and the release barely clicks. We test it before anyone decides whether it stays.

The third reason is newer and sits on the avenues. The 2005 rezoning mapped R6A with commercial overlays along Fifth Avenue and R8A along Fourth Avenue, and buildings followed. New York YIMBY reported a seven-story building at 643–645 Fifth Avenue with thirteen apartments, ground-floor retail and a seven-car parking lot; a four-story, ten-apartment building at 814 Fifth Avenue at 33rd Street with a seven-car garage on the ground floor; and Brownstoner reported a seven-story, seventeen-unit building at 236 17th Street with seven enclosed parking spaces. Buildings of that size cross the line in the Multiple Dwelling Law where a building built or converted after 1968 with eight or more apartments must have an intercom from every apartment to the front door. They often have two ways in, the lobby and the parking entry, and the entry system was picked by a developer who has since sold the apartments and left. When the developer hands the building to a condo board, the board inherits a system nobody on it chose, sometimes without the administrator login.

The fourth reason is the mix along Fifth Avenue itself. The planning department describes Fifth Avenue's three- and four-story buildings with stores below and apartments above, and City Neighborhoods notes the cafés and boutiques that have opened along it. Each of those buildings has a residential door next to a shop door, and the apartments upstairs need a system that keeps working whatever happens in the store. West of Fourth Avenue the picture changes again: large lots with warehouses and one-story commercial buildings, some converted to studios, and new work like the three-story building with 3,468 square feet of manufacturing space and enclosed parking that New York YIMBY reported in September 2026 at 156 21st Street. Those doors need a station that someone on a noisy floor will actually hear.

Repair in Greenwood Heights

Need Repair in Greenwood Heights? A Release That Clicks but Won't Open, Outside Buttons That Fail, or a Panel Nobody Can Log Into

The repair calls we get from Greenwood Heights fall into a few patterns, and each one points somewhere different. The most common is a door that will not open even though visitors can ring. The upstairs tenant hears the call, talks to the courier, presses release and nothing happens at the street door. Forum threads from Park Slope and the blocks around it describe exactly this, and the replies get to the heart of it: the fault is either in the release circuit, at the button or along the wire, or in the electric strike itself. A strike that has burned out, a keeper that no longer lines up with a latch on a door that has shifted in its frame, or a wire that has come off a terminal all produce the same symptom. A multimeter at the strike during a release tells us in a minute which one it is.

The second pattern is the outdoor station failing while everything indoors still works. On a frame house the station often sits on the siding, exposed to weather on the side of the street that takes the rain, and its buttons or the joint behind it corrode first. Brick Underground quoted an installer saying that the trouble is usually "all in the wire connections," and in our experience that holds for a lot of outdoor failures. The third pattern belongs to the newer buildings: a networked panel in the lobby that has gone dark or stopped calling some apartments, where the condo board has no login because the developer's installer set it up and left. The fourth is a station nobody can identify, left by a previous owner, with no label and no paperwork.

If the street door is not latching, deal with that first. An unlocked street door is the real risk, and most doors can be locked with the key cylinder while the intercom is down. If a breaker feeding the intercom keeps tripping, leave it off rather than resetting it over and over. Then call (347) 934-8335. We book the visit within 2 hours of your request. A diagnostic visit is $250, and repairs on our Brooklyn list run $150 to $1,200 depending on what failed. A loose wire at a button sits at the bottom of that range; a replacement outdoor station or a run of new cable up a frame house sits higher. If fixing the old system would cost a large share of a new one, or its parts are no longer made, we put both options on paper so nobody pays twice for the same door.

Frame two-family under siding: finding the wood behind the wall

The frame two-family is the Greenwood Heights house we see most. PropertyShark's records for 347 20th Street describe it exactly: two stories, two units, frame construction, aluminum or vinyl exterior walls, built in 1899. The street door opens into a small hall, the first-floor apartment opens off it, and a straight stair climbs to the second. The original bell, if it survives, is a two-button plate beside the door with a thin pair of wires running through the wall to a transformer in the cellar. When owners replace it, the question that decides the job is what the station is fastened to. Siding is a skin, not a wall. Behind it there may be sheathing, the old clapboard, or an air gap, and a station screwed only into the siding will loosen the first winter the door slams.

On the survey we locate the framing behind the siding beside the door and mount the station on a block or backing plate that bears on solid wood, with the cable entering through a sealed hole so water running down the siding cannot follow it in. Inside, the two households usually want different things. The owner downstairs is close to the door and may be content with an audio station. The household upstairs gets the most from video, because a screen saves a walk down the stair. Both stations sit on one system with one supply in the cellar, and each one releases only the street door. The apartment doors keep their own key locks, which the intercom never touches.

Restored frame house: a station that suits clapboard and trim

Some owners have done the opposite of siding: stripped it off and restored the wood front, sometimes with original door surrounds and transom glass. They care what the station looks like, and they do not want a box screwed to restored trim. We usually suggest one of three things. A slim surface station mounted on the flat casing beside the door, painted or finished to sit quietly against it. A flush station set into a shallow pocket in the wall, which works where the framing allows and the owner accepts a clean cut in the clapboard. Or a station on a short post or on the stoop railing area, where the door surround is too narrow or too valuable to touch.

Whatever the choice, the cable route inside matters more than the station. Restored houses often have plaster that the owner does not want opened. We look for a path that already exists: an old bell wire hole, a closet that stacks above the front hall, the space under the stair, or the cellar ceiling to a point below the second-floor apartment and up through a chase. If no hidden path exists, we tell the owner before we start, show the options, and price each one, rather than discovering it with a drill halfway through the day.

Brick and brownstone rows: the stoop and the door below it

Greenwood Heights also has masonry rows. Brownstoner's 2014 piece notes a mix of brick and brownstone rows among the frame houses, and the Brooklyn Eagle described red-brick rowhouses on 27th Street between Fourth and Fifth Avenues. Many of these have a stoop up to a parlor door and a second door below it at the sidewalk. Where a house holds two or three apartments, the first job is to decide which door visitors use and which one gets the strike. In most cases the stoop door carries the station and the strike, and the door below stays on a key for the household that uses it, but a garden apartment with its own entrance can have its own station on the same system.

Mounting on brick is a different craft from mounting on siding. The station goes on solid brick or stone, never into a soft mortar joint alone, with anchors sized for old masonry, drilled at low speed without hammer action where the face is fragile. Where an old brass speaker plate sits in a recess, we decide with the owner whether to reuse the recess with a trim plate or fill it and mount on the surface. A house with three apartments is a multiple dwelling under the city's housing rules, and that brings the self-closing door requirements discussed in the rules section further down.

Fifth Avenue store buildings: two doors, two sets of rules

The Department of City Planning describes the Fifth Avenue frontage as three- and four-story mixed buildings, with stores at street level and apartments above. The apartments are reached through a narrow door beside the shop window, and that door needs its own station, its own release and a system that keeps working whatever the shop does: closes late, opens early, renovates or changes tenant. We power the residential system separately from the store, run its cable on its own path through the building, and keep the store's door hardware and the apartments' door hardware apart, so nobody working in the shop can buzz a stranger into the residential stair.

These buildings are also where the line between landlord and tenant gets blurred. The building owner controls the facade and the residential door; the shop tenant controls the storefront. Before any work, we confirm in writing who approved what. If the shop wants its own station for deliveries after hours, it goes on the shop's own door, not on the residents' panel, unless there is no other way in and the plan has been agreed by everyone who uses the stair.

Post-2005 condo and rental buildings: the lobby, the garage and the developer's choices

The buildings that followed the 2005 rezoning are the other half of Greenwood Heights intercom work. New York YIMBY described 643–645 Fifth Avenue as seven stories with thirteen apartments, likely sold as condos, ground-floor retail, a roof deck, an exercise room, bike storage and a seven-car parking lot. 814 Fifth Avenue was reported at four stories and ten apartments over a seven-car garage, and 236 17th Street at seven stories and seventeen units with seven enclosed parking spaces. Each has a lobby door that needs a station by law, and each has a second way in through the parking. A system that calls apartments from the lobby but ignores the garage leaves residents letting guests in through a door the intercom does not know about.

These buildings usually have block or concrete walls around the lobby and the equipment, so cable reaches the panel through cored holes rather than hollow walls, and the system is often networked, with a switch and a router in a small room on the ground floor. When we take one over, we start with an inventory: what model is each station, where is every piece of equipment, who holds the administrator account, does the system depend on a cloud service with a subscription, and is it on battery backup. Many boards discover they have none of that written down. The building does not need a new system; it needs its existing one documented and under its own control.

Converted and industrial space: being heard over the work

West of Fourth Avenue the planning department described large lots with one-story warehouse and commercial buildings, and City Neighborhoods notes old industrial buildings converted into studios and lofts. New York YIMBY's September 2026 report on 156 21st Street, between Third and Fourth Avenues, describes a new three-story building with manufacturing space and four enclosed parking spaces. In these spaces the problem is rarely the station at the door. It is that nobody inside hears it. A door chime in an office at the front does nothing for a fabricator at the back with a saw running.

We solve that with where the call goes rather than with a louder button. The station can ring a loud indoor chime or a strobe on the shop floor, call a phone or a group of phones, or both. In a building shared by several studios, each gets its own button and its own way to answer, and the street door's release time is kept short so it does not stand open for someone carrying materials.

Field Notes

Field Notes: What a Technician Checks First on a Greenwood Heights Street

Knock on the siding. On a frame house, the technician taps the siding beside the door and listens. A dull, solid sound means sheathing or old clapboard close behind it; a hollow rattle means a gap. That decides whether the station needs a mounting block and how long the screws have to be before anything is drilled.

Press release and put a hand on the strike. A strike that hums but does not let the latch go has power and a mechanical problem. A strike that does nothing has no power reaching it. Feeling the difference takes a few seconds and saves an hour of tracing in the wrong direction.

Old Wire, New Cable

From 1899 Bell Wire to a Condo's Network Switch: How the Wiring Decides the Job

Most of what makes a Greenwood Heights intercom reliable happens out of sight. In a frame house it is the condition of a pair of thin wires that may be older than the siding. In a newer building it is a network cable, a switch and a power supply in a room that only the super visits. The price on our Brooklyn list moves within its range mainly because of what the wiring turns out to be, so this is what the survey looks at and why.

Testing the bell pair in a frame house. The original bell circuit was built to carry a small current to a chime. A modern two-wire video system can run over a single good pair, and "good" has a specific meaning: continuous copper, insulation that does not leak between the two conductors, and a run short enough and thick enough that the voltage at the second floor is still what the station needs. In a two-story house the distance is short, which helps. What hurts is splices, because a house that has had several owners has usually had several people add a doorbell, a chime or a second button by twisting new wire onto old. We test each pair, open every splice we can reach, and remake joints in labeled boxes with connectors made for low-voltage work.

Running new cable through a wood house. When the old pair fails, a frame house is easier to rewire than a brick one, because there is usually a hollow path between floors. We prefer routes that already exist: the hole the old bell wire used, a closet stacked over the front hall, the space under the stair. We avoid cutting plaster and lath in restored rooms, and where a short visible run is unavoidable we use a small paintable raceway along a baseboard or casing rather than staples. The cable we pull is jacketed multi-pair or network-rated, so the house is not locked into one system for the next thirty years.

Network cable in a newer building. In the buildings put up after 2005, the lobby panel, any garage station and the apartment stations are often networked, powered over the same cable that carries the data. That makes the switch and its battery backup the heart of the system. We check that the switch has enough power budget for every station, that the backup is sized to keep the entry system up through a short outage, and that the equipment room has a label on every port. If a board wants an apartment station moved or added, the cable path through block walls and floors is planned with the board's engineer or managing agent before any core is drilled.

The Rules at the Front Door

Two Apartments, Three, or Eight and Up: What New York Rules Mean for Greenwood Heights Intercoms

Greenwood Heights has buildings on every side of the lines the city draws, sometimes on the same block: two-family frame houses, three-apartment brick rows and new buildings of ten or more apartments. The rules at the door differ for each. We are installers, not lawyers, and none of this is legal advice. This is what the public sources say, so you can ask your attorney, managing agent or the city the right questions before choosing a system.

Two-family houses. HPD's guidance treats a building with one or two apartments as a private dwelling, and the best-known intercom requirements are written for multiple dwellings. Most Greenwood Heights two-families install an intercom because two households share a door, not because a law requires it. Once a tenant relies on a door system, though, keeping it working is part of keeping the apartment in good order, and NYC 311 lists "doorbells, buzzers, or intercoms that are broken or missing" among the apartment maintenance complaints it takes.

Three apartments and up. A building with three or more apartments is a multiple dwelling. HPD's ABCs of Housing says building entrance doors and apartment entrance doors in every kind of multiple dwelling must be self-closing, and that the building manager, superintendent or owner should immediately repair broken locks or intercoms. An intercom job on a three-apartment brick row is a good moment to check the closer and the latch on the street door as well.

Eight apartments and up. As Habitat Magazine summarized in 2024, under the Multiple Dwelling Law a building built or converted after 1968 with eight or more units "must have a two-way voice intercom system linking the front door to each apartment," and the housing maintenance code also requires that it work. The new buildings on Fifth Avenue reported at ten, thirteen and seventeen apartments fall within that rule. Habitat also reported that New York courts treat a working intercom as an essential service, so a co-op or condo board has an obligation to repair it, not just a reason to.

Heights at the door. The Department of Buildings rule 1 RCNY 42-01 says no push button may be more than six feet from the floor, and the speaking and listening device must be between four and five feet from the floor. On a stoop we measure from the step where the visitor stands, not from the sidewalk. Shops and offices open to the public also follow accessibility reach rules, which usually puts buttons lower.

Landmarks. Our searches turned up no Greenwood Heights historic district. The Weir Greenhouse at Fifth Avenue and 25th Street, now part of the Green-House at Green-Wood, is an individual landmark, and the cemetery itself is a National Historic Landmark. Neither affects a private house's front door, but if you are unsure about an address, the Landmarks Preservation Commission's map will settle it.

Commercial Intercom Installation

Business Intercom Installation in Greenwood Heights

Compliance notes for Greenwood Heights shops, studios and mixed-use buildings

  • Free exit. Any door with a strike or magnetic lock must open from inside without a key, code or button. The intercom controls entry only.
  • Self-latching residential doors. Where apartments share the building, the residential street door must still close and latch on its own after every release, in line with HPD's self-closing door rules.
  • Reachable controls. 1 RCNY 42-01 caps push buttons at six feet and puts the speaking device between four and five feet. Public-facing shops and offices also follow accessibility reach rules, so buttons sit within reach of someone using a wheelchair.
  • Residents apart from the business. The residential system gets its own power and cable path, so shop hours, renovations and changes of tenant do not affect the apartments' door.
  • Life-safety devices clear. Stations and cable never block an exit sign, a fire alarm device, a sprinkler head or the swing of an exit door, and low-voltage cable does not share a pathway with power wiring.
  • Landlord approval in writing. In leased space, the building owner's written approval comes before any facade mounting or new hole through a wall.
Your Questions Answered

Intercom Installation in Greenwood Heights: Questions Answered

These questions come from what Brooklyn owners, tenants and boards ask in forum threads and housing articles, reworded for the buildings in Greenwood Heights: the strike that will not open, the double doors, the worn handsets, the newer buildings and the arguments over who pays. Each is answered once here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Doors and strikes in the older houses

Visitors can ring us, but pressing our release does nothing at the street door. Is that the buzzer or the strike, and who should look at it, a locksmith or an electrician?

It is one of three things, and a quick test separates them. If the strike hums or clicks when you press release but the door still will not open, power is arriving and the problem is mechanical: the strike's keeper no longer lines up with the latch, or the door has dropped in its frame so the latch binds. That is door hardware work. If the strike does nothing at all, either the release signal is not leaving your station, or a wire between the station, the supply and the strike has broken or come off a terminal; a commenter in one Brownstoner thread found exactly that, a wire that had stopped touching the button. If the strike has power and still does nothing, the strike itself has failed. A locksmith can handle the first case and sometimes the last; the middle case is wiring. We handle all three in one visit, because on an old frame house it is often a mix: a tired strike and a corroded joint on the same circuit.

Our front door is a tall pair of doors with glass panels. Can an electric strike be fitted to double doors like that?

Usually, yes, though it is more work than a single door. In a 2007 Brownstoner thread, an owner who had been told his tall glass double door could not take a strike was answered by someone who had installed strikes on two pairs of double doors during a renovation: a channel was cut behind the astragal, the strip that covers the gap between the two leaves, and wire was brought to the door through a small hole in the leaf nearest the wall. In practice the inactive leaf is held by bolts at top and bottom, and the strike goes either in the inactive leaf's edge or in the frame head, depending on the lock. Glass panels limit where wire can run inside the door, so we plan the route before ordering anything. If the doors are original and the owner will not cut them, a surface-mounted strike or a magnetic lock at the head is the alternative, each with its own trade-offs on free exit and appearance.

The panel outside still works well, but the handsets upstairs are worn out. Can we change only the indoor stations?

Often, if the system's maker still makes compatible stations. Many audio and two-wire video systems accept newer indoor stations from the same family, and replacing them keeps the outdoor panel, the wiring and the supply that already work. What decides it is the model number on the outdoor panel and the supply, which we check before quoting. If the line has been discontinued, a compatible station may be impossible to find, and mixing brands on one bus rarely works. In that case the honest answer is that the outdoor panel has to change too, and we price both paths. On a two-family, swapping two handsets usually falls at the lower end of our $150 to $1,200 repair range; replacing the whole system moves you to the $600 to $1,800 video line.

Can I swap a failed electric strike myself? Some owners say they did it in five minutes.

Some can. In the 2006 Brownstoner thread, one owner wrote that he put in a new strike himself for about forty dollars and that it took five minutes, with a reminder to shut off the breaker first. That works when the new strike is the same size, the same voltage and the same type as the old one, the frame pocket is undamaged, and the door still lines up. It goes wrong when the replacement is fail-safe instead of fail-secure, so the door unlocks whenever power drops; when it runs on a different voltage from the supply; or when the real problem was the door's alignment, so the new strike fails the same way. In a rented two-family the owner should also remember that the tenant relies on that door. If you replace it yourself, test it from fully open several times before trusting it.

The newer buildings on Fifth Avenue

The developer is handing our new condo building over to the owners' board. What intercom records and logins should the board collect?

Ask for five things in writing before the handover is signed. The administrator login for the lobby panel and any garage station, transferred to an account the board controls rather than the developer's installer. A list of every station by model and location, including the parking entrance if there is one. The wiring and network diagram, with the switch, router, power supply and battery backup identified. Any cloud or subscription service the system depends on, who pays for it and when it renews. And the warranty terms, with the installer's contact. New York YIMBY reported buildings like 643–645 Fifth Avenue with thirteen apartments and a parking lot, and boards in buildings that size inherit systems nobody on the board chose. If the developer cannot produce those records, we can inventory the system on site and document it, which is cheaper than replacing hardware that works.

Our Fifth Avenue shop has an aluminum-and-glass storefront door. Will it take an electric strike, or does it need a different kind of lock?

Storefront doors are different from wood house doors. Narrow aluminum stiles leave little room in the frame, and many have a deadlatch or a pivot design that a standard strike does not fit. There are three usual answers. A strike made for aluminum storefront frames, which fits the narrow jamb. An electrified version of the door's own lock, which keeps its look. Or a magnetic lock at the head of the door, which holds by magnet rather than latch. ButterflyMX's guide contrasts strikes and magnetic locks, and the main point for a shop is exit: a magnetic lock must release for anyone leaving, usually through a motion sensor and a push button, and must not trap anyone if the power fails. We look at the door, the frame and the exit route, and recommend the one that keeps the door both secure and legal.

When it breaks: who pays and who fixes

Only the outside call buttons have failed and the buzzers inside still work, yet management says the whole system has to go. Should we believe that?

Not without a test. In a 2008 Brownstoner thread, a co-op of two 16-apartment buildings was told to replace its whole system when only a few exterior buzzers had failed, and the replies split: some said a good technician fixed theirs in minutes, one said troubleshooting cost hundreds without success and replacement was cheaper in the end. Both can be true. If the outdoor panel's buttons or the joint behind it have corroded, replacing the outdoor panel alone may fix it. If the bus wiring is failing, or the system's parts are no longer made, the panel is just the first symptom. ButterflyMX's own guide says not to replace a system on age alone. Ask for a diagnosis that names the failed part and says whether a compatible replacement exists. If it does, a repair is usually far less than a new building-wide system, which runs $1,200 to $4,500 on our list for a 4 to 20 unit walk-up.

A neighbor's renovation cut the intercom wire that runs through their apartment. Who is responsible for the repair?

It depends on the paperwork, and the first step is to get the system working again regardless. Habitat Magazine, discussing a co-op case, quoted an attorney saying the neighbor may be responsible depending on the terms of the lease or the alteration agreement they signed for the work, and that the neighbor may in turn have a claim against their contractor. In a co-op or condo, the board still owes the other residents a working intercom while that is sorted out. Our job is the practical part: find where the run was cut, decide whether to splice it in an accessible box or pull a new cable on a different path, and write down what we found and what we did. That written record is what the board, the neighbor and any insurer will want when they settle who pays.

In our co-op, does the board pay to fix a broken intercom, or do the shareholders?

Habitat Magazine reported that under the proprietary lease, intercom repairs are very unlikely to fall on the shareholder, and quoted an attorney saying the co-op "has a legal obligation to repair or replace the intercom." The common panel, the wiring and the supply are building systems. The exception that comes up is damage inside one apartment, such as a station pulled off the wall during a renovation, which a board may charge back under its rules. Small co-ops in Greenwood Heights, often a few apartments in a converted house or a small walk-up, sometimes run without a managing agent, and the shareholders end up arranging repairs themselves. Whoever arranges it, the written quote should go to the board so the spending is approved properly.

Our management company says the super can handle intercom repairs. When is that fine, and when does it need a licensed installer?

A super can handle a lot. Brick Underground noted that a super may be allowed to make repairs and that the building has to authorize any work. Cleaning sticky buttons, swapping an identical strike, checking a tripped breaker or a blown fuse at the supply, and replacing a cracked handset of the same model are within reach of a capable super. Where it changes is the wiring and the programming. Tracing an intermittent fault through an old bus, rewiring a corroded run, or reprogramming a networked lobby panel takes test equipment and training, and a mistake there can take every apartment off the system at once. In New York, low-voltage security work of this kind is done under a state license; ours is #12000287431. A useful split is for the super to handle first checks and simple swaps, and to call us when the fault is not where they expected.

What AI Gets Wrong About Intercom Installation in Greenwood Heights

The claims below are the ones that appear again and again in generic AI answers and in cost-guide pages from sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, set against what we find in Greenwood Heights buildings.

"An intercom costs somewhere between a few hundred and fifteen thousand dollars"

Cost guides publish ranges wide enough to cover every building in the country. HomeAdvisor's intercom page gives a headline range of $1,200 to $8,500, and elsewhere on the same page a range of $150 to $15,000. Generic answers repeat those numbers as if they described your door.

They describe no particular house. A frame two-family on 20th Street and a seventeen-apartment building on 17th Street are different jobs, and a national average blends them with suburban houses that have no shared door at all.

Our Brooklyn list breaks the work into building types instead: $600 to $1,800 for a video system on a one- or two-family, $400 to $900 for an audio buzzer system on a small building, $1,200 to $4,500 for a 4 to 20 unit walk-up, $800 to $3,500 for commercial doors and $150 to $1,200 for repairs. Where your job falls within its line depends on the wire and the door.

"A basic audio intercom is a couple of hundred dollars installed"

HomeAdvisor quotes a basic audio intercom at $200 to $300 installed, and AI answers pass that on as a floor for any building. That figure describes a simple room-to-room unit, not a street door.

A door system in a two-family needs an outdoor station that survives weather, an indoor station per household, a power supply, and above all a strike that lets the upstairs household open the street door. Without the strike, it is a talking doorbell.

That is why audio buzzer systems for small buildings sit at $400 to $900 on our list. The difference from the national figure is the door hardware and the outdoor station, which are the parts that make it an intercom.

"Wireless is the cheaper, easier choice"

Cost guides show wireless intercoms costing far less than hard-wired ones, and AI answers recommend wireless for older homes to avoid opening walls.

In a Greenwood Heights frame house the walls are often the easy part, because there is a hollow path between two floors and sometimes a usable bell pair already in it. A two-wire system over that pair gives video and a release with no new holes. A wireless kit still needs a wired strike and power at the door, so the wiring is not avoided, only moved.

Wireless and app-based stations have their place, especially where an owner lives elsewhere. The choice should follow a test of the existing wire, not a general rule that wireless always wins.

"When an old intercom fails, replace the whole system"

This is a common answer, and sometimes a management company's too. The 2008 Brownstoner thread about a co-op told to replace everything when only some exterior buttons had failed shows how often it comes up.

An installer quoted by Brick Underground put it the other way: the trouble is usually "all in the wire connections." ButterflyMX's own repair guide advises against replacing a system on age alone and recommends diagnosing the failed part first.

Sometimes replacement is right, when parts are no longer made or the bus wiring is failing throughout. But that is a finding from a test, not an assumption. We put the repair and the replacement side by side, with the failed part named.

"Every apartment building in New York must have an intercom"

Generic answers often state that New York requires intercoms in apartment buildings, without saying which ones.

The requirement people usually mean applies to buildings built or converted after 1968 with eight or more apartments, as Habitat Magazine summarized it. A Greenwood Heights two-family is not covered by that rule, and a three-apartment row is a multiple dwelling with self-closing door duties but not the same intercom mandate. The ten-, thirteen- and seventeen-apartment buildings reported on Fifth Avenue and 17th Street are covered.

Owners of small houses should install an intercom because two or three households share one door, and owners of larger buildings because the law and their residents require it. Both are good reasons; they are not the same reason.

"A video doorbell does the same job in a two-family"

AI answers frequently suggest a smart video doorbell as a cheap intercom for a small multi-family house.

A video doorbell shows a visitor and lets one account talk to them. In a Greenwood Heights two-family, it usually rings one household's phones, cannot tell the visitor which button is whose, and cannot release the street door unless a strike and a relay are added anyway.

If both households want to see and let in their own visitors, the house needs a station per household and a strike. A doorbell can stay on as an extra camera if the owner wants it, but it is not the intercom.

"Any electric strike fits any door"

Product listings make strikes look interchangeable, and generic answers treat them that way.

A century-old wood door that has dropped in its frame, a pair of glass double doors with an astragal, and an aluminum storefront door on Fifth Avenue each need a different solution. A strike set in the wrong place leaves the door closing but not latching, which defeats the point of the intercom and the city's self-closing rules.

We fit the strike to where the latch actually lands, choose a storefront strike or a magnetic lock where a standard strike cannot work, adjust the closer, and test the door from fully open until it latches every time.

FAQ

Greenwood Heights Intercom Installation FAQ

What can we check ourselves before paying for a repair visit?
+

Start with the simple things Brick Underground suggests. Press each button firmly and hold it for a second or two, because many failures turn out to be a press that was too short. If a button sticks, swab around it with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol. Check whether the breaker or fuse feeding the intercom supply has tripped. Note which apartments are affected and whether the fault is calling, talking or releasing the door. Leave the wiring alone, and send us what you found, because it often tells us the fault before we arrive.

Why do online intercom cost guides give ranges that are so far apart?
+

Because they average every kind of building in the country into one figure. HomeAdvisor's page gives $1,200 to $8,500 in one place and $150 to $15,000 in another. Those ranges include room-to-room units in single houses and large lobby systems alike. Our Brooklyn list breaks the work into building types, so a Greenwood Heights two-family, a small walk-up and a Fifth Avenue shop each have their own range.

How long will a five-apartment building be without a working door during the changeover?
+

Usually part of one day. An installer quoted by Brick Underground estimated a five-apartment building could be replaced in a day, which matches our experience. We pull new cable and mount the new stations first where we can, and switch the door over last, so the gap with no working intercom is a few hours. During that time the street door stays locked and residents use their keys.

We rent the top floor of a two-family frame house. Do the city's intercom rules apply to our house at all?
+

The best-known intercom requirement applies to buildings of eight or more apartments built or converted after 1968, and HPD treats a one- or two-apartment building as a private dwelling rather than a multiple dwelling, so that rule does not reach a two-family. A broken buzzer is still a repair issue, and NYC 311 lists broken or missing doorbells, buzzers and intercoms among apartment maintenance complaints. Raise it with the owner in writing first.

Visitors say our door station does not work, but it works every time we test it. What is going on?
+

Often the visitor is pressing too briefly or walking away before the call connects. Brick Underground quoted a locksmith saying people sometimes are not pushing hard enough or not waiting long enough. Clear labels, a lit nameplate and a short instruction on the station help. If the complaints come from one direction, such as couriers at night, we check the lighting and whether a particular button is intermittent.

What does a video intercom do that a video doorbell on a two-family cannot?
+

It gives each household its own button and its own screen, and it opens the street door. A video doorbell usually rings one account, shows the visitor and lets that account talk, but it cannot route the call to the right floor and cannot release the door without a strike and a relay added anyway. In a two-family where both households receive visitors, a station per household and a strike are what make it work.

How much of an intercom's cost is the door release itself?
+

On a simple job with a single wood door that still lines up, the strike is a modest part of the price. It grows when the door has dropped in its frame, when there is no strike pocket, or when the door is a pair or an aluminum storefront. Our written quote lists the door hardware on its own line, so you can see exactly what the release costs on your building.

How do we keep a repair bill from growing while someone hunts for an intermittent fault?
+

Agree a limit before the search starts. One Brownstoner commenter described spending hundreds of dollars troubleshooting an old system without success. We start with the $250 diagnostic visit, tell you what we found and what we suspect, and give you a written price for the repair or a replacement before doing more, so you decide when to stop.

Do we need the landlord's written approval before you work on a rental building's front door?
+

Yes. The street door, the wiring and the panel belong to the building, and Brick Underground notes that work needs the building's authorization. We take instructions from the owner, the board or the managing agent. A tenant can send us photos and we will describe what we see, which often helps the tenant make the case to the owner.

Our landlord is replacing the buzzer with a new system. Do tenants get any say in what is installed?
