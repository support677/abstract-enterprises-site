Plaster over brick (brownstone party walls)

We score the plaster to stop cracking, drill through it with a masonry bit, then set sleeve anchors at least two inches into solid brick. If the plaster is hollow-sounding, we bridge it with the bracket's full plate so the load sits on brick, not on loose plaster. Add $45 for plaster; if the anchors land in brick, the brick add of $65 applies instead.

Exposed brick (loft walls)

We map the brick courses and place each anchor in the center of a solid brick, never in the mortar joint. Old Brooklyn brick is soft, so we use longer sleeve anchors and drill without hammer mode where the face starts to spall. Brick and stone add $65.

Metal studs (new condos)

Light-gauge steel won't hold a lag bolt. We locate the studs, then use heavy-duty toggle anchors through the stud face. For full-motion arms or screens over 75″, we mount an $85 plywood backer across three studs first. Metal studs add $30.

Concrete and cinder block (towers and basements)

Hammer-drilled holes, wedge or sleeve anchors for poured concrete, and hollow-block-rated anchors for cinder block set in the solid web, not the hollow core. Basement walls with furring strips get anchors through the strips into the block behind them. Concrete or block adds $75.

Tile and marble (bathrooms, bars, kitchens)

We drill tile with diamond bits at low speed, with tape to keep the bit from walking, then anchor into the wall behind. Glazed tile and marble can crack no matter how carefully it's drilled, and you accept that risk. Tile and marble add $95.

Wood lath or drywall over wood studs

Some rowhouse interior walls and many renovated basements are plaster on wood lath or drywall over wood studs. TV mounting on wood studs is the simplest Brooklyn job: we find the studs and drive lag bolts into them. TV mounting on shiplap wall boards or TV mounting on wood paneling works the same way when framing sits behind. There's no wall add-on.

ADA protrusion limits in businesses

Under the 2010 ADA Standards, wall-mounted objects with a leading edge between 27 and 80 inches above the floor may stick out no more than 4 inches into a walkway. In a restaurant hallway or waiting room, that means a low-profile mount, a recess, or mounting the TV's bottom edge above 80 inches.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Answers Get Wrong About TV Mounting in Brooklyn

Ask a chatbot what it costs to mount a TV in Brooklyn and you'll get a tidy number and some general advice. Most of it is written for a suburban house with wood studs every 16 inches. Brooklyn's walls are older, harder and more varied than that. Here are seven claims we see in AI summaries and generic cost guides, and what actually happens on Brooklyn jobs.

1. AI says: “TV mounting in Brooklyn costs about $60 to $105.”

That range comes from posted task budgets on gig sites, and Airtasker's Brooklyn page shows a median of about $80. Those numbers are real for what they measure: usually a smaller TV, plain drywall, the customer's own bracket and no extras of any kind.

Brooklyn jobs rarely look like that. A brownstone party wall is plaster over brick, a loft wall is soft century-old brick, and a new tower wall is metal studs or concrete. Each one needs different drill bits and anchors, and each one takes longer than a drywall job in a suburban den.

Our Brooklyn base is $179.99, and the wall line is printed right next to it: $45 plaster, $65 brick, $30 metal studs, $75 concrete. The total you see when you book is the total you pay, unless we find something different on site and price it before we start.

2. AI says: “Brownstones have studs every 16 inches, just like any house.”

Brownstone interior partitions sometimes have wood framing behind lath, but the long side walls are usually brick party walls with plaster applied directly on top. There's no cavity and no stud, so a stud finder's beep on that wall means nothing at all.

Owners on Brownstoner's forum describe exactly this: fireplaces built into party walls with no studs, just plaster over brick. The lag bolts that come in a bracket box are made for wood studs, and in that wall they either spin in crumbling plaster or stop dead at the brick.

We test-drill at the bracket height first. If we hit brick within an inch or two, we switch to sleeve anchors set into the masonry, which hold better than wood studs. If we hit lath and an open cavity, we look for the framing instead.

3. AI says: “Exposed brick is the strongest wall you have, so any masonry anchor works.”

Brick can be very strong, but the brick in Brooklyn lofts and rowhouses is often more than a century old. The faces can be soft, and the lime mortar between bricks can be sandy enough to scrape out with a house key.

An anchor set into a mortar joint, which is exactly where many DIY guides say to drill, can loosen over time as the TV's weight works on it. A full-motion arm makes that worse, because it levers the top anchors every single time someone pulls the screen out.

We place anchors in the center of solid bricks, use sleeve anchors long enough to get past the weak face, and turn off hammer mode if the brick starts to spall. The $65 brick add covers that extra time and the heavier hardware.

4. AI says: “New construction is the easy case: just drywall over wood studs.”

Not in Brooklyn's towers. The high-rises that went up after the Downtown Brooklyn and Greenpoint-Williamsburg rezonings are typically steel-and-concrete buildings. Light-gauge metal studs usually divide the apartments, and some walls, especially around elevator cores and columns, are solid poured concrete.

Renters in r/Brooklyn ask whether a 65-pound TV can hang on metal studs at all. It can, but not on the wood screws that come in the bracket kit. Thin steel strips out under a lag bolt and won't hold a big screen for long.

We use toggles rated for steel studs, add a plywood backer when the screen is large or on an arm, and switch to concrete anchors when the bit hits concrete. Metal studs add $30, concrete adds $75, and you see which one applies before we drill.

5. AI says: “A decorative fireplace doesn't get hot, so mount the TV right above it.”

Heat is only half the question. Many Brooklyn fireplaces were sealed decades ago, but some brownstones and Bay Ridge houses still burn wood or run gas inserts. We check whether the firebox is working before we choose the spot and the mount.

The bigger issue is height. A brownstone mantel can sit high, and a TV above it puts the middle of the screen far over seated eye level. Brownstoner members warned about a sore neck in their thread on exactly this question, and they were right to.

If you still want it above the mantel, a tilt mount helps a little, and a pull-down MantelMount fixes the problem. Above-fireplace placement adds $130, a pull-down mount adds $160, and the brick or stone around the chimney adds $65.

6. AI says: “A finished basement is just drywall, so it's the cheapest room to mount in.”

Finished basements in Marine Park, Bergen Beach, Dyker Heights and Bay Ridge often look like drywall but sit right on the foundation. Behind the drywall or paneling there may be thin furring strips nailed to concrete block, not a full stud wall.

A lag bolt into a furring strip holds only as well as the few nails holding that strip to the block, and that isn't good enough for a 75″ screen. Basements can also have low ceilings, which push the TV lower than people expect.

We check what's behind the finish before drilling. Real wood studs mean no wall add. Block means anchors through the strip into the solid part of the block, at $75. Then we confirm the viewing height from your actual seat, not from a chart.

7. AI says: “A restaurant TV goes up the same way as one at home.”

A home TV gets watched from a couch. A restaurant or bar TV hangs over moving staff, gets bumped by trays and ladders, and has to be readable from across a crowded room. That changes the height, the mount and sometimes the hardware itself.

Businesses also have rules homes don't. In walkways the ADA limits protrusion to 4 inches. Managing agents want a certificate of insurance, and installs have to fit around service hours and deliveries. Screens in public spots may also need anti-theft hardware so they stay where they're hung.

That's why single screens come from the chart, and multi-screen rooms get a written quote, line by line. When owners want the work finished before the lunch crowd comes through the door, we book the earliest 2-hour window of the day for them.
