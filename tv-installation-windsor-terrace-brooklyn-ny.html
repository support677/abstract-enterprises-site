The Neighborhood

Why Windsor Terrace Properties Need TV Installation

Windsor Terrace is shaped by what surrounds it. Prospect Park runs along its east side, Green-Wood Cemetery along its southwest, Park Slope begins at Prospect Park West, Borough Park at McDonald Avenue and Kensington at Caton Avenue. Wikipedia puts the whole neighborhood at 0.503 square miles, with 20,988 residents in 2010. Because it is hemmed in, it never had the open land for big apartment complexes, and Brick Underground's 2022 guide notes that there are few new developments simply because there is nothing left to build on. The houses that are here went up in layers. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's 2013 report on the Prospect Avenue firehouse traces a land purchase in 1849, streets laid out in the early 1850s, roughly 150 houses by 1890, most of them small frame houses, and then the roughly 700 houses built by Calder, a builder who later became a U.S. senator, between 1902 and 1919, the rows Brownstoner calls "Calderville." In the 1920s, investors added apartment buildings on Prospect Avenue and commercial buildings on 11th Avenue. Each of those layers comes with its own walls, and a TV bracket only cares about the wall.

The walls are the first reason a TV here needs more thought than a template. A house of the 1890s or the 1910s was finished in plaster, and how that plaster was put up matters. On an interior partition or an outside wall of a frame house, it was usually troweled onto thin strips of wood lath nailed across wood studs. On the party wall shared with the house next door in a brick row, it was often troweled straight onto the brick, with no studs at all. Brownstoner's forum has a long thread from an owner of a house built around 1899 who wanted a TV above a bedroom fireplace and found exactly that: original plaster over brick, no studs to find, and a contractor who would not commit. Over a century, many of these walls have also been patched, furred out, skimmed or covered in drywall during renovations, so a single room can hold two or three kinds of wall. A stud finder that works beautifully on new drywall reads nonsense on these. That is why we treat the first fifteen minutes of every Windsor Terrace visit as an investigation, not a formality.

The second reason is the shape of the rooms. These are narrow houses. Brownstoner described 139 Windsor Place, an 1890s red-brick row house with a low stoop, as 19 feet wide, and Brick Underground's guide listed a 1910 brick townhouse on Windsor Place at 20 feet. Subtract the walls and the stair, and the parlor that remains is long and slim, with windows at one or both ends, a mantel on one long wall in many houses, and doorways that eat into the other. In a room like that, the obvious wall is often the wrong one: too close to the sofa for a large screen, facing a window that throws glare across it in the afternoon, or broken up by a chimney breast. Fitting a 65-inch or 75-inch TV, which many households now buy as a matter of course, into a room planned around a coal grate and a piano takes a few measurements and some honest advice before any drilling. We spend a whole section further down on the geometry, because it decides more about how happy you will be with the result than the brand of bracket.

The third reason is who lives here and how they hold their homes. Brownstoner's 2020 piece on 56 Sherman Street, a Calder two-family, described the classic arrangement of an owner's duplex with a floor-through rental above. Many of those houses have since been combined into single-family homes, as Homeworld Design showed in a 2024 conversion by Kimberly Peck Architect, while others still hold a tenant on the top floor. The apartment buildings around the edges, such as the 1929 six-story pre-war co-op at 135 Prospect Park Southwest with its two elevators and full-time superintendent, bring boards, managing agents and work-hour rules. And demand keeps rising: StreetEasy ranked Windsor Terrace third among the most in-demand New York City neighborhoods for 2026, Time Out reported in January, with searches up almost 45 percent. New owners arrive in old houses with new TVs, and the first thing many of them want is a screen on the wall that does not crack the plaster they just paid for.

Six Windsor Terrace facts that shape a TV job before the box is opened

  • The houses are narrow. Brownstoner measured 139 Windsor Place at 19 feet wide and Brick Underground listed a 20-foot Windsor Place townhouse. A narrow room limits how far back the sofa can sit, which limits how big the screen should be and how high it should go.
  • Many parlors still have mantels. 139 Windsor Place kept faux-painted stone mantels in the parlor and dining room and another, flanked by built-in bookshelves, in the street-facing bedroom. A mantel changes the minimum height of the screen and usually puts a chimney flue right behind the obvious spot.
  • Party walls are often brick with no studs. In an attached brick row, the long wall you share with the neighbors may be plaster laid straight onto masonry, which needs masonry fasteners and carries sound into the house next door.
  • Some houses are wood frame. Wikipedia describes clapboard Italianate homes with bay windows; Brick Underground listed a 1905 semi-detached clapboard house on East 5th Street; Brownstoner wrote up a narrow two-story frame house on East 2nd Street. Frame houses have studs, but they are often irregularly spaced and buried under lath.
  • The apartment buildings are mostly older, with a few newer ones. Corcoran lists the co-op at 135 Prospect Park Southwest as built in 1929, and a four-story, 50-unit condo at 1115 Prospect Avenue as built in 1990. Older buildings often mean plaster on block or terra-cotta partitions; newer ones mean drywall, sometimes on metal studs.
  • The land was not always solid ground. Community Board 7 notes that much of the neighborhood was built on a former City of Brooklyn landfill more than a century ago. Old houses settle, and settled houses have walls that are slightly out of plumb, which is why we level the bracket to gravity and then check how it reads against the ceiling line and the window trim.

Same-Day Repair in Windsor Terrace

Need a Fix in Windsor Terrace? Sagging Brackets, Cracked Plaster Around Anchors and TVs That Have Started to Tilt

The repair calls we get from houses like these follow a pattern, and the pattern starts with the anchor. A bracket put up with plastic plugs or hollow-wall toggles in plaster that sits on brick has nothing behind it to grip; it holds for a while because plaster is hard, then the plaster around each anchor crumbles and the TV starts to tip forward at the top. A bracket screwed into wood lath instead of the stud behind it pulls the lath away from the stud, and the first sign is a hairline crack that grows into a bulge. A full-motion arm installed on two screws because the stud was "close enough" leans a little more each time somebody pulls the screen out to watch from the kitchen. And sometimes the problem is not the wall at all, but a TV that was never locked onto its bracket and can be lifted off with one hand, which is the last thing you want in a house with small children or a dog.

If a TV is leaning, coming away from the wall or making a noise when touched, take it down if you safely can, or at least keep people and pets away from below it, then call (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list, removing an old bracket is $45, and remounting is priced from the same base and wall lines as any new job. When the plaster around the old holes has broken up, we move the fixing to sound material or bridge the damage with a plywood backer board, $85, fastened through to structure, rather than chasing anchors into a wall that has already failed. Where the damaged area is visible after the TV goes back up, we tell you plainly so a plasterer or painter can finish it; we do not pretend a patch is invisible when it is not.

The limestone or Beaux Arts multi-family

Larger three-family houses such as 552 Prospect Avenue, with its pale-brick Beaux Arts facade, bay windows and floor-through apartments, where each floor is its own household.

Six-story elevator buildings like the 1929 co-op at 135 Prospect Park Southwest, with boards, a super, work hours and masonry or terra-cotta partitions behind the plaster.

The 1890s brick row house: two kinds of plaster in one parlor

The older brick rows, such as the matching houses around 139 Windsor Place that Brownstoner dated to the 1890s, are where the wall question is most interesting. The long walls on either side of the parlor are party walls, shared with the houses next door. In many brick rows of this age, those walls were plastered directly onto the brick. There is no stud to find and no cavity to fish a cable through; there is a coat of plaster perhaps an inch thick, and then solid masonry. The short walls, the partitions between front parlor and back parlor or between bedrooms, are usually wood studs with lath and plaster. The front and rear walls are brick again, with window openings. So a single parlor can need two completely different fixing methods depending on which wall the TV goes on.

On a party wall, the bracket goes into the brick. We drill through the plaster with a sharp masonry bit at a slow speed so the plaster does not shatter around the hole, then continue into the brick, ideally into the face of a brick rather than the mortar joint, and set sleeve anchors or masonry screws rated for the load. Old brick is softer than modern brick, and lime mortar is softer still, so we choose fasteners that grip along their length instead of relying on a wedge at the tip. That is the brick or stone line on our list, $65 on top of the base price. On a lath-and-plaster partition, the bracket goes into the studs, and the plaster line applies, $45. Either way, the hole pattern is marked on painter's tape so the plaster is supported while it is drilled, and the dust is caught at the hole.

Party walls also carry sound. A soundbar or a subwoofer on a brick wall shared with a neighbor will be heard next door, especially the low notes, and the bedroom of the house next door may be right behind your parlor wall. When a household plans on a lot of late-night viewing, we suggest putting the TV on a partition or a front wall where possible, keeping the soundbar on the console rather than the party wall, and putting any subwoofer on a pad on the floor away from the shared wall. It costs nothing to plan this way, and it keeps the peace on a block where everyone knows everyone.

The Calder row house: parquet, trim and a two-family history

The houses Calder built between 1902 and 1919 account for many of the neighborhood's rows, and Brownstoner's pieces on 207 and 209 Windsor Place and 56 Sherman Street describe what came with them. The 1915 advertisements Brownstoner quoted promised hardwood trim, parquet floors, gas ranges, built-in iceboxes, basement laundries and open front porches. The 209 Windsor Place row was planned in 1914 by the architects Eisenla and Carlson, and Brownstoner noted that its kitchen steps up to a rear deck door. These are well-built houses, and the 1909 phrase Brownstoner quoted, "honestly constructed," is fair. Their interior walls are generally lath and plaster on wood studs, sometimes now covered with drywall, and the parlor floors are often parquet or narrow-strip hardwood that dents if a ladder foot is put down carelessly.

The TV question in a Calder house is usually about where, not how. The parlor is typically arranged front to back, with windows to the street at the front and to the yard at the rear. The partition walls are the natural place for a screen, and they hold a bracket well once the studs are found. What complicates it is the history of the house as a two-family. When two apartments become one home, as in the Kimberly Peck conversion Homeworld Design published in 2024, walls move: old doorways get closed with drywall, kitchens shift, and a wall that looks continuous may be plaster on one side of a seam and gypsum board on the other. We find those seams with a deep-scan wall scanner and a test hole behind where the TV will cover it, and we never assume the stud spacing on one side of a seam continues on the other.

The clapboard or sided frame house: studs that wander

Before the brick rows, Windsor Terrace was a village of small frame houses; the LPC report counts roughly 150 houses in 1890, most of them frame. Some survive, and others were built later in wood. Wikipedia describes clapboard Italianate homes with bay windows and stained glass, Brick Underground listed a 1905 semi-detached clapboard house on East 5th Street, and Forgotten NY's 2024 walk admired the small cottages on East 5th Street. Some are now covered in siding. Inside, a frame house is all wood studs, which sounds easy, but old framing was cut and spaced by hand, not to today's 16-inch standard. Studs can sit 14 inches apart in one bay and 22 in the next, doubled around a window, or replaced with a short header where a doorway once was.

So in a frame house we confirm each stud we plan to use, rather than finding one and measuring to the next. Where the studs do not land where the bracket needs them, the answer is a plywood backer board, $85, screwed into every stud it crosses and painted to match, which gives the bracket a solid surface anywhere along its width. Frame houses also carry their outside walls on the same studs, so the bracket on an exterior wall is fixed to the house frame itself. If you want a TV outside, on a rear deck for example, the outdoor line applies, $90 on top of the base price, and it goes into the frame through the siding with sealed fasteners so water does not follow the screws in.

on Prospect Park Southwest: board rules first

The six-story elevator buildings along Prospect Park Southwest and near Fort Hamilton Parkway are a different world. Corcoran lists the co-op at 135 Prospect Park Southwest as built in 1929, with two elevators, a full-time superintendent, a courtyard and a central laundry room. Buildings of this age typically have plaster on terra-cotta block or on masonry partitions, and their outside walls are solid masonry. A hollow terra-cotta block crumbles around a standard anchor, so we use fasteners made for hollow masonry or, where the bracket allows, find a solid course. The concrete or cinder block line applies where the wall is block, $75, and the brick line where it is brick.

The bigger difference is the paperwork. Co-op boards and managing agents usually want a certificate of insurance naming the building before a contractor enters, and many limit work to weekday hours and require the service elevator to be reserved and padded. A COI from us is free; send us the building's sample certificate or requirements and we send it to the managing agent before the visit. We also bring floor protection for the hallway as well as the apartment, and we carry boxes and old TVs down in the service elevator, not the passenger car.

The newer condo or rental building: drywall, and what is behind it

The neighborhood's newer buildings are few but real. Corcoran lists 1115 Prospect Avenue as a four-story, 50-unit condo built in 1990, Wikipedia describes the ten-story Windsor Tower and a condo tower at 279 Prospect Park West in a former paint-factory storage building, and an April 2026 permit filing reported by Marketproof covers a three-story, six-unit building with commercial space at 1259 Prospect Avenue. Interior walls in buildings like these are usually drywall on wood or metal studs, and the outside walls and columns are often concrete. Drywall on wood studs is the base case on our list, with no wall charge. Metal studs need different fasteners and a careful look at the bracket's leverage, and that line is $30. Concrete columns and walls take the concrete line, $75.

Field Notes

Field Notes: How We Read a Windsor Terrace Wall Before We Drill It

These notes describe how our technicians work through a wall in an older house like the ones here. They are our routine, not a story about any one address.

Look at the room first. Before anything touches the wall, the technician sits where you will sit and looks at the wall from there. Where is the light coming from at four in the afternoon? Is there a mantel, and how deep is its shelf? Is there a radiator under the spot, or a heating pipe running up the corner? Where are the outlets, and is there a switch-controlled one?

Then knock and listen. Plaster on lath sounds slightly hollow between studs and dull over them. Plaster on brick sounds the same everywhere, solid and flat. Drywall sounds hollow and light. A wall that changes sound halfway across is a wall with a patch, a seam or an old doorway behind it.

Then scan, and do not trust the first answer. A deep-scan wall scanner reads through plaster better than a cheap stud finder, but metal lath, old wiring and nails in the lath confuse it. The technician scans in two directions and marks what he sees on painter's tape.

Then make one small hole where the TV will hide it. A single small test hole behind the future screen tells us more than any tool: wood lath, metal lath, gypsum board, brick dust or block. It also shows how thick the plaster is, which decides the length of the fasteners.

Only then mark the bracket. With the wall known and the height agreed with you from your seat, the bracket holes are marked, checked for pipes and cables, and drilled. If something unexpected turns up, such as a flue behind a chimney breast or a gas pipe in a partition, the technician stops and shows you before deciding anything.

Compliance notes for Windsor Terrace businesses

  • Things that stick out from the wall. The 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design limit wall-mounted objects with leading edges between 27 and 80 inches above the floor to a 4-inch projection into a circulation path. A TV on a full-motion arm in a corridor or along a path of travel needs to sit above that zone or out of the path.
  • Power behind the screen. Electricians on the Mike Holt forum cite NEC 400.12, which says flexible cords shall not be run through holes in walls or concealed by walls. A TV's own power cord does not belong inside the wall; the in-wall power kit on our list puts a proper receptacle behind the screen instead.
  • Landlord and insurance. Commercial leases usually require the landlord's approval for alterations and a certificate of insurance from any contractor. We provide the COI at no charge and can send it before the visit.

The Narrow Room

Nineteen Feet Wide: Fitting a Big Screen Into a Windsor Terrace Parlor

The width of a Windsor Terrace house sets the limits of its living room before anyone chooses a TV. A house 19 or 20 feet wide on the outside, like the Windsor Place houses Brownstoner and Brick Underground described, leaves less than that inside once the walls are counted, and the stair and hall usually take a strip along one side. In most row-house parlors the TV ends up on one of the long side walls or on the wall between the front and back parlors, and the sofa ends up facing it from across the room. That puts the screen closer to your eyes than it would be in a wider suburban living room, and closeness changes three things: how big the screen should be, how high it should go, and how much the angle matters for anyone sitting off to the side.

Size. Manufacturers and reviewers publish viewing-distance charts, and they disagree with each other, so we do not quote one. What we do on a visit is simpler: we hold a tape or a piece of cardboard cut to the screen's size at the spot, and you sit on your sofa and look. Most people know within ten seconds whether it fills the view comfortably or makes them turn their head to follow the action from one corner to the other. In a narrow parlor, the answer for many households is a 55-inch or 65-inch screen rather than the 75-inch that looked right in the store. If you already own a large TV, it can still work; the way to make it comfortable is usually a little more distance, by moving the sofa back toward the opposite wall, or a little less height.

Height. The most common mistake we are called to fix in this neighborhood is a screen hung too high, and it is usually because of a mantel. Without a mantel, we start with the middle of the screen close to the height of your eyes when you sit on the sofa you actually own, which is lower than most people expect, and adjust from there. With a mantel, the screen has to clear the shelf, and in a close room the result is a TV whose center may be several feet above your eye line. That is where a tilting bracket, a full-motion bracket or a pull-down mount earns its price, and we take up that subject in the next section.

Angle. In a narrow room, people sit along the length of the space, not in a fan in front of the screen. Somebody on the window seat or at the dining table in the back parlor sees the TV from a sharp angle. Two things help. One is the type of panel, which we cannot change for you but can mention if you are still shopping, since some panels hold their picture better from the side than others. The other is a full-motion arm, $40 on top of the base price, that lets you turn the screen toward the dining table for the game and back toward the sofa for the movie. On a party wall of old brick, an arm like that needs a firm fixing into the masonry, because the leverage at full extension is several times the TV's weight.

Light. Row-house parlors are lit from the ends. A front parlor facing west catches low afternoon sun straight across the room; a house facing the park or the cemetery may have no building across the street to block it. A screen on a side wall catches window glare at an angle, and a screen on the wall facing the windows catches reflections head-on. Before choosing the wall, we look at it at the time of day you watch most, or ask you to send a photo taken then.

Mantels and Chimney Breasts

The Mantel Question: What to Do With the Fireplace Wall in a Windsor Terrace House

A great many of the older houses here kept their mantels even after the fireplaces behind them stopped being used. Brownstoner's 2024 write-up of 139 Windsor Place described faux-painted stone mantels in the parlor and the dining room, one with its original insert, and a third mantel in the street-facing bedroom with built-in bookshelves on either side. That is a familiar picture in this neighborhood, and it puts the mantel wall front and center as the place where a TV "should" go. Sometimes it should. Often, in a narrow room, it should not. Here is how we decide.

What is behind the chimney breast. The projecting section of wall above a mantel is the chimney breast, and inside it is the flue, or several flues, running up from the fireplace and sometimes from the floors below. The breast is usually brick, sometimes plastered directly, sometimes furred out with lath. A bracket goes into the brick of the breast, avoiding the flue opening, and the cables cannot simply be dropped down inside the breast the way they can in a stud wall. If the fireplace is decorative only, as many here are, heat is not a concern for the TV. If it is a working fireplace or has been converted to gas or an insert, the TV above it will see heat rising off the mantel, and we will want to know that before we plan anything.

How high the screen ends up. The mantel shelf sets the bottom of the TV. Add the clearance above the shelf for decorations and air, add half the screen height, and the center of the picture can end up well above the line of your eyes from the sofa. In a deep room with the sofa far away, that is tolerable; the farther you sit, the smaller the angle. In a narrow Windsor Terrace parlor with the sofa close, it can mean watching with your chin up for two hours. The Brownstoner forum thread on mounting a TV over a bedroom fireplace in an old plaster-over-brick house said it plainly: mounted that high, a TV can be a pain in the neck, though less so when you watch lying in bed.

The options. There are four honest answers, and we price each from the list. A tilting bracket over the mantel, angled down, makes a high screen more comfortable; above-fireplace mounting adds $130 to the base price, and the brick line adds $65 where the breast is masonry. A pull-down mount such as the MantelMount lowers the screen to eye level when you watch and lifts it back above the mantel when you do not; that is $160 on top of the base price, plus the mount itself, at list price, $199.95 for the MantelMount MM340 or $399.95 for the MM700. The third answer is a different wall: a partition or the wall between the parlors, with the mantel left as the room's centerpiece. The fourth is no wall at all: the TV on its stand on a low console beside the fireplace, $119.99, with nothing drilled.

What we will not do. We do not fix a bracket into a mantel's wooden overmantel or to the mantel shelf, we do not drill into a flue, and we do not cut a carved or marbled mantel to make a TV fit. If the only workable plan for a fireplace wall involves any of those, we tell you it is the wrong wall.

Cable Concealment

Hiding the Wires in Plaster, Brick and Frame Walls Without Breaking the Code

Everyone wants the wires gone. How we hide them in a Windsor Terrace house depends on the wall, and on one rule that the internet tends to skip. Electricians on the Mike Holt forum quote NEC 400.12, which says flexible cords shall not be run through holes in walls or concealed by walls. A TV's power cord is a flexible cord. Pushing it into the wall behind the TV and out at an outlet below is common, and it is not allowed. The fix that the same electricians recommend, and the one we use, is to put a proper receptacle behind the screen, fed in an approved way, so the power cord plugs in behind the TV and never enters the wall. That is what the code-compliant in-wall power and cable kit on our list does, at $169, with a pass-through for HDMI and other low-voltage cables alongside.

Lath-and-plaster partitions. In a stud wall with plaster on lath, the in-wall kit usually works well. We cut two openings, one behind the screen and one near the floor outlet, with a sharp tool and a steady hand so the plaster does not crack outward, and fish the cables down the stud bay. The complication is fire blocking or a horizontal brace in the bay, which old houses sometimes have, and old wiring or pipes in the same cavity. We check with a scope through the first opening before cutting the second.

Plaster on brick. On a party wall or a chimney breast, there is no cavity to use. Cutting a channel into the brick for cables is a job for a mason, slow, dusty and permanent, and we do not recommend it for a TV. The practical choices are a paintable cord cover, $59, run neatly from the TV to the outlet and painted to match the wall; a cable run along the baseboard to a cabinet or another spot up to 25 feet away, $99; or choosing a different wall. A paintable cover on old plaster looks better than people expect, especially when it runs straight down from the center of the screen to a console that hides its bottom end.

From the cellar up. In a row house, the cable or fiber line usually enters at the cellar, and the router often lives there or in a hall closet. When the TV is on the parlor floor or above, getting a wired network connection to it can be worth more than any amount of WiFi tuning, because thick plaster and brick walls weaken wireless signals. A run from the cellar to the parlor floor can sometimes follow an existing path such as an old gas pipe chase or the space beside a radiator riser; sometimes it cannot be done without opening walls, and we say so. A run within one floor, up to 25 feet, is the $99 line on our list.

Your Questions Answered

TV Installation in Windsor Terrace: Questions Answered

These are situations that come up in Windsor Terrace homes and businesses: mantels with original inserts, bedrooms with shelves built around the chimney, walls shared with the neighbors, two-families turned into one house, bay windows, and bars where the whole room wants to see the game. Each is answered here in full and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

The fireplace wall

Our parlor mantel is faux-painted stone with its original cast-iron insert, and the shelf sticks out about eight inches. Will a TV above it clear the shelf, or will the screen bump it every time we tilt it?

It can clear it, but the shelf depth decides which bracket you can use. A flat bracket holds the screen only an inch or two off the wall, so the bottom edge has to sit high enough above an eight-inch shelf that candlesticks, frames and the heat of anything on the shelf are not right under it. A tilting bracket swings the bottom of the screen out as it tips down, which is fine as long as the tilt happens above the shelf, not into it. A pull-down mount reaches out past the shelf and lowers the screen in front of it, and its arm geometry has to be matched to the shelf depth and the mantel height, which is why we measure both before you order one. We never fix anything to the mantel itself; the bracket goes into the brick of the chimney breast above it. Mounting above a fireplace adds $130 to the base price and a pull-down mount $160, with the brick line on top where the breast is masonry.

In our front bedroom the chimney breast has built-in bookcases on both sides. How wide a TV will actually fit in that recess?

Measure the clear width between the faces of the two bookcases at the height where the screen will hang, not at the floor, because older built-ins are not always straight. Then take off a couple of inches on each side: one for air and one for your fingers when you lift the TV onto the bracket or reach for its buttons. The TV's width, not its diagonal size, is the number to compare, and it is printed in the specifications. If the breast projects past the bookcases, the TV may also stand proud of the shelves, which looks fine; if it is set back, the TV sits in a box and needs more side clearance so it does not trap heat. We also check that the bookcases do not block the bracket's arms if you choose a tilting or swinging mount. In a bedroom you usually watch from the bed, looking slightly up, so a screen above the mantel is more comfortable here than in a parlor.

Walls you share and walls that changed

The only long wall in our living room is the brick wall we share with the house next door, and their bedroom is on the other side. Will a soundbar fixed to that wall keep them awake?

It may. Brick carries low frequencies well, and a soundbar fixed directly to a party wall turns the wall into part of the speaker. The bass and the thump of explosions travel further than dialogue. If that wall is the only place for the TV, the TV itself on a bracket is rarely the problem; it is the sound that comes with it. We suggest leaving the soundbar on a console or a shelf below the screen rather than on the wall, which is $79 placed instead of $129 wall-mounted, keeping any subwoofer on the floor on a pad and away from the party wall, and using the soundbar's night mode. Rear speakers on stands, rather than on the shared wall, keep the surround effect in your room.

We combined the two apartments of our legal two-family into one house, and the TV wall in the new living room used to be the doorway between them. Can a bracket go across that patched section?

Yes, but not by treating it as one wall. A closed-up doorway is usually framed with new studs and covered with drywall, while the wall on either side is the original lath and plaster. The two surfaces are different thicknesses, the stud spacing does not line up, and the seam between them is a weak line where cracks like to start. We find the edges of the old opening with a scanner and a test hole, then decide whether the bracket can sit entirely on one side of the seam, which is best, or needs to span it. If it spans, we fix it to the solid framing on both sides or put up a plywood backer board, $85, across the whole area so the load is shared and the seam is not stressed. The wall line for the job is whichever surface the bracket's fasteners actually go into.

Our front room's street wall is a big three-sided bay. Can a TV go in the bay itself or on an arm that swings out into it?

The bay is the brightest part of the room, which is the main reason not to put a TV in it: you would be watching against the light from three windows. The narrow piers of wall between the windows in a bay are also rarely wide enough for a modern screen, and on a brick front they hide the window framing and lintels. What often works is the opposite: a screen on a side wall, on a full-motion arm, $40 on top of the base price, that faces the sofa set in front of the bay in the evening and folds flat in the day. If the bay is the only option, a small screen on a pier between windows is possible after we check what is behind it, but we will show you the glare from your seat first.

Sound, signals and the whole house

Our parlor is narrow, and the sofa backs onto the doorway to the back parlor. Is there any room for rear surround speakers?

Usually, yes, as long as they do not go on the walls. In a long, narrow room, the rear speakers end up close to the listener and to the side rather than behind, which still gives most of the effect. Floor stands either side of the sofa, like the ones in the photo above, are the common answer; they move when you rearrange, they leave no holes in old plaster, and they keep the sound off a shared brick wall. If the sofa sits in front of a doorway, the stands go beside the arms of the sofa and the speaker wire runs along the baseboard or under a rug. Wireless rear speakers paired with a soundbar avoid the wire across the doorway, at the cost of needing an outlet near each. A full home theater setup is $350 on our list; a Sonos system is $125 to set up; an A/V receiver is $129.

The internet comes into our row house at the cellar, the router sits there, and the new TV is two floors up in the parlor. Why does it keep buffering, and can you fix that?

Two floors of old plaster, wood joists and possibly brick between the router and the TV can weaken a WiFi signal badly, and the parlor TV ends up at the edge of the network. There are three fixes, in order of reliability. A wired connection from the router to the TV is best, and in a row house it sometimes follows an existing path upward beside a pipe or riser; whether that is possible is something we can only tell by looking. Moving the router up to the parlor floor, if the service line can be extended, helps every device in the house. A mesh WiFi system with a unit on each floor is the easiest and usually good enough for streaming. On the setup-only line, $195 for the first hour, a technician can test the signal at the TV, set up the connection and tell you which fix your house needs.

We are gutting the parlor floor this winter. What should our contractor put in the TV wall while it is open?

Three things, all cheap now and expensive later. First, solid blocking between the studs where the TV will hang, wide enough for any bracket you might choose, so the bracket can be fixed anywhere along it. Second, a recessed power and low-voltage box behind the screen position, with a receptacle installed by your electrician and a conduit or open pathway from it down to the console location, so cables can be pulled later without opening the wall. Third, a wired network cable from that location back to wherever the router will live. Take a photo of the open wall with a tape measure in the frame before it is closed; that photo is worth more to the next installer than any drawing. When the work is done, we come back and hang the TV on the base price, since the wall will by then be drywall on wood studs with blocking in it.

Buildings with rules and walls with weather

Our co-op on Prospect Park Southwest allows contractor work only on weekdays and wants the service elevator booked. How do you fit into rules like that?

We plan around them from the first call. Tell us the building's work hours and how the elevator is reserved, and we book a weekday slot that leaves time to finish well before the cutoff, since most single-TV jobs take a few hours at most. We send the certificate of insurance to the managing agent in advance, free, using the building's sample if it has one, and we ask whether the super needs to see it or let us in. The technician brings floor protection for the hall as well as the apartment, uses the service elevator for tools and boxes, and leaves the common areas as clean as he found them. If your board requires an alteration agreement for anything that opens a wall, a surface-mounted bracket with a paintable cord cover often avoids the need for one; your managing agent can confirm.

Our frame house has vinyl siding, and we want a TV on the outside wall over the back deck. Is that a reasonable thing to fix to siding?

Never to the siding itself, which is a thin skin that flexes and cracks. The bracket goes through the siding into the wall framing of the house, and we have to find the studs from the outside, which is harder than from the inside. We seal every penetration so water cannot follow the screws into the wall, and we keep the bracket off the siding's seams. An outdoor TV, or a TV under a well-protected roof, matters too: an ordinary TV on an open deck will not last long. Outdoor mounting adds $90 to the base price, and outdoor brackets are on our list at list price, from the $149.99 Samsung Terrace bracket to the $589.99 SunBriteTV full-motion mount. Power has to come from a weather-rated outdoor receptacle, which, if you do not have one in the right spot, is an electrician's job.

We run a small bar on Prospect Avenue and the room is long and narrow. Where do screens go so the stools at the front and the tables in the back both see the game?

Rarely in one place. A single large screen on the back wall is too far from the front of the room, and one on a side wall is seen edge-on by half the bar. Two or three screens of moderate size, spaced along the room and angled slightly toward its center, usually give everyone a view without anyone turning around. Above the back bar is a natural spot if the bottles and shelving leave room; otherwise ceiling mounts, $85 on top of the base price, put screens over the bar where they block nothing. All the screens should be fed from one cable box or streaming device in a back-bar cabinet so they can show the same game, or different ones on a busy day. If any screen sits along a path people walk, it has to be high enough or out of the way so nobody walks into its edge.
