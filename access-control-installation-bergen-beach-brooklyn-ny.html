The Neighborhood

Why Bergen Beach Properties Need Access Control

Bergen Beach began as an island. Community Board 18's history of the area says it was once Bergen Island in Jamaica Bay, mostly low and marshy, until a seawall built in the late 1890s joined it to the Mill Basin uplands and created the embankment the Belt Parkway later used. Today the board describes Bergen Beach and its Georgetowne section as zoned predominantly for one- and two-family homes, with small commercial overlays and some recreational waterfront uses. Georgetowne, bounded by Ralph Avenue, Avenue N, Avenue U and Paerdegat Basin, was built up slowly from the 1960s through the 1980s, mostly with one- and two-family houses and some low-rise condos. That history tells us almost everything about the doors we are asked to control here.

The first thing it tells us is that the people who decide are homeowners, not managing agents. In most of Brooklyn an access control project starts with a board vote or a landlord's budget. In Bergen Beach it usually starts at a kitchen table, with a family that has outgrown a ring of keys. Detached and semi-detached houses here tend to have more outside openings than a city rowhouse: a front door, a side door off the driveway, a door from an attached garage, sometimes a walk-out basement at the back and a sliding door onto a yard. Each one is a way in, and each one has its own set of keys that has been copied, lent and lost over the years.

The second thing it tells us is that many houses hold two households. The board's zoning description and the listings we read both point to a lot of two-family houses, many with an owner upstairs and a rental unit below or behind, often with its own private entrance. That arrangement makes the door plan a matter of fairness as much as hardware. The tenant needs a door that is truly theirs, the owner needs a way to reach the boiler and the meters, and neither household should be able to read the other's comings and goings from an app. New York's Multiple Dwelling Law treats a building occupied by no more than two families as a private dwelling, so most of the city's entrance-door and tenant-data rules for apartment buildings do not reach these houses. We still set them up as if they did, because those rules are good practice for anyone sharing a house.

The third thing is that some of the neighborhood is gated. Mill Harbor, on Royce Street, is sold as a gated community; a listing for one of its homes gives the year built as 2005, notes an assigned carport, a clubhouse and a pool, and an association fee that covers common-area and exterior maintenance, snow removal and trash. Another listing for the complex says an intercom connects to the front desk. In a complex like that, the association controls the vehicle gate, the pedestrian gate and the common doors, and an owner controls the door to their own home. Our work there has two clients at once, and the plan has to respect both.

The fourth thing is water. When the city updated its hurricane evacuation map, Brownstoner reported that Zones 1 and 2, the first to be told to leave, include all of Bergen Beach and Mill Basin. News 12 has reported residents fed up with street flooding after heavy rain. For access control that is not a reason to worry, it is a reason to plan: the controller and its battery go high and dry, not on a basement floor; the exterior hardware is sealed and chosen for wet, salty air; and the system is set up so that a family that has evacuated can see from their phones whether the house still has power and every door is still shut.

We do not sell any of this with fear. CrimeGrade, which models crime from reported data rather than publishing police figures, gives Bergen Beach an A+ for burglary and estimates its rate at well under the national average. The stories that reach us from the 63rd Precinct are smaller ones. In March 2022 the Canarsie Courier reported that a porch pirate stealing packages in Bergen Beach had been identified because neighbors posted their camera footage online, and that high-end cars in nearby New Mill Basin were being stolen with universal key fobs the neighborhood coordination officer said cost as little as $20. Neither of those is solved by a lock alone, but both are about the same question: who can open what on your property, and how would you know?

What the materials mean for the install

Brick is the forgiving case. Readers, keypads and stations are fixed into the mortar joints rather than the face of the brick, so a later change leaves a joint to repoint, not a broken brick. Cable enters through one sealed hole, usually low and close to the door frame where it is hidden by the trim, and drops to the basement inside the wall. Stucco fronts need more care: we score and core a clean hole rather than hammer-drilling through, seal the edge of the hole and the back of every exterior box, and avoid fixing anything across a stucco control joint.

Siding is a different job again. Vinyl siding moves with heat and cold, so a station is mounted on a proper mounting block fixed through the siding into the sheathing behind it, never screwed tight to the siding itself, where it would crack the panel or hold water against the wall. Fiberglass and insulated steel doors, common on newer and renovated houses here, are left alone wherever possible: the strike goes in the frame, the keypad lever goes in the door's existing bore, and nothing is cut into the door slab that would break its seal or its maker's warranty.

The most important material in Bergen Beach is not a wall at all. It is the air. Salt, damp and freeze-thaw cycles get into exterior housings, cable entries and lock bodies and work on them all winter. Every outside device we fit here is rated for outdoor use, sealed where its cable enters, and mounted where rain runs off it rather than into it. The controller, its power supply and its battery stay indoors, high on a wall, never on a basement or garage floor.

The Garage, the Side Door and the Walk-Out: Planning a Detached Bergen Beach House

A detached house in Bergen Beach often has five or six outside openings, and the family uses only one or two of them. That is the starting point for a sensible plan. We ask the family which door they come in by after work, which door the children use after school, which one a home attendant or a relative would use, and which ones nobody opens from one month to the next. On most houses the answer is the side door off the driveway and the door from the garage into the house. Those get the controlled locks. The others get contacts that report when they open, which costs far less and tells you just as much.

The garage entry deserves special attention, because it is the door people forget. When the overhead garage door is raised, the door from the garage into the kitchen or hall becomes the front door of the house, and it is very often left unlocked because it feels like an inside door. A strike on that door, a keypad or reader on the garage side and a contact that reports when it is left unlatched close a gap that no front-door lock touches. If the garage holds a freezer, tools or bicycles, the overhead door can also report its position so you can see from a phone whether it was left up.

A listing for a single-family Bergen Beach house describes a full walk-out basement with a half bath, which is common on lots that slope toward the back. A walk-out door at yard level, out of sight of the street, is the kind of door that is opened twice a summer and checked never. It rarely needs a keypad. It needs a contact, a solid deadbolt and a place on the app's list of doors so that the family sees it the same way they see the front door. Sliding doors onto a deck or patio are treated the same way: a contact on the panel, and a secondary bar or pin lock, because a sliding door cannot take an electric strike the way a hinged door can.

Last comes the question of power and cable. Most detached houses here have a basement or a garage that makes the cable run short: down inside the wall beside the door, along the basement ceiling and up to a controller near the electrical panel. The controller goes high on the wall, with its battery, on a dedicated outlet rather than one shared with a freezer or a sump pump. In a house that has taken water before, it goes on the first floor instead, inside a closet or utility room, and we run the extra cable to get it there.

What we leave in place

We keep the house's existing deadbolts on every door that gets a contact rather than a lock, and we keep a working key cylinder on every door that gets an electric lock. Nothing about a house's access control should depend on one app, one phone or one battery. If the system is down, the family opens the door with a key, the same way they always have.

Two Households, One House: The Two-Family With a Rental Entrance

One Bergen Beach listing we read describes a brick two-family house as an owner's triplex with a separate one-bedroom rental unit with its own private entrance, an interior garage and a private driveway. That layout is common here, and it is the one that most needs a careful door plan. The owner and the tenant live a few feet apart and share a driveway, a basement and often a boiler, but each needs a door that belongs to them.

We start by giving each household its own independent system or, on one controller, completely separate accounts. The tenant administers their own door: they choose their code, issue fobs to their own household and see their own door's history. The owner administers theirs. Neither can read the other's log. Where the owner needs access to the tenant's side for emergencies, such as a leak or a no-heat call in January, that access is set up openly, agreed in writing between owner and tenant, and logged where the tenant can see it was used.

The second question is power. Many two-family houses have two electric meters. If the controller for the tenant's door sits on the owner's circuit and the owner turns off a breaker for a repair, or falls behind on a bill, the tenant's door is affected. We put each household's door on that household's own power, or on a common circuit if the house has one, and fit a battery large enough to ride out an ordinary outage. Either way, both doors always open from inside by hand.

The third is the shared spaces. Many two-families share a basement laundry or a boiler room, or have a door between the two units for the owner's use. A shared interior door is never put on a schedule that could trap someone on the wrong side of it. It gets a lock that always opens from the tenant's side, or a contact rather than a lock at all. When a tenant moves out, the owner deletes the tenant's credentials and the next tenant sets new ones on the first day.

New York's Multiple Dwelling Law defines a private dwelling as a building for no more than two families, and the city's Tenant Data Privacy Law is written for multiple dwellings, so these houses are usually outside both. We follow the spirit of the privacy law anyway: collect only what the door needs, keep logs only as long as they are useful, and never use the door to watch the other household.

Inside the Gate: Condo Associations in Georgetowne

Community Board 18 notes that Georgetowne was built with some low-rise condos alongside its houses, and complexes like Mill Harbor on Royce Street are sold on being gated. In a gated complex the perimeter is the association's responsibility. The pedestrian gate, the vehicle gate, the clubhouse, the pool enclosure and any mail or storage rooms are common property, controlled by the board and usually run day to day by a managing agent or a front desk. The door to each home belongs to its owner, subject to the association's rules.

The association's system has three jobs. It has to let residents in quickly, by fob, by phone or by windshield tag at the vehicle gate. It has to let visitors call the right home, which in a complex with a front desk means deciding what happens when the desk is closed: a call station at the pedestrian gate can ring the desk during the day and the resident's phone at night. And it has to let the contractors who keep the complex running, such as snow removal, trash pickup and landscaping, come and go on their own schedules, ideally with one credential per company so that a lost card or a change of contractor is a five-minute change rather than a new code for everyone.

The board also has to think about leaving. Every gate we install in a Bergen Beach complex is planned for the day the city tells Zone 1 and 2 to evacuate. The gate has a way to be held open from the clubhouse or the app, a manual release that works with no power at all, and a battery that keeps the readers working while residents load their cars. Fire and police access is agreed with the board at the start, not discovered on the night it is needed.

Owners who want a keypad or a video station on their own front door need to read the association's rules first, because exterior changes in a condo usually need the board's written approval. We can send the board a short description of the device, how it is mounted and what it records, so the request is easy to approve. The owner's device stays on the owner's account; the association's system never controls or records the owner's door.

Doors in an Evacuation Zone: Planning for Storms and Standing Water

No other part of a Bergen Beach access control plan matters as much as where the electronics live. All of Bergen Beach sits in the city's hurricane evacuation Zones 1 and 2, and News 12 has reported long-running street flooding in the neighborhood after heavy rain. Water and electronics do not mix, and a door controller standing in an inch of water is a door that will not open. So before we choose a keypad, we choose a dry, high spot for the controller, the power supply and the battery.

In a house with a basement that has never taken water, that spot is high on a basement wall, near the electrical panel. In a house that has, or in a garage at the level of the driveway, it is on the first floor, in a closet or utility room, even if that means a longer cable run. Exterior devices are outdoor-rated, sealed where the cable enters and mounted with a drip loop in the cable so water runs off rather than along it into the wall.

The door hardware is chosen with storms in mind too. On a house, the main door's strike is usually fail-secure, which means it stays locked if the power fails while the inside handle still opens it; nobody wants a house that unlocks itself when the power goes out during a storm. The battery is sized to keep the keypad or reader working for a day or more. And the app is set up to warn the family when the controller loses mains power, when the battery runs low, and when any door opens, so a family that has left for higher ground can watch the house from a relative's sofa.

After the water goes down, we check every exterior device for moisture before it is trusted again, test each door on battery and with no power, and replace anything that took water. A device that worked once after a flood can fail a week later as the corrosion sets in.

Georgetowne Shopping Center

About 147,000 square feet on Ralph Avenue between Avenues K and L, with large anchors and smaller national and local tenants. Tenants' back doors open onto service areas the landlord controls, so a reader goes on the tenant's own door and nothing beyond it.

Avenue N

Avenue U

The southeast edge of Georgetowne toward Mill Basin. Businesses here share blocks with houses, so cameras and stations at a shop door are aimed at the shop's own entrance, not the neighbors'.

Home offices and practices

Professionals who see clients in a ground-floor room of a house. A separate controlled door for clients, kept apart from the family's own entrance and the rest of the house.

Associations and clubs

Condo clubhouses and the recreational waterfront uses the board describes. Gates, clubhouse doors and storage on schedules a volunteer board can manage without an IT department.

Shops in a shopping center

A tenant in a shopping center usually controls less than it thinks. The front door and the storefront are the tenant's, but the rear service corridor, the loading area and often the roof belong to the landlord, and the lease says what a tenant may change. Before quoting a reader on a back door in a center like Georgetowne, we ask for the lease's alterations clause and the landlord's tenant guidelines, and we send the landlord a one-page description of the device, the cable route and the power source. The reader goes on the tenant's own door, the controller stays inside the tenant's space, and the landlord's corridor is not touched. When the tenant leaves, the hardware comes out cleanly or stays by agreement.

Closing up alone

The commonest worry we hear from shop and restaurant owners on Ralph Avenue is not a break-in. It is the moment an employee steps out of the back door at closing, alone, into a dark lot. A video station or a camera on the outside of the back door, with a small screen or a phone view inside, lets that person look before they open. A light on a sensor over the door helps more than any lock. And a code per employee shows the owner who closed and when, without anyone having to call in.

Rules that apply to Bergen Beach business doors

  • Egress first. Under the NYC Building Code, an electrically locked door on a path of exit has to release when the power fails and when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate, and a magnetic lock needs a release built into the door hardware that works with one hand. A shop's exit door always opens from inside without a key, card or code.
  • Listed equipment. Controllers and locks on exit doors should be listed to UL 294 for access control; we fit listed equipment and keep the listings in the job file for the landlord and the insurer.
  • Apartments above shops. Where a building on Avenue N or Ralph Avenue holds three or more apartments, it is a multiple dwelling under state law, and the city's Tenant Data Privacy Law applies to any fobs, apps or other smart access on the residential door: a written policy, consent, limits on what is collected and destruction of authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized.
  • Licensing. New York State requires a license to install, service or maintain security systems for others. AESS works under license #12000287431, and certificates of insurance are issued to landlords and associations on request.
  • Employees. A door log records when each employee's code was used. Tell staff in writing that the door system records entries, keep the log for business purposes only, and limit who can read it.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Bergen Beach: Questions Answered

Detached houses, garages and the back of the house

The door from our attached garage opens straight into the kitchen. Should it be on the system, or is the front door enough?

Put it on the system. Once the overhead door is up, the garage entry is the easiest way into the house, and it is the door families most often leave unlocked because it feels like an inside door. We fit a strike or an electrified lever lock on it, a keypad or reader on the garage side, and a contact that tells you if it is left unlatched. Many families end up using it as their main entrance.

If the overhead garage door has its own opener, a position sensor can show on the same app whether it is up or down. We do not make the overhead door open from the app unless you want that, and we never set the garage entry to unlock automatically when the overhead door rises.

Our house has a walk-out basement door and a sliding door to the yard as well as two regular doors. Which openings get readers and which just get sensors?

Readers or keypads go on the doors people use to come and go, which on most Bergen Beach houses means the side door and the garage entry. The walk-out basement door and the sliding door get contacts that report when they open, plus good mechanical locks. Sliding doors cannot take an electric strike the way hinged doors can, and a walk-out door that is opened a few times a summer does not need a keypad.

All of them appear in the same app, so you can see at a glance that the back of the house is shut. Adding a reader to a contact-only door later is straightforward if the cable is run to it at the start, and we usually run it.

Car thieves in New Mill Basin were opening cars with cheap universal fobs. Does a door fob have the same weakness?

It can, if it is the old kind. The Canarsie Courier reported in 2022 that thieves in New Mill Basin were using universal key fobs, which the neighborhood coordination officer said cost as little as $20, to open high-end cars. Door credentials have a parallel problem: the older 125 kHz proximity fobs still sold for doors carry no encryption and can be copied with an inexpensive device. A copied door fob opens the door as if it were the real one.

We install encrypted credentials, which cannot be copied that way, and phone keys that are tied to the owner's phone. If your house or complex already uses fobs, we can tell you which kind they are on the first visit, and whether the existing readers can read encrypted credentials or need replacing.

The house next door has stood empty for over a year. Should that change what we put on our own doors?

A little. CrimeGrade counts about 15.7 percent of Bergen Beach homes as empty, roughly twice the national share, and notes that places with many empty homes tend to report more burglary and vandalism. An empty house next door gives someone cover, a place to wait and a yard to cross. The doors on your side facing it, often the side door and the back, are the ones to strengthen first.

Access control is only part of the answer. A contact on every door facing the empty lot, a motion light on that side and a camera aimed along your own side yard, not into the neighbor's property, do more together than a keypad alone. Tell the 63rd Precinct's neighborhood coordination officer about a house that is open or being used; their community council meets monthly at 2335 Bergen Avenue.

Two-family houses and rental units

If we as owners ever use our emergency access on the tenant's door, should the tenant be told, and can the system show it?

Yes on both. We set up owner emergency access on a tenant's door as a separate, named credential, so every use appears in the tenant's own log with the date and time. The tenant sees it; the owner cannot delete it. That protects both of you: the tenant knows the access is not being used casually, and the owner has a record that shows exactly when and why they went in.

Agree in writing, before move-in, what counts as an emergency, such as a leak, no heat or a fire alarm, and how the tenant will be told afterwards. For anything that is not an emergency, the owner asks first and the tenant opens the door, just as they would with keys.

Our two-family has separate electric meters. Whose power should run the lock on the tenant's door?

The tenant's, or a common circuit if the house has one. If the tenant's door runs off the owner's meter, a breaker the owner switches off for a repair, or an owner's unpaid bill, can affect the tenant's door, and that is not fair to either side. We put each household's door controller and power supply on that household's own circuit, label the breaker, and add a battery so an ordinary outage does not matter.

Whatever the power arrangement, the tenant's door always opens from inside by hand, and its lock is usually fail-secure, so it stays locked if the power fails rather than swinging open. The tenant keeps a working key as well.

Gated condo complexes

Our complex's front desk answers the gate intercom during the day. Can visitors reach the right resident's phone at night instead?

Yes. A video call station at the pedestrian gate can follow a schedule: during desk hours, visitors press one button and reach the desk; after hours, they choose the resident from a directory and the call rings that resident's phone or indoor screen, where they can see the visitor and open the gate. The schedule follows the desk's real hours, including holidays, and the board can change it from the app.

Each resident decides which phones ring, so a household with two adults can have both phones ring together. Residents who prefer not to use a smartphone can keep an indoor screen or a call to a landline where the station supports it.

Our snow removal and trash contractors come through the gate before dawn. Should each company have its own credential, or is one shared vendor code enough?

Give each company its own credential, and if possible each crew member. A shared vendor code ends up with every contractor the association has ever used, and changing it means telling every current vendor at once. Separate credentials can be limited to the hours and gates each company actually needs: the snow contractor only when it snows, the trash crew only on pickup mornings, the landscaper only in season.

When the association changes contractors, the old company's credential is switched off in a minute and nobody else is affected. The board can see which company came in and when, which also helps settle disputes over whether a service visit actually happened.

If the city orders an evacuation before a storm, what should our complex's gate be set to do?

Plan it now, not on the night. Bergen Beach is in evacuation Zones 1 and 2, so a complex here should decide in advance who can hold the gate open, how, and for how long. We set up a one-tap hold-open from the clubhouse panel or the board's app, a manual release on the gate that works with no power, and a battery large enough to keep readers and the call station working while residents load their cars and leave.

The board should also decide when the gate closes again, and how residents and emergency crews get back in once the storm has passed. We write those decisions into the hand-over guide so a new board or a new managing agent can find them.

Shops, restaurants and offices

Our store's back door opens onto a receiving corridor the shopping center landlord controls. Can we put a reader on our door without touching the landlord's side?

Usually, yes. The reader or keypad goes on your side of your own back door, or on the frame inside your space, and the controller sits in your stockroom or office. Cable stays inside your premises. Nothing is fixed to the landlord's corridor walls, and the corridor doors stay on the landlord's own system.

Your lease almost certainly requires the landlord's written consent for alterations, so we prepare a one-page description of the device, the cable route, the power source and how the door still opens from inside without a key. Most landlords approve that kind of request quickly because it does not touch their property.

Our staff close up late and leave by the back door into the parking lot. Can they see outside before they open it?

Yes. A camera or video station on the outside of the back door, with a small indoor screen beside the door or a view on the closing employee's phone, lets them check the lot before stepping out. Add a motion light over the door, and the employee sees a lit, empty lot or knows to wait.

Each employee gets their own code, so the door log shows who closed and when, and the owner can get a message when the back door is locked for the night. If the lot is shared with other businesses, the camera is aimed at your own door and the path to the cars, not at your neighbors' entrances.

Storms and time away

When we leave the house for a storm, what can the door system tell us while we are away?

Quite a lot, if it is set up for it. The app can tell you that the controller has lost mains power and is running on its battery, how much battery is left, whether any door has opened, and whether every controlled door is still locked. If the house's internet goes down with the power, those messages stop, so the app also tells you when the system has gone offline. That is itself useful information about what is happening at home.

Before you leave, check in the app that every door shows closed and locked, and switch off any codes you do not want used while you are away. When you return, have the exterior devices checked before you rely on them if there has been water near them.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Bergen Beach

These are generalizations we hear repeated in quick online answers, vendor blogs and national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each is set against what a Bergen Beach door actually needs. Where we quote a figure, we name where it came from; we do not attribute any claim to an AI answer we cannot cite.

"Access control means a monthly software bill for every door."

Many national guides are written by companies that sell cloud platforms, and their numbers reflect that. Kisi's own cost guide, for example, lists software licensing and maintenance at $30 to $200 a month and a basic door license at $600 to $1,600 a year per door. For an office with dozens of doors and an IT department, a subscription like that can make sense.

For a Bergen Beach house, a two-family or a small shop, it usually does not. A local controller with an app runs the doors without any subscription, and the family owns it outright. Cloud management is useful for a condo association whose board changes every year or a business with several sites, and we offer it where it earns its cost, but it is a choice, not a requirement.

Ask any installer, including us, to put the ongoing cost in writing before you sign. A system that is cheap to install and expensive to keep is not cheap.

"Access control is for offices and apartment buildings."

That is how the industry describes itself, and most of its marketing shows a lobby or a glass office door. Bergen Beach does not look like that. Community Board 18 describes it as predominantly one- and two-family homes, and the projects we are asked for here are side doors, garage entries, rental unit entrances and the gates of a few condo complexes.

The same parts that run an office door, a strike, a reader, a contact and a controller, run a house door just as well, and on a house they solve problems an office does not have: a teenager who loses keys, a home attendant who comes three days a week, a tenant who moves out, a contractor redoing the kitchen.

The difference is scale and tone. A house system has two or three doors and a few users, and it should feel like a better front door, not like a workplace.

"A smart lock from the hardware store does the same job."

For one door and one household, sometimes it does. A battery keypad deadbolt is quick to fit, and Vivint's own article puts a basic one at roughly $50 to $100. The same article gives battery life as "a few months to about a year" in one place and "six months to two years" in another, which tells you how much it depends on use and weather, and it notes that Bluetooth control works only within about thirty feet.

The limits show up on Bergen Beach houses with several doors and two households. Each smart lock is its own device, often on its own app, and many do not relock themselves when the door closes unless auto-lock is set and the door actually latches. A wired strike with a door contact relocks every time and reports when the door has not latched.

Smart locks also live entirely in the door, outside, in the cold and damp. A wired system keeps its electronics indoors, high and dry, and puts only a sealed reader outside.

"Landlords have to follow the city's smart-access privacy law."

Some do, and many Bergen Beach owners do not. The city's Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, applies to smart access systems in multiple dwellings, and New York's Multiple Dwelling Law defines a building for no more than two families as a private dwelling, not a multiple dwelling. An owner-occupied two-family house with a keypad on the rental entrance is usually outside the law.

That does not mean anything goes. A tenant who feels watched through their own front door will not stay long, and a door log used as evidence in a dispute can cut both ways. We set up two-family houses as if the law applied: each household administers its own door, logs are kept only as long as they are useful, and the owner's emergency access is visible to the tenant.

Where a building holds three or more apartments, for example over a shop on Avenue N, the law does apply, and we provide the written policy and consent forms it requires.

"Bergen Beach is quiet, so ordinary keys are fine."

Bergen Beach is quiet by most measures. CrimeGrade's model grades it A+ for burglary, and we say so happily. But access control is less about crime figures than about keys: who has one, who used to have one and who might have copied one. In a house that has been in a family for decades, that list is usually longer than anyone expects.

Keys cannot be switched off, and they leave no record. A code can be removed in a minute when a contractor finishes or a tenant moves out, and the door can tell you it was left open. CrimeGrade also counts about one Bergen Beach home in six as empty, and an empty house is the one where nobody notices a door left ajar.

The porch pirate the Canarsie Courier reported in 2022 was caught because neighbors had cameras. A door station with a camera at the door people actually use, and a sensible plan for parcels, would have made the thefts harder in the first place.

"Put the controller in the basement next to the electrical panel."

It is the standard advice, and in most of Brooklyn it is right: the panel is there, the cable run is short and the basement is out of the way. In Bergen Beach it needs a second look. The neighborhood is in hurricane evacuation Zones 1 and 2, and News 12 has reported repeated street flooding after heavy rain.

In a basement that has never taken water, we mount the controller and battery high on the wall, well above the floor, on a dedicated outlet. In a basement that has, or in a garage at driveway level, we move it to the first floor, into a closet or utility room, and accept the longer cable run.

A flooded controller fails quietly, sometimes days after the water is gone, as corrosion spreads. A dry one keeps the doors working, and the alerts coming, through the storm.

"Any key fob is more secure than a metal key."

Not if it is the old kind. Unencrypted 125 kHz proximity fobs, still widely sold for doors, can be copied with an inexpensive device, and a copied fob leaves no sign that it was copied. The Canarsie Courier's report on cars being opened with $20 universal fobs in New Mill Basin is a different technology, but the lesson is the same: a cheap radio credential is only as safe as its encryption.

We install encrypted credentials and phone keys, and on a takeover we check which kind of fob an existing system uses before reusing anything. If a condo complex's gate still runs on old fobs, moving to encrypted credentials is often the first and cheapest improvement the board can make.

Whatever the credential, keep a mechanical key backup and keep the house fob off the car key ring, so a stolen car does not come with the house attached.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Walk Every Opening With a Flashlight

This describes how we approach a detached house, not a particular customer. Before we talk about keypads, we walk the outside of the house with the owner and a flashlight, and we count every opening: front door, side door, garage entry, overhead garage door, walk-out basement, sliding door, the basement window someone once used as a door. Families are often surprised by the count. Then we ask who uses each one, and the list of doors that matter shrinks to two or three.

Next we go down to the basement, or into the garage, and look at the floor. A tide line on the wall, rust at the bottom of the water heater or a sump pump that runs often tells us where the controller must not go. We look for the electrical panel, a clear stretch of wall above any water mark and a straight run along the joists to the doors that matter. Only then do we check each door itself: does it close and latch on its own, and is the jamb solid where the strike will go? A good lock on a door that does not latch protects nothing, and a good controller on a wet floor will not last a season.

FAQ

Bergen Beach Access Control FAQ

Will a Bergen Beach keypad work when we are wearing winter gloves?
+

It will if it has physical keys. We fit outdoor keypads with raised, backlit buttons that can be pressed through ordinary gloves and found in the dark, rather than glass touch panels, which need a bare finger. Fobs and phone keys also work without taking gloves off, so most families use a fob in winter and the code as the backup.

Can a Bergen Beach door station play a message to couriers when nobody answers?
+

Many stations can play a short recorded message when a call goes unanswered, such as asking a courier to leave parcels in a particular place or to call back. Whether yours can depends on the model, so tell us at the visit if it matters. The recording should never say that the house is empty, only where to leave things.

For a Bergen Beach house, is a locked parcel box better than giving couriers a door code?
+

For most houses, yes. A code that lets couriers into a vestibule or a garage also lets them into part of your home, and drivers change constantly. A locked parcel box by the side door or inside a front gate keeps deliveries out of sight without opening the house, and a camera on the door station records each drop-off. Codes for couriers make more sense in a condo complex's package room.

Could our dog set off the exit sensor inside a Bergen Beach front door and unlock it?
+

It should not, if the sensor is chosen and aimed properly. On a house we usually use a push button or the door's own handle to signal an exit rather than a motion sensor, and where a motion sensor is used we aim it high and narrow so that it sees a person reaching for the door, not a pet crossing the hall. We test it with your pet at hand-over.

Should the house fob for a Bergen Beach home be kept on the same key ring as the car keys?
+

Better not. A stolen car whose key ring carries the house fob, with the registration in the glove box, gives a thief both the address and the way in. Keep the house fob on a separate ring or use a phone key, and if a car is ever stolen with a house fob in it, delete that fob from the app straight away.

Can we get a firm price for Bergen Beach access control from photos alone?
+

Photos let us tell you which line of the Brooklyn list your job falls in, and often roughly where in that range. The firm written price comes after one visit, because the things that move it, such as where the controller can go, the state of the frame and whether the basement has taken water, need to be seen in person.

Can the system warn us if a parent with memory loss opens a Bergen Beach door at night, without locking them in?
+

Yes. A contact on each outside door can send an alert to your phone, or sound a chime in your bedroom, when a door opens during the hours you choose. The door still opens normally from inside, because nobody should ever be locked inside a house, but you know straight away and can go and help. For a medical or safety plan, talk to your parent's doctor as well.

Will a keypad or strike void the warranty on our Bergen Beach house's fiberglass front door?
+

It should not if the door slab is left alone. We put the strike in the frame and the keypad lever lock in the door's existing bore, so nothing is cut into the fiberglass. Read your door maker's warranty first, because some limit changes to the door or its hardware, and send it to us if you are unsure.

Can a Bergen Beach door station ring two phones at once, with whoever answers first opening the door?
+

Yes. Most stations we install can ring several phones and an indoor screen together, or in order, and the first person to answer can see the visitor, talk and open the door. Each household chooses its own phones, and the other phones stop ringing once someone answers.

Should a Bergen Beach front door unlock by itself when our car pulls into the driveway?
+

We advise against it. Location-based unlocking depends on a phone's location being accurate, and a door that unlocks because a phone is nearby can unlock when you are only driving past or sitting in the car.
