The Neighborhood

Why Fort Greene Properties Need Intercom Installation

Fort Greene covers a little over half a square mile between Flushing Avenue and the Navy Yard to the north, Flatbush Avenue Extension and Downtown Brooklyn to the west, Atlantic Avenue to the south and Vanderbilt Avenue and Clinton Hill to the east. Most of its side streets, among them South Portland, South Oxford, Cumberland, Carlton and Adelphi, were built up with Italianate brick and brownstone rowhouses in the 1850s and 1860s, and building had largely stopped by the 1870s. Those houses were designed with a bell pull at the parlor door and a service entrance under the stoop. Nobody who built them imagined three or four separate households living inside, each needing to know who is at the door and each wanting to open it from a different floor.

That is exactly how many of them are used now. Brownstoner's 2016 feature on one Fort Greene house describes an 18-foot-wide, five-story brownstone divided into three apartments, a one-bedroom on the garden level, a two-bedroom duplex on the parlor and second floors, and a two-bedroom duplex at the top, each "a discrete apartment with complete privacy" with its own internal stair. A house like that has one street door at the top of the stoop and possibly a second under it, and the household at the top is four flights of stairs above the person ringing. A doorbell wired to the parlor hall, or a single video doorbell on one person's phone, does not serve that house. An intercom does: a button for each household, a station or phone on each floor that needs one, and a release so the person upstairs can let a guest in without walking down.

The second thing that shapes the work is the facade. The Fort Greene Historic District and the BAM Historic District were both designated in 1978, five years after neighbors first proposed the Fort Greene district, and they cover most of the rowhouse blocks. The Historic Districts Council lists late Greek Revival, Italianate, Anglo-Italian, French Second Empire and neo-Grec houses in the district. Anything mounted on the front of those houses, a panel, a camera, a light or a run of conduit, falls under the Landmarks Preservation Commission. The Commission's rules for intercoms are not complicated, but they are specific: one panel per door opening, mounted in or next to the doorway or on a wall or fence in front of it, attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, never through cast iron or a decorative surface, and with conduit hidden in plain masonry or limited to about ten inches of visible run. Planning to those rules from the start is much cheaper than fixing a panel that was mounted in the wrong place.

The third is the mix of buildings that are not rowhouses at all. Fort Greene has two large public housing campuses, the Walt Whitman Houses and the Ingersoll Houses, whose entry systems belong to NYCHA. It has a 41-story landmark tower at 1 Hanson Place whose upper floors became condominiums between 2005 and 2007. It has new rental buildings such as The Garrison at 151 Carlton Avenue, finished in 2025 with 63 units on eight floors, a virtual doorman and a package room. And it has the shopping streets, Fulton Street, Myrtle Avenue and DeKalb Avenue, where a restaurant or a shop occupies the ground floor and the apartments above share a narrow door beside it. Each of those front doors needs something different, and a page that treats them all as one job would be useless to the people who actually live and work behind them.

The last reason is that old intercoms here fail in a predictable way. A brownstone that has been renovated floor by floor over forty years usually has a buzzer system installed during one conversion and then cut, spliced, painted over and extended during the next three. BrickUnderground's repair guide quotes Daniel Vasquez of Paragon Security and Locksmith on older systems: "It's all in the wire connections." In our experience that is right more often than owners expect. The handsets may be decades out of date, but the wire inside the walls is often sound, and finding that out before anyone opens a wall is the first step of every Fort Greene job we plan.

Eight Fort Greene facts that shape an intercom plan

  • Two historic districts, both from 1978. The Fort Greene Historic District and the BAM Historic District were designated in the same year. The same Landmarks guidebook rules apply to an intercom panel in either one. In 2001 the Historic Fort Greene Association proposed joining them along Fulton Street, from Ashland Place to South Oxford Street, and adding Willoughby Avenue between Carlton and Clermont Avenues; a proposal is not a designation, so check the Commission's map before assuming either way.
  • One intercom panel per door opening. The Commission's staff can issue a permit for an intercom panel that meets its criteria, including a limit of one panel and two light fixtures per door opening. A house with a parlor door and an under-stoop door has two door openings.
  • Replacing like for like is lighter paperwork. The guidebook says elevation drawings are not required for in-kind replacement of existing intercoms, light fixtures, security cameras and mailboxes. A new panel of a similar size in the same place is the simplest Landmarks application there is.
  • Divided houses rarely reach eight apartments. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires an intercom at the self-locking entrance of a class A multiple dwelling with eight or more apartments erected or converted after January 1, 1968, and the city's rule at 1 RCNY 42-01 sets requirements for older buildings of eight or more. Most divided Fort Greene brownstones have two to four apartments, so an intercom there is a choice the owner makes, not a legal requirement, although HPD's guidance still calls for building entrance doors in all multiple dwellings to be self-closing.
  • The city sets the mounting heights where the rule applies. In the buildings 1 RCNY 42-01 covers, no push button may be more than six feet from the floor, and the speaking and listening device must be between four and five feet from the floor. We use the same heights at every Fort Greene door because they suit people at the top of a stoop as well as on a sidewalk.
  • App and fob entry is regulated. The city's Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, applies to owners of multiple dwellings that use smart access systems such as fobs, cards and apps. It requires written or in-app consent, a data retention and privacy policy, and destruction of authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized.
  • Two large public housing campuses. The Walt Whitman Houses have 15 buildings and 1,659 apartments, and the Ingersoll Houses have 19 residential buildings. Their doors and intercoms are NYCHA's to maintain and replace, not something a private installer can be hired to change.
  • Busy commercial streets with apartments above. The Myrtle Avenue business improvement district counts 179 ground-floor retail businesses on 40 block faces in Fort Greene and Clinton Hill, and the Fulton Area Business Alliance covers 37 block faces. Many of those storefront buildings have a residential door beside the shop that needs its own entry system.

Many Fort Greene owners pair a new intercom with a camera over the door or a keypad or fob reader at the street, and the survey is the right moment to plan them together, because the camera count on the facade and the cable path into the house are shared. If that interests you, say so in your first message and we will price both.

Repair Calls in Fort Greene

Need Intercom Repair in Fort Greene? A Silent Top Floor, a Release That Hums but Will Not Open, or Buttons That Ring the Wrong Apartment

Plenty of Fort Greene calls are not about new systems at all. They start with something that stopped working, and they tend to follow a handful of patterns. The most common is one floor that went silent while the others still ring. In a divided brownstone that almost always means a break between the point where the wires leave the old control box and the station on that floor, and very often it follows work inside one apartment: a kitchen moved, a wall opened for a bathroom, baseboards replaced, a new floor laid over the old one. A cable stapled along a baseboard or hidden in a closet gets cut, painted or buried, and only that household loses the bell. The fix is a trace from the control point to the station, which is why we carry a tone generator and probe to every repair.

The second pattern is a release that buzzes or clicks but does not open the door. The intercom side is working; the problem is at the door. Original Fort Greene doors are heavy, they swell and shrink with the seasons, and a strike that was fitted to the door in one decade can bind against a frame that has moved since. Sometimes the door closer is pushing the latch into the strike so hard that the strike cannot release it. Sometimes the power supply is too weak for a strike on a long run of thin wire. We test the voltage at the strike, check the closer and the latch, and tell you honestly whether the fix is an adjustment, a different strike or a new power supply.

The third is buttons that ring the wrong apartment, which happens after a house is divided differently from the way it used to be. Four buttons from a time when the house had four apartments, three households now, and the labels never changed. Or the owner combined two floors into a duplex and one button now rings a station nobody uses. We map which button reaches which station before we change anything, and then either relabel and reprogram or replace the plate so the buttons match the house as it is.

The fourth is a system that simply died: no ring, no talk, no release anywhere in the house. That usually points to the power supply or the control unit, which in an older Fort Greene house can be in the cellar, under the stoop, inside a closet on the garden floor or somewhere above the vestibule ceiling. BrickUnderground's guide lists the same places to look for a fault, the base unit, the apartment station, the door strike and the power supply. Finding the box is half the job, and owners who send a photo of anything that looks like a transformer or a gray metal box save time on the visit.

The fifth is the complaint that the intercom "never works" for some people but works perfectly when the owner or the super tries it. Pete Carmello of Lock and Tech told BrickUnderground that "a lot of times it's user error," such as a button pressed too briefly or a caller who does not wait long enough. We take those reports seriously anyway, because a sticky button, a station whose volume was turned down, or a talk button that only works when held can look like user error and be a real fault. Repairs are booked like any other request, with a callback within two hours. If the repair will cost more than it is worth because the system has reached the end of its life, we say so and price both options side by side.

What We Install

Intercom Installation Services in Fort Greene

Every Fort Greene intercom plan starts with three questions. Which door do visitors actually come to, the top of the stoop, the gate under it, or a lobby? Who in the building needs to answer, and from which floor? And can the wire already inside the walls carry what the new system needs? These are the services we plan most often for the neighborhood's brownstones, apartment buildings, condos and storefront buildings.

Panels for stoop doors

A slim audio or video panel at the parlor door with one button or directory entry per household, mounted in or beside the doorway to suit a historic facade, with the release on the door behind it.

Under-stoop and garden entrances

A separate panel or video station for a garden apartment, office or studio that has its own door under the stoop, so its visitors never ring the households upstairs.

Stations on every floor

Video screens, audio handsets or hands-free stations placed where each household actually lives, including a second station in a duplex so nobody has to run down a flight to answer.

Phone and app answering

Calls from the door to residents' phones, alone or alongside a wall station, with each household managing its own users so a tenant's guests never reach the owner's phone.

Releases for original doors

Electric strikes and locks matched to heavy wooden parlor doors, iron gates and modern lobby doors, with a power supply sized for the run and a mechanical exit that always works.

Rewiring and riser repair

Testing every old pair from the control point to each apartment, reusing what tests good and replacing only the runs that fail, through cellars, closets and old service paths rather than new holes.

Lobby systems for apartment buildings

Directory panels, apartment stations, phone answering and front-desk routing for walk-ups, co-ops, condos and rentals with eight or more apartments, planned to the city's intercom rules.

Shop-and-apartment doorways

Separate entry systems for the residential door beside a storefront on Fulton Street, Myrtle Avenue or DeKalb Avenue, so shop deliveries and apartment visitors never share buttons.

Takeovers of existing systems

Documenting a system someone else installed, recovering the administrator account where the owner can prove ownership, and moving it into the owner's or board's name.

Building Specialists

Intercom Installation for Fort Greene's Architecture

For an intercom, a building is four things: the wall the street panel goes on, the path cable can take from the entrance to each apartment, the distance between each household and its door, and whether anyone staffs the entrance. Fort Greene has six kinds of building that differ on all four. The cards name them; the notes underneath explain how we work in each one.

Single-family rowhouses

Brick and brownstone houses from the 1850s and 1860s still lived in by one household, where the intercom is really a house system: the door, the kitchen, the top floor and sometimes the garden gate.

Divided brownstones

Houses split into two, three or four apartments, often an owner's duplex over a garden rental, with one stoop door, sometimes a second under it, and households stacked four or five floors high.

Prewar walk-ups and flats

Small apartment buildings with a vestibule, a narrow stair and an audio buzzer system that has been patched for decades, often just under or just over the eight-apartment line.

Landmark tower condos

Large buildings with a concierge, many apartments per floor and protected lobbies, like the former Williamsburgh Savings Bank Tower at 1 Hanson Place.

New mid-rise rentals

Recent elevator buildings such as The Garrison at 151 Carlton Avenue, with app-based entry, a virtual doorman, a package room and network cable in every apartment.

Storefront buildings

Shops, restaurants and offices at street level on Fulton Street, Myrtle Avenue and DeKalb Avenue, with apartments above reached through a separate door beside the shop.

Single-family rowhouses: an intercom for a house, not a building

In a Fort Greene house that still has one household, nobody is worried about apartment buttons. The problem is height. A typical rowhouse has a garden floor, a parlor floor and two or three floors above, and the front door is at the top of the stoop. Whoever is in the garden-floor kitchen cannot hear a bell at the parlor door, and whoever is on the top floor cannot see who is ringing. What these houses need is one panel at the door, a station on each floor where people spend time, and often a second, plainer station at the garden entrance under the stoop or at a back garden gate.

The wiring question is where the stations go and how the cable reaches them. Most of these houses already have a path for low-voltage cable that we can use: an old bell wire run, a dumbwaiter shaft, a chase beside a chimney breast, or the space behind a staircase stringer. We look for those before we suggest anything new. Where a house has been gut-renovated and the plaster replaced with drywall, routing is easier; where original plaster and moldings survive, we plan around them and, if a cable cannot reach a floor without opening a wall, we say so and offer a station that uses the network or Wi-Fi instead.

Owners of single-family houses also ask most often whether the intercom can do more than answer the door: call from the kitchen to the top floor, announce dinner, or let the person upstairs see the garden-floor entrance. Many systems can. We cover that in the design rather than as an extra, because adding a room-to-room feature later can mean pulling cable twice.

Divided brownstones: one door, several households, several floors

This is the building type that brings us to Fort Greene most often. The house has one street door at the top of the stoop, and behind it a shared vestibule and stair serving a parlor apartment or duplex and the apartments above. The garden apartment may share that door or may have its own entrance under the stoop. Each household wants its own button, its own way of answering and its own way to open the door, and none of them wants the others' visitors ringing their bell.

The design starts with a list of the households and the floors each one occupies. A duplex usually needs two stations, one on each level, so the person who answers does not have to run up or down a flight. The top-floor household needs a release time long enough for a guest to get from the sidewalk up the stoop and through the door before it locks again, and often a phone connection so a call can be answered from anywhere in a tall apartment. The garden apartment, if it has its own door, gets its own panel at that door, which the Landmarks rules allow because it is a separate door opening.

The wire is the second half. Divided houses were usually converted one floor at a time, and the old buzzer cables were run up whatever route was convenient that year. We test each pair, label it and map it. Where a pair is good it carries the new system, which is why we prefer two-wire video systems for these houses when the old cable tests well. Where it is not, we look for a route through the cellar, a closet stack or the stair hall that reaches the failed floor without disturbing the others. The goal is that no household loses its bell for more than the changeover itself.

Prewar walk-ups and flats: patched buzzers and the eight-apartment line

Fort Greene's small apartment buildings, the flats buildings and walk-ups mixed in among the rowhouses, usually have a vestibule with an outer door, an inner door, a bank of mailboxes and an audio buzzer panel that has been repaired many times. Some have seven apartments, some eight, some a dozen. That count matters, because Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a and the city's rule at 1 RCNY 42-01 both turn on buildings with eight or more apartments, and an owner who crosses that line through a conversion takes on a legal requirement as well as a practical one.

In these buildings the panel itself is rarely the hard part. A new directory panel fits the vestibule wall where the old one was, and the city's heights tell us where the buttons and the speaker go. The harder part is the riser, the cable that runs from the vestibule up through the building to each apartment. We test it pair by pair. In many prewar buildings it is in better shape than the equipment it connects, and a two-wire system can reuse it. Where it is not, the shortest new route is usually up the stair hall in a painted raceway or through a closet stack, and we show the board or owner the route before quoting it.

The inner and outer doors also need a decision. If the mailboxes sit between them, the release should open the inner door, and the outer door can stay on a latch or a separate lock for mail carriers. If both doors lock, both may need releases. We look at how the building actually uses its vestibule before we decide.

Landmark tower condos: protected lobbies and a concierge in the loop

The former Williamsburgh Savings Bank Tower at 1 Hanson Place is the clearest example in Fort Greene: a 41-story building finished in 1929, with its exterior designated a landmark in 1977 and parts of its interior in 1996, and condominiums created on the upper floors between 2005 and 2007. A building like that needs an entry system that starts at a concierge desk, reaches hundreds of apartments, and is installed inside spaces where the Landmarks Commission has a say about what can be fastened to the walls.

In a tower, the intercom is part of a larger system: a lobby panel or a desk station, apartment stations or phone answering for every unit, a door release that may also be tied to the building's access control and fire alarm, and a network behind it all. Our role is usually a defined piece of that system, a panel replacement, a desk station upgrade, apartment stations on a renovated floor, or a takeover of a system whose original installer has gone. We work through the managing agent and the board, and we prepare the photos and cut sheets any Landmarks application needs.

Because a tower's lobby is shared by hundreds of households, the changeover has to be planned so the desk is never without a way to reach an apartment and the door is never left unattended. We schedule panel swaps for the quietest hour the building can name and keep the old system working until the new one has been tested.

New mid-rise rentals: app entry, package rooms and privacy rules

Fort Greene's newer buildings, like The Garrison at 151 Carlton Avenue with 63 apartments on eight floors, usually arrive with a networked entry system already chosen by the developer: an app on residents' phones, a video panel at the door, sometimes a virtual doorman service and a package room. The work in these buildings is less about installing from scratch and more about extending, repairing or taking over what the developer left behind.

The common requests are a second panel at a side or garage entrance, wall stations for residents who do not want to rely on a phone, a change of provider, or recovery of an administrator account that is still in a construction manager's name. Each depends on the system already in place, so we start by identifying the panel, the software and who holds the account, and we tell the owner plainly what the existing system can and cannot do before suggesting anything new.

These buildings also bring the city's Tenant Data Privacy Law into play. An owner who uses fobs, cards or an app must get residents' consent, publish a data retention and privacy policy and destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized. We set up the system so the owner can meet those obligations, and we leave the policy itself to the owner and their lawyer.

Storefront buildings: the shop door and the apartment door

Along Fulton Street, Myrtle Avenue and DeKalb Avenue, many buildings put a shop, restaurant or office on the ground floor and apartments above, with the apartment door beside the shop door, sometimes sharing a vestibule. The intercom's job is to keep the two kinds of visitor apart. Deliveries and customers go to the shop; guests go to the apartments; and neither should be able to buzz into the other's space.

We give the residential door its own panel and its own release, with buttons only for the apartments, and give the business its own video station at its own door if it wants one, ringing the counter, the kitchen or a manager's phone. Where the shop and the apartments share a vestibule, the inner residential door gets the release and the shop's door stays under the shop's control. If the apartments' old intercom power supply sits in the shop's cellar, we look at moving it to a place the landlord can reach without going through the business.

Commercial doors also bring their own compliance points, covered further down this page, including free exit, accessible signals and doors tied to a fire alarm. We find those during the survey rather than on installation day.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Count the Households, Then the Flights of Stairs

This is a working habit, not a story about one address. On a divided Fort Greene brownstone, before we talk about equipment, we ask for two lists. The first is every household in the house, with the floors it occupies. The second is every door a visitor might come to: the parlor door, the gate or door under the stoop, a back garden gate if anyone uses it. Then we walk from the sidewalk to each household's living space and count the flights.

Those two lists settle most of the design before a single model is chosen. A household that lives on two floors gets two stations, or a station and a phone. A household four flights up gets a longer release time and usually phone answering, because a guest will not wait the time it takes to walk down. A door under the stoop that a tenant actually uses gets its own panel, because routing that tenant's guests to the parlor door means someone else answers for them.

The same walk tells us where cable can travel. A dumbwaiter shaft, an old bell wire, a closet stack, a stair hall: the route visitors take up the house is often close to the route the cable can take. Doing this first is the reason our quotes come after a visit rather than before one, and the reason they rarely change once the work starts.

The Law

Two Apartments, Four or Twelve: What New York Requires at a Fort Greene Front Door

Owners in Fort Greene ask two different legal questions, and it helps to keep them apart. The first is whether the building must have an intercom at all. The second is what happens once it has one and it stops working. The answers depend mostly on how many apartments the building has and when it was built or converted, and in this neighborhood those numbers vary from one house to the next on the same block.

The state rule is Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a. As summarized by Parker Custom Security, it requires every class A multiple dwelling erected or converted after January 1, 1968 that contains eight or more apartments to have an intercommunication system at its self-locking main entrance door, letting each apartment speak to the visitor and release the lock. Buildings erected or converted before that date are addressed by the city's rule on entrance doors, 1 RCNY 42-01, whose text says buildings "erected or converted prior to January 1, 1968 containing eight or more apartments, shall be equipped with automatic self-closing doors and self-locking doors and shall also be equipped with an intercommunication system." BrickUnderground's guide for renters describes the pre-1968 obligation as triggered when a majority of tenants request it, at the tenants' expense. Because those two descriptions differ, a board or owner close to the line should have its own lawyer read the current text rather than rely on any summary, including ours.

The same rule sets the minimum and the heights. It accepts a bell or buzzer system, or a speaking and listening device, as the minimum acceptable system. It says no push button device may be more than six feet from the floor, and the speaking and listening device must be between four and five feet from the floor. At a Fort Greene stoop we measure those heights from the landing the visitor stands on, not from the sidewalk below, and we use them at every door we fit, including the small houses the rule does not reach.

For most divided brownstones in the neighborhood, the eight-apartment line is never crossed. A house with a garden apartment, a parlor duplex and a top-floor apartment is a three-family building, and nothing in §50-a requires it to have an intercom. That does not mean the door is unregulated. HPD's ABCs of Housing guide says that in all multiple dwelling types, building entrance doors must be self-closing, and HPD treats a broken self-closing door seriously. An owner who chooses to install an intercom in a three-family house is choosing convenience and security, and should still keep the door closing and locking on its own.

Once an intercom exists, the question becomes upkeep. Parker Custom Security's summary describes a non-working intercom or buzzer under the city's Housing Maintenance Code as a Class B hazardous violation, enforceable on a tenant's complaint, and HPD's own guidance tells owners, managers and supers to repair broken locks or intercoms immediately. BrickUnderground's renter guide describes one tenant who waited three months for a city inspector after a 311 complaint, which is a reminder that a fast repair is better for everyone than a slow inspection. Owners who keep a record of who was called and when a repair was made are in a much better position if a complaint is ever filed.

Finally, a conversion changes the answer. If a brownstone that was a single house becomes a legal three-family, or a small building is enlarged until it reaches eight apartments, the entry system should be planned as part of that work rather than added later. The best time to run new intercom cable is when the walls are already open for the conversion, and the best time to settle the number of buttons is when the apartment count is being decided.

Historic Districts

Fort Greene's Doorways and the Landmarks Guidebook: Where a Panel, a Camera and the Cable May Go

Chapter 11 of the Landmarks Preservation Commission's Permit Guidebook covers the small installations that health and safety need on a landmark building, and intercom panels are among them. The key sentence for an owner is that "staff can issue permits approving light fixtures and intercom panels if the installation meets the following criteria." In other words, a panel that follows the criteria is approved by the Commission's staff, without the public hearing that larger changes to a historic facade go through. The criteria are what we design to.

The first criterion is location. The guidebook says light fixtures and intercom panels, "as well as doorbells, must be installed within or adjacent to a door opening," and that an intercom may also go "on a fence or wall in front of the entranceway." On a Fort Greene rowhouse that usually means the door frame or the plain masonry right beside it at the top of the stoop, the reveal inside a deep door surround, or the low wall or fence along the areaway. It does not mean the middle of the facade, a window surround or the face of the stoop.

The second is quantity. "A maximum of two light fixtures and one intercom panel can be installed per door opening." For most houses this means one panel at the parlor door. A house with a separate under-stoop entrance has a second door opening, and can have a second panel there. A house that has accumulated three bell plates and a mismatched button over the decades should end up with one panel at that doorway, which is often the best visual improvement an owner can make to the front of the house.

The third is how the panel is fastened. "Equipment must be attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood, or metalwork," and it "must not be attached through cast iron or on any decorative surfaces." Fort Greene's doorways often combine a carved brownstone surround, a wooden door frame and cast-iron railings, so this rule decides a lot. We fasten into mortar joints or plain masonry wherever possible, into the wooden frame where that is the plainest surface, and never through a carved bracket, a molded surround or a cast-iron newel or railing. Fastening at mortar joints matters because a mortar joint can be repointed later; a hole through carved stone is permanent.

The fourth is conduit. "If installation requires conduit, the conduit must be concealed by going through plain masonry," and where that is not possible, staff may approve "a maximum of approximately ten inches of visible conduit along a wall surface," painted to match. In practice we bring the cable to the back of the panel through the wall it is mounted on, so it enters the vestibule or hall directly, and then route it inside the house. That is also better for the system, since cable hidden inside a wall is not exposed to weather, paint or a passing ladder.

The fifth is appearance. The design, size and finish "must be compatible with the architecture of the building," which the guidebook describes as neutral and discreet. Slim panels in dark bronze, black or a brushed finish usually suit Fort Greene's dark wooden doors and brownstone better than large silver or plastic panels. We bring finish options to the survey and photograph them against the actual doorway.

Video panels raise one more point. The guidebook treats security cameras separately from intercom panels, and on a small building it allows "no more than two security cameras" for every 25 feet of street frontage, well spaced and finished to match. Most Fort Greene rowhouses are around twenty feet wide or less, so an owner who wants both a video panel and separate cameras should plan them together. The guidebook also says elevation drawings are not required for "in-kind replacement of existing light fixtures, intercoms, security cameras, and mailboxes." When a new panel of similar size goes where the old one was, the application is simpler. When the panel moves, or a new door gets one, we prepare the photos, a marked-up elevation and the cut sheets the staff need.

One more note for the larger buildings. The Williamsburgh Savings Bank Tower's interiors were designated in 1996, which means some of its interior spaces are regulated by the Commission as well as its facade. Work in a designated interior is a Landmarks matter in its own right, and we raise it with the managing agent before any equipment is chosen for a protected lobby.

Answering the Door

Wall Screens, Handsets, Phones or All Three: How Each Fort Greene Household Answers

The decision that divides most Fort Greene households is not the panel at the door but how each apartment answers it. There are three main choices, and many houses end up mixing them. The first is a station on the wall: a video screen or an audio handset, usually near the apartment's entrance or in the kitchen. The second is a phone: the door calls a resident's mobile through an app, or rings an ordinary phone number with audio only. The third is a hands-free station, a speaker and microphone on the wall that answers with one button and suits people who cannot easily pick up a handset.

Wall stations are the most reliable. They work when phones are dead, when the building's internet is down and when a resident is a guest who has never installed anything. In a tall Fort Greene apartment, a duplex or a triplex, the usual answer is two stations, one on the floor where people live during the day and one near the bedrooms. Phones are the most flexible. They let the top-floor household answer from anywhere in the apartment or from the subway, and they let an owner who lives elsewhere see who is at the door of a rental. Many owners choose both, a wall station for daily use and the app for when they are away.

In a divided house, the most important rule is separation. Each household should control its own phone users and its own release permission. A tenant should not be able to see the owner's visitors, and the owner should not be able to answer the tenant's door from an app without the tenant knowing. We set up each household as its own group in the system, with its own users, and we hand each one its own login.

Release time matters more in Fort Greene than in most neighborhoods, because so many front doors are at the top of a stoop. A guest buzzed in from the sidewalk has to climb the steps and push open a heavy door before the strike locks again. We set the release long enough for that, and no longer, and test it with a person walking the route rather than with a stopwatch at the door.

Accessibility is part of the same choice. Visual call signals, a flashing light or a screen notification, help residents who are hard of hearing; hands-free stations help residents who cannot hold a handset; and phone answering helps residents who cannot get to the wall quickly. We ask about each household's needs at the survey rather than assuming one station suits everyone.

Apps, Fobs and Privacy

App Entry and the City's Tenant Data Privacy Law in Fort Greene Apartment Buildings

More Fort Greene buildings now open by app or fob, and some new rentals advertise a virtual doorman, a service that answers the door remotely. These systems are convenient, but they collect information about who comes and goes, and New York City regulates that. HPD's page on the Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, says the law applies to "property owners of multiple dwellings that utilize smart access (keyless) systems," naming key fobs, electronic or computerized technology and radio frequency identification cards, among other digital technology.

The obligations are specific. Owners must obtain "express consent either in writing, or through a mobile application" before collecting reference data from a user. They must give tenants a data retention and privacy policy, and keep the system protected with "robust security and safeguards." They must destroy authentication data "no later than 90 days after such data has been collected or generated," unless it is kept in an anonymized form. HPD's page also says owners may not "require use of a smart access system to gain entry to the tenant's dwelling unit."

For an installer, those rules shape how the system is set up. We choose systems whose logs can be set to keep only what the owner's policy allows, put the administrator account in the owner's or board's name rather than ours, and explain which data each feature collects, so the owner can write an honest policy. Features such as face recognition raise their own questions under the law, and we recommend an owner get legal advice before turning them on in a residential building.

In a divided brownstone, the law's reach depends on the building and the system, so we do not tell an owner of a two- or three-family house whether it applies. We do tell them what the system they are buying collects, and we set it up so each household controls its own users. That is good practice whether or not a statute requires it.

Occupied Buildings

Keeping a Fort Greene Building Locked While the Intercom Is Being Replaced

An intercom changeover is one of the few jobs where the work itself can make a building less secure for a few hours. The old system comes off, the new one is not yet tested, and the easiest thing for everyone is to prop the door. Fort Greene has a reminder of why that is a bad idea. DNAinfo reported in May 2014 that two apartments at the Ingersoll Houses on St. Edwards Street were burglarized after tenants left them unlocked for mandatory renovation work, and that NYCHA then ordered its contractor to lock stairwells, hallways and apartment doors whenever no work was under way.

We plan every changeover with the same principle. The street door is never left unlocked and unattended. In a small house, the swap of the panel and the strike is done in one block of time with a technician at the door, and the old release keeps working until the new one is tested. In a larger building, we keep the old panel live while the new cable and stations go in, and switch over in a short, scheduled window that the building announces in advance. If a door must stand open for a few minutes while a strike is fitted, someone from our crew stays with it.

The same thinking applies to access inside the building. A new station needs a visit to each apartment, and we schedule those visits with each household rather than asking anyone to leave a door unlocked. If a resident cannot be home, they can arrange for a neighbor, the super or the owner to let us in, or we come back. Nobody should have to choose between getting a new intercom and keeping their apartment locked.

Many Fort Greene buildings also have contractors coming and going for renovations in individual apartments. A new intercom can help there too: instead of propping the street door or handing out spare keys, a resident can buzz a crew in from a phone, and an owner can give a contractor a code or app access that works only on the days and hours of the job and stops when the job ends.

Commercial Intercom

Business Intercom Installation in Fort Greene

Fort Greene's businesses are spread along a few busy streets and around its cultural institutions, and most of them share their buildings with apartments. That shapes the intercom more than the type of business does: a shop or office needs its own door station and its own way of answering, and the apartments above need a door the business does not control. These are the settings we plan for most often.

Fulton Street storefronts

Shops, cafés and services along the Fulton Area Business Alliance's 37 block faces, many in older buildings with a narrow residential door beside the shop and apartments on the floors above.

Myrtle Avenue businesses

Part of the 179 ground-floor businesses the Myrtle Avenue district counts across Fort Greene and Clinton Hill, often with back doors on side streets for deliveries.

DeKalb Avenue restaurants

Restaurants and bars near Fort Greene Park whose kitchens take early deliveries at a side or cellar door while the dining room is closed.

Arts organizations near BAM

Offices, studios and small cultural groups on the blocks around Lafayette Avenue, Ashland Place and Fulton Street, with visitors arriving for appointments and events.

Practices on garden floors

Professional offices and practices in converted rowhouse garden floors, entered under the stoop, with a receptionist who needs to see and admit each visitor.

Offices near the Atlantic Terminal edge

Suites in the larger buildings around Hanson Place and Flatbush Avenue, where a landlord's lobby system already exists and the tenant needs a station at its own door.

For a shop with apartments above, the first decision is the split. The residential door gets a panel with buttons only for the apartments; the shop gets a video station at its own entrance, ringing a counter screen, a kitchen station or a manager's phone. Where both share a vestibule, the inner residential door gets the release, and the shop's own door stays on the shop's lock and schedule. This keeps delivery drivers from buzzing tenants and keeps tenants' guests out of the shop.

For a restaurant, the back door matters as much as the front. Early deliveries often arrive before the front of house opens, at a side door or a cellar entrance. A video station at that door, ringing the kitchen or the manager's phone, lets whoever is prepping see and admit the driver without walking through a locked dining room. We pick stations that stand up to kitchen conditions and mount them where the line staff can actually hear them.

For an office or practice on a garden floor, the station at the under-stoop door rings the reception desk, often as a desk screen or through the office phone system, and the release is limited to office hours. Outside those hours the button can be set to ring a manager's phone or nobody at all, so a late visitor does not ring through to an empty office or, worse, to the apartment upstairs.

For a suite in a larger building, we start where the landlord's system stops. The lobby panel and the building's access control belong to the landlord; our work is the suite door and how the tenant answers it. We confirm with the building manager what may be mounted at the suite door and how cable can reach the tenant's space before we quote.

Compliance points for Fort Greene business intercoms

  • Free exit. A door with an electric release must still open from the inside without a key, special knowledge or effort. We match the strike or lock to the door and test the exit with the power cut on every job.
  • Accessible entry. Where a two-way communication system is provided at an entrance, the ADA Standards call for both audible and visual signals. We choose door stations that give both and mount them within reach of a seated visitor.
  • Doors tied to a fire alarm. If a door's lock is connected to the building's fire alarm, that connection belongs to the fire alarm system and its approvals. We find those doors at the survey and coordinate with the fire alarm side rather than rewiring it.
  • Rated doors. Doors between a shop or office and a residential stair are often fire-rated. Any release hardware on a rated door must be listed for that use.
  • Landmarks. A panel on the facade of a storefront in a historic district follows the same one-panel-per-door-opening rule as a rowhouse. On Fulton Street, check whether your building is inside the BAM Historic District.
  • Cameras and recording. A video station records whoever stands in front of it. Decide who can view the footage and how long it is kept, and let staff and regular visitors know the station has a camera.
Your Questions Answered

Intercom Installation in Fort Greene: Questions Answered

These are the situations Fort Greene owners, boards, tenants and businesses are most likely to bring us, grouped by topic. Each is answered here once and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Historic districts and the front of the house

Our house is in the BAM Historic District, a few blocks from the Fort Greene district. Is an intercom panel judged by different rules there?

No. Both districts were designated in 1978, and the Landmarks Permit Guidebook's criteria for intercom panels apply to buildings in any historic district: one panel per door opening, in or beside the doorway or on a wall or fence in front of it, fastened at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, and finished to suit the building. What differs between the two districts is the architecture, since the BAM district includes more commercial and institutional buildings along Fulton Street and Lafayette Avenue. We design the panel to the building in front of us and prepare the staff application the same way in either district.

The only plain surface beside our Italianate doorway is the wooden door frame. Is a panel allowed to be fastened into the wood?

Yes. The guidebook lists wood among the surfaces equipment may be attached to, along with mortar joints, plain masonry and metalwork. What it rules out is fastening through cast iron or onto a decorative surface. On many Fort Greene doorways the carved brownstone surround and its brackets are decorative, and the wooden frame inside them is the plainest surface available. A slim panel on the frame, with the cable passing straight through to the vestibule behind it, often meets the criteria with nothing new on the stone at all.

Our condo is in One Hanson Place, the former Williamsburgh Savings Bank, where some interior spaces are designated landmarks. Does a new lobby station go through Landmarks too?

It can. The tower's exterior was designated in 1977 and parts of its interior in 1996, so work that touches a designated interior space is a matter for the Commission as well as the board. Not every lobby or corridor in the building is necessarily part of the designation. Before choosing equipment, we ask the managing agent which spaces are designated and where the existing desk and panel sit, then plan the station and its cable so the application, if one is needed, shows minimal change to the protected surfaces.

What is the least the city accepts as an entry system for a building that needs one, and does a plain doorbell count?

For the buildings 1 RCNY 42-01 covers, the rule calls a bell or buzzer system, or a speaking and listening device, the minimum acceptable system. A doorbell alone does not let a resident speak to a visitor, and where §50-a applies, the intercom must let each apartment communicate with the visitor and release the lock. For a small Fort Greene house outside those rules, a doorbell is legally fine, but it does not tell a tenant on the fourth floor who is outside or let them open the door, which is why most divided houses choose a real intercom anyway.

Divided houses and the households inside them

Our 18-foot-wide house is now three apartments, and the top one is five flights above the front door. How does that household answer before a visitor gives up?

Two things help most. The first is answering from a phone as well as a wall station, so the top-floor household can answer from wherever they are in a tall duplex instead of walking to one spot. The second is a release time set for the climb a guest has to make, from the sidewalk up the stoop and through a heavy door. We also make sure the call keeps ringing long enough before it times out, and we test the whole route with someone actually walking it, because a visitor who rings and hears nothing for twenty seconds often leaves.

Each apartment in our house belongs to one of three grown siblings. Should every household control its own release and its own app users?

Yes, even in a family house. Each household should be its own group in the system, with its own button, its own stations, its own phone users and its own right to open the door. That way one sibling can give a babysitter app access without the others being involved, and a household that rents out its apartment later can hand control to its tenant cleanly. We set up a single administrator for the building as a whole, chosen by the family, and separate logins for each household underneath.

Old systems, failures and what is worth keeping

Our handsets still say Lee Dan. Does a system that old mean the wire behind it is finished too?

Not necessarily. Daniel Vasquez of Paragon Security and Locksmith told BrickUnderground that the core wiring in older systems such as Lee Dan is typically fine even when the equipment is outdated, and that "it's all in the wire connections." That matches what we find in many Fort Greene houses. We test each pair from the control point to each apartment before recommending anything. If the pairs test good, a two-wire video system can often run on them, which saves opening walls; if some fail, only those runs are replaced.

Some tenants say the buzzer never works for them, yet it works every time the super tries it. Is that the equipment or the way it is used?

It can be either. Pete Carmello of Lock and Tech told BrickUnderground that "a lot of times it's user error," such as not pressing a button firmly or not waiting long enough, and that sticking buttons are a common culprit. But an apartment station with its volume turned down, a talk button that only works while held, or a release button that needs a longer press can look like user error and be a real fault. We test from the street and from each apartment, show residents how their station works, and fix anything that does not behave the way it should.

The Comptroller found broken locks on four in ten public-housing entrance doors in 2022. In a private Fort Greene building, how do you tell whether the lock, the closer or the intercom is failing?

The NYC Comptroller's observation of NYCHA developments found 40.1 percent of entrance door locks broken and 36.8 percent of entrance doors open, a reminder that the door often fails before the intercom does. In a private building we check them in order: whether the door closes fully on its own, whether the latch catches, whether the strike releases when buzzed with the door pulled shut, and whether the intercom sends a release signal at all. A door that does not close will not stay locked no matter how good the intercom is, so we tell you if the closer or the latch needs attention from a locksmith before or alongside our work.

Renovations, apps and the years ahead

Contractors renovating apartments in our building keep asking residents to leave doors unlocked for them. Can the intercom give crews a way in instead?

Yes, and it is much safer. The 2014 burglaries at the Ingersoll Houses happened when apartments were left unlocked for renovation crews. With a modern intercom, a resident can buzz a crew in from a phone, and an owner or board can issue a code or app access that works only during the job's days and hours and stops automatically when the job ends. That keeps the street door locked, gives the building a record of when the crew came and went, and means nobody has to hide a key or leave an apartment open.

If the company behind our app-based intercom stops supporting its app in a few years, does our street door stop working?

It depends on how the system is built, which is why we ask before recommending one. HomeAdvisor notes that most intercom systems last about 20 years, but that smart systems can become outdated in as little as 10. A system with wall stations and a local release keeps opening the door even if its app or cloud service ends; an app-only system may not. For Fort Greene houses we prefer systems that answer at a wall station and release locally, with the app as an addition, and we tell you on the quote which features depend on a subscription.

Our arts group shares a Fulton Street storefront near BAM, with volunteers rotating on the front desk. Can the door ring whoever is on shift?

Yes. The door station can ring a desk screen at the front, and also ring a group of phones so whoever is on shift that day can answer from the back room or the gallery. When the space is closed, the same button can ring a manager or nobody. Volunteers can be added to and removed from the phone group as the schedule changes, without anyone touching the hardware, and the release can be limited to the hours the space is open to visitors.

Our landlord is adding fobs at the street door of our Fort Greene rental. What does the city's privacy law require before the fobs are handed out?

HPD's page on the Tenant Data Privacy Law says owners using smart access systems must obtain express consent in writing or through a mobile app before collecting reference data from a user, must give tenants a data retention and privacy policy, and must destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized. It also says owners may not require use of a smart access system to gain entry to a tenant's dwelling unit. Tenants with questions about their own building should ask the owner for the policy, and an owner should have a lawyer review it; we set up the system so it can follow whatever policy the owner adopts.

What AI Gets Wrong About Intercom Installation in Fort Greene

Short answers about New York intercoms, from search summaries and lead sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, are usually written for either a single-family house or a big apartment building. "A brownstone is too small to need more than a doorbell"

Quick answers often treat a rowhouse as a single home with a single front door, where a doorbell camera is all anyone needs.

Many Fort Greene brownstones are two, three or four apartments stacked on five floors. Brownstoner's 2016 feature describes one Fort Greene house of 18 feet wide and five stories divided into three apartments, each with its own internal stair. The household at the top of a house like that is four flights above the door.

A panel inside the vestibule cannot be reached by a visitor standing outside a locked door. In Fort Greene the right place is the doorway, designed to the guidebook's criteria, not hidden behind it.

Some summaries say any New York building with rental apartments must provide an intercom.

A national average blends single-family homes across the country with very different buildings and labor markets. It says nothing about a five-story brownstone with three households, an original wooden door and a facade under Landmarks rules, or a walk-up whose riser needs testing floor by floor.

The cost of a Fort Greene job is set by the number of households, the stations each needs, the condition of the wire, the door and the facade. That is why we quote after the survey and the wire test rather than before.

"App and fob entry is just a convenience feature"

Product pages present app entry, fobs and face recognition as simple upgrades with no strings attached.

In New York City, the Tenant Data Privacy Law applies to owners of multiple dwellings that use smart access systems. Owners must get express consent, provide a data retention and privacy policy, and destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, according to HPD.

An owner adding app or fob entry to a Fort Greene apartment building is taking on legal duties along with the convenience. We set the system up so those duties can be met and recommend legal advice before turning on biometric features.

Photos of the front of the house, the doorway, the bell plate or panel and any equipment in the cellar, with every household listed by the floors it occupies and the door it uses.

Whether the address is in the Fort Greene or BAM Historic District, whether the building has eight or more apartments, and who has to approve the work.

A changeover booked so the street door is never unlocked and unattended, with a time agreed with each household for the visit to its apartment.

6. Cable, release and stations

Good wire reused, new cable pulled where needed, the release fitted to the door, and the panel and indoor stations mounted and labeled.

Buttons or directory entries, call routing, phone and app users, release times and permissions set for each household, with the administrator account in the owner's or board's name.

8. Test, train and hand over

Every apartment called from the street, every release and free exit tested with the power cut, and the cable record, logins and warranty terms handed over.

FAQ

Fort Greene Intercom Installation FAQ

Can a single worn-out handset in one apartment be replaced on its own, without changing the stations in the others?
+

Often, yes. If the manufacturer still makes a compatible station, or a current model that works on the same system, one apartment can be replaced and the rest left alone. If the old system is no longer supported, we tell you which compatible options exist, if any, and whether replacing one handset now is worth it compared with a planned upgrade of the whole house.

What happens to the old panel, the handsets and the power supply once they come out?
+

Unless you want to keep something, we take the old equipment away when we finish. Some Fort Greene owners keep an antique brass bell push or plate as a keepsake, and that is fine. The holes left behind in plaster or stone are noted in the quote, because AESS does not patch or paint walls, and we tell you which trade to call for the finish work.

Is the low masonry wall beside our front steps an allowed place for the door panel?
+

It can be. The Landmarks guidebook allows an intercom on a fence or wall in front of the entranceway, as well as in or beside the door opening itself. The panel still has to be fastened to plain masonry or mortar joints, not to a decorative surface or through cast iron, and it still counts as the one panel for that entrance. We check the wall, the cable route to it and what visitors will see before recommending that spot over the doorway.

At the end of the job, do we get a written record of which wire goes to which apartment?
+

Yes. Every run we test, reuse or install is labeled at both ends, and the handover includes a written cable record listing each run, the apartment or door it serves and where it passes through the house. In a brownstone that may be renovated again, that record is what lets the next repair take minutes instead of a day of tracing.

Will the quote show each part and the labor separately, so our board can compare it with another bid?
+

Yes. The written quote lists each panel by model, every station and app account, the release and its power supply, the cable work for each floor, any network equipment, any Landmarks paperwork and the labor, each on its own line. That makes it easy for a board, an owner or a managing agent to compare with another bid item by item and to see exactly what changes if one item is dropped.

On a dark block at night, can visitors see the buttons, and will the camera still show their faces?
+

Most current door panels have lit buttons or name tags and a camera with its own low light or infrared, so a visitor can find the right button and the household can see who is there. Where the stoop is very dark, a light at the door helps both, and the Landmarks guidebook allows up to two light fixtures per door opening alongside the panel. We check the doorway after dark if the owner says it is a problem.

Does a video door panel count against the Landmarks limit on cameras at the front of a house?
+

The guidebook treats intercom panels and security cameras as separate items, with its own rules for each, and limits small buildings to two security cameras per 25 feet of street frontage. A video panel is approved as the door's intercom panel. If you also want separate cameras on the facade, we plan the panel and the cameras together so the whole front stays within the guidebook's limits and looks deliberate rather than cluttered.

Can we answer the door on a tablet we already own instead of a wall screen?
+

With many current systems, yes. The same app that rings a phone can run on a tablet, which can sit in a stand in the kitchen or on the top floor. The catch is that a tablet depends on Wi-Fi and on staying charged, so for at least one spot in each household we still suggest a wall station that works without the internet, with the tablet as an extra.

We already have a camera doorbell on the parlor door. Can it stay, or does the new panel replace it?
+

Usually the panel replaces it, because the Landmarks rules allow one intercom panel per door opening and a doorbell camera at the same door is a second device. In a single-family house outside a district, some owners keep both. In a divided house, a panel with a button and a station for each household does everything the doorbell did, for everyone, and adds a release, so the doorbell can come off and its spot can be tidied.

Is the door release tested with the power cut before your crew leaves?
+

Yes, on every job. We cut the power to the release and confirm the door opens from inside without a key or effort, then restore power and confirm it locks and releases normally. We also call every household from the street, time the release for someone climbing the stoop, and show each household how to answer and open the door before we hand over.

Who receives the instructions for the new stations, the owner or each household?
+

Both. The owner or board gets the administrator login, the cable record and the warranty terms. Each household gets a short walk-through of its own station and app at handover, and its own login for adding and removing phones, so tenants do not have to go through the owner to give a guest or a dog walker access.
