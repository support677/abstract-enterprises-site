The Neighborhood

Why Columbia Street Waterfront Properties Need Access Control

The best short description of the place we have found is 6sqft’s: a “quirky 22-block enclave wedged between Red Hook and the Brooklyn-Queens Expressway.” The expressway was built below street level along Hicks Street in 1957, and it cut these blocks off from Carroll Gardens and Cobble Hill. That isolation is why real-estate listings cannot agree where the district is. The same block can be advertised as Cobble Hill, Carroll Gardens or Red Hook depending on who wrote the listing, and buildings here carry both 11201 and 11231 addresses. For a door job none of that changes anything except the paperwork, but it explains why owners sometimes tell us they live in three neighborhoods at once.

What matters more for access control is how the housing was rebuilt. In 1975 a sewer accident led the city to demolish 33 buildings, and many residents and businesses left. The city then treated the district as an urban-renewal area. Phase I of Columbia Terrace opened on President Street in 1984 with 51 condominium units, and three later phases added 129 more apartments on President and Carroll Streets, in 17 low-rise buildings. One listing for 42 Carroll Street gives the building as a three-floor townhouse condo built in 1986 with a “Voice Intercom.” Another describes First Columbia Terrace at 250 Columbia Street as a 27-unit walk-up finished the same year. Those are condominiums, not rentals, which means the street doors are common property and every change to them is a board decision. A big share of the access control work in the district starts in a board meeting, not at a door.

The second group of buildings is the factory stock that survived. Louis Comfort Tiffany’s former factory on Tiffany Place became condominiums in 1986, and one building record lists 1 Tiffany Place at 70 units on five floors. A former pasta factory at 25 Carroll Street became 17 loft condos in 2010. Lofts like these have one or two street entrances serving dozens of households, long corridors, an elevator, and walls of brick or concrete that nobody wants opened. They need a lobby system, but they often have the cable routes of a warehouse.

The third group is newer and taller. Columbia Commons at 110 Warren Street, finished in 2010, is listed as a 48-unit, six-story elevator condo with an intercom, a virtual doorman system rather than a doorman, and an underground garage. GACE, which worked on it, describes Columbia Hicks and Columbia Commons together as three six-story buildings with 136 units and basement parking just south of Atlantic Avenue, and 6sqft breaks that down as 42 market-rate condos and 94 income-restricted rentals. A listing for 222 Columbia Street describes a 2002 condo with an elevator, a garage and a video intercom; 414 Hicks Street is listed at 95 units on six floors; and 255 Columbia Street, a six-story, 13-unit Passive House building, was reported by Brownstoner in 2014. In 2026 a six-story, ten-unit building was completed at Columbia and Degraw Streets. Buildings like these have garages, elevators, package rooms and a mix of owners and renters, and every one of those is a separate access decision.

The fourth group is the oldest. Townhouses and small walk-ups still stand along Columbia Street itself: one listing gives 129 Columbia Street as a four-unit building from 1910 with a voice intercom, another gives 181 Columbia Street as a three-apartment townhouse from 1931, and building records list 219 and 279 Columbia Street as 1930s buildings with a store or office at street level and one or two apartments above. Here the access question is usually one street door shared by a landlord, two or three households and sometimes a shop tenant, still running on keys that have been copied for decades.

We do not sell door systems here with fear, and the numbers would not support it. CrimeGrade gives the district an A for burglary, at about 1.1 reported burglaries per 1,000 residents a year, roughly eight a year, against a national rate of 2.4. What owners actually call about is control: knowing who has a key to the condo’s street doors after 40 years of resales, stopping a garage door from becoming the building’s back entrance, giving a restaurant’s staff a door that opens for their shift and no one else, and being ready for the changes the city keeps proposing at the edge of the district. A Columbia Street resident quoted by BKReader after the Brooklyn Marine Terminal vote put the mood plainly: “We haven’t heard of any kind of plan to introduce sufficient greenspace.” Plans for the waterfront are still plans. The doors are already here.

Not every call is a new system. Building Specialists

Access Control for Columbia Street Waterfront’s Architecture

The building decides the hardware, and in this district the building also decides who has to agree. Here are the six kinds of property we plan for most, with what each usually needs.

Renewal-era townhouse condos

Low brick buildings from the 1980s on President and Carroll Streets, many entrances under one condominium, framed interior walls and voice intercoms of the period. Readers at each street door, one board account, credentials per owner.

Factory and warehouse lofts

Converted industrial buildings on Tiffany Place and Carroll Street with dozens of units behind one lobby, an elevator, heavy masonry and long halls. Vestibule control, a directory panel and surface cable routes planned with the board.

Six-story elevator buildings

Condos and rentals from 2002 onward near Atlantic Avenue and along Columbia and Hicks Streets, with garages, package rooms and amenity floors. Networked readers, elevator control and garage credentials.

Pre-war townhouses and walk-ups

Brick buildings from about 1910 to 1931 on Columbia Street with three or four apartments and one street door. A reader and strike at the street door, cellar door control, and the intercom left working.

Buildings with a store below

Two-family and small mixed buildings with a shop or office at street level. Two systems or two separate accounts: one for the shop’s doors, one for the residents’ street door.

Small new buildings

Six-story, ten- to thirteen-unit buildings finished in the last decade, including high-efficiency and Passive House construction. Fewer doors, but newer frames, tighter shells and developer-chosen systems the owners inherit.

What the materials mean for the install

Brick is the forgiving material. Readers and stations are fixed into mortar joints rather than the face of the brick, so if the hardware ever changes the repair is a joint to repoint, not a cracked brick. The 1980s condo buildings usually have framed interior walls and drop the job straight into familiar territory: cable fished down a stud bay to a closet or the cellar, a strike in a sound frame, and a short day. The pre-war townhouses are where frames need attention. A wood jamb that has been bored for three or four generations of locks will not hold a strike until the damaged section is filled or replaced, and we price that repair openly rather than burying it.

The factory lofts are the opposite of the condos. Their walls are often solid masonry or concrete, their ceilings may be original timber or tin that owners treasure, and their corridors are long. There we plan surface routes with the board in advance, using painted raceway along existing lines, cable through closets and shafts that already exist, and the elevator shaft’s own pathways only where the elevator company allows it. The newer elevator buildings have aluminum storefront entrances with narrow stiles that leave almost no room for a lock in the door edge, so the lock usually goes into an electrified panic bar or the frame header, and the reader goes on the frame or on a separate post. In airtight buildings every hole through the shell is sealed as carefully as a window, which matters when a reader’s cable has to cross from outside to inside.

Seventeen Low Buildings, One Board: Fobs for the Renewal-Era Condos

The Columbia Terrace buildings and their neighbors were built to replace what the 1975 collapse destroyed, and they were built as condominiums for owners who would stay. Forty years on, many of those apartments have changed hands more than once, and each sale has left keys behind. A seller hands over the keys they remember. The keys given to a dog walker in 1995, a contractor in 2008 and a tenant in 2016 are another matter. In a condo where every street door is a common element, the board has no practical way to know how many working keys exist, and rekeying every door after every sale is expensive enough that most boards stop doing it.

That is the problem individual credentials solve. Each owner and each resident gets their own fob, code or phone key, issued by the board or its managing agent and tied to a name and a unit. When an apartment sells, the seller’s credentials are switched off at the closing and the buyer’s are switched on, and the street door never needs a locksmith. When an owner rents their apartment out, the tenant gets credentials with the lease dates on them, and the owner can be kept on the list or taken off it as the bylaws say. A lost fob is cancelled in a minute without touching anyone else’s.

What makes the renewal-era complexes unusual is that the doors are many and small. A 1980s townhouse condo may have one street door per building and several buildings under one board, rather than one grand lobby. That changes the design. We usually put a reader and strike on every street door, run each building’s doors back to one controller or to small controllers linked over the network, and give the board one administrator account that can see every door. Each owner’s credential can then be set to open only their own building’s doors, or every door in the complex if the board prefers, and the choice can be changed later without new hardware.

The voice intercoms that listings still mention in these buildings usually stay. Adding a reader to a street door does not require replacing the intercom, and owners who are used to answering a handset can keep doing so. Where the board wants video later, the reader and controller we install now can stay in place when the intercom is replaced.

What we ask a condo board for before the first visit

  • A door list. Every street door, cellar door, garden or yard gate and service door the board controls, and which units use each one.
  • Who decides. Whether the board can approve the work alone or whether the bylaws send it to the owners, and who will hold the administrator login afterwards.
  • Who has keys today. As complete a list as the board can make, including staff, contractors and former renters, so the switch-over day can be planned.
  • Renting owners. How many apartments are rented, so tenants’ credentials can carry lease dates and the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law paperwork can be prepared where it applies.

Factory Doors and Long Halls: Lofts on Tiffany Place and Carroll Street

A converted factory has the opposite proportions to a townhouse condo: one entrance, dozens of households, and a building that was never designed to carry low-voltage cable from a front door to anywhere. 1 Tiffany Place is listed at 70 units on five floors, and 25 Carroll Street holds 17 loft condos in a former pasta factory. One tenant review of the Tiffany Place building lists “24h noise from the BQE” as a drawback, which tells you how close these buildings sit to the trench and the trucks that use the streets around it.

The access plan for a loft building normally has four layers. The street door is the first: a reader and a lock on a door that will be opened hundreds of times a day, so the lock and closer have to be heavy-duty. The vestibule is the second, with an inner door that releases only for a credential or for a resident answering a visitor, which stops a stranger who follows someone through the outer door from getting further than the mailboxes. The elevator is the third, where a reader can limit each credential to its own floor and to the shared floors such as a roof or a storage level. The cellar, storage cages, bike room and any roof access are the fourth, each with its own list. Roof doors are a special case: under the Multiple Dwelling Law, a roof door in a multiple dwelling must be self-closing but not self-locking and must open from inside without a key, so a roof reader controls who can go up, never whether someone on the roof can get back down.

The hard part is the cable. Loft owners bought these apartments for the brick, the timber and the tin, and they do not want any of it opened. We start with what the building already has: the elevator machine room, existing telephone and cable risers, closets stacked above one another, and any pathway the original conversion left. Where none of that reaches a door, we use painted surface raceway along existing lines such as the top of a baseboard or the edge of a door casing, and we show the board the route on a photo before we start. Wireless locks are an option for a few interior doors where cable truly cannot go, but we do not put the street door of a 70-unit building on batteries.

Many loft conversions are now old enough that their first entry systems have reached the end of their lives. A building converted in 2010 may still run on 125 kHz proximity fobs, the kind that can be copied with an inexpensive device, and on a controller whose maker no longer supports it. Often the strikes, closers and cable are still sound. In that case we keep them, test them, and change only the readers, the controller and the fobs, which is far cheaper than starting over.

Garages, Package Rooms and Two Kinds of Resident: The Elevator Buildings Near Atlantic Avenue

The newest part of the district is its northern end, where Columbia Commons, Columbia Hicks and buildings such as 414 Hicks Street and 222 Columbia Street went up after 2000. These are six-story elevator buildings, many with parking below. A garage changes the security of a building more than any other feature, because a garage door is an entrance that opens wide, stays open for several seconds and is usually out of sight of residents. Someone on foot can walk in behind a car, and from there the garage door into the building is often the weakest door in the place.

So in a garage building we plan the doors in pairs. The garage door itself opens to vehicle credentials, either tags on the windshield or fobs on the driver’s key ring, and closes promptly behind each car, with a contact that reports when it stays open. The pedestrian door from the garage into the building, or the elevator’s garage-level call button, is the second check: a reader there means that someone who slips in behind a car reaches a locked door, not a lobby. Stairs from the garage get the same treatment, always opening freely in the direction of exit.

Columbia Commons and Columbia Hicks together hold condo owners and income-restricted renters, and that mix is common in newer buildings. It matters for access control because New York City’s Tenant Data Privacy Law protects tenants in buildings that use smart access: owners must give tenants a written privacy and data-retention policy, collect only the data the system needs, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, never sell it, never use it to track a tenant outside the building, and never use it to harass or evict. A condo owner who lives in their own apartment is not a tenant, but a renter in the same building is. The simplest answer is to run the whole development to the tenant standard, so that nobody has to remember which rules apply to which door.

The smaller new buildings, like the ten-unit building completed at Columbia and Degraw in 2026 or the 13-unit Passive House building at 255 Columbia Street, raise a different question: what did the developer install, and who controls it now? A board that takes over from a developer should get the administrator login, the controller’s location, the model of every reader and lock, and a written list of every credential the developer issued, including those held by contractors and brokers. If any of those are missing, the board does not really control its own front door.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control in Columbia Street Waterfront

Columbia Street is the district’s main street, and most of its businesses sit in buildings with apartments above. That one fact shapes almost every commercial door we plan here: the shop’s doors and the residents’ street door share a wall, sometimes a hallway and often a cellar, but they belong to different people and run on different hours.

Columbia Street at Kane Street

The blocks around Kane Street, where Laurel Bakery opened at 115 Columbia Street in April 2024 and Krok has been listed at 117 Columbia Street. Bakeries and restaurants that start work hours before they open need staff doors on early schedules.

Columbia Street between Sackett and Union

Restaurant frontage such as The Longshoreman, listed at 215 Columbia Street. Kitchen doors, cellar stairs and staff entrances, with the residential street door kept separate.

Columbia Street south toward Hamilton Avenue

Smaller storefronts such as Popina, listed at 327 Columbia Street since 2017, and two-family buildings with a store at street level. Split systems for a shop and the apartments above.

Union Street and the side streets

Storefronts, studios and small offices on the cross streets between Columbia and Van Brunt. Back doors, cellar access and doors that should lock on a schedule.

Studios, workshops and music rooms

Shared workshops, clay studios, artist collectives and the Jalopy music venue, listed in the district by 6sqft. Member credentials, hours-based access and doors that stay free to exit when an audience is inside.

The port edge

Warehouses, yards and industrial users near the container terminal and along the streets west of Columbia Street. Steel doors, gates and early shifts, with trucks starting work before dawn.

Restaurants and food shops under apartments

The common job on Columbia Street is a restaurant or bakery on the ground floor of a three- or four-story building. The front door is a glass storefront door behind a roll-down gate at night. The back of the house has a kitchen door to a yard or hallway, and the cellar holds stock, refrigeration and often the building’s meters. A shop like that needs three things from an access system: staff who open the door with their own codes or fobs rather than shared keys, a schedule that relocks the back and cellar doors when the last shift leaves, and a record of who came in early or stayed late. Laurel Bakery’s published hours, 8:30 a.m. to 4 p.m. from Wednesday to Sunday, are a reminder that a bakery’s working day starts long before its shop opens, and that a baker’s door has a different schedule from the customers’ door.

What the shop’s system must not do is take over the residents’ street door. If the restaurant and the apartments share an entrance or a hallway, the residential door stays on the building owner’s system and its own credentials, locked at all times under the Multiple Dwelling Law, and the restaurant gets its own doors and its own administrator. Where the cellar is shared, each party gets a credential for the cellar door, and the owner keeps the ability to cancel the shop’s access at the end of a lease.

Studios, collectives and performance rooms

6sqft’s 2014 survey of the district listed a membership-based collective of artists and designers, a clay studio and gallery, and Jalopy, a live folk, blues and roots venue that also teaches instrument classes. Spaces like these have members or students rather than employees. Each member should hold a credential that ends when the membership does, and doors should unlock only for the hours the space is staffed. A room that fills with an audience in the evening is a place of assembly while the show is on, and its exit doors must open from inside with one push however the entry side is controlled. We control who comes in, never who can get out.

Rules that apply to Columbia Street Waterfront business doors

  • Egress first. Under the NYC Building Code, an electrically locked door on a path of exit must release when power fails and when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate, with a push-to-exit button 40 to 48 inches above the floor and within five feet of the door that cuts power to the lock for at least 30 seconds, and a magnetic lock needs a release built into the door hardware that works with one hand. Exit doors from shops, studios and venues always open from inside without a key, card or code.
  • Listed equipment. Controllers and locks on exit doors should be listed to UL 294 for access control; we fit listed equipment and keep the listings in the job file.
  • Apartments above shops. Under Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a, the street entrances of class A multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968 must be self-closing and self-locking and kept locked unless an attendant is on duty, and buildings with eight or more apartments need an intercom with voice and remote release. In older buildings these are required once a majority of tenants ask in writing. A restaurant cannot leave a residential street door unlocked for its own convenience.
  • Tenant data. Where a building with apartments uses fobs, apps or other smart access, the Tenant Data Privacy Law requires a written policy, consent, limits on what is collected and destruction of authentication data within 90 days unless anonymized. A shop’s own staff door is not covered; a shared residential door is.
  • Permits. Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Columbia Street Waterfront: Questions Answered

Columbia Terrace and the renewal-era condos

Columbia Terrace went up in phases from 1984 into the late 1980s. If each phase got different doors and locks, can one system still run every street door?

Usually, yes. What matters to a controller is not the age of the door but what electric lock can be fitted to it, and almost any sound door can take one: an electric strike in a wood or steel frame, or an electrified lever where the frame is unsuitable. We survey each street door separately, list the frame, the existing lock and its condition, and choose the lock door by door. The readers and the controller are the same across the whole complex, so owners carry one kind of credential and the board manages one list.

What sometimes differs between phases is the condition of the frames and the route back to a cellar or closet. Those differences show up in the price for each door, which is why our written quote lists the doors one by one rather than as a single figure.

Our townhouse condo’s street doors are common elements. Does putting them on fobs need a vote of all the owners, or can the board decide?

That depends on your condominium’s declaration and bylaws, not on the hardware, and your board’s attorney or managing agent is the right person to answer it. Many boards treat a lock change on a common door as ordinary maintenance they can approve; others treat a new system with a privacy policy and an ongoing cost as a change that owners should see first. We can supply what either route needs: a written door-by-door plan, the price, a short plain-language description of what data the system keeps, and a sample notice for owners.

Whatever the decision route, plan the switch-over date with the owners. Credentials are easier to hand out when everyone knows when the old keys stop working.

Lofts in the old factories

A loft building like 1 Tiffany Place has about 70 apartments behind one entrance. How many readers does a building that size actually need?

Fewer than most boards expect, but more than one. A typical plan is a reader on the street door and on the inner vestibule door, a video entry panel for visitors, a reader in or beside the elevator if the board wants floor control, and readers on the cellar, storage, bike room and any other shared room that holds owners’ property. That is often six to ten controlled openings for the whole building. Apartment doors stay on the owners’ own locks.

The two doors that matter most are the vestibule door and the cellar door. The vestibule stops someone who follows a resident through the street door from reaching the elevator, and the cellar is where storage and building equipment sit. If the budget allows only a few readers this year, start there.

Our loft building was converted in 2010 and still uses the developer’s fob system. Which parts are worth keeping sixteen years on?

Often the strikes, closers, door contacts and cable are perfectly good, and they are the expensive part of any install. What has usually aged out is the controller, the readers and the fobs. Systems from that period commonly use 125 kHz proximity fobs, which can be copied with an inexpensive device, and controllers whose makers have stopped issuing updates. We test every lock, contact and cable run, then quote two options: replacing only the readers, controller and credentials while reusing what tests sound, and a full replacement where the old parts are failing.

Ask for the developer’s door schedule and wiring notes if the board has them. They make the survey faster and the quote more exact.

Elevator buildings with garages near Atlantic Avenue

Our development has condo owners and income-restricted renters. Should the renters’ fobs follow a different privacy policy from the owners’?

We recommend one policy for everyone, written to the tenant standard. The city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law protects tenants in buildings with smart access: a written privacy and retention policy, consent, minimum data, authentication data destroyed within 90 days unless anonymized, no sale of data, and no use of entry records to harass or evict. Owner-occupants are not tenants under that law, but applying a second, looser policy to the owners’ fobs on the same doors creates two sets of records, two retention schedules and endless room for mistakes.

One policy at the stricter standard is simpler to run and easier to explain. We set the system’s retention to match it and give the board and the rental manager the documents the law expects.

Someone on foot can walk down our garage ramp behind a car. Where should the second check be?

At the door between the garage and the building. The garage door is built to let vehicles through and will always be open for a few seconds behind each car, so it cannot stop a determined pedestrian on its own. Put a reader and a strike on the pedestrian door from the garage into the building, or on the elevator’s garage-level call, and a person who slips in behind a car reaches a locked door instead of the lobby. Add a contact on the garage door that reports when it stays open, and make the door close promptly behind each vehicle.

Stair doors from the garage are part of the same plan. They can be locked on the garage side, but must always open freely in the direction of exit.

A ten-unit building just went up near us at Columbia and Degraw. Does a building that small need what a 95-unit building on Hicks Street has?

No. A ten-unit building usually needs a reader at the street door, a video entry panel or a call station so visitors can reach each apartment, and readers on the one or two shared rooms that matter, often the cellar or the bike and storage room. Elevator floor control, separate amenity schedules and a staffed package process are for larger buildings. What a small building does need, just as much as a large one, is a clean credential list, an administrator who is not the developer, and doors that close and latch every time.

Because it has eight or more apartments, a ten-unit building also needs a working two-way voice intercom with remote release at the main entrance under Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a, so the entry panel is not optional.

Shops, stops and the street

There is a B61 stop right outside our street door on Columbia Street, and people waiting for the bus stand in our doorway. Does that change where the reader goes?

It does. The B61 runs north along Columbia Street, and a doorway beside a stop will always have people in it. We mount the reader where a resident can reach it without anyone standing behind them at eye level, often on the hinge side of the door or set into the reveal, and we prefer fobs or phone keys to keypad codes on a door like this, because a code can be read over a shoulder and a fob cannot. If a keypad is wanted for staff or deliveries, it gets a shrouded face.

We also tune the door alerts. A contact that reports a door held open will otherwise complain every time someone sheltering from the rain leans on it; a sensible delay and a check that the door actually latched fix that.

Our small performance space fills with an audience on show nights. Can the street door be controlled at all while people are inside?

Yes, on the entry side only. While the room is occupied the exit doors must open from inside with one push, with panic hardware where the occupancy calls for it, and the Building Code requires electrically locked exit doors to release on power loss and on fire alarm. What the system can do is decide who gets in: unlocked to the street for the hour before a show while staff are at the door, back to credentials once the show starts, and locked to everyone but staff and members after closing.

Your certificate of occupancy and any assembly permit set the rules for the room, so we design around them. Bring us those documents and the door schedule follows from them.

The city has floated closing the block of Columbia Street north of Hamilton Avenue to traffic. If deliveries can no longer stop at our front, should our side door take them?

Not yet. Streetsblog reported in August 2026 that the proposal would close that block entirely and that the changes are still years off, and a proposal can change many times before it is built. Design your doors for how deliveries arrive today. What is worth doing now is making sure the side or back door could take a reader later: a sound frame, a door that closes and latches, and a spare cable or empty conduit run to it while the walls or ceilings are open for other work.

If the street does change, a door that is already wired becomes a delivery entrance with a reader and a schedule in a single short visit.

The plans for the waterfront

The Brooklyn Marine Terminal review could take a decade. Should our condo wait to see what happens before replacing its door system?

We would not wait on the plan. Time Out reported that environmental review and permitting could take about a decade, and Borough President Antonio Reynoso said of it, “This is more of a proposal than it is an actual plan.” An entry system has its own clock: if your readers are copyable, your controller is unsupported or your keys are uncounted, those problems exist today, and a decade is close to the working life of the electronics anyway.

What the plan does argue for is flexibility. Choose readers and credentials that are not tied to one cloud provider, a controller with room for more doors, and a contract that gives the condo its own administrator account and its data on request, so the system can grow or change if the street around it does.

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Columbia Street Waterfront

These are generalizations we hear repeated in quick online answers, consumer articles and national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each is set against what a Columbia Street Waterfront door actually needs. We do not attribute any specific claim or figure to any one of those sources.

“Columbia Street Waterfront is just part of Carroll Gardens or Red Hook.”

Quick answers often fold the district into one of its neighbors, and listings encourage it. For a door plan, the difference is real. Carroll Gardens is known for its brownstone rows; the Columbia Street Waterfront is mostly 1980s condos, converted factories and six-story buildings from the last 25 years, with a handful of pre-war townhouses on Columbia Street.

Those buildings have different owners and decision-makers. A brownstone usually has one owner who can decide on a Tuesday; a Columbia Terrace building has a board, bylaws and owners who rent their units out.

We plan for the building in front of us, and the price list is the same Brooklyn list either way.

“Fobs make a building more secure on their own.”

A fob is only as strong as the door it controls and the list of people who hold one. A reader on a loft lobby door that does not close by itself, or a list that still includes the developer’s brokers, adds cost without adding security.

Habitat’s guide to small co-op and condo security quotes the board president of a ten-unit condo making the same point from the other side: “If you see someone you don’t know on the stoop, ask them to move on. Be polite, but say something.” Neighbors who know each other are part of the system.

We start every job by making each door close and latch on its own, and hand over a simple routine for keeping the credential list current.

“A magnetic lock is the simple way to secure any door.”

Magnetic locks are easy to mount, which is why they turn up in answers about lofts and storefronts with awkward frames. They are also the lock that most often gets egress wrong.

The NYC Building Code allows them on exit doors only with a release built into the door hardware that works with one hand and releases immediately, release on power loss, and a push-to-exit control, and Lori Greene notes in Doors & Hardware that “some jurisdictions require special permits for the use of electromagnetic locks.” A mag lock fitted without that release is a hazard, not a shortcut.

On most doors in this district an electric strike or electrified lever does the job better, and the inside handle keeps working whatever the power is doing.

“Access logs let a landlord or board see who comes and goes.”

Generic answers treat entry logs as a management perk. In New York City, a smart-access system in a building with tenants comes with legal limits on what is collected, how long it is kept and what it can be used for.

In a development like Columbia Commons, which mixes condo owners with income-restricted renters, the tenant rules apply to the renters’ use of shared doors. Boards also need their own rules on who may look at logs and why; Habitat’s reporting on fob buildings has long advised limiting that to serious incidents.

We set short retention by default, separate administrator rights, and give the board a written policy to adopt.

“Access control costs a few hundred dollars a door.”

National answers often quote the price of a lock on a shelf and call it a door. That is one part.

A controlled door with a reader or keypad, a strike, the cable, a programmed controller and ten fobs is $1,500 to $2,500 on our Brooklyn list. A condo or loft building with several doors on one cloud-managed system is $4,000 to $15,000, and a full building system with lobby, vestibule, elevator, garage and amenity doors is $15,000 to $40,000.

Columbia Street Waterfront pays the Brooklyn base list, with no surcharge for a waterfront address, a condo board or a factory building.

“Wireless locks avoid all the wiring problems in old buildings.”

In a loft with brick walls and an original ceiling, a battery lock looks like the obvious answer, and on a storage room door it can be. Consumer guides recommend them for exactly that reason.

On a street door used by 70 households, batteries are a weak point: they run down faster with heavy use and cold, and when they fail the door either stops locking or stops opening. A wired strike with a battery-backed controller in the cellar does not depend on anyone remembering to change batteries.

We wire the doors that carry the building and use wireless only for light-use interior doors where cable genuinely cannot go.

“Crime rankings tell you what to install.”

Some answers open with neighborhood crime grades. CrimeGrade gives this district an A for burglary, about 1.1 reported burglaries per 1,000 residents a year against 2.4 nationally, and notes that burglaries most often happen between midnight and 6 a.m.

That is good news, and it tells you nothing about which of your doors is left unlatched, who still has a key from three owners ago, or whether your garage door closes behind each car.

We plan from the doors and the people who use them. A low burglary rate is a reason to buy carefully, not a reason to leave the key problem unsolved.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Ask Who Holds the Admin Login Before Asking Which Reader

This describes how we approach a condo or loft building here, not a particular customer. In a building with a board, the first question that decides whether a system will still work in five years is not about hardware at all. It is who will hold the administrator login. Boards change every year or two, managing agents come and go, and a developer may still hold the only password to a building’s front door years after the last unit sold. So before we talk about readers, we ask the board to name two people, usually an officer and the managing agent, who will each hold an administrator account, and we write down how that access passes to the next board.

Then we walk the doors with the person who knows the building best, often an owner who has lived there since the 1980s. We look for the door everyone actually uses, which is not always the one the drawings call the main entrance, and the door nobody watches, which is usually a cellar or garage door. We check that each door closes and latches by itself. Only when those three things are right do we choose the reader, because a good reader on a door that does not latch protects nothing, and a good system nobody can log into is not a system at all.

Listings put our Columbia Street Waterfront building in Cobble Hill, Carroll Gardens or Red Hook. Does that change anything about the job?

+

It will, with the right reader and mounting. Metal behind a reader can shorten its read range, so on aluminum frames we use readers designed for metal mullions or fit a spacer behind a standard reader, and we test the read distance with real fobs and phones before we leave. Where the frame is too narrow, the reader goes on a short post or on the masonry beside the door instead, still within easy reach of the handle.

Can our condo spread the doors over two budget years without paying for the controller twice?
+

Yes. In the first year we install a controller sized for every door you plan to add, put readers on the doors that matter most, usually the street doors and the cellar, and run spare cable or empty conduit to the doors that will follow while the walls and ceilings are open. In the second year the remaining doors are added to the same controller and the same board account, and owners keep the credentials they already have. Each phase is quoted separately from the Brooklyn list.

Should our building’s fobs be labeled with apartment numbers or colors so we can tell them apart?
+

Not with apartment numbers. A lost fob marked 3B tells whoever finds it exactly which door it opens and which apartment it belongs to. The system already knows whose fob is whose by its serial number, so the label adds risk without adding information. Plain colors are fine if they help, for example one color for owners, another for renters and a third for staff, as long as the color does not reveal an apartment.

Can one crew install both the fob reader and a new video intercom at our street door on the same visit?
+

Yes, and it usually costs less than two separate jobs. Our technicians install access control and video intercoms, so the reader, the entry panel, the strike and the cable to each are planned together and go in at once. Combining them also means one panel can carry the reader inside it, one controller can release the door for both, and the board has one company to call. The written quote shows each part separately so the board can see what it is paying for.

If the controller in our cellar is ever damaged, is the building’s list of fobs and users lost?
+

It should not be. Cloud-managed systems keep the user list and schedules off site, so a replacement controller downloads them when it is installed. For local systems we export a copy of the configuration at handover and after any major change, and keep it with the job file. Either way we mount the controller well above the cellar floor, on its own labeled circuit, so the most likely kinds of damage are kept away from it in the first place.

Could our board try one door on fobs for a month before committing the whole building?
+

Yes, and for a hesitant board it is a sensible start. Choose the door that causes the most trouble today, often the cellar or the street door of one building in a complex, and we install it with a controller big enough for the rest. Owners get used to fobs on one door, the board sees how the administrator account works, and if the building goes ahead the other doors join the same system. If it does not, the board has one well-controlled door and has lost nothing.

How often should our condo board review the list of active fobs and codes?
+

Twice a year at a minimum, and after every sale, lease end or staff change. +

No. A reader, a strike and a controller are installed at the door and in the common areas, and nobody needs to enter an apartment for them. The only time apartments are involved is when the job also replaces the intercom handsets or adds indoor video screens, which are mounted inside each unit. If that is part of your plan, we schedule apartment visits in blocks and post the dates in advance so owners can arrange to be home.

Will an electric strike change how our street door feels when someone opens it from inside?
+

It should not change at all. A strike sits in the frame and controls only whether the latch can be pushed open from outside; the inside handle, knob or push bar works exactly as before, with no key, code or button needed to leave. What residents may notice is that the door now always latches when it closes, because we adjust the closer and hinges so it does. A soft click as the strike releases is the only new sound.

Once our board approves the price, how soon can the work be scheduled?
+

That depends on the equipment. Standard readers, strikes and controllers are ordered after the deposit and the job is scheduled once they arrive. Some entry panels and specialty locks are special-order items that do not sit on a supplier’s shelf, and their shipping time is outside our control, so we tell you before the deposit if anything in your plan is special order and give you the arrival date once it is confirmed. The installation date is set when everything is in hand.

Do you take on small jobs, such as one street door on a three-apartment townhouse?
+

Yes. A single controlled street door is the first line of our Brooklyn list, $1,500 to $2,500, and many of the pre-war townhouses on Columbia Street need exactly that: one reader, one strike, a controller in the cellar and a fob for each household, with the landlord’s key kept as an override. We size the controller so the cellar door or a yard door can be added later on the same system if the owner wants it.
