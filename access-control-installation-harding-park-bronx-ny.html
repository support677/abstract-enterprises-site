The Neighborhood

Why Harding Park Properties Need Access Control

Harding Park is the small community at the very southwestern tip of the Clason Point peninsula, where the Bronx River meets the East River. The Department of City Planning’s 2016 study of the neighborhood calls it “a small, private waterfront community” and counts about 400 residents in 226 single-family homes, with the Harding Park Homeowners Association administering the community and managing “the privately-owned properties and roads.” A 2019 Reader’s Digest profile described it as a blue-collar neighborhood of 236 tidy homes on the East River. The two counts differ by ten houses; we quote both and rely on neither.

The reason it feels like a separate place is its history. According to NYC Parks’ historical sign, in the 1920s Thomas Higgs, who owned about 100 acres of beachfront, began leasing tents to visitors; Higgs’s Beach and the neighboring Killian’s Grove were later merged, incorporated and cut into small lots for a summer bungalow colony that the residents named for President Warren G. Harding. After World War II a severe housing shortage pushed many tenants to turn their bungalows into year-round homes, and the community grew to about 250 families, mostly Irish, Scandinavian, German and Italian. For decades those families owned their houses but rented the ground beneath them. They survived Robert Moses’s 1957 attempt to clear what he called the “Soundview Slums,” won rent-control protection from the state legislature in 1962, and when the last private landowner, Federated Homes, defaulted on its taxes and the land passed to the city, they set out to buy the ground itself.

They succeeded. Parks and the association’s own history page date the cooperative to 1982 and describe it as the first cooperatively owned low- and moderate-income community in New York City; City Planning’s report gives 1981 and says the residents secured $2.5 million of public investment to rebuild the private sewer system and resurface the private ways, then reacquired the land from the city for $700,000. (A secondary neighborhood guide puts the purchase price at $1,000,000; the sources do not agree, so we quote the planning report and flag the difference.) The association says the purchase covered 18.7 acres, including common areas, gardens, a former volunteer firehouse that was replaced by a community center in 2002, and 22 easement accesses that residents call the “Private Ways.” Those lanes are the single most important fact for anyone planning a gate or a door system here.

The people have changed while the ownership model held. Wikipedia’s article on Clason Point notes that in the twenty-first century the Harding Park section became known as “Little Puerto Rico,” with narrow streets and bungalow homes along the waterfront, a name already used in a 1996 New York Times article about Hispanic settlers transforming the neighborhood. City Planning describes the population as largely Hispanic with many residents from Puerto Rico, and the homeowners association calls itself the first and only homeowners association governed by predominantly Latino homeowners, listing members from Puerto Rico, the Dominican Republic, Cuba, Jamaica, Haiti, Guyana, African nations, Europe, the Middle East, India and Bangladesh. Reader’s Digest counted 22 national flags flying outside the association’s building. Notes from a 2022 public hearing of the city’s districting commission sum it up as “Little Puerto Rico – generational households.”

The houses themselves explain much of what follows on this page. City Planning describes most of the 226 homes as one- to two-story bungalows built in the early twentieth century, light-frame and combustible, and expanded over the years with ad hoc additions, second stories and accessory structures, so that the neighborhood has a “dynamic and varied architectural character.” North of Gildersleeve Avenue the lots are uniformly narrow and front narrow mapped streets and “even narrower private lanes,” with little or no front, side or rear yard; south of Gildersleeve the lots are wider and vary drastically, and most of the streets are privately owned and between nine and thirteen feet wide. An architect who rebuilt one bungalow in 2016 wrote that when the city handed over the deed it effectively grandfathered the noncomplying construction, and that there were no certificates of occupancy, no sidewalks and few paved roads at the time.

Put those facts together and the access control problem looks different from almost anywhere else in the borough. There is no lobby and no gatehouse. Each household defends its own small lot, and the first line of that defense is usually a front gate set on the edge of a lane that belongs to the association. Visitors arrive at that gate looking for a number rather than a street address. The house behind the gate is wood, often with several generations inside and relatives coming and going. Neighbors are close enough that a careless camera or chime becomes their problem too; the Reader’s Digest piece even describes a retired NYPD detective using his motion-sensor cameras to identify the neighbor who had secretly shoveled his walk. Our job is to fit an entry system to that setting rather than to impose an apartment-building template on it.

Repairs

Same-Day Help for a Harding Park Gate, Keypad or Door Lock

When a front gate on a private way will not latch, the whole lot is open to the lane; when a keypad goes dark, the family is locked out of a house with no other easy way in. Ring (646) 490-0629 and describe what you see: a gate that swings free because its electric lock lost power, a keypad that beeps but will not turn the bolt, a doorbell station at the gate that rings nothing inside, or a door whose strike buzzes while the latch stays put. In small wood-frame houses the cause is often mechanical — a gate post that has leaned so the latch misses its keeper, a frame that has moved with the seasons, or batteries that finally gave out.

We will work on equipment that another company installed, and on locks and doorbells an owner fitted himself. If you rent your house, the owner needs to authorize the work; if the problem is on association property, such as a shared gate or the community center, the association’s officers are the people to call us.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Harding Park’s Bungalows and Cottages

City Planning’s description of the housing — light-frame bungalows from the early twentieth century, expanded piece by piece, with some lots overbuilt and others wide and irregular — matches what an installer finds at the door. No two houses are alike; Reader’s Digest’s nominator put it simply: no home looks like another. These are the types we plan for.

Original one-story bungalows

Small wood-frame houses that began as summer cottages, often with a front porch, a single entrance door and a low-pitched roof. Doors are usually wood in wood frames; the frames may be thin and shimmed. A surface-mounted keypad deadbolt or a light-duty strike suits them better than heavy commercial hardware that the frame cannot hold.

Bungalows with a century of additions

Houses that grew outward and upward: an enclosed porch, a rear room, a second story, a kitchen extension. The “front door” may now open into an addition, and there may be a forgotten door where two eras of the house meet. Cable routes have to follow the additions, not the original walls.

Rebuilt and two-story houses

Homes rebuilt to current code, sometimes raised, with insulated walls, modern steel or fiberglass doors and room in the walls for cable. These take wired readers and strikes cleanly, and a renovation is the cheapest moment to run a cable to the gate.

Houses on the narrow northern lots

North of Gildersleeve Avenue, where City Planning found uniformly narrow lots with little or no yard and houses very close together. Here a front gate may be only a few steps from the door, side doors face a neighbor’s wall, and every device has to respect the house next door.

Waterfront and southern-lane houses

South of Gildersleeve and below the lane known as Harding Park/South Street, where lots are wider and irregular, lanes are private and 9 to 13 feet wide, and back yards may face the river or city-owned shoreline. These houses tend to have a front gate, a back gate and more than one outside door.

Association property

The community center on Gildersleeve Avenue, gardens and other common property owned by the homeowners association. Doors here serve many people, are run by elected volunteers, and need administration that survives a change of officers.

The Private Ways

Lanes, Numbers and Shared Ground: Planning Entry on the Private Ways

Almost every access control decision in Harding Park starts with the lane in front of the house. The association’s history page describes 22 easement accesses that residents call the Private Ways, and City Planning found that most of the streets south of Gildersleeve Avenue are privately owned and between nine and thirteen feet wide, with homes fronting “narrow, paved lanes without sidewalks.” A handful of city-owned streets cross the area — the planning report counts six — but the lanes most houses face belong to the association, and their surfaces, drainage and lighting are association business.

The gate is the front door

On a city block, the front door of a house faces a sidewalk that belongs to the public. On a private way, the first thing a visitor meets is usually a low gate in a fence a step or two from the lane. That gate is the real entrance to the property, so it is where the entry system does most of its work: it is where a visitor is identified, where a delivery waits, and where the family wants to come and go without fumbling for keys. We usually recommend that the gate carry the credential — a keypad, a reader or a phone key — and that the house door keep a good deadbolt plus a contact, rather than the other way round.

Nine to thirteen feet changes the hardware

A keypad post that would be unremarkable on a suburban driveway can be a hazard on a lane only as wide as a car and a half. In many places there is no curb between the lane and the gate. So every device at the gate has to sit inside the property line and flush with the fence: keypads set into the gate pillar or on a slim post behind the fence line, gate locks on the yard-side face, cables run inside the post or under the yard rather than along the lane. Where a family wants a driver to reach a keypad from a car window, we explain honestly that on most private ways there is nowhere to put one without narrowing the lane.

Numbered houses and visitors who cannot find you

Harding Park addresses are typically a number followed by the words “Harding Park,” with no street name — public property listings show addresses such as 603, 721 and 901 Harding Park. To a delivery driver, a new home aide or a relative arriving at night, that can mean walking several lanes looking for a number. A video station at the gate helps in two ways: the visitor can press the button and ask, and the household can see who is there and talk them to the right gate. We also light the station and, where the owner wants it, mount the house number beside it at a readable height so that the gate itself answers the visitor’s first question.

Ground that belongs to everyone

Because the association owns the land under the lanes and common areas, any work that touches the lane — a trench across it, a post on its edge, a cable to a parking spot on the other side — is work on shared ground. Our practice is simple: we keep our work inside the homeowner’s own lot wherever possible, we prefer wireless links and through-the-post cabling to digging, and when a job genuinely needs to cross or touch association property, the homeowner raises it with the association before we start. The association’s board meets on the second Wednesday of each month at 7 p.m. at the community center on Gildersleeve Avenue, according to its website.

Water that sits in the lane

City Planning reports that the association-owned land is unsewered apart from a private sanitary network, so rainwater on the private streets has nowhere to go and “regular inundation of streets, yards, and homes” follows heavy rain. For an entry system that is a daily-weather problem, not just a storm problem. Gate keypads and readers need housings rated for outdoor use and mounted well above where puddles stand; buried cable needs conduit that is sealed and sloped to drain; and the battery-backed power supply that runs the gate lock belongs inside the house, off the floor, rather than in a box at the bottom of the gate post.

Commercial Access Control

Business and Community Access Control In and Around Harding Park

Harding Park itself is almost entirely residential. Wikipedia notes that Clason Point’s main shopping is “fairly distant,” along Story Avenue, the Bruckner Expressway and White Plains Road, and that the nearest retail strip is almost a mile away. The non-residential doors in and near the community are a short list: association buildings, home-based work, and the shops and offices along the corridors to the north. Places named below are landmarks for orientation, not clients.

Association buildings and community rooms

A community center hosts board meetings, events and programs, and is run by volunteers who change with each election. A reader or keypad on the main door with schedules for events, a separate code for each officer and staff member, and a log the board can review replace a key ring that nobody can account for.

Home-based businesses and workshops

Contractors, caterers and craftspeople who work from a house or a yard building need a door for tools and stock that is separate from the family’s door. A keypad with its own codes on that one door keeps helpers out of the house.

Shops on White Plains Road, Story Avenue and Soundview Avenue

Stock rooms, back offices and staff doors in the small shops that serve Clason Point, with a code per employee, a schedule that follows opening hours and a contact on the rear door that reports any opening after closing.

Offices, clinics and programs nearby

A reader for staff, video entry so the front desk can release the door for visitors, and an inner door that keeps records or medication rooms closed. Free exit from inside, always.

Rules That Apply

The Rules Behind a Harding Park Entry System

Much of New York City’s access control law was written for apartment buildings, and Harding Park is a community of single-family houses on cooperatively owned land. Some rules that dominate apartment neighborhoods barely apply here; others matter more. This is a plain-language summary of what we build to, not legal advice.

  • Single-family homes are not multiple dwellings. Under Multiple Dwelling Law §4, a multiple dwelling is a building occupied by three or more families living independently. City Planning describes Harding Park’s homes as single-family, so the self-closing, self-locking entrance-door and intercom rules of §50-a, and the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law for smart access in multiple dwellings, generally do not reach a family’s own gate or front door. For rented houses we still collect only what the system needs.
  • Zoning sets limits on structures, not on locks. City Planning notes that Harding Park is zoned R3A, where two residential structures on a single lot are prohibited, and that many homes are grandfathered because they predate the 1961 Zoning Resolution and cannot be altered in ways that increase their noncompliance. Readers, keypads, contacts and gate locks add no floor area and do not change a home’s zoning position.
  • Low-voltage work and permits. The city’s Administrative Code (§28-105.4.7) exempts most electrical work under 50 volts from Department of Buildings electrical permits, which covers the keypads, readers, strikes and contacts we install. A new 120-volt circuit or outlet to feed a power supply is licensed electrician’s work, and we say in the quote whether one is needed and who will do it.
  • Free exit, every time. The NYC Building Code’s rules for electrically locked egress doors (sections 1010.1.9.8 and 1010.1.9.9) require locks that release on loss of power and simple exit hardware where electromagnetic locks are used in the buildings those sections cover. In a family house we go further than the code requires: every controlled door opens from inside with one ordinary motion, with no code, card or power needed to get out.
  • Motorized gates. On the rare Harding Park lot with room for a motorized vehicle gate, the operator must meet the UL 325 safety standard, with entrapment protection that DASMA’s technical data sheet explains in detail. Most front gates here are pedestrian gates and need no operator at all.
  • Who may install. Security and access control installers in New York State must be licensed under General Business Law §69-m. Ours is #12000287431.
Light Frame, Close Neighbors

Wood Houses Built Close Together: What That Means for the Hardware

Two facts from City Planning’s report shape how we choose and mount equipment in Harding Park more than any brand decision. The first is that the building stock is “old, light-frame, and combustible.” The second is that, especially on the narrow northern lots, homes have “limited to nonexistent front, side, and rear yards and are located extremely close to one another.”

Light framing: hardware the house can actually hold

Commercial access control is designed for steel frames and solid-core doors. Put a heavy electric strike into a thin wood jamb that has been shimmed, patched and repainted for decades and the frame becomes the weak point: the strike holds, the jamb splits. In bungalows we choose hardware for the frame in front of us — keypad deadbolts that replace an existing bolt, light-duty strikes with reinforced strike plates, long screws driven into the stud behind the jamb — and we sometimes recommend replacing a tired door and frame as a unit before adding anything electronic.

Combustible construction: getting out matters more than keeping out

In a small wood house with several generations under one roof, the most important property of any lock is how fast it lets people out. We never put a lock that needs a code, card or key to exit on a door that people use to leave, we avoid magnetic locks in houses altogether, and we choose fail-safe or fail-secure behavior door by door with the family, explaining what each door will do if the power fails during a fire. Battery-backed supplies sit where a fire would not trap anyone trying to reach them, and nothing we install blocks a window that serves as a second way out of a bedroom.

Close neighbors: devices that respect the house next door

When a neighbor’s window is a few feet from your side door, a video doorbell aimed carelessly records their kitchen, a chime set loud wakes their baby, and a motion-triggered light shines into their bedroom. We set camera fields of view and privacy masks so door stations see your gate and your path, not the next lot; we choose chimes and indoor stations over loud outdoor sounders; and we aim and time lights so they help your visitors without disturbing anyone else. A system that annoys the neighbors is a failure even if it works.

Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Harding Park: Questions Answered

These are the questions that come up when the property is a bungalow on a private way rather than an apartment behind a lobby. They are grouped by the part of the lot they concern. Where an answer touches on law, it is a summary rather than legal advice.

The Lane, the Gate and the Number on It

Drivers and visitors keep wandering the private ways looking for our house number. What can the front gate do to bring them to the right door?

Give the gate a face and a voice. A small video door station on the gate post, lit at night, with the house number mounted beside it at eye level, does three jobs at once: it shows a visitor that they have found the right number, it lets them press a button and ask if they are unsure, and it lets someone inside — or on a phone at work — see who is there and talk them to the gate. For deliveries we can add a one-time or scheduled code so a regular courier or grocery driver can open the gate and leave parcels inside the fence rather than on the lane.

We mount the station where a standing adult reads it easily, angle the camera at the visitor rather than the neighbor’s porch, and pick a model whose button lights up in the dark.

Our gate opens onto a lane barely wider than a car. Where can a keypad go so it does not stick out into the lane?

Inside your fence line, flush with the gate. On most private ways there is no planting strip or curb between the lane and the fence, so a post set in front of the gate would sit on association ground and in the path of every passing car. Instead we set the keypad or reader into the masonry pillar if the gate has one, mount it on the gate post itself with a low-profile housing, or set a slim post just behind the fence so the keypad reads through or over the gate. The person using it stands at the gate, exactly where they would stand to open it with a key.

Cable follows the same rule. We run it inside the hollow gate post or the fence pillar, then under the front yard to the house, never along the surface of the lane. If the front yard is paved and too small to trench, a short wireless link between a battery keypad and the lock, or between the gate and a hub in the house, keeps the lane untouched.

The mailbox is inside our front gate. If the gate locks, how does the mail carrier get to it?

There are three workable answers, and the right one depends on how you like to receive mail. The simplest is to move the mailbox to the outside face of the fence or gate pillar, so the carrier never needs to open anything; a locking mailbox there also protects checks and medication deliveries. The second is a gate that stays on the latch during the hours the mail usually comes and locks on a schedule afterwards, which suits households where someone is home most days. The third is a schedule-limited code given to the regular carrier, which some families use for parcel couriers but which depends on your local carrier and route rules.

Whatever you choose, do not leave the gate permanently unlatched for the mail; that defeats the lock.

Rainwater stands in front of our gate after every heavy storm. Can a keypad and buried cable survive that?

Yes, if they are chosen and placed for it. City Planning reports regular ponding on Harding Park’s unsewered private streets, so we treat the area in front of a gate as a place where water will sit for hours several times a year. Keypads and readers go on the post at chest height, in housings rated for outdoor exposure, with the cable entering from below through a drip loop so water runs away from the electronics. Underground, cable goes in sealed conduit laid to fall toward the lane, with no splices underground, and the lock’s power supply and battery stay inside the house, off the floor.

The gate lock itself is the part most likely to suffer, because it sits low and takes splash. We use weather-rated gate locks and keepers, mount them on the yard side of the gate, and check that the latch does not sit in standing water. If your gate post has started to lean because the ground stays soft, we will tell you to fix the post first; a lock cannot compensate for a gate that no longer meets its keeper.

Our front gate swings outward into the private way. Should it be rehung to open inward before a lock goes on?

In most cases, yes. A gate that swings out over a lane nine to thirteen feet wide blocks part of a shared path every time it opens, catches on passing cars and bicycles, and can be pulled open from the lane side more easily than one that closes against a stop. Rehanging it to swing into your own yard keeps the lane clear, puts the hinges and the lock keeper on your side of the fence, and lets an electric gate lock pull the gate firmly against its stop. Where the yard is too shallow for an inward swing, a sliding pedestrian gate or a narrower leaf may fit better.

We check the swing, the hinge side and the latch side at the survey and price any rehanging separately, because the lock we choose depends on which way the gate closes.

We park on a pad across the private way from our house. Can anything protect the car there without running cable across the lane?

Yes. Because the lane belongs to the association, we avoid trenching across it unless the association has agreed. Instead we use a camera at the house that sees across the lane to the parking pad, a wireless sensor on a parking-pad gate or chain if there is one, or a battery-powered camera or alert device on the far side that links back to the house wirelessly. Any of these will tell you when someone is around the car at night, which is the useful part; none of them will physically stop a determined thief, and we will not pretend otherwise.

Vehicle theft does happen here — a LoJack dealer bulletin, for example, describes an NYPD Bronx Auto Larceny case in which an owner reported a vehicle stolen while it was parked at a residence on Harding Park — so the conversation is worth having. For a car, a camera that captures the approach and an alert that wakes you are worth more than a lock on a gate that does not exist.

Our back yard opens toward the shoreline and city parkland. Should the back gate lock from the water side but open freely from our yard?

That is usually the right arrangement. City Planning notes that Harding Park includes more than 12,000 square feet of city-owned parkland and open space along the waterfront, so some rear gates face ground the public can reach. A gate there should open from the yard side with a simple lever or thumb-turn, so the family can always get out toward the water, and should lock from the outside, opening only for a family code or fob if you use it as an entrance at all. Many families never use the back gate as a way in and prefer a plain mechanical lock plus a contact that alerts them whenever it opens.

A rear gate facing open water takes wind and salt spray, so we use stainless or powder-coated hardware and keep electronics to a minimum.

Inside an Old Bungalow

Our bungalow sits on piers over a crawl space instead of a basement. How do you get cable from the house to the front gate?

Through the crawl space, if it is reachable and dry enough to work in, or around the outside of the house in a protected path if it is not. Many original Harding Park bungalows were never built with full basements, and later additions may sit on different foundations from the original house. We look at each section separately: often a cable can enter the crawl space near the front wall, run along a joist to the point nearest the gate, and leave through a sealed sleeve into a shallow conduit under the front yard. Where the crawl space is too low, wet or closed off, we use a wireless link from the gate to the house instead.

Inside, the controller or hub needs a dry, accessible home with an outlet nearby — a closet shelf, the back of a utility room or a high shelf in the kitchen pantry, rather than the crawl space floor. We plan that location first, because every other route on the job leads back to it.

Our neighbor’s window is about six feet from our side door. How do we add a camera-equipped door station there without recording into their home?

With careful aiming and the privacy settings most modern stations already have. We mount the station so the lens points along your own path or at the person standing at your door, not across the gap at the neighbor’s wall, and then draw privacy masks in the configuration so any part of the frame that shows their window, door or yard is blacked out before recording. Motion detection zones get the same treatment, so the station does not record every time the neighbor walks past their own window.

If a door cannot be covered without seeing into the next house, we fit an audio-only station or a simple contact there and keep the camera at the gate.

Dozens of neighbors’ Wi-Fi networks show up on our phone. Will a wireless keypad or gate sensor be reliable on such a crowded lane?

It can be, but crowded radio space is a real factor in a neighborhood of small houses built close together. Many consumer smart locks and sensors use the same 2.4 GHz band as home Wi-Fi routers, and when dozens of networks overlap, connections can drop or lag. Before recommending a wireless device for the gate, we measure signal strength and congestion at the gate with the household’s own network running, and choose between options: a lock that talks to a dedicated hub on a less crowded protocol, a hub placed close to the gate side of the house, or a short cable run that removes the radio question entirely.

The rule we follow is that the gate must open by keypad or key even when every radio is down. A wireless link should add convenience — phone unlocking, notifications, remote codes — never become the only way in.

Our house never had a certificate of occupancy, like many here. Will an installer’s work create a filing that affects the house?

Not for the low-voltage part of the job. City Planning and an architect who has worked here both describe homes that predate current zoning and, in many cases, never had certificates of occupancy. Keypads, readers, strikes, contacts and door stations run at under 50 volts, and the city’s Administrative Code exempts most such work from electrical permits, so installing them does not by itself require a Department of Buildings filing. Nothing we install adds floor area or changes the building.

Two exceptions are worth knowing. A new 120-volt circuit or outlet must be installed by a licensed electrician, who files for it. And replacing an exterior door and frame is ordinary repair in most cases, but changing an opening’s size or location is construction. When a job needs either, we say so in writing before work begins, so the owner can decide with full information.

Families, Absences and the Association

We spend part of each year in Puerto Rico and rent the house to relatives while we are away. Can we manage the doors from there?

Yes, with a system built for remote management and a plan for when the internet fails. A cloud-managed lock or controller lets you add and delete codes, see who opened the gate and when, and receive alerts from anywhere with a phone signal. For a house that is occupied by relatives or tenants while you are away, we set the occupants up with their own codes, give a local relative or neighbor an emergency code, and keep the owner’s administrator account separate from everyone else’s.

The weak point is the house’s internet connection. If the router is unplugged or the service lapses, remote control stops, so we choose locks that keep working by keypad and key without the network, and we can add a cellular connection to the hub for owners who need to stay in touch however the house internet is doing. Before you travel, we walk you through changing a code from your phone so you are not learning it from another time zone.

Our association’s officers change after each election. How should door access for a shared building pass from one board to the next?

Plan the handover into the system on day one. The administrator account for a shared building should belong to the association as an organization, using an association email address and recovery details kept with the association’s records, not to whichever volunteer happened to set it up. Each officer then gets their own administrator login, which can be removed the day their term ends without affecting anyone else, and each staff member or regular volunteer gets their own code with a schedule.

At each changeover, outgoing and incoming officers review the user list together and delete codes nobody needs; we leave a one-page handover sheet by the controller listing those steps.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Harding Park

We could not see Google’s AI Overview answers for Harding Park while preparing this page, so nothing below quotes one. Instead we test claims common in generic answers and cost guides against Harding Park’s lanes, lots and houses.

“A private community needs a guarded gate at its entrance”

Generic advice about private communities starts at one perimeter gate with a guard booth and a barrier arm. It does not fit Harding Park, where the association owns the lanes but the community is threaded through by city streets and bounded by parks and water, and where each household’s lot opens directly onto a private way.

In practice, security here is house by house. The meaningful entry point is each family’s own front gate, and the meaningful decisions are about who has a code to it, how a visitor is identified there, and what the back of the lot does. A perimeter gate is not on the table, and advice built around one wastes a homeowner’s time.

So we plan one lot at a time: a waterfront house with two gates needs a different plan from a narrow-lot bungalow.

“Access control is for offices and apartment buildings, not houses”

Many overviews still treat access control as a commercial product: badge readers, server software, a facilities manager. Homes are steered toward a smart lock and nothing more. For a Harding Park household with grandparents, adult children, visiting relatives, a home aide and a contractor working on an addition, the problem of who can open which door, when, is exactly the problem access control solves.

The equipment scales down well. A keypad on the gate, a keypad deadbolt on the house door and a pair of contacts on the back door and rear gate, all in one app, give a family most of what a building gets — individual codes, schedules, a history and alerts — without a server or a monthly fee.

What changes at house scale is the priority list: free exit, simplicity for older relatives, and keys that still work when the batteries or internet do not.

“Wireless smart locks are plug-and-play in any small home”

Product listings promise a twenty-minute install and an app that just works. In a small house in an open suburb, that is often true. In a neighborhood of small houses packed close together, dozens of overlapping Wi-Fi networks compete for the same radio band, and a lock at a front gate thirty feet from the router, on the far side of a wood-and-plaster wall, may connect intermittently or drain its batteries searching for a signal.

The fix is not to avoid wireless; it is to test before choosing. We measure signal at the gate, consider a dedicated hub, and keep a wired option ready for the openings that matter most.

And whatever the radio does, a keypad and a mechanical key should always open the gate. A lock that only works through an app is not ready for a lane where the internet can be unreliable during storms.

“Access control costs a few hundred dollars installed”

Cost figures in generic answers vary widely because they mix very different products. Angi, for example, publishes $560 to $2,900 for security door installation and Fixr $525 to $1,200 for a security entry door — figures for doors, not electronic entry. Installer blogs describe wired access control at $1,500 to $5,000 per door, and installers on Reddit’s access control forum have suggested budgeting around $2,500 to $3,000 per door.

Our own published Bronx figures, which apply in Harding Park at 1.00 times our Brooklyn base, start at $1,500 to $2,500 for a single controlled door or gate with keypad or reader, lock, battery-backed supply and programming. A homeowner-fitted keypad deadbolt can cost far less, and for a single bungalow door it may be the right answer; we will say so.

What a few-hundred-dollar figure leaves out is the gate: the lock, the keeper, the post work, the cable or link to the house and the setup that makes it one system with the door.

“ADT, Ring or SimpliSafe will cover it”

National brands such as ADT, Ring and SimpliSafe sell good doorbells, cameras and alarm packages, and plenty of Harding Park homes have one. They are designed around a front door in a typical American house. They do not survey a lane nine feet wide, decide where a keypad can stand without blocking it, reinforce a soft gate stile, or tie a front-gate lock and a back-gate contact into one schedule for a household of eight.

A local installer’s value is in those details and in being reachable when the gate lock fails on a Sunday. We integrate with doorbells and alarms owners already have where the products allow it, rather than ripping them out.

If a national package already meets your needs, keep it.

“Older houses need a city permit before you add electronic locks”

Owners of grandfathered houses worry, understandably, that any work will draw the Department of Buildings’ attention. Generic answers sometimes reinforce that worry by telling readers to “check with your local building department” for every lock.

In New York City, the Administrative Code exempts most electrical work under 50 volts from DOB electrical permits, which covers keypads, readers, strikes, contacts and door stations. A new 120-volt outlet or circuit is electrician’s work that is filed, and changing the size of a door opening is construction. Swapping a lock or adding a keypad to an existing door is neither.

We spell out in every Harding Park quote whether any part of the job needs an electrician or a filing, so the owner decides with the facts in hand.

“More cameras always mean more security”

Security content online often treats coverage as the goal: a camera on every side, motion lights everywhere, alerts for every movement. On a narrow Harding Park lot, that approach records the neighbors, floods their bedrooms with light and buries the household in notifications about people walking down a shared lane.

Good access control on a small lot is selective. The gate gets a station that identifies visitors; the house door gets a credential; the back gate gets a contact; and cameras, if any, look at your own path with privacy masks over everything else. The city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law applies to multiple dwellings rather than single-family homes, but its principle — collect only what the system needs — is a good rule for any house.

Fewer, better-placed devices are cheaper and kinder to the neighbors.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Measure the Lane Before You Choose the Keypad

This is a note on method rather than a story about a particular house. On a private way, the first thing we do at a survey is not look at the door. We stand in the lane with a tape measure and find three lines: where the association’s ground ends and the homeowner’s lot begins, how much clear width the lane has at the gate, and where water stands after rain. Those three lines decide where the keypad can go, how the cable can travel and which way the gate is allowed to swing, long before any product is chosen.

The second thing we do is open the gate and close it, several times, and watch the latch. A surprising number of front gates on small lots no longer latch on their own: a post has settled, a hinge has dropped, the keeper has been bent by years of being kicked shut. An electric lock fitted to a gate like that becomes a gate that is locked only when someone remembers to slam it. Fixing the post and the latch is often the cheapest and most valuable part of the whole job.

FAQ

Harding Park Access Control FAQ

How slim can a keypad or video station be for a narrow gate post?
+

Slimmer than most people expect. Many door stations and video doorbells are made in mullion widths of about two inches, designed for the narrow frames of storefront doors, and the same models fit a square gate post or the edge of a fence pillar. Single-gang keypads and readers are similar in size. What sets the minimum is not the device but the cable: it needs a protected path into or behind the post. On a hollow metal post we run it inside; on a wooden post we route a groove behind the device or fit a slim cover.

If the association repaves our lane and the gate post has to come out, can the keypad be removed and refitted?
+

Yes. We install gate devices with a service loop of spare cable inside the post or the yard box and with connectors at the post, so a keypad, reader or gate lock can be unbolted, set aside safely and refitted once the new post or paving is in place. Tell us when work on the lane is scheduled and we will remove and store the devices, then reinstall and retest them at our standard service rate.

Can a new keypad lock be keyed to match the house keys we already use?
+

Usually, yes. Many keypad deadbolts and keypad levers accept a standard cylinder, and a locksmith can rekey that cylinder to match your existing keys, so the family carries one key for the override as well as the other locks. Some smart locks use proprietary cylinders that cannot be matched, and we tell you before you buy. One key for everything is a useful backup for the day the batteries are flat.

Is an electric strike or gate lock noisy enough to bother neighbors close by?
+

Most are quiet. A strike or gate lock makes a short click or a brief buzz when it releases, and many models are silent apart from the latch moving. The sounds that bother neighbors are usually the doorbell chime set loud on an outside wall or a station that announces every visitor by speaker. We set chimes indoors, choose stations with adjustable volume, and turn off spoken prompts at night if the household wants. On lots where a window is a few feet from the gate, we test the sound with the neighbor's window in mind before leaving.

Does the job happen mostly in the yard, or do you need to work inside the house?
+

Both, but the inside work is usually brief. Gate locks, keypads and video stations are mounted outside, and cable is pulled from the gate across the yard. Inside, we need to reach the spot where the cable enters, the place where the hub and power supply will live, and each door that gets a lock or a contact. In a small bungalow that may mean an hour or two of work in a closet, the crawl space access and at the doors.

If we start with one battery keypad on the front door, can it join a larger system later?
+

That depends on the lock you choose, which is why we ask about future plans first. Some keypad deadbolts work only on their own or with their maker's app; others connect to a hub using common smart-home protocols and can later be managed alongside a gate lock, contacts and a video station. If you expect to add the gate within a year or two, we recommend a lock that can join a hub, so the first purchase is not wasted. If the front door is all you will ever need, a good standalone keypad deadbolt is fine.

Can worn buttons on a keypad give away our code?
+

Over time they can. On keypads with soft or painted keys, the four or five numbers in a family code wear shiny or dull before the rest, and a passer-by can narrow the guesses. The remedies are simple: choose a keypad with hard or backlit keys that wear evenly, use codes of six digits or more, change the household code once or twice a year, and give each person their own code so no single combination is used dozens of times a day.

Will you take a job as small as one keypad deadbolt on one door?
+

Yes. A single keypad deadbolt on a bungalow door is a common Harding Park job. If you buy the lock, we install it on a labor-only basis, priced after we see the model and the door; if you want us to supply it, it is priced as a single opening.

Can a trusted neighbor who watches the house have a code, and can we see when it is used?
+

Yes. The neighbor gets their own code, fob or phone key, separate from the family's, and you can choose whether it works at all times or only while you are away. Most systems can notify you whenever that particular code is used, without sending alerts for the family's own comings and goings. When the arrangement ends, deleting that one code removes their access without changing anyone else's.

Should the front gate and the house door use the same code, or different ones?
+

For most families, one personal code that works at both is easier to live with, and it is what we usually set up for household members. Different rules make sense for others: a delivery code that opens only the gate, an aide's code that opens the gate and the side door during set hours, a contractor's code that opens the gate and a workshop door but not the house. A system that manages both openings from one app lets you mix these freely, so each person carries one code with exactly the reach they need.
