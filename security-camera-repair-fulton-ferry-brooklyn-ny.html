The Neighborhood

Why Fulton Ferry Properties Need Security Camera Repair

Fulton Ferry is one of the smallest named neighborhoods in Brooklyn: the old ferry district at the foot of Old Fulton Street, between DUMBO and Brooklyn Heights, cut in two by the approach to the Brooklyn Bridge. The Landmarks Preservation Commission made it a historic district in 1977, and most of its buildings date from the 1830s to the 1890s: warehouses, stores, a bank, an insurance office and a railroad company building, two to four stories high, with the eight-story Eagle Warehouse as the tall exception. Since the 1970s many of them have been turned into apartments, usually as small cooperatives. Around them sit newer condominiums on Furman Street, restaurants at the landing, and the entrance to Brooklyn Bridge Park at Pier 1. Almost every one of these buildings has cameras now. The repairs they need are shaped less by crime than by water, by age and by the way small buildings are run.

Water comes first because the river is right there. When Hurricane Sandy pushed the East River over its banks in October 2012, the Brooklyn Eagle found a building owner on lower Old Fulton Street pumping out a basement that had been "flooded from floor to ceiling with murky seawater," and a restaurateur at 7 Old Fulton Street who said, "I've been pumping the basement all night." A block away at Main and Water Streets, a bakery owner stood in a shop that had taken at least four feet of water and said, "We don't have power, so I can't check to see if the equipment is working." In the park, the basement that held the electronics for Jane's Carousel filled with about five feet of water. Those are the same rooms where camera recorders, power boxes, internet modems and network switches are usually kept, and a flood does not have to reach the ceiling to finish them.

What happened next is just as important for repair work. After the storm, the city changed its building code, and new buildings in the park were designed, as Untapped New York put it, "with all mechanicals and electricity housed above the new flood plain line." The Empire Stores warehouse had its ground floor dug out and replaced with a concrete slab. Older buildings in Fulton Ferry were not rebuilt that way. Many put their equipment back where it had been, or moved some of it up while leaving cables running down through the cellar and back out. When a camera system here fails in a strange way years later, the cause is often a part that was soaked once, dried out, and kept working until it could not.

Power is the second pressure. On June 23, 2025, Con Edison asked about 68,000 customers in DUMBO, Vinegar Hill, Brooklyn Heights, Downtown Brooklyn and the neighborhoods to the south to cut back on air conditioning and heavy appliances while it made repairs, and it lowered the voltage in the area by eight percent to protect its equipment. The zone it described ran from the East River inland, which takes in Fulton Ferry. A voltage cut like that does not usually black out a building, but recorders, cheap plug-in adapters and older power boxes do not like running low for hours, and some of them do not recover cleanly when the voltage comes back up.

The third pressure is who is in charge. A converted nineteenth-century building with ten or twenty apartments is often a self-managed co-op, with no super on site and a board of volunteers that changes every year or two. The Eagle Warehouse, at 28 Old Fulton Street, became an 84-unit co-op in 1980; the former Brooklyn City Railroad Company building at 8 Old Fulton Street was turned into about ten loft apartments in the 1970s. In buildings like these the camera system was usually chosen by one shareholder, installed by one company, and remembered by nobody once that person sold. Brick Underground has pointed out that "there's no city law requiring rental buildings to have functioning security cameras," so a broken camera in a small building gets fixed only when someone decides it is their job.

The fourth pressure is the crowd outside. Brooklyn Bridge Park reported nearly 5 million visitors in its 2023 season, and Old Fulton Street is one of only four ways into it. On a summer weekend the line for the pizzerias runs up the block, the ferry lands people every few minutes, and the sidewalk in front of a residential door can be full from late morning until well after dark. That traffic changes what a door camera has to do, and how its recording and alerts should be set.

So a camera repair in Fulton Ferry is more than swapping a dead camera. It is getting the system recording again, taking the parts that matter out of the water's way, making sure the board or the owner is told the next time something stops, and leaving behind logins and labels that will outlast the next change of officers. Many co-ops that call us about cameras also ask about the street door, because a camera at the entrance and a working intercom or entry system protect the same doorway; we can look at both on the same visit.

Field Notes: How We Work a Fulton Ferry System That Has Seen Water

These notes describe how our technicians approach a typical Fulton Ferry co-op, condo or restaurant system when cameras are out and the cellar has taken water at some point, recently or years ago.

Ask about the water before touching the power. The first question is when water last reached the cellar or ground floor and how high it came. A recorder that is off after a flood stays off until it has been opened up and inspected; a recorder that is running and dry gets its footage exported before anything else.

Look for the waterline. Stains on the walls, rust on shelf legs and salt crust on boxes tell us how high the water came, and therefore which power boxes, switches, connectors and cable junctions need to be opened and checked, even if they still seem to work.

Trace every cable that goes low. In older buildings many runs drop down into the cellar on their way between floors. Each one is toned out, labeled and tested, because a splice that sat in water years ago is the most common hidden fault here.

Check the outdoor end. At each camera facing the river or the street, the technician opens the connector box, checks the seal and looks for salt or green corrosion on the contacts before deciding whether it is the camera or its cable that failed.

Price, raise and hand over. You hear the cause and get a written price before any part is replaced. When the repair is done, anything we can lift above the waterline is lifted, every camera is checked by day and after dark, alerts for lost video and drive errors are turned on and sent to the right person, and the board gets the logins and a list of what is connected where.

Fault by Fault

Water, Salt, Low Power, Crowds and Construction: Why Fulton Ferry Camera Systems Fail

Five causes account for most of the camera failures we find in Fulton Ferry. Knowing which one you are dealing with is the difference between a $150 visit and a new system.

Water: the damage you cannot see on the screen

Seawater is worse for electronics than rain, because the salt it leaves behind keeps conducting and corroding after the water has drained. A recorder that sat in a few inches of surge water can dry out, start up and show every camera, then fail weeks or months later as corrosion spreads across its board or its drive heads stick. The same is true of power boxes, network switches and the connectors on cables that pass through a cellar. After a flood we open and inspect what sat low, clean and keep what is sound, and replace what is not. Then we put what remains above the level the water reached, because the next storm will find the same shelf.

Salt and wind: the riverfront end of the cable

Cameras facing the East River or the landing live in salt air and wind that drives rain into every gap. The camera bodies usually last; the weak points are the connectors behind them, the seals on the junction boxes and the screws that hold the bracket. When a picture starts to flicker, roll or turn soft in wet weather, the fault is usually water creeping into a connector, not a dying camera. Re-terminating the connector inside a sealed box, with the cable looped so water runs off rather than in, is a small repair on our Brooklyn list. A new camera is only needed when the camera itself has taken water.

Low power: what a hot afternoon does to a recorder

The eight percent voltage reduction Con Edison put on northwest Brooklyn in June 2025 is the kind of event that shows up in a camera system days later. Recorders, switches and plug-in adapters running on low voltage in a hot closed cellar work harder and run hotter; some restart themselves, some corrupt the file they were writing, and some cheap adapters simply die. Outages and surges do the rest. A battery backup that holds the recorder and switch steady through dips and short outages, sized for the equipment it carries and with a fresh battery, prevents most of these faults. A recorder that was never on one is often the reason a system keeps failing after every heat wave.

Crowds: when the camera is working but useless

A surprising share of Fulton Ferry repair calls involve cameras that never stopped working. A camera over a co-op door on Old Fulton Street can record every minute of a summer Saturday on a drive that only holds four days, so the night that mattered is gone by the time anyone looks. Motion alerts that fire every few seconds get muted. A camera aimed to take in the bridge and the street makes the person at the door a few pixels tall. None of this needs new hardware: it needs recording schedules, motion zones around the door, a lens zoomed in on the entry, and a drive sized to the days the board wants.

Construction: the neighborhood is still being built

Fulton Ferry looks finished, but it rarely is. CityRealty has reported foundation work on a new brick commercial building at 20 Old Fulton Street, a waterfront pavilion approved for 1 Water Street, and a rezoning that would allow a five-story building at 50 Old Fulton Street. Even the ferry landing was rebuilt in 2021. Construction next door brings vibration, dust, scaffolding and crews who work along party walls and sidewalks where camera cables run. When a camera dies the same month a neighbor's job starts, the cable or the connector nearest the work is the first thing we check, and we write down what we find so you can raise it with the contractor.

State licensing. The historic district. Every building in the Fulton Ferry Historic District falls under the Landmarks Preservation Commission. Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Repair in Fulton Ferry: Questions Answered

These are the situations Fulton Ferry co-op boards, condo managers, owners and shopkeepers describe when they call. Each answer is written for the buildings and the waterfront here.

Water, the cellar and where the recorder lives

After the last flood our board moved the recorder from the cellar to a closet on an upper floor. Since then the two cameras at the street door freeze and drop out. Could the longer cable be the cause?

Very possibly. Moving a recorder up several floors often means the cameras that were closest to it are now the farthest away, and the cables were extended to reach. Network camera cable has a working limit of about 100 meters per run, older coax cameras lose picture quality over long runs, and a quick splice made to add length is a common weak point. We test each extended run, replace the splice or the run where it fails, and where the distance is too long, put a small switch or extender part of the way, kept above the flood line, rather than moving the recorder back down.

Our recorder and power boxes were raised off the cellar floor after Sandy, but the camera cables still drop down into the old cellar and come back up. Should those runs be rerouted?

If the cables only pass through the cellar inside their jacket, with no splice or junction box down there, they are usually fine; good camera cable tolerates getting wet better than any connector does. The risk is the joints: a splice box, a coupler or a power tap that sits low will corrode the first time it is flooded. On a repair we find every joint below the waterline, move it up or replace the run through that section with one continuous piece, and note on the cable map where each run passes low so the next flood check is quick.

Water got into our cellar during a storm. Before we throw anything out, what should we photograph or keep for the flood insurance claim?

Before anything is removed, photograph the waterline on the walls, the shelf or floor where the recorder, switch and power boxes sat, the label on each device with its model and serial number, and any visible corrosion. Keep the damaged recorder and drive until the adjuster has seen them or said they can go, because the drive may still hold footage and the adjuster may want to see the hardware. We write up which parts were damaged by water, which were already failing, and the replacement price for each, which is the document most insurers ask for.

Our recorder sits in one of the old brick storage vaults in the cellar. The thick walls keep it cool, but the phone app keeps losing it. Why?

The vault walls that keep the recorder cool also block any wireless signal, so if the recorder reaches the building's internet over Wi-Fi or a powerline adapter, the link will be weak and drop under load. The cameras keep recording; only the remote connection fails. The fix is a proper network cable from the recorder to the building's router or switch, or a small switch inside the vault fed by one cable. We also check that the vault stays dry after storms.

Is there a camera that can survive being underwater, so the ones on our ground floor stop dying every time the street floods?

Not in a practical sense. Outdoor cameras are rated to keep out rain and spray, and some ratings allow brief immersion in fresh water, but a camera's cable connection, its power source and the box behind it are not built to sit in salt water. A surge that covers the camera will usually also cover the connector, and that is where it fails. The better repair is to mount ground-level cameras as high as the view allows, keep their connectors and power above the water mark, and accept that a camera low on a riverfront wall is the one most likely to need replacing after a storm.

The landmark district, the street and construction

Our co-op wants the replacement for a dead facade camera to be smaller and darker than the old one. Is that still a repair, or does a change in look need Landmarks approval?

The commission's guidebook treats ordinary maintenance and repair of existing safety equipment as work that needs no permit, and it handles like-for-like replacements with a simple filing that includes the maker's cut sheet rather than drawings. A smaller, darker camera on the same mounting point usually moves the building closer to what the guidebook asks for, which is equipment that is small and painted or finished to blend in. If the new camera needs a new mounting point, a longer arm or new conduit on the facade, that part follows the commission's mounting and conduit rules, and we tell the board before we quote it so it can check with the LPC first.

Foundation work has started on the lot next to our building. What should we check on our cameras while it goes on, and after it ends?

During the work, watch the cameras on the side facing the site and any cable that runs along the party wall, the sidewalk or the roof edge next to it. Vibration can loosen mounts and connectors, dust can coat lenses, and crews can cut or unplug cables they do not know are there. Photograph your cameras and cables before the job starts. When the work ends, have the cameras on that side checked for focus, aim and recording; if a cable was damaged, our notes on what failed and where are what you need to take the cost back to the contractor.

A former board member aimed our street camera to take in the bridge and the skyline. After a theft on the block it showed a lovely view and nothing about who came to our door. Is re-aiming part of a repair?

Yes, and it is one of the cheapest repairs we do. We re-aim the camera at the door and the steps, zoom the lens in so a face fills enough of the picture to recognize, and set the recording and motion zone around the entry instead of the street. If residents want a view of the bridge, a second inexpensive camera can be pointed at it, so the security camera does not have to do two jobs badly.

Our building is on the corner by the park entrance, and the park has its own cameras watching the same sidewalk. Can we rely on their video instead of repairing ours?

No. The park's cameras belong to the park, are placed for the park's needs and are not set up to show your door, your lobby or the stretch of sidewalk where a delivery was left. You also cannot pull their footage yourself; it goes through the police or the park's own process. The Brooklyn Eagle reported in 2018 that the park's president wanted more cameras and police for crowd control, which shows what those cameras are for. Your own camera is the only one that answers the question of who came to your building and when.

For the legal side, the board should talk to its own lawyer.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Fulton Ferry

These claims show up in AI-generated answers, forums and national repair guides. Each holds true somewhere, and each one sends Fulton Ferry owners and boards toward the wrong repair or a lost clip.

"Outdoor cameras are waterproof, so a flood won't hurt them"

Quick answers point to a camera's weather rating as proof that water is no concern. The rating describes rain and spray in clean water.

A storm surge on the East River is something else: salt water that rises over the camera's connector, its power box and the junction behind it, then drains and leaves salt behind. In Sandy a basement on lower Old Fulton Street filled from floor to ceiling, and the electronics basement under Jane's Carousel took about five feet. Weather ratings do not cover any of that, and the parts of a camera system that sat in it are the ones that fail later.

Treat anything that has been under seawater as suspect even if it still works. We open and inspect it, keep what is sound and replace what is not, and move connectors and power up where the next surge is less likely to reach them.

"If the recorder turns back on after it dries out, the footage and the hardware are fine"

Many troubleshooting guides suggest letting wet electronics dry for a few days and then powering them up to check.

With a recorder, that test can destroy the thing you most want to keep. Powering up a board with salt or silt on it can short it, and a drive whose heads or platters were contaminated can be scraped as it spins, taking footage with it. A recorder that starts cleanly may still fail weeks later on corroded contacts.

If water reached it, leave it unplugged and call. If you need footage from the drive, we pull it before the recorder is tested, and we tell you plainly whether the recorder is worth cleaning and keeping or should be replaced.

"Any camera work in a landmark district needs a permit first"

AI answers about historic districts tend to warn that nothing can be touched without approval, which leaves some co-op boards living with dead cameras rather than starting paperwork.

The Landmarks Preservation Commission's own permit guidebook says no permit is needed for ordinary maintenance, repair and retrofitting of existing health, safety and utility equipment, and it handles like-for-like replacements with a simple filing and the maker's cut sheet. What it controls is new equipment and changes on the facade: where cameras are fixed, how many there are per stretch of frontage, and how much conduit shows.

Repairing a camera where it already is, replacing it with a similar one, fixing its cable inside the building or replacing the recorder needs no special permission. If a repair calls for moving a camera or adding conduit outside, we tell you before we quote it so the board can check with the commission.

"Busy public areas are already covered by city and park cameras"

Some answers suggest that a building beside a big park or a tourist area does not need its own cameras working, because the public spaces are already watched.

Public cameras watch public spaces for public reasons. When the Brooklyn Bridge Park Conservancy and 84th Precinct dealt with repeated thefts at the athletic piers in 2016, the answer was lockers and police warning signs, and a thief who took a bag from Pier 5 was identified from a selfie, not from a camera covering a residential doorway. Your lobby, your mailroom, your back door and your cellar are not on anyone else's system, and you cannot pull a park camera's footage yourself.

If something happens at your door, your recorder is the only one that will show it, so the entrance, lobby and service door cameras are the ones we check hardest.

"Wi-Fi and cloud cameras avoid the flood problem because nothing is stored in the cellar"

Some answers suggest that the way to stop worrying about a flooded recorder is to switch to Wi-Fi cameras that save to the cloud.

In a flood, the building's internet equipment is usually in the same cellar as the recorder, so the cloud upload stops at the moment the water arrives. Wi-Fi does not travel well through the masonry of the old warehouses here, battery cameras drain faster in cold river wind, and each camera depends on an app account that someone has to own and pay for.

The steadier repair is usually to keep the wired system, move its recorder, switch and modem up out of the water's way, and add a battery backup so they keep running through short outages. A cloud copy can be added for the most important cameras, but it should be a second copy, not the only one.

"Small buildings don't need anyone in charge of the cameras"

General answers tend to assume a camera system takes care of itself once installed, or that a super or a managing agent will notice when something stops.

Many Fulton Ferry buildings are self-managed co-ops with volunteer boards and no super on site. The person who set up the cameras often sold years ago, the app is on their old phone, and the recorder has been dark for months by the time anyone needs it. Brick Underground has noted there is no city law requiring rental buildings to keep cameras working, and in a co-op nobody else will do it either.

A repair should leave someone in charge: alerts for lost video and drive errors sent to at least two board members, the admin login in the co-op's name, a one-page list of cameras and what they cover, and a reminder to check the system once a year and after every big storm.

National averages often put a camera repair at a couple of hundred dollars, which sets expectations that do not survive a cellar that has taken water.

FAQ

Fulton Ferry Security Camera Repair FAQ

After a flood, who should we call first, the electrician or the camera company?
+

The electrician, if water reached outlets, panels or wiring at line voltage, because the building has to be safe to power up before anything else. Tell them the camera recorder, switch and power boxes are on those circuits and ask them to leave them unplugged. Then call us to inspect the camera equipment, recover footage from the drive and replace what the water ruined.

Is salt air off the East River as hard on cameras as an ocean beach?
+

Usually less, but it still matters. The East River is a tidal strait of salt and brackish water, and wind carries spray onto the fronts and decks that face it. Camera bodies tend to cope; the weak points are outdoor connectors, junction box seals and bracket screws. A yearly check of the cameras facing the water catches corrosion before it takes a picture down.

Can camera cables that sat underwater be reused, or do they all have to be pulled out?
+

A continuous run of good camera cable with no joint in the water can often stay, once it is dry and tests clean from end to end. The connectors on its ends, any splices and anything powered that was submerged should be replaced. We test each run after a flood and only replace the ones that fail or have a joint that sat in the water.

Is flood damage to a camera system covered by a warranty?
+

Generally no. Equipment warranties, ours included, cover parts that fail in normal use and exclude acts of nature such as floods, as well as power surges. Flood damage is a matter for the building's or business's insurance. That is why the photos and the written list of damaged parts we prepare after a flood are worth keeping with the claim.

How can we tell whether a camera was killed by the flood or was simply old?
+

The evidence is usually inside. A water-damaged camera or connector shows salt crust, green or white corrosion and rust on contacts that were clean before, while an old camera that failed on its own shows worn infrared lights, a cloudy cover or a dead board with no sign of water. We open what failed, photograph what we find and say which it was in the written report.

A contractor's crew cut our camera cable. Can your repair notes help us get them to pay?
+

They can help. We photograph the damaged section, note where it was, what the cut looks like and when the camera stopped recording, and price the repair in writing. That gives the board or owner a clear record to send to the contractor or their insurer. Whether they pay is between you and them, but a dated, itemized record makes the request hard to ignore.

How do we show that a clip we hand over has not been edited?
+

Export it straight from the recorder in the recorder's own format along with its player, keep the original export untouched, and write down who exported it, when and from which camera. Many recorders also add a checksum or watermark to their exports. If a detective or a lawyer needs the original, we can do the export with you and note the recorder's clock against the real time.

Can a camera in the cellar warn us when water starts coming in?
+

Not reliably. A camera can show water once it is there, but it will not send an alert for it. A water sensor on the cellar floor, connected to an alarm panel or a smart-home hub, is the right tool, and it can warn you while there is still time to unplug equipment. The camera is useful for checking how high the water is from your phone.

After a storm passes, is it worth having the cameras checked even if they all look fine?
+

If water reached the cellar or the ground floor, yes, because corrosion works slowly and a system that looks fine can fail weeks later. If the building stayed dry, a quick look at the app and a check that every camera is still recording is usually enough. Many boards fold a post-storm look into the annual maintenance visit.

Our phones buzz all weekend afternoon from people walking past on Old Fulton Street. Can alerts be limited to the hours we actually care about?
+

Yes. Most recorders let you set an alert schedule and a detection zone separately from recording. We keep the cameras recording around the clock, draw the alert zone tight around your door and steps, and turn alerts on only for the hours the building wants them, such as overnight, so a crowded Saturday stops sending notices while the camera keeps recording everything.

Our board changes every year. How do we keep the camera logins from leaving with the outgoing officers?
