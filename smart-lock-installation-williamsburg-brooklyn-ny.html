The Neighborhood

Why Williamsburg Doors Need a Smart Lock Plan Built Around Their Second Life

Ask what a Williamsburg door was originally for and the answer is usually something other than "an apartment." The neighbourhood's first great building boom came after the Williamsburg Bridge opened in December 1903, when families leaving the crowded Lower East Side filled new walk-ups so quickly that Williamsburg became the most densely populated neighbourhood in the city. Its second came from industry along the river — the Domino sugar refinery ran from 1856 until 2004, cooperages and textile mills lined the waterfront, and the Gretsch musical-instrument factory at 60 Broadway went up in 1916. Its third began in the 1970s, when artists moved into empty factory floors for the space and the low rent, and by the mid-1990s an estimated 3,000 artists lived here. Its fourth was the May 2005 rezoning of roughly 175 blocks of the Greenpoint and Williamsburg waterfront for high-density housing.

Each wave left doors behind, and almost none of them was replaced when the use changed. The Loft Law — Article 7-C of the state Multiple Dwelling Law, passed in 1982 — created a legal class of "interim multiple dwellings" for former commercial and manufacturing buildings where three or more families had made homes, and the city's Loft Board still oversees their legalisation. Units inside the city's industrial business zones are generally left out of it, but the Greenpoint and Williamsburg zones are named exceptions. In practice that means apartments whose entry door is a steel factory door hung for forklifts and fire separation, sometimes with a surface bolt and a padlock hasp where a deadbolt should be. The Gretsch building alone became 130 apartments in a 2003–2005 conversion; the Wythe Hotel occupies a cooperage built in 1901; the Austin, Nichols warehouse on Kent Avenue was turned into apartments in the 2010s.

South of Grand Street, which divides the North Side from the South Side, the stock changes again. Los Sures — the largely Puerto Rican and Dominican South Side between Grand Street and the bridge — is tenement and walk-up country, with Graham Avenue's "Avenue of Puerto Rico" and the shops of Havemeyer Street at its heart. Southside United HDFC, known as Los Sures, was founded there in 1972 and in 1975 became the first community-based organisation in Brooklyn to take on management of city-owned buildings. Further south, below Division Avenue and centred on Lee and Bedford Avenues, is South Williamsburg's Hasidic community, largely Satmar, which settled from the late 1940s and rebuilt abandoned housing in the 1950s and 1960s. A 2023 UJA-Federation study counted about 21,000 Jewish households across Williamsburg, with roughly 36,000 adults and 32,000 children. Those are big households, in multi-family buildings, where the door has to serve a family's religious week as well as its working week.

Then there are the people who come and go. Williamsburg is one of Brooklyn's busiest nightlife districts — the North Side–South Side area logged 3,746 noise complaints between March 1 and June 14, 2021, more than any other neighbourhood area in the borough, according to a RentHop analysis of 311 data — and its venues, bars and shops keep hours that a residential lock was never designed around. The Census Bureau's 2024 survey counts about 218,800 residents in Brooklyn Community District 1, which covers Williamsburg and Greenpoint together, and NYU's Furman Center puts the district's homeownership rate at 16.6 percent in 2024. Most doors here, in other words, have a renter, a roommate, an employee or a delivery rider on one side and an absent owner on the other.

Package theft shows how the pieces fit together. In late 2023, police said, a woman on an e-bike walked into a Bedford Avenue building through its main entrance and took parcels from the mail room, then hit a Kent Avenue building in December. A resident on r/williamsburg summed up a common experience in four words: the packages went "because our front door lock is broken." And early in January 2023, burglars hit several businesses along and near Kent Avenue in one night, cutting through the metal locks on one shop's front door and taking a sledgehammer to another's. None of those is fixed by buying a smart lock off a shelf. Each is fixed by a door that closes and holds, hardware that suits the door it is on, a credential for each person who should have one, and a record of who came in — which is the subject of this page.

Williamsburg's housing was built in bursts, by very different builders, for very different purposes. Seven building types cover nearly every door we are called to here, and each one changes what a lock has to cope with.

Four- and five-storey brick walk-ups from the decades after 1903, across the South Side and the blocks off Broadway, Grand Street and Graham Avenue. Narrow wood or kalamein unit doors, many bored and re-bored by generations of tenants, and a street door with a buzzer strike that the whole building leans on.

Loft Law Factory Buildings

Former mills, warehouses and workshops where people made homes before the zoning allowed it, now legalising under the Loft Board as interim multiple dwellings. Unit doors are often steel or heavy timber, hung for industry, and the corridors were designed for freight rather than for neighbours.

Condo Conversions of Landmark Factories

Industrial buildings converted under developers, such as the 1916 Gretsch factory at 60 Broadway, which became 130 apartments with ceilings of twelve to thirteen feet. Uniform unit doors, a board or sponsor with rules about hardware, and long, echoing corridors.

Three- to six-storey multi-family buildings south of Division Avenue, many built or rebuilt for large families, with stroller traffic through the street door all day and a household rhythm that changes completely on Shabbat and festivals.

Twenty four-storey buildings on 23.3 acres, finished in 1938 as Brooklyn's first large-scale public housing and landmarked in 2003. Housing-authority rules on locks are different from private buildings, so tenants there should check with NYCHA before adding anything.

Mixed-Use Storefront Buildings

Two- to five-storey buildings on Bedford Avenue, Grand Street, Metropolitan Avenue, Broadway and Lee Avenue with a shop or bar at street level and flats above, reached through a side door the business does not control.

Doors that change job after dark

A Williamsburg bar or venue door usually has three lives in a day. In the afternoon it is a delivery and staff door, used by people who need to get in quickly with their hands full. During service it is a public entrance and an exit for a crowd, and its only job is to let people out instantly. After closing it is the most attacked opening on the block. One lock cannot be configured perfectly for all three, so we separate the jobs: an electric strike or electrified lock under a schedule for the daytime and closing roles, free mechanical egress at all times, a latch guard and a reinforced frame for the overnight role, and a door-position contact that tells the owner the door is actually shut when the last person leaves.

Rules that shape a commercial door here

  • Licensed cannabis shops. New York's Office of Cannabis Management requires licensees to secure every entrance against unauthorised access, lock perimeter doors, keep codes and passwords away from anyone not authorised, keep locks and security equipment in full working order with tests at least every 30 days and records kept for five years, and keep a visitor log (9 NYCRR 125.3).
  • Free egress. Whatever locks the outside, the inside must open without a key, a code or special knowledge. A keypad never replaces the push bar or lever on the way out.
  • Outdoor dining and sidewalk structures. Restaurants running roadway cafés under the city's Dining Out rules take them down outside the season; door keypads and strikes go on the building, not on a temporary structure.
  • Landmarked and listed buildings. On designated buildings such as the Williamsburgh Savings Bank, the Landmarks Preservation Commission treats replacing hardware such as locks differently from altering a door; the door and its surround are what draw scrutiny.
  • Mixed-use side doors. Where a business shares a building door with residential tenants, the tenants' rights to a working, self-locking entrance do not change because the shop wants keyless entry; we plan both sets of credentials together.

Your Questions Answered

Smart Lock Installation in Williamsburg: Questions Answered

Loft tenants, South Williamsburg families, venue managers and walk-up residents ask us different things, so the answers below are sorted by door. Anything that applies citywide is answered once on the NYC smart lock hub, and stoop-and-vestibule matters for rowhouses are on the Brooklyn smart lock page, so they are not repeated here.

Lofts and Loft Law buildings

Our loft door still carries the surface bolt and padlock hasp from its factory days — do those come off when a smart lock goes on?

Usually yes, and they should come off before anything electronic goes on. A hasp and padlock on the outside of a dwelling door is a warehouse habit, not an apartment lock: it can be cut in seconds, it shows the whole street when you are away, and a padlock on the inside of an occupied apartment can delay someone leaving in an emergency. Surface slide bolts are the same story — useful on a freight door that is locked from the inside at night, a problem on a door people live behind. We take them off, fill and patch the bolt holes in the steel or timber, and then look at the door with fresh eyes: thickness, whether it has ever been bored for a cylinder, how much it sags, and whether the frame is solid where a strike will go. On many loft doors the answer is a heavy-duty smart deadbolt in a new bore with a reinforced strike plate screwed deep into the frame; on very thick or hollow-metal doors it may be a surface-mounted or wired lock instead. If the hardware is original and you want to keep it for its look, it can stay on the door as decoration, but it should be disabled so it can never be used.

Our building is partway through Loft Board legalisation — should a smart lock wait until the fire-safety work reaches our unit?

It depends on what the owner's legalisation plans say about your door. The Loft Law requires interim multiple dwellings to be brought up to residential fire and safety standards in stages, and the work often includes unit entrance doors, frames and stair doors. If your door or frame is due to be replaced, a lock fitted now may have to come off again, and drilling a door that is about to be swapped is money wasted. Ask the owner or the tenants' association for the scope of the next phase; if the unit door is in it, we can fit a retrofit unit that mounts on your existing deadbolt and moves to the new door later, or wait. If the door is staying, there is no reason to wait. Either way we do not alter fire-rated doors or labelled frames in ways that would undo the legalisation work, and we tell you in writing what we did to the door so you can show it to the owner's architect. This page is not legal advice about the Loft Law; your tenants' association or a housing lawyer is the right source on that.

The Loft Board's fire rule keeps lithium battery chargers away from an IMD unit's front door — does that limit where a smart lock or its charger can go?

Not the lock itself, but it is worth knowing about. The Loft Board's fire-egress rule for interim multiple dwellings says lithium battery charging devices may not be stored in or near the front door of an IMD unit — a rule aimed squarely at e-bike and scooter batteries, which have caused serious fires in New York. A typical smart deadbolt runs on ordinary AA cells inside the lock and is not a charging device. Where the rule matters is the gear around the lock: a rechargeable lock battery pack being topped up on a shelf by the door, a docking station for a video doorbell, or the habit of parking an e-bike on charge in the entry because that is where the outlet is. We place hubs and bridges away from the door where we can, we recommend charging any rechargeable lock pack somewhere else in the unit, and if the only outlet near the entry is the one the e-bike has been using, we will say so. The point of the rule is to keep the way out clear of the thing most likely to catch fire.

Our loft entry is an oversized, very heavy door — will a smart deadbolt cope with it?

The lock copes with the weight; the problem is what the weight does to the hinges over time. A wide, heavy door slowly drops on its hinges, and a door that has dropped even a few millimetres makes the bolt rub against the top or bottom of the strike. A manual deadbolt hides that because people push or lift the door while turning the key. A motorised smart deadbolt cannot, so it strains, drains its batteries, reports a jam, or stops halfway. Before we fit anything to a big loft door we check the hinges and the gap at the top of the frame. If the door has dropped we tighten or replace the hinges, add a fourth hinge or a heavier-duty one where the frame allows, and only then set the strike so the bolt slides in without touching. Smart locks are also rated for door thickness — common models list 1-3/8 to 1-3/4 or 2 inches, a few up to 2-1/4 — and a heavy timber loft door can be thicker than that, in which case the plan changes to a lock designed for the thickness rather than one forced onto it.

Our loft's back door opens onto the old freight stair — can it be locked from the stair side and still let us out in one motion?

Yes, and that is exactly how it must be set up. The Loft Board rule says the door to an indoor stair has to be self-closing, and city fire-safety guidance is clear that an apartment door must open from the inside without a key — double-cylinder deadbolts, which need a key on both sides, are illegal on apartment doors. So a keypad or smart deadbolt can secure the stair door from the outside, but on the inside you need a thumb-turn or lever that opens it in one simple movement, with no code, no phone and no key. We also look at whether that door is a second exit for the unit: if it is, it cannot be chained, barred or blocked with furniture, and the lock we fit cannot have an "inside lockout" mode. Where the stair door has a closer, we check that the closer and the new bolt work together so the door latches every time it swings shut behind you.

South Williamsburg households and Shabbat

Our landlord wants to make the building door fob-only, and several families here keep Shabbat — has that been fought over before?

It has, in Queens. In 2015 Orthodox Jewish residents of LeFrak City, a complex of rental high-rises in Corona, sued the management company in federal court after lobby doors were changed to open with an electronic key, saying that using it on Shabbat would break their religious law and left them waiting outside for someone to let them in. They asked for one door per building with an ordinary lock. We do not know how that case ended and it is not a ruling on your building, but it shows the problem clearly. For an owner in South Williamsburg the practical answer is to keep one entrance with a mechanical key or a mechanical push-button lock alongside the fob or keypad, so nobody's access depends on electronics. We can fit an electronic reader and strike that leaves the mechanical cylinder fully working, so residents who keep Shabbat use a key or a push-button code and everyone else uses the fob. If you are a tenant facing this change, raise it with the owner in writing; whether the fair-housing rules require it in your case is a question for a lawyer, not for us.

Can a mechanical push-button lock we use on Shabbat count as the extra lock a tenant is allowed to add?

Possibly, but read the law before you rely on it. Section 51-c of the Multiple Dwelling Law lets a tenant in most multiple dwellings install a lock of their own in addition to the owner's, as long as it is no more than three inches in circumference, and requires the tenant to give the landlord a duplicate key on request. A mechanical push-button deadlock fits the spirit of that well — it needs no power, keeps no log and works the same on Saturday as on Tuesday — but it has no key to hand over, so most tenants give the landlord the code in writing or choose a push-button model with a key override. The law does not cover buildings run by a public housing authority, hotels or dormitories. We fit push-button locks as well as electronic ones, check that the inside opens without a code, and leave you the paperwork describing what was installed. Whether a particular model meets 51-c in your lease is a question for a housing lawyer; whether it is right for Shabbat is a question for your rabbi.

Shops, venues and short leases

We run a music venue — can a touring crew get in for load-in without a staff member standing at the stage door?

Yes, with a credential that only works during the load-in window and only on the doors the crew needs. A wired keypad or reader on the stage or loading door, controlled from the venue's own system, can issue a code that becomes valid at, say, two in the afternoon, stops working once doors open to the public, and only releases the loading door — not the office, the bar store or the box office. Every use is logged, so if gear goes missing you know when the door opened. The production manager can create or cancel those codes from a phone. What we do not do is set the loading door to stand open by itself for hours: a door left on a schedule to stay unlocked becomes a public entrance, and venues are exactly where uninvited people try the side door. During the show the same door must open from inside instantly for anyone leaving, so it keeps its push bar and its exit signage, and the lock never interferes with either. If the venue has a fire alarm, our Williamsburg fire alarm team can check how the door hardware and the alarm interact.

Our pop-up on Bedford is only leased for three months — is keyless entry worth it, and what happens when we leave?

For a short lease the answer is usually a lock that fits the existing preparation and comes off cleanly. Cutting an electric strike into an aluminium shopfront frame makes sense for a long tenant and very little sense for a summer pop-up. Instead we look at what the door already has — often a mortise cylinder or an existing deadbolt bore — and fit a keypad or smart lock that uses those holes, so staff get individual codes and you can see who opened up in the morning. When the lease ends we take it off and reinstall the landlord's original cylinder or lock, which we keep labelled for you. Agree this with the landlord before we start, because some leases say any change to the doors belongs to the building at the end. If you are likely to renew, tell us; it may be cheaper to do the job properly once than to fit a temporary lock now and a permanent one later.

Tenement walk-ups

Our smart lock keeps telling us the door is open when it is shut, and our walk-up shakes whenever trucks pass on Broadway — are the two connected?

Very possibly, if the door rattles in its frame. Many smart deadbolts use a small magnetic sensor to know whether the door is shut before they auto-lock, and a door that moves in a loose frame can make the sensor read "open" for a moment and the lock refuse to throw, or report the door ajar at three in the morning. The fix is not in the lock: it is a door that sits firmly closed. We adjust the strike so the latch holds the door snug against its stop, add weatherstrip that cushions the door rather than letting it chatter, and set the sensor magnet where the gap is most stable. We also use thread-locking compound on the lock's mounting screws on doors that get a lot of vibration, so they do not walk loose over months. If the lock still reports false openings after that, most apps let the sensor be switched off and auto-lock run on a timer instead, which is a reasonable trade on a street-facing door in an old building.

"A smart lock costs about $300 to have installed"

"Fitting a smart lock is a quick DIY job"

Product reviewers have described fitting a retrofit lock with only a Phillips screwdriver in about half an hour, and on the right door that is true. It assumes a standard bored deadbolt in a door of standard thickness, square in its frame. A steel factory door with no preparation needs drilling, tapping or a surface-mount solution; a heavy timber door may be thicker than the lock is listed for; a sagging door will make a motorised bolt jam however carefully the screws are tightened. Doing the carpentry first is what makes a smart lock last.

"A smart lock will keep parcels from being stolen"

A smart lock on your own apartment door does nothing for parcels left in the lobby or mail room, which is where most building package theft happens. In the 2023 Williamsburg cases police described, the thief walked in through a building's main entrance and went to the mail room. The New York Times has noted that landlords are not legally required to provide delivery lockers or a concierge where a building has not offered them. "Any smart lock will work on any door"

Manufacturers publish the door thicknesses and backsets their locks fit — common residential models list doors from 1-3/8 inches to 1-3/4 or 2 inches, and a few go to 2-1/4 — and many retrofit units work only over a single-cylinder deadbolt. Williamsburg is full of doors outside those ranges: steel doors with no bore at all, heavy timber doors, aluminium shopfront stiles too narrow for a deadbolt, and fire-rated tower doors that must not be modified in the field. The right question is not "which smart lock is best" but "which locks are made for this door."

"Smart locks are simply the more secure choice"

Security comes from the door, the frame and the strike more than the electronics. The Kent Avenue break-ins of January 2023 involved cut locks and a sledgehammer, and no keypad helps a door whose frame splits. A smart lock's real advantages are different: no keys to copy, a credential per person that can be removed, a log of who opened the door, and an alert when it is left open. Those are worth having, on a door that is already strong.

"Put the bridge near the lock and it will connect"

August's own support pages say its Connect bridge should be within about 10 to 15 feet of the lock and in a spot with good Wi-Fi. In a Williamsburg loft the router can be eighty feet away across an open floor, behind a steel door and a brick wall; in a tower unit the door sits deep in a concrete corridor. Factory Doors

Fitting a Smart Lock to a Door That Was Built for Freight

The most distinctive Williamsburg job is the loft door that has never had a proper residential lock. It might be a steel door in a steel frame from a 1920s warehouse, a timber door that once closed off a workshop, or a door some earlier tenant hung themselves from a salvage yard. People who moved into these buildings in the 1980s and 1990s often secured them with whatever was at hand: a padlock on a hasp, a slide bolt on the inside, a cheap rim lock screwed to the surface. Decades later those doors are still in service, now in buildings that are legalising under the Loft Board or have been sold as condos.

We start by finding out what the door actually is. A magnet tells us whether it is steel, and a tap and a look at the edge tell us whether it is solid, hollow-metal with a reinforced core, or a timber door clad in sheet metal. We measure the thickness at the lock height, because doors built for industry are often outside the range residential smart locks are made for. We look at the hinges: how many, what size, whether the screws are holding, and how far the door has dropped. We check the frame where the strike would go — some old steel frames are hollow, some are filled with grout, and some timber frames are split from years of a padlock being yanked. And we look for a fire label on the hinge edge or the frame, because a labelled fire door cannot be drilled and cut freely without affecting its rating.

Then there is the question of what to remove. Surface bolts, hasps and padlock eyes come off; the holes are filled with plugs or welded and ground on steel, or doweled and filled on timber. Old rim locks that might still be relied on in an emergency are removed rather than left as a second lock that someone could engage from inside. If the owner wants the industrial look, the original hardware can be refitted as decoration once it can no longer be locked. Only after that do we choose a lock. On a sound door within the listed thickness, a heavy-duty smart deadbolt in a new bore, with a long-screw reinforced strike, is usually the answer. On a door that is too thick, or a heavy steel door where drilling is impractical, we look at mortise or surface-mounted electrified locks wired back to a small power supply. On a labelled fire door, the change is limited to what the door's listing allows, and anything more is a question for the building's architect.

The finish matters too. A loft door often faces a long corridor where every neighbour sees it, and a keypad that looks like a suburban front door can be out of place. We show owners the darker, flatter keypads and inside-only options, and for buildings with a board or sponsor we send the product sheets before anything is drilled.

South Williamsburg

Doors That Must Work From Friday Evening to Saturday Night

In South Williamsburg a door plan starts with the calendar. Many households and buildings south of Division Avenue keep Shabbat and the festivals, and for those days the question is not which app to use but how a family gets in and out without any electronics at all. What is and is not permitted is a matter for each family and its rabbi; our job is to make sure the hardware can follow that decision rather than force a different one.

In practice that means three things. First, at least one way into the home or the building that is purely mechanical — a keyed lock, or a mechanical push-button lock that needs no battery and records nothing. Whether a family can carry a key on Shabbat is itself a question with religious answers; as a writer in The Jewish Link put it in 2017, a key "is only a viable solution, of course, for those living in an area with an eruv," which is why many households prefer a push-button code. Second, electronic convenience on the doors the household is comfortable with on weekdays: keypads for children coming home from school, codes for a babysitter or cleaner, alerts when the door is left open. Third, settings that can be changed: auto-lock, logs, chimes and notifications that can be turned off or scheduled, bridges on a timer, and keypads that do not light up when someone walks past.

Building-wide, the same thinking applies to the street door. A fob-only lobby is simple for a landlord and unworkable for residents who will not use a fob on Shabbat, which is what the LeFrak City residents in Queens told a federal court in 2015 when their lobby doors went electronic. The workable design is an electronic reader for everyday use with a mechanical cylinder or push-button lock on the same door, so no resident's access depends on power or a phone.

Large households bring their own requirements. A family with many children, grandparents nearby and regular helpers needs more codes than a couple does, and needs them named so the right one can be removed. Stroller traffic means the street door is propped more often than elsewhere, so a door-position contact and a closer that actually finishes the swing are worth more here than an extra feature on the lock. And businesses on Lee Avenue that close for Shabbat need door schedules that move with sunset through the year, set by someone at the shop rather than fixed once by us.

Shared Walk-Ups

Credentials for a Building Where the Faces Keep Changing

On the North Side especially, a walk-up can have more former occupants than current ones. Apartments are shared, roommates rotate, sublets come and go, and every one of those people may have had a key cut or been told the street-door code. In a building with a mechanical lock, the only real fix after a few years is to re-key everything, which landlords rarely do. A keyless street door changes the arithmetic: every resident gets their own code or fob, and when someone moves out their credential is switched off without anyone else noticing.

That only works if somebody keeps the list. At handover we set up two administrators — usually the owner and a managing agent or a trusted long-term tenant — and give them a written register of every credential: who it belongs to, which doors it opens, and when it should end. Courier and delivery access is handled as its own credential rather than a resident's code passed around, so it can be changed when it leaks. For buildings with a super who lives elsewhere, we set the super's credential to work at the street door, the cellar and the roof, and nowhere else.

The apartment doors are the tenants' own business, within the law. City rules require the owner to provide a key lock on each apartment door, and a tenant may add a lock of their own under the Multiple Dwelling Law; neither may make an apartment door that needs a key to open from the inside. Inside those limits a retrofit unit on the existing deadbolt is often the simplest choice for a renter, because the landlord's key still works and nothing changes when the tenant leaves except that the retrofit goes with them.

After Dark

Running Codes for a Bar, Venue or Late Kitchen in Williamsburg

A Williamsburg hospitality business can have more people with door access than a small office. Bartenders, barbacks, kitchen staff, managers, cleaners, promoters, sound engineers and delivery drivers all need to get in at different times, and staff turnover is high. A shared key or a single code that "everyone knows" means that after a year nobody can say who can open the door.

The answer is the same discipline that works for a residential building, applied more strictly. Every person gets their own code or credential. Credentials are tied to roles — opening manager, closing manager, kitchen, cleaning crew, deliveries — and each role works only on the doors and at the hours it needs. When someone leaves, their credential is removed the same day by the manager on duty, not when the owner gets round to it. And the log is actually read: a door opened at four in the morning by a code that should only work at noon is a conversation to have the next day.

The physical side matters just as much. Rear and side doors are the ones forced overnight, so they get latch guards, long-screw strikes and, where the frame is weak, a reinforced jamb. Front doors behind roll-down gates need a plan for which is locked first and which unlocked last. And every door the public uses during service keeps its push bar or lever so that the lock is irrelevant to anyone leaving. If the venue uses an electrified lock on any exit route, it has to release on loss of power or fire alarm as the code requires, and we check that before we leave.

Field Notes

Field Notes on Williamsburg Loft and Factory Doors

How our technicians survey an old industrial door before quoting — a working routine, not an account of a particular job

We lift the door. With the door half open, a hand under the latch edge and a gentle upward push shows straight away whether the hinges have worn: if the door rises, the bolt will rub. Then we close it slowly and watch the gap along the top of the frame, which tells us which way it has dropped.

We hold a magnet to it, measure it at lock height with calipers, and look along the hinge edge and the frame for a fire label. We count the old holes — padlock eyes, slide bolts, rim locks, abandoned cylinders — and decide which come out and how they get filled.

We ask who uses the door, and when. A loft shared by three people, a studio where clients come by appointment and a family that keeps Shabbat are three different setups on the same kind of door. We check the inside: one motion to get out, no key, no code. Only then do we pick a lock, and we write down why, so the owner, the board or the Loft Board's inspector can see what was done.

FAQ

Williamsburg Smart Lock Installation FAQ

Our loft door was custom-made by a metal fabricator and has no lock preparation at all — can a smart lock still go on it?
+

Usually, yes. A door with no bore or cut-out is a blank, which can be easier than a door already riddled with old holes. On a steel door we drill and tap for a smart deadbolt where the thickness suits one, or fit a surface-mounted or mortise electrified lock with a strike when it does not. Before cutting we check the door is solid where the lock will sit and that the frame can take a strike, and we agree the position with you so it suits the door's design.

Our building's only lift is a freight elevator that needs an operator — can you work around that?
+

Yes. Tell us the operator's hours when you book and we will plan the visit inside them, carry tools up the stairs if the elevator is out of service, and finish the noisy drilling early in the window. Most single-door jobs need only a toolbag and a small parts case, so the elevator is a convenience rather than a necessity. For larger jobs with a power supply or long cable, we arrange the elevator time with the building in advance.

Can the auto-lock delay be stretched long enough to wheel a bike, a cello case or a double stroller through the door?
+

On most smart locks, yes. Auto-lock is a setting rather than a fixed feature, and the delay can usually be set from a few seconds up to several minutes, or switched off. For doors people come through with their hands full we often pair a longer delay with a door-position sensor, so the lock waits until the door is actually closed before throwing the bolt. We set it up with you at handover and show you where the setting lives in the app.

Does a licensed cannabis shop need anything particular from its door locks?
+

It needs locks that can be shown to be under control. New York's cannabis regulations require licensees to secure entrances, lock perimeter doors, keep codes and passwords away from anyone not authorised, keep locks in full working order with tests at least every 30 days and records kept for five years, and log non-employee visitors. A keypad or reader with individual credentials and an audit log makes most of that easier. Your compliance plan and the Office of Cannabis Management decide what is required; we fit hardware that supports it.

Our recording studio uses heavy sound-isolation doors with compression seals — can a smart lock go on one?
+

Sometimes, but rarely on the isolation door itself. Those doors close against heavy seals using cam or compression latches, and cutting into them can let sound through. The usual answer is to secure the studio at its outer entrance with a keypad or reader and leave the isolation doors with their own latches. If an isolation door has to lock, we look at hardware that fits its existing preparation and check that it still seals and still opens from inside in one motion.

Our shop's door is set back in a deep recessed entry off the sidewalk — does that change where the keypad goes?
+

It often helps. A recessed entry gives the keypad shelter from rain and keeps it out of the line of sight of people walking past, but it also gives someone a hidden place to work on the door at night. We usually mount the keypad on the door or the frame inside the recess, angled so the person entering shields it, and add lighting or a camera that covers the recess. If a roll-down gate closes the recess, the keypad goes behind the gate.

Our loft gets almost no mobile signal indoors — will the phone app still open the door?
+

Yes, if the lock is set up for it. Most smart locks talk to a phone directly over Bluetooth at the door, so opening it does not need mobile data or the internet; the internet only matters for remote access and alerts when you are away. In buildings with heavy masonry or steel, the lock's bridge or hub is connected to your Wi-Fi rather than the mobile network, and the keypad code works whatever the phone is doing. We test both at handover, standing outside the door with the phone's mobile data on and off.

The street door of our walk-up slams and its strike buzzes loudly at night — can a new setup be quieter for the ground-floor flat?
+

Usually, and it is a fair thing to ask for on a busy block. A slamming door is a closer problem: adjusting the latch speed so the door finishes its swing gently, or replacing a worn closer, stops most of the bang. A loud buzz comes from older AC strikes; a modern DC strike on a proper power supply releases with a click rather than a hum. A rubber door stop and fresh weatherstrip deaden what is left. We can do all three during a keypad installation or as a separate repair.
