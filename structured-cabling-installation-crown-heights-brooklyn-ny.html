The Neighborhood

Why Crown Heights Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Structured cabling is the permanent part of a network: the cable inside the walls, the jacks in the rooms, the patch panel where every cable lands, and the labels that tell the next person which is which. Routers, access points and switches get replaced every few years. The cable, if it is put in properly, stays for decades. That is why the building matters more than the gadget, and in Crown Heights the buildings come in layers. The 6tocelebrate history of Crown Heights North dates the move from farmland to suburb to 1854, when the Lefferts family, the area's main landowner, auctioned parcels of land. I Love NY's listing for the historic district points to the oldest surviving house, the George B. and Susan Elkins House at 1375 Dean Street, from the 1850s. The row houses came over the following decades, in brownstone, brick and limestone. When the subway reached the area in 1920, apartment construction picked up, and by the 1930s many of the old mansions had been replaced by the large Tudor and medieval-inspired apartment buildings that line St. Marks, New York and Brooklyn Avenues. Every one of those generations hides a different kind of wall, floor and ceiling between the modem and the desk.

The neighborhood itself is large. Wikipedia draws it from Washington Avenue on the west to Ralph Avenue on the east, and from Atlantic Avenue down to Empire Boulevard, across five ZIP codes: 11213, 11216, 11225, 11233 and 11238. The northern half sits in Community Board 8, the southern half in Community Board 9. The Brooklyn Eagle's history of the neighborhood traces the free Black communities of Weeksville and Carrville on its eastern side in the nineteenth century, and the walk-up apartment buildings built in the twentieth century for Lubavitch Hasidim who had come from the Soviet Union in the 1940s. Brick Underground describes the three main shopping streets as Nostrand, Kingston and Franklin Avenues, each with its own character. So a cabling job in Crown Heights may be a family's parlor-floor home office, a co-op apartment off Eastern Parkway, a school or store on Kingston Avenue, a salon on Utica, or a restaurant on Franklin. We cover all of it at the same Brooklyn rate. What changes the work is how the building was put together.

The landmark designations shape more of our planning here than in most of Brooklyn. Gothamist reported in 2007 that the Landmarks Preservation Commission protected 472 buildings in the first Crown Heights North Historic District, across blocks between Bedford and Kingston Avenues along Pacific and Dean Streets, Prospect Place and St. Marks Avenue, built from the 1860s to the 1930s. The Commission's own press material shows a second phase designated on June 28, 2011, with 630 buildings and sites. CityLand reported that the third phase, designated March 24, 2015, sits mostly between Brooklyn and Albany Avenues and from Pacific Street down to Lincoln Place, and that it brought the total number of landmarked buildings and sites in the Crown Heights area to 1,752. Brownstoner's coverage of that third phase pointed out the one- and two-block stretches of Hampton, Revere and Virginia Places inside it. For a network installer, a landmarked house changes one thing above all: the outside. Provider lines, conduit and junction boxes on a facade are the kind of visible change that, according to industry guidance on landmarked brownstones, can require a Certificate of No Effect or a Permit to Alter. So in these houses the route has to stay indoors.

Inside, the old houses are hard on signals and on cable alike. A Brooklyn brownstone wiring guide from another installer describes working around original plaster walls, balloon-framed floors and decades of layered utility runs, and notes that masonry party walls block mesh Wi-Fi signals well enough that a typical four-story brownstone usually needs one access point per floor. In practice, that is the most common reason a Crown Heights homeowner calls us: the internet arrives in the cellar or at the front of the parlor floor, a single router or a set of wireless mesh units tries to reach the top floor through plaster, brick and several floors of joists, and the back bedroom on the fourth floor barely connects. The fix that lasts is a cable to each floor, ending at an access point on the ceiling or a jack at the desk, with everything coming back to one labeled panel. The question is never whether that is possible. It is which closet, chase or corner lets the cable climb without opening finished walls in a house that may have had its plaster for more than a century.

The apartment buildings bring their own reasons. Building entry systems, lobby cameras and door access all ride on low-voltage cable, and Crown Heights building owners have had pressing cause to look at them. News 12 reported on a man who appeared to use a small child in a stroller to get a resident to open the door at 240 Crown Street, a 135-unit building, so that he could take packages out of the lobby. Crownheights.info reported an arrest for package theft on October 28, 2026, after Shomrim volunteers retrieved video footage and followed the suspect until police arrived, and COLlive listed more than two dozen arrests over summer 2026, including package thefts around Lincoln Place, Carroll Street and President Street at Albany Avenue. A guide summarizing Multiple Dwelling Law 50-a notes that a Class A multiple dwelling with eight or more apartments, erected or converted after January 1, 1968, needs an intercom at its self-locking main entrance, and that a dead intercom or buzzer is a Class B violation. Whatever camera or entry system a building chooses, it needs a clean cable path from the lobby to the room where the recorder and switch live. That path is our part of the job.

Then there is what is being built now. 6sqft reported that the Atlantic Avenue Mixed-Use Plan, a rezoning of 21 blocks in Bedford-Stuyvesant and Crown Heights, allows roughly 4,600 new homes and buildings up to 18 stories. New York YIMBY listed nine new residential buildings coming to Crown Heights in June 2026, including Prosper Brooklyn, a 17-story building at 1042 Atlantic Avenue, a ten-story building at 1070 Pacific Street and an eight-story building at 311 Eastern Parkway. The Arch at the Bedford Union Armory, 1101 President Street, rises 16 stories with 355 rentals, and near the Botanic Garden there is Loden at 54 Crown Street, with 569 units, and a ten-story rental at 962-972 Franklin Avenue. A new building gets one easy chance at good cabling: before the drywall goes up. After that, every change costs more.

Same-Day Repair in Crown Heights

Need Repair in Crown Heights? Dead Jacks, a Closet Full of Unmarked Cable, or a Connection That Drops When It Matters

Most urgent cabling calls from Crown Heights fall into three patterns. The first is a jack that used to work and has stopped. In a row house, that is often a cable that was stapled along a joist in the cellar and later crushed, nicked by a plumber or chewed through; in an apartment building, it is often a cable that was pushed into an old chase alongside heating pipes and has been cooked for several winters. The second is the closet that nobody can read: a shelf in the cellar or a corner of a storeroom with a modem, a router, two or three small switches, a tangle of cords and a dozen cables with no labels, left by a previous owner, tenant or contractor. The third is the intermittent fault, the one that only shows up when the card machine is busy on a Saturday on Franklin Avenue or when two people are on video calls at once in a parlor-floor office. Those are the hardest to find, because nothing is obviously broken.

We handle all three the same way. First we find out what is there. A tone generator goes on the cable at one end and a probe finds it at the other, so each mystery cable gets traced to its room and labeled. Then a certifier tests each run from end to end, which tells us whether the cable itself is sound, whether a jack or punch-down is bad, or whether a run is simply too damaged to save. Then we write down what we found and what we recommend: re-terminate this one, replace that one, rebuild the closet so that the cables land on a labeled panel instead of hanging loose. You decide what gets done. If the problem turns out not to be the cable at all, but the provider's equipment or a failing router, we tell you that plainly, so you are not paying to replace good wire. To get a technician out, call (347) 934-8335; same-day estimates are available on most weekdays, and we book within two hours of your call.

One more thing about repairs in older buildings. Sometimes the right answer is not to fix the old run but to retire it. A cable that was fished through a wall cavity next to a radiator riser, or stapled across the face of a joist where every future contractor will hit it, will fail again. When a run like that is replaced, we route the new cable away from the heat or the hazard and leave the old one disconnected and labeled as abandoned, so the next person does not waste an afternoon tracing it. That sounds like a small courtesy. In a house with a hundred and twenty years of utility work in its walls, it is the difference between a closet that makes sense and one that only gets worse.

Building Specialists

Structured Cabling for Crown Heights' Architecture

Six kinds of building make up nearly every cabling call we get from Crown Heights. They differ in three ways that matter for a network: where the internet line enters, what the cable has to pass through on its way up, and who besides you has to agree before anything is drilled or opened.

The landmarked row house

Brownstone, brick and limestone houses in the three Crown Heights North district phases, many with original plaster and interior woodwork, where nothing new should appear on the front of the house.

The English-stoop row house

Rows in Crown Heights South whose stoops, as 6tocelebrate notes, rise only a few steps above the sidewalk, often with finished basements that change where a cable can run.

The row house shared by two or three households

Houses where an owner lives on one floor and rents the others, and where each household may want its own provider, its own router and its own jacks.

The 1930s apartment building

The Tudor and medieval-style buildings along St. Marks, New York and Brooklyn Avenues and the Parkway, many run by co-op boards or managing agents with house rules for contractors.

The walk-up and mid-century building

Brick walk-ups and mid-century multi-unit buildings, where concrete, block and plaster walls mix and where cable often has to follow hallways and baseboards.

The new tower

Buildings of eight to eighteen stories on the Atlantic Avenue edge, around the Botanic Garden and at the Armory, where the best cabling decisions are made while the walls are still open.

The landmarked row house: everything happens inside, and the cellar is where it starts

In a landmarked house the outside of the building is the one place we do not want to work. The LPC Permit Guidebook chapter on health, safety and utility equipment asks that equipment be kept off the primary facade unless there is no other option, and that any conduit needed be concealed. An installer's guide for landmarked brownstones adds that visible changes to the exterior, including conduit and mounts, may require a Certificate of No Effect or a Permit to Alter, and a homeowner's newsletter on historic district work notes that LPC approval can be needed even when the Department of Buildings does not require a permit at all, with applications going through the Commission's Portico system. So the route starts below grade. In most Crown Heights row houses the internet provider's line already comes in through the cellar or the rear, and that is where we put the panel: on a cellar wall near the line, or in a closet on the parlor or garden floor if the cellar is damp or unfinished.

From there the cable has to climb. A brownstone wiring guide from another installer describes plaster walls, balloon-framed floors and decades of layered utility runs as the normal conditions in these houses, and that matches what we look for. Balloon framing, where it exists, can leave continuous cavities between floors, which makes fishing easier but also means a cavity may already hold old wiring or pipes. Stacked closets are the other classic route: a closet on the parlor floor directly above a closet on the garden floor and below another on the third floor gives a hidden vertical path where a small hole in each closet floor or ceiling disappears behind the door. Where there are no stacked closets, we look at the back of built-in cabinets, the space beside a dumbwaiter or chimney breast, or a corner where a narrow box can be painted to match the trim. What we avoid is cutting a long channel through finished plaster on a stair wall. Old plaster can crack well beyond the line you cut, and in a house with original moldings the patch is often more noticeable than the cable would have been.

Two details are specific to landmarked blocks. First, old provider cable. Many row-house fronts carry dead coax or phone lines left by earlier services. Removing dead cable from a facade is the kind of tidying a homeowner usually wants, but where the removal leaves fixings or holes in the masonry, we suggest checking with the Landmarks Commission about the patch. Second, the rear. The back of a row house is generally less sensitive than the front, but a cable that crosses a rear wall or a yard to a garden studio is still exterior work on a designated building. We prefer to bring a new provider line in below grade and keep every cable we add inside the envelope of the house.

The English-stoop row house in Crown Heights South: finished basements and lower ceilings

South of Eastern Parkway the row houses tend to sit lower on the street. 6tocelebrate's description of Crown Heights South notes that many houses have English-style stoops rising only a few steps above street level, which usually means the lowest floor is closer to grade and more often finished as living space than a deep brownstone cellar would be. That matters for cabling because the cellar is our favorite place to run horizontal cable: open joists, no finished ceiling, easy access to every wall above. When the lowest floor has a finished ceiling, the horizontal runs have to find another way, usually through a utility room, behind a soffit that already hides pipes, or along the top of a wall behind a crown molding.

The good news is that these houses are often simpler above the ground floor. With fewer stories than a four-story brownstone, a single well-placed panel and one access point per floor usually covers the whole house. The best spot for the panel is often a hall closet on the main floor near where the provider's line enters, because it keeps every run short and gives easy access later without anyone having to crawl behind a finished basement wall. If you are planning to finish a basement in one of these houses, call before the ceiling goes up. A few cables pulled to the upper floors and to the TV wall while the joists are open cost far less than the same cables fished later.

The row house shared by two or three households: one cable plant, separate networks

Many Crown Heights row houses hold more than one household. An owner may live on the garden and parlor floors and rent the top two, or a house may be split into three apartments with a tenant on each level. Each household usually has its own internet service, its own router, and its own idea of where the desk and the TV go. The worst outcome is the one we see most often: three providers have each drilled their own way into the house, three sets of cable run up the stairwell or the facade, and nobody knows whose is whose.

The cleaner approach is one cable plant that everyone shares physically but nobody shares logically. The provider lines all come in at one point in the cellar or utility room. A labeled panel there has a section for each apartment, and each apartment's jacks come back to its own section. Each household plugs its own router into its own provider line, and its own jacks into its own router, so the networks never touch. When a tenant moves out, the next one connects to the same labeled jacks without another hole in the house. For the owner, that also means one tidy closet instead of three tangles, and a place to put a building network for a shared door camera or a cellar sensor that belongs to the house rather than to any tenant. If you rent one of these apartments, ask the owner before any wiring work; it is their building, and a short note describing the route is usually all they need.

The 1930s apartment building: shared walls, house rules and a board that wants paper

The large prewar apartment buildings on St. Marks, New York and Brooklyn Avenues and along the Parkway are where the paperwork matters most. Many of them are co-ops or are run by a managing agent. A contractor guide to co-op and condo cabling notes that some alteration agreements require licensed contractors regardless of whether a permit is needed, and that buildings commonly ask for a certificate of insurance naming the board plus a scope-of-work letter. We send both before the visit. The scope letter says which rooms get jacks, how the cable gets there, whether any wall or ceiling is opened, how big any openings are, and that nothing is connected to the building's electrical system.

Inside the apartment, walls in buildings of this age are often plaster on masonry, hollow tile or block, and many of the interior partitions are not hollow in the way modern stud walls are. That rules out fishing a cable through many walls. The usual routes are along the top of a wall behind picture rail or crown molding, through closets that back onto each other, along baseboards in a slim raceway painted to match, or around a door frame. We never use the building's shared risers, chases or hallway ceilings for a single apartment's network without written permission from management, because those spaces belong to the building and often carry other systems. And if a provider tells you they need to bring new cable up through the building to reach your apartment, that is a conversation between the provider and the building, which we explain in the questions below.

The walk-up and mid-century building: block, plaster and long hallways

Crown Heights also has many brick walk-ups and mid-century multi-unit buildings, which a local real estate guide describes as the affordable options for families and first-time buyers. For cabling they are a mix. Walk-ups often have plaster partitions and brick or block around the outside and the stair, while mid-century buildings can have concrete floors and block walls throughout. Concrete and block cannot be fished. Cable either runs on the surface in a raceway, goes through a drilled or cored hole into the next space, or follows a hallway ceiling to a room where it can drop.

For a building owner, the job in these buildings is often a common-area backbone: a path from the cellar where the provider lines enter to a small rack or wall-mounted cabinet, then out to the lobby, the stair halls, the laundry and the back door for whatever cameras, entry panel or access readers the building is installing. That backbone is where good cabling pays off for years, because it is the same path every future system will use. We plan it so that it can carry more cable later without tearing anything open again, and so that every penetration through a floor or a fire-rated wall is sealed afterward.

The new tower: decide while the walls are open

The new buildings at the north and west edges of Crown Heights are a different job altogether. Prosper Brooklyn at 1042 Atlantic Avenue, 1070 Pacific Street, 311 Eastern Parkway, The Arch at 1101 President Street and Loden at 54 Crown Street are all buildings where, at some point, the steel or concrete frame stood open with no drywall. That is the only moment when cabling is truly cheap. Sleeves through floors, paths across ceilings, boxes in the walls and a telecom room with space to grow can all be planned on drawings and installed in days. A year later, adding the same thing means cutting finished drywall, coordinating with tenants and patching paint.

For developers and general contractors working under the Atlantic Avenue plan, our role is the low-voltage rough-in and trim-out: pulling cable to each apartment's media panel, to common areas, to the entry and to the roof; terminating and labeling it; certifying it; and leaving a map. We coordinate with the electrical contractor on boxes and with the general contractor on fire-stopping wherever cable passes through a rated floor or wall. For owners buying into one of these buildings later, see the question below about media panels with more jacks than anyone can explain.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Mapping a Crown Heights House Before a Single Cable Comes Off the Box

These notes describe how our technicians plan cabling in houses and buildings like those in Crown Heights. They are our working practice, not the story of any one address.

Start where the internet starts. The first stop is not the room with the bad Wi-Fi; it is the place where the provider's line enters. In a row house that is usually the cellar or the front of the garden floor. We look at what is already there: the provider's box, any old phone and coax lines, any earlier network cable, and the electrical panel, because the network closet needs a nearby outlet and should not share a wall cavity with heavy wiring.

Find the vertical. Every multi-story job lives or dies on one vertical path. The technician opens every closet on every floor and looks for ones that stack. He looks at where heating pipes rise, because the floor around a pipe often has a gap, and at any chase, dumbwaiter or abandoned flue that the owner knows about. A path that works is marked on a sketch of each floor before anything is drilled.

Mark both ends and count. Each drop gets a number on the sketch, at the room end and at the panel end. That number becomes the label. Counting first also catches the jacks nobody thought of until the cable was already pulled: the TV wall, the back bedroom, the access point at the top of the stair.

Pull once. When there is only one good path up a house, every cable that will ever need it should go in at the same time, including a spare. Pulling cables one at a time through the same small opening in old lath risks damaging the ones already in and widening the hole.

Leave it readable. Before we pack up, the technician walks the owner through the map: which jack is which, where the panel is, where the spare ends, and what was left disconnected and why. A house that has been wired this way can be worked on by anyone afterward, including someone other than us.

Where the Line Comes In

From the Curb to the Top Floor: How Internet Service Enters a Crown Heights Building, and What Happens After

People often call a cabling company when the real question is about the internet provider, and people often call the provider when the real question is about the cabling. It helps to know where one stops and the other starts. The provider brings its line to a point in or on your building and installs its own equipment there, usually a modem or, for fiber service, an optical network terminal. Everything from that box to the rooms where you use the network is inside wiring. In most homes the provider will plug a router into its box and call the job done, and everything past that is either Wi-Fi or cable someone else runs.

In apartment buildings, the provider usually also needs a path through the building to reach each apartment, and that path belongs to the landlord or the board. A commercial guide to Brooklyn and Bronx internet options notes that for Verizon Fios to come into a building, the landlord or building management has to grant access for the infrastructure inside the building, including conduit, risers and in-apartment terminal boxes. That is why one apartment building on a block can have a fiber option and the building next door does not. We are not a provider and cannot bring service to a building. What we can do, for a building owner, is plan and install the internal pathways that make a provider's job easier and that keep every provider's cable organized instead of draped down a stairwell.

We would like to tell you which providers serve which Crown Heights blocks, but the public data we found was not reliable enough to repeat. One provider-comparison site listed several fiber services for Crown Heights while also claiming zero fiber coverage for Brooklyn, which cannot both be true. The honest answer is to check your exact address with each provider. Once you know what is coming in and where, we plan from that point onward.

Where the panel should live in each kind of building

In a row house, the best place for the network panel is usually as close as possible to where the provider enters, with an outlet nearby and somewhere dry. That is often a cellar wall or a utility closet on the garden floor. If the cellar floods or is very damp, we move the panel up to a closet on the floor above and bring the provider line to it, rather than leave electronics where water can reach them.

In an apartment, the panel can be as simple as a small wall-mounted enclosure in a closet, with the provider's box and router inside it and each room's cable landing on a short row of jacks. It does not need to be a full rack. What it needs is to be in one place, labeled, and reachable without moving a bookcase.

In a building's common areas, the panel is usually a wall-mounted cabinet or small rack in the cellar or a secure closet, holding the switch that powers the cameras and readers, the recorder or controller, and a battery backup so the building's door and cameras ride through a short power interruption. Management keeps the key.

In a new building, the telecom room or rooms are on the drawings. Our part is making sure they have space, power, ventilation and labeled pathways to every apartment and every common-area device, and that the spare capacity the developer paid for is actually usable later.

Wi-Fi is part of the cabling plan, not a substitute for it

Most Crown Heights customers do not want a jack in every room; they want Wi-Fi that works everywhere. The way to get that in a house with masonry party walls and several floors of plaster is to run cable to a few access points, each one placed where it can serve a floor or a zone, and connect them all back to the panel. The access points then talk to the router over cable instead of over the air, and each one gives full speed to the devices near it. Wireless mesh units can be a reasonable stopgap, but each hop through a plaster floor costs speed, and a mesh unit on the fourth floor that can barely hear the one on the third floor will not do better just because it is newer. Run the cable once, and any future access point can be swapped in on the same wire.

Rules that shape commercial and building cabling in Crown Heights

  • No electrical permit for data cabling under 50 volts. Local Law 128 of 2024 added Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, effective with the 2025 New York City Electrical Code in December 2025, saying an electrical permit is not required for wiring under 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data. A Bronx contractor guide notes the exemption applies to work by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person. Related work, such as core drilling through fire-rated assemblies or new electrical outlets, can still need permits from other trades.
  • Fire alarm wiring is not part of that exemption. An industry guide on New York data cabling notes that fire alarm wiring falls outside the low-voltage exemption and needs specialized licensing and additional permits. We keep network cable out of fire alarm pathways and never share a cable with a fire alarm circuit.
  • Cameras, alarms and access control need a state license. A 2026 summary of New York low-voltage licensing notes that pure structured cabling is generally exempt from state licensing, but burglar alarm, fire alarm, camera and access control work requires an Article 6-D license. AESS holds NYS license #12000287431, so the cable path and the system it serves can be handled by one licensed company.
  • Penetrations get sealed. Any hole we make through a floor, a fire-rated wall or a shaft is sealed with an approved fire-stopping product once the cable is in, and new buildings follow the general contractor's fire-stopping plan.
  • Landmarked storefronts are still landmarked. Many commercial buildings on Bergen, Dean and the avenues inside the historic districts are designated. Cable, conduit and boxes on the outside of those buildings raise the same Landmarks Commission question as they would on a row house.
  • Entry systems carry legal duties. For apartment buildings covered by Multiple Dwelling Law 50-a, a working intercom at the main entrance is required. The cable behind that intercom is worth doing right, because a failure becomes a violation, not just an inconvenience.
Your Questions Answered

Structured Cabling in Crown Heights: Questions Answered

These are the situations Crown Heights owners, tenants, boards and businesses describe when they call. Each one is answered here in full and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Row houses inside and outside the historic districts

Our house is on a Crown Heights North historic district block, and the internet company wants to run its new line down the front of the building. Does the Landmarks Commission get a say?

Very possibly, yes. Industry guidance on landmarked brownstones says visible changes to the exterior of a designated building, including conduit, can require a Certificate of No Effect or a Permit to Alter, and LPC approval can be needed even when no Buildings Department permit is. The LPC Permit Guidebook asks that utility equipment be kept off the primary facade unless there is no other option and that conduit be concealed. In practice, the simplest answer is usually to ask the provider to bring the line in below grade, at the cellar or the rear, where most row houses already have an entry. We then take it from that point to wherever you need it inside, so nothing new appears on the front of the house. If the provider insists on a facade route, ask them in writing who is responsible for the Landmarks question before they start.

The internet comes in at the cellar of our four-story house. Should we move where it enters, or leave it there and wire outward?

Usually leave it and wire outward, as long as the cellar is dry. The point where the provider enters is the natural home for the network panel, because the provider's equipment and your patch panel then sit together and every run starts in one place. A cellar also makes horizontal cable easy, with open joists under every room on the floor above. The exceptions are a cellar that takes water, which is no place for electronics, and a house where the only workable vertical path starts on an upper floor, in which case one cable from the provider's box up to a closet on the parlor floor is cheaper than fighting the layout. We decide that on the walk-through, after finding the path up through the house.

Three households live in our row house, and each has its own provider. Can one cabling job give each apartment its own private network?

Yes, and it is one of the better investments an owner can make in a converted house. All the provider lines come in at one spot, usually the cellar or utility room. The patch panel there is divided by apartment, with each apartment's jacks landing in its own labeled section. Each household plugs its router into its own provider's box and its jacks into that router, so the three networks never connect. When tenants change, the next one plugs into the same jacks, and no new provider has to drill a fresh hole through the house. Owners often add a fourth section for a building network, for a door camera or a cellar water sensor that belongs to the house. Tenants should get the owner's agreement first; the route goes through the owner's walls.

We are gutting the garden and parlor floors of our row house but leaving the upper floors as they are. What cable should go into the open walls now?

More than you think you need on the open floors, and a way up for the floors you are not touching. On the garden and parlor floors, put a drop wherever a desk, a TV or a ceiling access point could reasonably go, and bring them all back to one panel location. Then, while the ceilings are open, install a short length of conduit or an empty sleeve from the parlor floor up into a closet on the floor above, with a pull string inside. That empty path is how the upper floors get cable later without opening their plaster. Ask your contractor to call us before insulation and drywall, so the cables are pulled, labeled and tested while they can still be reached. Pulling a few extra cables at that stage costs a fraction of fishing them through finished walls afterward.

Prewar apartment buildings and co-ops

Our co-op board on St. Marks Avenue wants a certificate of insurance and a scope-of-work letter before we put ethernet into our apartment. What does the letter need to say?

It should tell the board exactly what will happen inside your apartment, in plain terms. Ours lists the rooms that get jacks and the number in each, the route the cable takes between them, whether any wall or ceiling is opened and roughly how large each opening is, that no shared riser, chase or hallway is used without management's written permission, that nothing connects to the building's electrical system, that any hole through a floor or rated wall is sealed, and the dates and hours of the work. A contractor guide to co-op cabling notes that buildings often ask for exactly this pair, a certificate of insurance naming the board and a scope letter, and that some alteration agreements require licensed contractors even where no permit applies. Send us the names the managing agent wants on the certificate and we send both before the visit.

We rent in a 1930s building on Eastern Parkway and the lease does not allow holes in the walls. Is there any way to get a wired connection to the desk in the back bedroom?

Usually yes, with a surface route instead of an in-wall one. A slim raceway along the top of the baseboard or tucked under a picture rail, painted to match the wall, can carry one or two network cables from the router to the bedroom with only a few small fixings, which are easy to fill when you move out. Around door frames the raceway follows the casing, and where two rooms share a closet, the cable can sometimes pass through the closet wall with landlord permission. Ask your landlord first and describe the route. If even raceway is off the table, the fallback is a single access point in the hallway on a short cable from the router, which often gets better coverage than a router alone in the front room.

Our building wants cable for lobby cameras, a package room and laundry card readers. Can that network be kept completely apart from what the tenants use?

It should be. A building network for common areas belongs on its own cable, its own switch and its own internet connection, in a locked cabinet that management controls. It should never pass through any tenant's apartment or share a provider account with one. We pull that backbone from the cellar or service room to the lobby, the stair halls, the package room, the laundry and the doors, and label every run as building property. After the package-theft reports around 240 Crown Street and the Albany Avenue blocks, more buildings are adding cameras and entry equipment, and the cable plan is the part that decides how easily the next system can be added. The cameras or intercom themselves are a separate choice; AESS installs those under the same license if the building wants them.

A fiber provider says it needs permission from our landlord before it can serve our apartment. What exactly is it asking the landlord to allow?

It is asking for a path through the building. A commercial guide to Brooklyn internet options explains that for Verizon Fios to come into a building, the landlord or management has to grant access for conduit, risers and the terminal boxes inside each apartment. That means the provider wants to run its own cable from the street into the cellar, up through the building's shared spaces and into your apartment, and to fix equipment along the way. A landlord may say yes, no, or yes with conditions about where the cable runs and who restores walls. You can ask the landlord directly; tenants sometimes get further when several apartments ask together. We do not install provider service, but for an owner who agrees, we can plan clean internal pathways so that every provider uses the same organized route.

New buildings near Atlantic Avenue, the Armory and the Botanic Garden

Twice before drywall, at least. We just moved into a new rental near the Botanic Garden. There is a panel in the closet with several jacks, and nobody can tell us which room each one goes to. How do we find out?

The jacks can be traced. A tone generator on each panel cable makes a signal that a handheld probe picks up at the wall jack it reaches, so in an hour or so every jack can be matched to its room and labeled at both ends. A certifier then shows whether each cable works end to end. New buildings sometimes leave out the labels or the map during the rush to open, and some media panels hold provider cable and network cable side by side with no explanation. Ask building management first, since the developer or the building's own contractor may have a cable map. If not, and the lease allows work inside the panel, a short visit gives you a labeled map you can use from then on. Storefronts, restaurants and shops on the avenues

Our restaurant on Franklin Avenue runs the registers, kitchen printer, card readers and guest Wi-Fi off one router behind the bar. What should the cabling look like instead?

Everything that does not move gets its own cable, and the network gets a proper home. That means wired drops to each register, the kitchen printer, the back-office computer and any fixed card terminal; one or two ceiling access points on cable to cover the dining room and the garden; and a small wall cabinet or rack in a back room for the switch and router, away from the bar's ice machine and the kitchen's heat. The guest network then sits on its own segment so that a full dining room on a Saturday night cannot slow down the card readers. The kitchen run is the one to plan carefully, keeping cable out of grease and steam and away from the hood. We work before the restaurant opens for prep so service is never interrupted.

Our barbershop on Utica Avenue wants a TV for customers, music, and a card reader at every chair. Can one cabling job cover all of it?

Yes. In a long, narrow storefront like most on Utica and Nostrand, we usually run cable along the ceiling or behind a soffit from a small cabinet at the back to the front desk, to the TV wall, and to a ceiling access point in the middle of the shop that covers every chair's wireless card reader. Wiring each chair separately is possible, but readers usually move with the barber, so strong Wi-Fi on cable is the better fit. If the TV goes on the wall at the same visit, the data drop ends behind it, and our Brooklyn TV list prices the mount from $179.99, with a paintable cord cover at $59 or an in-wall power and cable kit at $169 to hide the rest. Talk to your landlord first if there are apartments upstairs.

Our auto shop on Atlantic Avenue needs a connection in the service bays, not just the office. Will network cable survive a working garage?

It will if it is protected. In the office it can run like any other drop, but out in the bays the cable needs to be up high, inside conduit or a sturdy raceway, and away from lifts, the swing of an overhead door and anywhere a vehicle or a creeper could catch it. A wall-mounted jack at each bay, placed above bench height, is usually better than a long patch cord across the floor. Oil, solvents and temperature swings near an open door are harder on equipment than on cable, so we keep the switch in the office and run cable out to the bays rather than putting network gear in the shop. Diagnostic computers that move between bays can use a ceiling access point instead of a jack at each one.

What AI Gets Wrong About Structured Cabling in Crown Heights

We could not capture Google's AI Overview for this topic in our build environment, so nothing below quotes it. These are claims that come up again and again in generic online answers and in national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, paraphrased, each followed by what Crown Heights buildings actually show.

"A good mesh system will cover any home"

Generic answers often treat wireless mesh as the cure for every dead zone, with the cable left out of the conversation entirely. Buy enough units, the advice goes, and the signal will reach every room.

A Crown Heights row house is not an open-plan suburban house. Masonry party walls, thick plaster and several floors of joists sit between the router and the top bedroom, and a brownstone wiring guide from another installer says these walls block mesh signals well enough that one access point per floor is the usual answer. Each wireless hop through that construction costs speed.

The lasting fix is cable to each floor. Once it is in, any brand of access point can sit on the end of it, and the mesh units can retire to a smaller apartment.

"Running network cable needs an electrical permit," or "there are no rules at all"

Online answers swing between two extremes. Some say every cabling job in New York City needs a Buildings Department permit; others say low-voltage work is completely unregulated.

Neither is right. Local Law 128 of 2024 added Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, which says no electrical permit is required for wiring under 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data, and a contractor guide notes the exemption applies to work by licensed or qualified people. Fire alarm wiring falls outside it, and camera, alarm and access control work needs a state Article 6-D license.

In Crown Heights, add the rules the generic answers leave out: Landmarks review for anything on a designated facade, and co-op alteration agreements that can demand licensed contractors whatever the permit status.

"Just run the cable along the outside of the building"

DIY guides sometimes suggest the shortest route between two floors is up the outside wall, clipped to the brick, with a hole drilled at each end.

On more than seventeen hundred landmarked buildings and sites in the Crown Heights North districts, that is exactly the kind of visible change the Landmarks Preservation Commission looks at. Its permit guidebook asks that utility equipment stay off the primary facade where possible and that conduit be concealed. Even off the landmarked blocks, cable on the outside is exposed to weather and is the first thing to fail.

We plan every route inside the building, from the cellar or rear, and suggest owners ask providers to do the same.

"The highest category of cable is always the right one"

Many answers say to buy the newest, highest-rated cable for every drop, because it is future-proof and the price difference is small.

The cable grade matters less in an old house than the route. A top-rated cable crushed under a staple, kinked around a radiator riser or squeezed into a hole in old lath will not perform to its rating, and a heavier, stiffer cable can be harder to fish through the narrow paths a row house offers.

We choose the grade for each job's runs and uses, then protect it on the route so it actually tests to that grade, and we show you the certification result for every drop.

"A national average tells you what cabling costs"

Cost guides publish average figures for network wiring, sometimes for Brooklyn as a whole, built from many jobs in many buildings.

An average cannot know that your house is landmarked, that your board wants a scope letter, that your only vertical path is a closet on the third floor, or that your building is a new tower with open ceilings where the work is fast. Those are the things that move a Crown Heights figure up or down, often by more than the number of jacks.

We walk the building and put a written figure at our Brooklyn rate in front of you before any cable is pulled, so you are not relying on someone else's average.

"New buildings come fully wired"

It is common to read that apartments in new construction are already wired for everything, so residents never need a cabling contractor.

Many new buildings around Atlantic Avenue and the Botanic Garden do have a media panel and some jacks. But what goes in is decided by the developer, not by you: perhaps one or two jacks, sometimes without labels or a map, sometimes holding provider cable rather than a full network.

The panel is a good starting point. Tracing and labeling what is there, and adding an access point or a desk drop, usually turns it into the network you expected.

"Anyone handy can run ethernet"

Forum answers often say that running ethernet is easy, a weekend job with a drill and a fish tape, and that a professional is only needed for offices.

In a modern house with drywall, a careful owner can do a run or two. In a Crown Heights row house with century-old plaster, abandoned utilities and a single path up through the floors, an inexperienced pull can crack plaster, damage existing lines or leave cable that never tests well. Co-op boards may also require a licensed contractor whatever the permit status.

What a licensed installer adds is the planning, the labeling, the certification report and the insurance certificate, all of which matter more in old buildings than the cable itself.

FAQ

Crown Heights Structured Cabling FAQ

Can the new network cabling also carry our landline phone or fax?
+

Usually yes. A category-rated data cable can carry a phone or fax line if it is terminated for that purpose at both ends, and on the patch panel any jack can be switched between data and phone with a short cord. Tell us which rooms need a phone line so we label those jacks clearly.

Do I call the internet company or an electrician to get wired jacks in my rooms?
+

Neither is quite right. The internet company brings service to one point in your building and usually stops there, and an electrician works on power. Jacks in your rooms are low-voltage inside wiring, which is what a cabling contractor installs, starting from the provider's box.

Our building's intercom stopped working. Is that a violation, and could the wiring be the cause?
+

For Class A buildings with eight or more apartments erected or converted after January 1, 1968, Multiple Dwelling Law 50-a requires a working intercom at the main entrance, and a guide to the rules says a dead one is a Class B violation. Damaged or corroded wiring is a common cause, and we can test it before anyone replaces the panel.

When cable goes through the floors of a row house, does the hole need to be sealed?
+

Yes. Every hole we make through a floor, a fire-rated wall or a shaft is sealed with an approved fire-stopping product once the cable is in. An open hole between floors lets smoke and fire travel, and sealing it is part of the job, not an extra.

Can the TV be mounted on the same visit the network cable is run to it?
+

Yes. The TV mount is priced from our Brooklyn list, starting at $179.99 for up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs, with $45 added for plaster and $65 for brick. The data drop to the TV wall is part of the cabling quote, and an in-wall power and cable kit is $169.

Can the work be scheduled around Shabbat or other times our household does not want contractors in?
+

Yes. Tell us the days and hours that do not work for your home or business when you book, and the walk-through and installation are planned around them. Many shops on Kingston Avenue close early on Fridays, and we schedule commercial work there the same way.

What should I have ready before the walk-through?
+

Access to every floor and closet, the cellar or utility room where the internet line enters, a list of the rooms and devices that need a connection, and any building rules, board requirements or landlord contact. If you know where earlier cable runs, tell the technician.

If you have to open old plaster, who patches it afterward?
+

We keep openings small and put them where a jack plate, closet or trim will cover them, and we fill small holes we make. Larger plaster repairs or painting to match are a finishing trade, and we tell you before work starts if any opening will need one.

What happens to the old phone wire and coax already in our walls?
+

If it is still used, we leave it alone. If it is dead, we can disconnect it and label it as abandoned so nobody wastes time tracing it later, and sometimes an old cable makes a useful pull string for a new one along the same path.

Can we keep living in the house while the cabling goes in?
+

Yes. Most residential jobs happen in occupied homes. We work room by room, cover floors and furniture, keep noisy drilling to short bursts and clean up each day, so the house stays usable throughout.

Do you ever need to get into a neighbor's apartment or a shared cellar?
+

Sometimes, if the only path runs through a shared space. We never enter another apartment or a shared area without the owner's or management's permission, and we look for a route that stays inside your space first.

Can you take the old provider cables off the front of our row house?
+

We can remove dead cable once we confirm it is not in use. On a landmarked house, removal that leaves fixings or holes in the masonry may raise a patching question for the Landmarks Commission, so we point that out before starting.

Can you set up the router and devices once the cabling is in, and what does that cost?
+

Yes. A setup-only visit for routers, switches, access points and devices is $195 for the first hour, then 30-minute blocks on our Brooklyn list. Have your provider account details and passwords ready so the setup goes quickly.
