The Neighborhood

Why Nassau County Properties Need Fiber Optic Installation

Ask a Nassau office manager whether the building "has fiber" and the honest answer is almost always yes. Using FCC broadband data current to the end of 2025, one availability tracker puts Verizon's fiber at 99.32 percent of the county's population and Optimum's at 96.57 percent. Residents on the Long Island forum argue about which provider is cheaper for a gig up and a gig down, not about whether fiber exists. Verizon's own map lists Hempstead, Garden City and Mineola among its central Nassau service areas. In a county of about 1.38 million people, glass at the curb stopped being news years ago.

The catch is age and reach. Verizon told the Town of Hempstead in the mid-2000s that it already served Valley Stream, Hicksville and Plainview, would build Freeport, Baldwin, Merrick, Elmont and Franklin Square next, and would reach the Village of Hempstead, Uniondale, West Hempstead and Long Beach by the end of 2007. Many of the boxes on Nassau basement walls today were hung in that first wave. They brought light to the building and stopped there. Behind them, the building kept whatever it already had: phone-era copper risers, a cable TV splitter tree, a network closet that grew one switch at a time, and in a surprising number of 1970s and 1980s offices, a run of early multimode fiber that somebody pulled for a mainframe or a phone system and nobody has tested since.

That is the Nassau pattern, and it is different from both the city and eastern Long Island. Nassau is built out. Most of the buildings we work in already exist, are already occupied and were designed long before anyone planned for gigabit service at every desk — low-rise office buildings along Marcus Avenue and Old Country Road, medical suites around the hospital in Mineola, two-storey village blocks with a shop below and apartments above, school buildings in fifty-six separate districts, and post-war houses that have been expanded twice and now hold a home business. The work is rarely about crossing a field. It is about finding a clean path through a building that is already full, and about deciding what to keep, what to re-terminate and what to retire.

The county's patchwork adds a layer of questions. Two cities, three towns and sixty-four incorporated villages each keep their own building department, so the same job can have different paperwork on either side of a street. Commercial and residential work are treated differently: Nassau's Office of Consumer Affairs licenses home-improvement contractors who work on houses, but says plainly that contractors who work only on commercial buildings don't need that licence. Fire alarm changes go to the Nassau County Fire Marshal. None of this is hard once it is planned, but it is why a Nassau fiber quote starts with a walk and a few questions rather than a price per foot. When the walk shows that copper and cameras need attention too, our Nassau County structured cabling crew scopes it the same day.

The Inside Gap

Carrier Fiber at the Wall, Old Wiring Behind It

Most Nassau calls start the same way. The business pays for a fast fiber circuit, the speed test in the equipment room looks right, and the people upstairs or at the far end of the building still complain that everything is slow. The carrier did its part. The problem lives in the forty or four hundred feet between the carrier's box and the desks, and it usually takes one of four forms.

The handoff is in the wrong room. When fiber first came to many Nassau buildings, the installer put the optical terminal wherever power and a wall were handy — a boiler room, a basement storage cage, a janitor's closet, the back wall of a garage. The firewall and switches then went next to it because that was the easy thing to do. Years later the business has a real network room on another floor, and a single copper patch cord, sometimes coupled two or three times, carries the whole office's traffic between them. A short, properly terminated fiber link from the carrier's handoff to the network room fixes speed, tidiness and reliability at once.

The backbone is copper that was never meant to be one. In two- and three-storey offices the closets on the upper floors often hang off the main room by a single Category 5 or 5e cable, sometimes routed through spaces nobody can now reach. That one cable caps every person on the floor at whatever it can carry. A small multi-strand fiber cable between closets removes the bottleneck and leaves spare strands for the next tenant.

There is old fiber, but no one knows what it is. Offices that housed banks, insurers, defence suppliers or hospital departments in the 1980s and 1990s often still have fiber in their ceilings: orange-jacketed multimode, ST connectors, a wall box with a few strands patched and the rest coiled. Some of it is perfectly usable for gigabit links; some of it is the wrong type for anything faster; some of it is broken. Testing it costs a fraction of replacing it, which is why an audit comes before any proposal for new cable.

Several occupants share one path. A village block with a pharmacy downstairs and four apartments above, a medical building with six practices, a strip with a landlord's closet at one end — all have a single route for every service. Fiber is useful here not because of distance but because one small cable can carry separate strands to each occupant, landed in separate enclosures, so nobody's network crosses anybody else's.

None of these jobs needs a trench, a pole or a carrier construction order. They need someone to find the path through an occupied building, respect the people working in it, and leave behind glass that has been measured. If the same walkthrough turns up switches, firewalls or wireless that need replacing, our network installation team for Nassau County can quote that work alongside the fiber.

Legacy Fiber

Old Fiber Already in Nassau Ceilings: Keep, Re-terminate or Retire

Before we suggest pulling new cable through a Nassau office, we look for what is already there. In buildings of this age it is common to find fiber left by two or three earlier tenants, and the question is never simply "does it light up." It is whether this particular glass, with these particular connectors, can carry the speed you need over the distance it covers.

The Fiber Optic Association's link tables make the stakes clear. Older 62.5-micron multimode fiber, known today as OM1, supports 10-gigabit short-wave Ethernet for only about 33 metres, or 108 feet. Early 50-micron fiber, OM2, manages about 82 metres. For one-gigabit links the same glass is far more forgiving: roughly 275 metres on OM1 and 550 on OM2. So a forty-metre run of orange OM1 between two closets may be perfectly good for a gigabit uplink and useless for ten. Modern OM4 stretches 10-gigabit to about 450 metres, and single-mode fiber removes the distance question for any building in the county.

How an audit runs

  • Read the jacket. Printed markings usually give the fiber type, the strand count and the fire rating. Colour helps too: orange often means older multimode, aqua laser-optimised multimode, yellow single-mode — but we confirm rather than assume.
  • Trace both ends. We find where each cable starts and finishes, which is not always where the labels say.
  • Inspect and clean. Every connector end-face goes under a scope. A dirty or scratched end-face is a common cause of high loss, and sometimes cleaning is the whole fix.
  • Measure loss. A light source and power meter give the loss on every strand in both directions; on longer runs an OTDR shows where any loss sits.
  • Decide per strand. Strands that pass are labelled and kept; strands with damaged connectors are re-terminated; cable that is the wrong type or broken is documented and, with the landlord's agreement, removed or left marked as abandoned.

The result is a short report: what exists, what it can carry, what we recommend. Sometimes the recommendation is to re-terminate twelve existing strands and move on — a few hundred dollars instead of a new backbone. Sometimes it is to leave the old cable alone and run single-mode alongside it. Either way, the decision is made on measurements.

Your Questions Answered

Fiber Optic Installation in Nassau County: Questions Answered

Below, sorted by the kind of building that raised them, are the questions we hear most often in Nassau — from office managers and landlords, school facilities staff, and homeowners with a detached studio or garage. The answers are condensed from what we tell people on site.

The carrier is already here

The Fios equipment in our basement went in around 2007 — should anything on our side of it be replaced now?

The carrier's terminal, its battery unit and its drop cable belong to the carrier; if they misbehave, the provider replaces them, and a service call is the right first step. What usually needs attention is everything you connected to it afterwards. In buildings of that vintage we often find the firewall sitting on a shelf beside the terminal, a long copper cord coupled two or three times running to the real network room, an unlabelled power strip feeding both, and no surge or battery protection on your side. We would leave the carrier's box where it is, mount a small fiber enclosure beside it, and run a short fiber link to your equipment room, with a proper rack, labels and a battery backup for your own gear. That way a technician from Verizon or Optimum can work on their equipment without touching yours, and your network no longer depends on a cord lying across a basement floor.

Our building has fiber service at the wall, but the wiring to the desks is decades-old phone cable — where should the money go first?

Start at the middle, not the ends. The single most common bottleneck we find in Nassau offices is the link between the main equipment room and the closets that serve each floor or wing; one old copper cable there throttles everyone behind it. Replacing that with a small fiber backbone usually produces the biggest improvement per dollar. Next, check the horizontal cabling: if the desks are on Category 3 or early Category 5 cable, they need new copper, and our Cat6 installation in Nassau County covers that work. Wireless comes last, because new access points can only be as good as the cable feeding them. We will give you the work in that order with a price for each step, so the budget can be spread across two or three phases if needed.

Shared buildings and changing tenancies

We own two attached storefronts and are merging them into one business — can fiber join the two networks through the shared wall?

Yes, and it is usually a short, simple link. The two back rooms are often only a few metres apart, so the main decisions are where to cross the party wall and how to seal it. Where that wall is fire-rated, the hole is kept small, sleeved and closed with a listed firestop product once the cable is through, so the wall's rating is preserved. Each side gets a small wall-mount enclosure, and the strands are tested and labelled so the merged network has one documented link rather than a cable passed through a hole. On Main Street blocks in Rockville Centre, Great Neck Plaza or Port Washington, where neighbouring shops share walls, this is often cheaper than a second internet account.

We're handing back half our floor to the landlord — what happens to the fiber that crosses the space we're giving up?

This comes up constantly in Lake Success and Garden City office buildings as tenants shrink. If your backbone runs through the area you are returning, you have three options: re-route it around the new demising wall through the corridor ceiling, which is the cleanest result; leave it in place under a written arrangement with the landlord, which is fragile because the next tenant's contractor may cut it; or replace the far end of the run with a new closet on your side. We look at the lease terms, measure the reroute and give you the cost of each option. Whatever you choose, it should be done before the demolition crew for the returned space arrives, because a backbone cut during the neighbouring fit-out is an avoidable emergency.

Rules, licences and the fire marshal

Does Nassau County's home improvement licence cover fiber work in a private house?

Nassau's Office of Consumer Affairs requires a home improvement licence for contractors who remodel residential homes, and its FAQ says contractors working only on commercial buildings don't need one. The FAQ does not mention low-voltage or data cabling either way. Running one fiber cable from a basement to a home office is a much smaller job than a remodel, but if the work involves opening walls or ceilings in a house, it is reasonable to ask the contractor about county licensing, and you can confirm any contractor's status with the Office of Consumer Affairs directly. We will tell you what we hold — including our NYS low-voltage licence #12000287431 — in writing with the proposal.

If our fire alarm panel will report over the new fiber, does the Nassau County Fire Marshal get involved?

It can. Plain data fiber is not part of the fire alarm system, but when a project installs or modifies a fire alarm system — for example, by changing how a panel communicates or linking panels in two buildings — that work is filed with the Nassau County Fire Marshal. The Town of North Hempstead's commercial plan-review checklist spells this out. In practice we keep fire alarm communication on paths and equipment designed and approved for it, coordinate with the alarm company of record, and make sure our general data fiber doesn't become something the alarm quietly depends on. Our Nassau fire alarm installation team handles the alarm side when you need it.

Schools, sites and homes

Our school district's internet arrives over a BOCES connection — where does district-owned fiber take over?

Many Nassau districts buy their wide-area network through Nassau BOCES; Mineola's board, for example, approved a five-year BOCES agreement for fiber connectivity, internet and voice back in 2011. That service typically ends at a handoff in each school's main equipment room. Everything from there to the classroom wings, the gym, the portable classrooms and the field house belongs to the district. That is where we work: fiber between the main room and each wing's closet, outdoor-rated cable to detached buildings, and documentation that matches the district's own records. We schedule pulls for summer, winter and spring breaks, and we ask for the building's asbestos survey before opening any ceiling.

Our office is upstairs from a restaurant kitchen — does the heat and grease in the shared ceiling affect fiber?

The glass itself is indifferent to the warmth of a normal building, and the cable jackets we use are rated for ceiling spaces, so a restaurant below is not a reason to avoid fiber. What we do differently is routing and housekeeping. We keep the cable well clear of kitchen exhaust ducts, flue pipes and any hot surface, cross them at right angles rather than running alongside, and support the cable on its own hooks so it never rests on a greasy tile or duct. Connectors are where trouble would start: an open adapter in a ceiling that collects cooking residue will eventually need cleaning. So every joint lives inside a closed enclosure in the office or the basement, never loose in the shared ceiling. On Nassau's main streets, where a pizzeria or bakery often sits under the office we are wiring, that is all it takes.

I run a business from my house — can my office get its own wired, documented connection separate from the family network?

Yes. In an expanded Nassau Cape or split-level, the provider's equipment is usually in the basement and the office is over the garage, in a dormer or in a rear addition. We run a small fiber cable from a wall enclosure next to the provider's box to a compact enclosure in the office, with a media converter or a small switch at each end. The business gets its own firewall and its own cable; the family network stays on the existing router. If your provider can deliver a second service, it can land in the same enclosure and travel on its own strands. Many home-based accountants, therapists and consultants want exactly this: no shared Wi-Fi for client data and a clear line between home and work.

Inside older office buildings

The space above our ceiling tiles carries the building's return air — what fiber is allowed up there?

When the space above a suspended ceiling is used as a return-air plenum, as it is in many 1970s and 1980s Nassau office buildings, any cable left exposed there must be plenum-rated — for fiber, the OFNP listing, tested to the NFPA 262 standard — or it must run inside metal conduit. Riser-rated OFNR cable is for vertical shafts, and general-purpose OFN cable is for neither. We confirm on the walkthrough whether the ceiling is a plenum by looking at the air-handling layout, then specify the cable to match. Plenum cable costs more than riser cable, so a quote that ignores the question can look cheaper and fail inspection later.

Riser-Rated (OFNR) Cable

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Fiber Optic Installation in Nassau County

Search summaries and cost guides answer fiber questions with confident one-liners. Here are seven we see repeated — five quoted as published, two summarised — and what they miss in Nassau.

“Verizon Fiber: 99.32%”

That figure, from an availability tracker built on FCC data, is accurate as far as it goes, and it is why so many people assume their business is already "on fiber." Availability measures whether a carrier can serve an address. It says nothing about what happens past the carrier's terminal.

In a Nassau office, the carrier's fiber typically ends at a box in the basement or utility room. From there the building may rely on a single old copper cable to reach upstairs closets, or on a patch cord stretched across a storage room. The circuit is fiber; the network is not.

The useful question is therefore not "is fiber available here" but "how far does fiber reach inside our building, and what carries the traffic the rest of the way." That is a five-minute question on a walkthrough, and the answer decides whether you need any work at all.

Online estimate: $300–$800 a drop for ordinary office work

This range from a national cost guide is a fair orientation, and our own Nassau figures fall inside it: $403 for a multimode drop and $460 for single-mode, terminated and tested. The trouble is the word "typical."

In Nassau's older office buildings a run is rarely typical. A plenum ceiling changes the cable, an asbestos survey can change the route, and a shared core with no spare sleeve can turn a simple pull into a coring job. Those are the factors that move a price, and a per-drop number cannot show them.

That is why we publish the unit prices and then walk the route before writing a proposal. You see the same numbers everyone else sees, plus the specific conditions of your building that change the total.

“Nobody publishes a flat per-strand rate”

A New York cabling site says this about OTDR testing, quoting hourly rates instead. It is true of a lot of contractors, and it makes comparing quotes difficult, because the testing line on one bid can't be lined up against another.

We do publish one. In Nassau an OTDR test is $46 per strand, on top of the light-source and power-meter loss test every strand receives anyway. A 12-strand backbone therefore carries $552 of OTDR testing, stated as a line on the proposal.

Whoever you hire, ask for the test plan in writing: which strands, which tests, at which wavelengths, and in what form you receive the results. A bid that says only "fiber tested" leaves you no way to know what you paid for.

“Singlemode glass is now cheaper than OM4”

The same site makes this point, and it is right about the cable. It also notes that single-mode optics have cost more than multimode ones, which is where the real decision lies.

In Nassau the deciding factor is often what is already installed. If an office has working OM3 or OM4 between two closets and multimode optics in the switches, extending that system can make sense. If the old fiber is OM1 or OM2, it will not carry 10-gigabit over most closet-to-closet distances, and new single-mode is the better long-term choice.

We make that call per link, after testing the old glass and looking at the switches at each end. A blanket rule in either direction wastes money somewhere.

“Plenum rating adds 15–25%”

That premium is in line with what we see. The mistake is treating plenum rating as an optional upgrade, the way some summaries present it.

In a Nassau office building where the ceiling cavity returns air to the air handler, exposed cable in that space must be plenum-rated or enclosed in conduit. It is a fire-code requirement, not a quality tier. A cheaper riser-rated bid for a plenum ceiling is a bid that will need to be redone.

On the walkthrough we identify which ceilings are plenums and which are not, so plenum cable is specified only where it is required and you are not paying the premium where it is not.

Claim: fiber takes months — roughly 30 days off site, 30 on site and 40 to 60 more to activate

That timeline comes from a carrier describing how it brings fiber to a multifamily property that has none. Lifted out of context, it reads like a general answer to how long "fiber installation" takes.

In Nassau the carrier's construction is almost always finished; the fiber is already at the building. Owner-side work inside an occupied building is a different scale of job. A demarc-to-network-room link is often done in a day, and a multi-closet backbone in an office building typically takes several working days once approvals are in hand.

What actually sets the calendar in Nassau is access: landlord approval, the asbestos survey, after-hours windows, and for schools the break calendar. We give you a schedule built on those, not on a carrier's construction timeline.

Claim: Verizon wired the whole Town of Hempstead, so Nassau is done

In the mid-2000s Verizon told the Town of Hempstead it would reach the whole town within five years of its franchise, and the build that followed is why so much of Nassau has fiber at the curb. It is easy to read that history as "Nassau is already wired."

Coverage meant streets and drops. It did not rewire the inside of any office building, school or apartment block, and the equipment installed in that first wave is now close to twenty years old. Many of the owner-side connections made to it at the time were improvised.

The practical result is that Nassau's next round of fiber work is inside buildings, not in the street, and it is owner-funded. That is the work this page describes.

Our Installation Process

Seven steps, built for buildings that are already full of people and old cable. The second step is the one that saves Nassau clients the most money.

1. Walk from the demarc outward

We start at the carrier's equipment and follow the existing path to every closet and room that needs service, lifting ceiling tiles and opening closets as we go, and noting plenum ceilings, sleeves and suspect materials.

2. Test what is already there

Any existing fiber is identified, scoped, cleaned and loss-tested before we recommend new cable, so the proposal re-uses good glass and retires only what fails.

3. Written proposal and paperwork

Route, cable type and rating, strand count, enclosures and test plan, priced line by line from our Nassau rates, plus the landlord submission, certificate of insurance and any building-department or district forms the job needs.

4. Prepare the path

Dust protection and floor cover in occupied areas, existing sleeves re-used where allowed, new penetrations cored and sleeved, enclosures and racks mounted before any cable arrives.

5. Pull and support

The crew feeds the cable by hand or with a measured pull, never past the maker's tension rating or tighter than its bend radius. It hangs from its own hooks or rides in innerduct instead of resting on ceiling tiles, and each hole it passes through is sealed with a listed firestop and photographed.

6. Splice, terminate and prove

The glass is joined to factory-made connectors by fusion splice inside each enclosure. Then comes proof: a scope image of the end-faces, a two-way loss reading for each strand and, where the run is long or single-mode, an OTDR trace.

7. Cut over and hand over

Traffic moved onto the new fiber at a time that suits you, old links labelled or removed as agreed, and a results file with a simple route map delivered to you and your IT provider.

Sizing the Cable

How Many Strands a Nassau Building Really Needs

The cable is the cheap part of a fiber job; the labour to pull it is not. That is why we size strand counts for the life of the building rather than for today's switch. A typical Nassau office link uses two strands for the main uplink. We rarely install fewer than twelve.

  • Two strands for the network uplink between the main room and each closet, which is all most switches use today.
  • Two more for redundancy, so a second uplink or a failover switch can be added without another pull.
  • Strands for other systems — building controls, cameras, access control or a second carrier — each on its own pair so no system depends on another's equipment.
  • Strands for other occupants in shared buildings, one pair per tenant or apartment plus a few unassigned.
  • Spares, because a strand damaged during a later renovation can simply be swapped for a spare instead of re-splicing the cable.

For a single tenant in a two-storey office, twelve strands is usually plenty. For a multi-tenant building, a school wing or a backbone that might one day serve several businesses, twenty-four is the safer choice. The difference in material cost is small next to the cost of opening the ceilings again.

Honest Advice

When a Nassau Job Doesn't Need Fiber at All

Not every slow network needs glass, and we would rather tell you that on the walkthrough than sell you a backbone you don't need. Three situations come up often in Nassau.

The run is short and the copper is good. In a single-floor office where every closet is within copper's reach of the main room, a properly installed and certified Category 6 or 6A cable between them will carry the speeds most small businesses use. If the existing link is simply a damaged or badly terminated cable, repairing it is the right answer.

The bottleneck is the equipment, not the cable. An old switch with ten-megabit or hundred-megabit ports, a firewall that cannot keep up with the circuit, or a router doing a job it was never designed for will cap speeds no matter what connects to it. We test the link and tell you plainly if the glass is not the problem.

The building can't be opened. Occasionally a lease, an asbestos finding or a landlord's rule rules out any new cable for now. In those cases a temporary solution — better wireless, or re-using an existing pathway as far as it goes — can carry you until the space is renovated, when fiber can be added cheaply while the ceilings are already open.

Either way, you leave the walkthrough with a clear picture of where the bottleneck is and what it would cost to remove it.

South Shore

Rebuilt, Raised and Rewired: Fiber Along the South Shore

When Sandy came through in October 2012, AP reported more than a thousand homes destroyed in the Town of Hempstead alone, and more than seventy-one thousand Nassau households applied for federal help. In Long Beach, Island Park, Oceanside, Freeport, Baldwin and Massapequa, the buildings that came back were often raised, gutted and rewired, and the carriers rebuilt their drops at the same time. The result, more than a decade later, is a South Shore with a lot of relatively new carrier equipment and a lot of owner-side wiring that was put back in a hurry.

Three habits make fiber work here last longer. First, keep the electronics high. If a carrier terminal has to stay low by the service entrance, a short fiber link lets your firewall, switches and battery backup live on a raised shelf or an upper floor, where the next storm surge won't reach them. Glass and properly sealed enclosures tolerate damp far better than powered equipment does.

Second, use enclosures and connectors suited to salt air in anything near the water: sealed wall boxes, covered adapters and no bare connectors left hanging in a garage or a bulkhead-side storage room. A scoped and capped connector that sat in salty air for a summer is much easier to clean than one left open.

Third, write the route down. After Sandy, many owners could not tell insurers or new contractors which cable did what. A one-page map with photographs of every penetration and enclosure costs little and makes the next rebuild, or the next claim, much simpler.

Test Results

Reading the Test Report You Receive at Handover

Every Nassau job ends with a results file. It is worth knowing what is in it, both to check our work and to compare it with what another contractor gives you.

  • Strand list. Each strand by number and colour, with the panel and port it lands on at each end. This is the page your IT provider will use most.
  • Loss results. The measured loss of each strand, usually at two wavelengths, against a calculated budget based on the length, the number of connectors and the number of splices. A pass means the measured loss is inside the budget.
  • OTDR traces. On longer runs and on single-mode backbones, a trace that shows the length of the cable and any event along it — a splice, a connector, a tight bend. A clean trace is the best evidence the cable was not damaged during the pull.
  • End-face images. Where a connector is critical, an inspection image showing it was clean when we mated it.
  • Route map and photos. A simple drawing of where the cable runs, with photographs of each penetration and firestop, each enclosure and each label.
  • Legacy notes. If we audited old fiber, which strands were kept, re-terminated or abandoned, and where the abandoned cable is labelled.

If you are handed a single sheet that says "all fiber tested OK," ask for the per-strand numbers. A contractor who took them will have them.

Getting Ready for a Nassau Walkthrough

A walkthrough takes about an hour in a typical office and less in a house. These five things make it more useful.

  • Access to the carrier's equipment. The key to the basement, boiler room or utility closet where the Verizon or Optimum box lives.
  • Access to every closet and the ceiling. Ceiling tiles we can lift and closets we can open, including any shared or landlord-controlled rooms.
  • The asbestos survey, if one exists. For schools and older commercial buildings especially, so we can plan the route around it from the start.
  • Your IT contact. Your managed-service provider or in-house IT person, by phone if not in person, to confirm what switches and optics sit at each end.
  • Lease, landlord or district rules. Any building rules for contractors, work hours, insurance requirements or approvals you already know about.

You don't need drawings. If there are some, they help; if there aren't, the walkthrough and our route map will become the drawing.

Field Notes

Field Notes From Nassau Ceilings and Basements

Three habits our crews bring to every Nassau site. These describe routine practice; they are not an account of a particular customer.

In a Nassau office the first tile we lift is above the main closet, not above the problem desk. That is where the history is: old fiber coiled in a loop, a telephone block nobody has touched, cable from three earlier tenants tied together. Ten minutes there tells us more about the building than an hour anywhere else.

The second habit is to bring the scope and the cleaning kit on the walkthrough, not just on the install. If there is legacy fiber, we would rather know on day one whether it is reusable, because it changes the whole proposal.

The third is to treat the people downstairs as clients too. In a village block with a shop below and apartments above, the shop owner's Saturday and the tenants' evenings matter as much as the landlord's schedule. We ask, we put it in writing, and we keep to it.

FAQ

Nassau County Fiber Optic Installation FAQ

Do you cover the North Shore, the South Shore and the incorporated villages in between?
+

Yes. We work across all of Nassau County: the North Shore from Great Neck and Port Washington to Glen Cove and Oyster Bay, the central corridor from Lake Success through Garden City, Mineola and Hicksville to Bethpage, and the South Shore from Valley Stream and Rockville Centre to Long Beach and Massapequa. Villages, hamlets and the two cities are all covered, and we confirm local building-department rules for each address.

Can our Wi-Fi access points run straight off the new fiber backbone?
+

Not usually, and they don't need to. Ceiling access points draw their power and data over a copper Cat6 cable from a PoE switch, and that cable has to stay within 100 metres of the switch. The fiber's job is the step before: it joins the closet holding that switch to the main equipment room, so every access point on the floor shares a fast, interference-free uplink. If an access point has to sit farther than copper allows, we place a small switch near it and feed that switch with fiber.

Can fiber be added to a finished office without opening the walls?
+

Most of the time, yes. In a typical Nassau office the cable travels above the drop ceiling, down inside existing conduit or an empty wall cavity we can fish from above, or in a slim surface raceway painted to match the wall. We only cut drywall where no hidden route exists, and when we do the opening is small and patched. The walkthrough is where we find the route, and the proposal says plainly whether any wall has to be opened.

What happens to our fiber when the landlord replaces the drop ceiling?
+

Fiber supported on its own hooks or in innerduct, rather than lying on the tiles or tied to the old grid, usually survives a ceiling replacement untouched. Before the ceiling contractor starts, we can tag the route, photograph it and, if needed, temporarily support the cable while the grid is removed. After the new ceiling is in, we re-test the strands so you know nothing was damaged.

Will you test fiber another company installed before we accept their work?
+

Yes. Independent acceptance testing is a sensible step before you sign off on a fiber job. We inspect the connectors, measure loss on every strand and, on longer or single-mode runs, take OTDR traces, then compare the results with what the installer promised. Testing is billed at our published Nassau rates, and you receive the per-strand results whether the work passes or not.

How much downtime should we plan for when the new backbone goes live?
+

Very little, if the cutover is planned. We pull, splice and test the new fiber while your existing link keeps running, so the office works normally through installation. Going live is then a matter of moving uplinks and patch cords onto the new strands, which we schedule in a window you choose — often early morning or after closing — with the old link left in place until the new one has carried traffic successfully.

Can a fiber link carry a separate network for our building's heating, cooling and energy controls?
+

Yes. Many Nassau office buildings have upgraded their energy management systems, and those controls are best kept off the tenants' networks. A backbone cable can dedicate a pair of strands to building controls, landing in their own enclosure or panel position, so the controls contractor has a private path between the plant room and each floor without touching any tenant's equipment.

Will a new fiber backbone work with the network switches we already own?
+

Usually. Most business switches bought in the last decade have SFP or SFP+ slots that accept a small optical module, and the module is matched to the fiber type and distance. We check the model numbers on the walkthrough and tell your IT provider which optics to use; if a switch has no fiber slots, a pair of media converters bridges the gap until the switch is replaced.

Can the phone company's dead boxes and old cables on our wall be removed during the fiber job?
+

Equipment and cable that belong to the carrier are the carrier's to remove, so we don't take them out ourselves; we can help you ask the provider to retire dead equipment. Old wiring that belongs to the building, such as abandoned copper or fiber from earlier tenants, can be removed or labelled as abandoned with the owner's agreement, which leaves the wall and ceiling clear for the next contractor.
