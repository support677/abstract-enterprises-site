The Neighborhood

Why Flatbush Properties Need TV Installation

Flatbush is one of the oldest names in Brooklyn and one of the most varied places to hang a television. Wikipedia places it in Brooklyn Community District 14, across the 11203, 11210, 11225 and 11226 ZIP codes, and describes three kinds of housing that sit within a few blocks of each other: the Victorian-era planned communities such as Prospect Park South, the Beverley Squares, Ditmas Park, Fiske Terrace and the Albemarle-Kenmore Terraces; row houses at the north end, where Lefferts Manor may hold the neighborhood's earliest; and the six-story Art Deco and Colonial Revival elevator buildings that line Ocean Avenue and the streets around it. Between them run the commercial avenues, Flatbush, Church, Nostrand, Rogers and Cortelyou, where the ground floors are stores and the floors above are apartments. Each of those building types puts a different wall behind a TV, and the wrong guess about that wall is the single most common reason a mount fails in an older Brooklyn home.

The Victorian houses are the part of Flatbush people photograph. Brick Underground counted roughly 2,000 to 2,500 of them over about 100 square blocks, and quoted borough historian Ron Schweiger calling the area "probably the largest collection of this type of architecture anywhere in the United States, in one area." They are detached, often large, with porches, bay windows, turrets and stained glass, and most of them were built between the 1890s and the 1910s. When the Landmarks Preservation Commission designated two more historic districts here in November 2025, Beverley Square West with 118 houses and Ditmas Park West with 127, its release noted that the houses were built with "gas lighting, hot and cold running water, indoor plumbing, and coal-burning furnaces." In practice that means the walls are almost always plaster on wood lath, the electrical outlets were added long after the plaster went up, and the best wall for a TV is often already taken by a fireplace, a bay or a wide doorway into the next parlor.

The apartment buildings are where most Flatbush households actually live. Brownstoner's 2024 history of the Cohn Brothers, one of the firms that built across the neighborhood between the wars, points to six-story buildings such as 113-121 Lenox Road from 1926, in tapestry brick with cast stone, and the Tudor-style 2101 Albemarle Road from 1939, along with earlier four-story walk-ups on many side streets. Buildings of that era usually have plaster partitions inside, often over clay tile or gypsum block rather than wood, and solid masonry around the outside walls, the stair and the shafts. The same article describes two- and three-story tapestry-brick buildings from about 1912 with one or two apartments over a storefront, which are still common along Nostrand, Flatbush and Rogers Avenues. A TV in one of those front apartments may be going onto a wall that is brick on one side of the room and plaster on lath on the other.

The people living in those buildings shape the work as much as the plaster does. The NYU Furman Center's 2024 profile of Flatbush/Midwood, the city district that holds most of the neighborhood, counts 147,911 residents in 66,991 homes, with a homeownership rate of 24.4 percent and a rental vacancy rate of 3.1 percent. In plain terms, roughly three households in four rent, and apartments rarely sit empty. That puts a landlord, a managing agent or a co-op board between many Flatbush residents and the drill, and it means people are moving in and out of apartments they did not choose the walls of. The same profile reports severe crowding in 7.8 percent of renter households. Where a family shares a few rooms, the TV is often going into a bedroom as well as the living room, it has to clear bunk beds and dressers, and it has to be out of reach of small hands. We plan for all of that before we arrive.

Same-Day Repair in Flatbush

Need a TV Fixed in Flatbush? An Arm Drooping Off Old Plaster, Holes Where the Last Tenant's Mount Was, or a Screen That Will Not Find the Box

The urgent calls from Flatbush come in three shapes. The first is a mounted screen that has started to droop, usually on a full-motion arm that gets pulled out every evening. On plaster over wood lath, a bracket held only by the plaster and a few plastic plugs loosens a little every time the arm swings, because plaster is hard on its face and crumbly behind it. On plaster over clay tile, the kind found in many of the 1920s and 1930s apartment houses, an anchor can feel solid on the day and then crack the hollow tile web behind it. In both cases the warning signs are the same: a hairline crack spreading out from the bracket plate, a gap opening between the plate and the wall, or one corner of the screen sitting lower than the other. When that starts, the bracket is not going to tighten itself.

The second is the wall left behind by somebody else. Flatbush apartments turn over often, and many new tenants move in to find a bracket screwed into the wall, a cable poking out of a hole, or a row of anchor holes with no bracket at all. Whether the old bracket can be reused depends on how it was fixed and whether it is rated for your TV, and we look at that from a photo before anything else. The third is the picture itself: a TV that powers on but shows no signal from the cable box, a soundbar that stopped answering the remote, or a streaming app that buffers in the back bedroom even though the internet is fine in the front room. That one is often a cable or network problem rather than a television problem, and it can usually be found and fixed in one visit.

If a screen is pulling away from the wall, do not push it back or tighten the screws yourself. If two people can lift it safely, take the TV off the bracket, unplug it, lay it face up on a blanket or soft rug, and call (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list. If the same screen goes back on a properly anchored bracket during that visit, it is priced as a new mount on that wall, with the take-down added and the plaster or brick add-on that applies. If the problem is the picture rather than the bracket, a setup-only visit is $195 for the first hour and then 30-minute blocks, and it covers the TV's inputs, the cable box, streaming devices, the soundbar and the remote.

Building Specialists

TV Installation for Flatbush's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for nearly every TV call we get from Flatbush. What separates them is not the size of the screen; it is what the wall is made of, how far the television has to be carried from the curb, and who besides you gets a say in what is drilled. Those three things decide the plan before anyone talks about brands or brackets.

The detached Victorian house

Queen Anne, Colonial Revival and shingle-style houses from the 1890s to the 1910s, with plaster on wood lath, bay windows, pocket doors and fireplaces in the main rooms.

The Ocean Avenue elevator building

Six-story Art Deco and Colonial Revival apartment houses from the 1920s and 1930s, with plaster partitions over tile or block and a managing agent who sets the rules.

The side-street walk-up

Four-story buildings with stairs only, tight turns at each landing, and living rooms that often back onto the stair or the building next door.

The apartment over the store

Two- and three-story tapestry-brick buildings on Nostrand, Flatbush and Rogers Avenues, with one or two apartments over a storefront and brick on more walls than you expect.

The attached row house

Brick and limestone row houses at the north end near Lefferts Manor, with party walls of solid masonry on both sides of the parlor.

The new building

Rentals and condos finished in the last few years and those still rising along Flatbush Avenue, with drywall on steel studs and wide window walls.

The detached Victorian: the best wall is usually spoken for

Walk into the parlor of a house in Prospect Park South or Beverley Square West and count the uninterrupted stretches of wall. There are fewer than you think. A typical main room has a bay window on the street side, a fireplace on one long wall, a wide opening or pocket doors into the next room, and radiators under the windows. The wall facing the sofa is often the fireplace wall, and that is the one everyone wants for the TV. We will mount there if it works for you, and the above-fireplace add-on is $130 on our list, but we always talk through the height first. A Brownstoner forum thread from an owner of an old Brooklyn house put it bluntly: a TV over a mantel is "awfully high and invites a sore neck." If the screen will be watched for hours, a lower wall on the other side of the room, or a stand beside the hearth, is often the more comfortable answer.

Behind the paint, these houses are mostly plaster on wood lath. That is good news for a TV when it is done properly, because the lath is nailed to wooden studs and the studs can carry a lot of weight. The trouble is finding them. Magnetic and electronic stud finders get confused by the lath, the nails and the varying thickness of the plaster, and they can beep along the entire wall. We locate the framing by measuring from corners and openings, by scanning for the lines of lath nails, and by drilling one small pilot hole where the bracket plate will cover it. Plaster is brittle, so every hole is pre-drilled with a sharp bit at low speed, and the fasteners go through the plaster into the wood. On our list that is the +$45 plaster add-on.

The Ocean Avenue elevator building: easier stairs, harder walls

The six-story buildings along Ocean Avenue and its side streets solve the problem that makes walk-ups hard: the TV goes up in an elevator. What they bring instead is a mix of wall types that can change from one room to the next. The partitions between rooms in buildings of that era are often plaster over clay tile or gypsum block. The wall that faces the street, or the court, or the building next door, is usually brick. The wall around the elevator shaft or the stair is masonry too. A wall that knocks hollow in one spot and dead a foot away is not unusual. Each of those takes a different anchor, and an anchor meant for one can fail in the other, so we confirm the material with a pilot hole before the bracket goes up, and price the job on the add-on that matches what we find.

Clay tile deserves its own sentence, because it fools people. It is a hollow fired block with thin walls inside, so a screw driven straight into it finds almost nothing to grip, and an expanding plug can split the thin web behind the face. The fixings that work are ones that spread the load across the inside of the hollow, or that reach past the tile into something solid, and for a heavy screen on a long arm we prefer a wall that is brick or a partition where we can reach framing. When the only practical wall is tile and the TV is large, we tell you that before you book, along with the option of a fixed or tilting bracket that keeps the screen close to the wall and puts far less leverage on the fixings than a full-motion arm.

The side-street walk-up: the carry is part of the job

The four-story walk-ups on Flatbush's side streets are where TVs get heavier than their box says. A 65-inch screen is not hard to lift; it is hard to turn. It has to go up four flights in its box, around tight landings, past radiators and banisters, without being laid flat or flexed, and then through an apartment door that may open against a hallway wall. We carry it wrapped in moving blankets, keep it upright, and look at your photos of the stairs and the door before the visit so we know whether one technician or two is the right call. If the box will not make the turns, the TV comes out of it downstairs and travels in blankets the rest of the way. Tell us the floor when you book, so the arrival time allows for it.

The apartment over the store: brick in more places than you think

The two- and three-story tapestry-brick buildings on Nostrand, Flatbush and Rogers Avenues, many of them from around 1912 by Brownstoner's account, put one or two apartments over a shop. They are narrow, and their side walls are commonly brick shared with the next building along the avenue. The front room faces the street through large windows, and the back room may face a yard or an air shaft. In apartments like these we expect solid brick on at least one long wall, plaster partitions inside, and an old chimney breast in one of the rooms. That makes them very good for holding a big screen, as long as the anchors are set into the brick itself rather than the mortar between bricks. As the bracket maker MantelMount puts it, "mortar joints are much softer than the bricks themselves."

The attached row house: party walls both sides

At the north end of Flatbush, near Lefferts Manor, the housing changes from detached houses to attached brick and limestone row houses. In a row house the two long walls of the parlor are party walls shared with the neighbors on each side. They are solid masonry under the plaster, which holds a TV very well, but it means a hammer drill, masonry bits and sleeve or wedge anchors rather than screws into studs, and it means your neighbor may hear the drill clearly for a few minutes. The interior walls, between the parlor and the hall or the back room, are more often plaster on wood framing, which is quieter to drill and easier to hide wiring inside. We usually suggest looking at both before choosing.

The new building: steel studs, glass and a building office

Bedford Square, which New York YIMBY reported nearing completion in January 2026, and 757 Flatbush Avenue are a different job. Their interior walls are usually drywall on light-gauge steel studs. Drywall on steel looks and sounds just like drywall on wood until a wood screw spins in place without biting, which is exactly what happens. Steel studs need toggles or fasteners made for sheet metal, or a backing board fixed across several studs, and a heavy screen on a full-motion arm needs the load spread. Many new apartments also have floor-to-ceiling windows on the wall you would naturally face, which limits the walls that can take a screen and means glare control matters more than in a prewar apartment.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Order We Check a Flatbush Room Before Marking a Single Hole

Every Flatbush TV visit starts the same way, and it is not with the drill. The first thing a technician does is stand where you will sit and look at the wall the way you will: where the light comes from at the hour you usually watch, whether the radiator under the window will be blowing heat up the screen, and how high your eyes are from that seat. Then the hands come out. A knock along the wall at several heights tells us whether it is the same material all the way across, which in the older apartment houses it often is not. The scanner goes over it next, slowly, and we treat a long stripe of beeping as a sign of plaster on lath or metal lath, not as proof of a stud.

Then comes the one small hole that settles the question. It goes where the bracket plate will hide it, and the dust that comes out tells the story: pale, soft powder and a sudden give means plaster over a hollow; red or orange grit means brick or clay tile; grey and gritty means block or concrete; a bright metal shaving means a steel stud. Only after that do we confirm the price with you, mark the bracket with painter's tape, and talk about where the wires go. It takes about ten minutes. It is the ten minutes that decide whether the screen is still level in five years, and on a Victorian wall or a 1920s partition it is never worth skipping.

Wall by Wall

Lath, Tile, Brick or Steel: Matching the Anchor to a Flatbush Wall

The wall decides the anchor, and the anchor decides whether the TV stays up. A bracket maker's rule of thumb is worth repeating: MantelMount advises that "the total anchor capacity is at least four times the TV's weight." That safety margin is easy to reach on a wooden stud or solid brick and very hard to reach on hollow material, which is why we spend so long working out what the wall is. These are the walls we meet most often in Flatbush, roughly in the order we find them.

Plaster on wood lath. The wall of almost every Victorian house and many of the older walk-ups. It is strong where it meets the studs and weak between them. Fasteners go through the plaster into the stud, with holes pre-drilled so the plaster does not crack outward, and where the studs do not line up with the bracket we span them with a board. As MantelMount puts it, "plaster is brittle; always pre-drill and let the anchors do the work." On our list this is the base price plus $45.

Plaster on clay tile or gypsum block. Common in the partitions of the 1920s and 1930s apartment buildings. There is nothing to screw into, so the fixing has to spread its grip across the inside of the hollow block. It is fine for a fixed or tilting bracket holding a mid-size screen and much less suitable for a long full-motion arm. We price it as plaster, and we tell you honestly if the size and arm you want are a poor match for the wall.

Solid brick. Party walls in row houses and apartments over stores, chimney breasts, and the outer walls of the apartment houses. It holds a heavy TV very well when the anchors go into the brick, not the mortar joints. It takes a hammer drill and masonry anchors, and on our list it is the base price plus $65. Exposed brick looks good behind a TV; brick hidden under thick plaster needs care that the plaster is still bonded to it.

Drywall on wood studs. Found where houses and apartments have been renovated, in basements finished in recent decades, and in some newer buildings. It is the wall the base price of $179.99 is written for, with fasteners into the studs. Drywall on steel studs is the version found in most new construction around the avenue; it needs toggles or a backing board, and it is the reason we never assume a new wall is easy just because it is smooth.

Keeping the wires out of sight without breaking the electrical code

The question we hear most after "what will it cost" is "can you hide the wires." The answer is yes, but not by pushing the TV's power cord into the wall. The National Electrical Code says that flexible cords, which is what a TV's power cord is, "are not permitted to be concealed by walls, floors, or ceilings." Gizmodo's summary of that rule adds that a cord run inside a wall could cause trouble with an insurance claim if something goes wrong, and that the proper fix is an outlet directly behind the TV. A StreetEasy discussion about mounting TVs in New York apartments made the same point from the other direction, warning that some do-it-yourself power kits do not meet the city's wiring rules.

That is why our in-wall option is a code-compliant in-wall power and cable kit, $169 on our Brooklyn list. It places a power inlet behind the screen and another near the floor, joined inside the wall by wiring rated for that purpose, with a separate pass-through for the HDMI and other low-voltage cables. The TV plugs in behind itself, and nothing hangs down. It works best on drywall over wood or steel studs and on plaster over lath where the cavity between studs is clear. It is not something we put into a wall of solid brick, and in buildings that forbid opening walls we do not do it at all.

Streaming and Signal

Fiber at the Curb, Buffering in the Bedroom: Getting a Steady Picture to a Flatbush TV

A wall-mounted TV is only half the job if the picture stutters. In Flatbush the service coming into the building is rarely the problem any more. The FCC's availability figures for Flatbush-Ditmas Park, as of December 2025, show Verizon and EarthLink fiber available at about 92.7 percent of addresses and Optimum at 73.5 percent, with smaller shares for Starry and Astound. Verizon's fiber has been in the area for more than a decade; Bklyner reported in December 2012 that FiOS had reached Ditmas Park and that residents from Prospect Park down to Avenue J could order it. The problem is more often the last fifty feet, from the router to the screen, through walls that were never designed for radio signals.

Old Flatbush buildings are hard on Wi-Fi. Plaster on metal lath, clay tile, brick party walls and chimney breasts all weaken a wireless signal, and a Victorian house with the router in the front hall may have a back parlor or a third-floor bedroom where a streaming app keeps dropping to a blurry picture. In an apartment building, dozens of neighbors' routers crowd the same channels. Before we mount a TV in a room that is far from the router, we check the signal at the spot where the screen will hang. If it is weak, we will tell you, and we will tell you what the options are: moving the router, adding a wired connection to the TV, or a mesh system if wiring is not practical.

Renters and Owners

Leases, Boards and Deposits: A TV on a Wall in a Neighborhood Where Most People Rent

With a homeownership rate of 24.4 percent in Flatbush/Midwood by the Furman Center's count, the wall behind most Flatbush TVs belongs to somebody else. That does not mean renters cannot mount a TV. It means checking one clause first. Real estate attorney Steven Kirkpatrick told Brick Underground in 2022 that "it's not common for a landlord to ban you from hanging items," but that the place to look is the lease's alteration clause. Some leases say nothing about wall fixtures; some ask for written consent for anything beyond pictures; a few forbid drilling outright. Bill Kowalczuk, in the same piece, advised asking permission before installing things like "closet systems and TV brackets." A two-line message to the landlord or managing agent before the visit, saying which wall and roughly how large the holes will be, settles most of these questions.

The deposit is the next worry, and the honest answer is that it depends on the size of the holes and how they are left. Arik Lifshitz of DSA Property Group told Brick Underground that "a nail or screw-sized hole from a picture frame or shelving system would fall within wear and tear," while attorney Justin C. Brasch warned that "careless drilling of the wall or drilling to hold heavy hangings" can be charged to the tenant. A TV bracket sits between those two: a handful of screw-sized holes, but holding a heavy object. What protects your deposit is clean, small, well-placed holes and a plan for the day you leave. When you move, we can take the TV down ($85 on our list) and you can fill and touch up the holes, or have your landlord's painter do it. Brick Underground also notes that a landlord has 14 days after you move out to return the deposit with an itemized list of deductions.

Landmark Blocks

Six Landmark Districts, One Rule of Thumb: What Changes for a TV Inside a Flatbush Historic District

A lot of Flatbush is protected. Wikipedia lists the Albemarle-Kenmore Terraces (designated in July 1978), Prospect Park South (1979), Ditmas Park (July 1981) and Fiske Terrace-Midwood Park (March 2008) among the historic districts of Victorian Flatbush, and the Landmarks Preservation Commission added Beverley Square West and Ditmas Park West on November 25, 2025. People living in those blocks sometimes assume that every change to the house now needs permission. For a TV, the rule of thumb is simpler. What happens inside the house, on an interior wall, is generally your own business. LPC's own permit guidance says a Landmarks permit is required for "any interior work that requires a permit from the Department of Buildings," and for interior work that affects the outside of the building. Mounting a bracket on a parlor wall, running cables in a cord cover and setting up a soundbar does not fall into either group.

Where the district matters is the outside. A satellite dish on a porch roof, an antenna on a chimney, or a new cable run up the front of the house is a change to the exterior, and that is where the Commission, the federal antenna rule and the city building code all have something to say. The FCC's over-the-air reception rule protects dish antennas one meter or less in diameter and TV antennas in places you have exclusive use of, such as a private balcony or patio, but it allows restrictions for safety reasons or "where a historic site is involved," and it does not cover common areas such as the roof or outside walls of a multiple dwelling. In an apartment building, that usually means the roof and the facade belong to the landlord or the board, not to you.

Safety at Home

Small Rooms, Big Screens and Children: Keeping a Flatbush TV Where It Belongs

The Consumer Product Safety Commission's 2023 report on tip-overs is not easy reading. It counted 217 deaths from furniture, appliance and television tip-overs reported between January 2013 and July 2023, and 103 of them, 47 percent, involved a television falling. Of the 217 deaths, 155, or 71 percent, were children under 18. The same report estimated an annual average of 17,800 emergency-department visits for tip-over injuries from 2020 to 2022. Those numbers are national, but the situation behind them is familiar in Flatbush, where the Furman Center counts severe crowding in 7.8 percent of renter households and a TV often ends up on a dresser in a bedroom shared by children, or on a narrow stand in a living room that doubles as a play space.

A TV fixed properly to the wall cannot tip, which is the strongest argument for mounting it in a child's room or a busy living room. When mounting is not possible, because the lease says no drilling or the family expects to move soon, the next best thing is to put the TV on a low, wide piece of furniture made for it, push it back from the front edge, and secure both the TV and the furniture with straps. We include that in a stand setup whenever children are in the home. It costs nothing extra to do it while we are already there, and it removes the most common cause of those injuries.

Compliance notes for Flatbush shops and buildings

  • Power stays out of the walls and ceilings. The National Electrical Code bars flexible cords from being "concealed by walls, floors, or ceilings, or located above suspended or dropped ceilings."   • Your lease controls the building. With 79 percent of Flatbush merchants renting by the city's count, the landlord's permission for drilling, and for anything on the facade or roof, comes before the visit.
  • Nothing on the fire escape. The Department of Buildings does not allow antennas or wires on fire escapes, which matters for shops with apartments above that want an antenna or a dish.

Your Questions Answered

TV Installation in Flatbush: Questions Answered

These are the questions Flatbush customers bring to us, grouped by the kind of building they come from. The answers are the ones a technician would give you on the phone.

Inside the Victorian houses

The only clear stretch of wall in our Prospect Park South parlor is the one the pocket door slides into. Can a bracket go on that wall at all?

Not directly over the pocket. A pocket door slides into a hollow cavity inside the wall, and that section is usually a thin skin of plaster on light framing with the door itself moving behind it. Any fastener long enough to hold a TV would either miss the framing or stop the door from sliding, and could scratch or jam a door that may be original to the house. What we can often do is find the solid framing at the end of the pocket, past the point where the door stops, and set the bracket there, or use a wider bracket that spans from the framing beside the pocket. If the pocket takes up most of the wall, we will suggest another wall or a stand rather than risk the door.

We opened a small hole in a Beverley Square West wall and saw old cloth-covered wires inside. Does that rule out an in-wall power kit?

It changes the plan. Cloth-insulated wiring is a sign of an older circuit, and in a house that was built for gas lighting it may be one of several generations of wiring in the walls. An in-wall power kit works by plugging into an ordinary outlet near the floor and carrying power inside the wall to an inlet behind the TV, so it does not connect to the old wiring at all, but it does need a clear cavity, and we do not cut into a stud bay that has old wiring running through it without knowing what it is. In that situation we usually recommend a paintable cord cover on the surface for $59, and suggest that you have an electrician look at the old circuit separately.

Apartment buildings and walk-ups

Before approving our TV bracket, the managing agent for our Ocean Avenue building wants to know what anchors you will use and how much weight they will carry. What can you send?

We can send a short written note for the agent once we know the wall: the TV model and its weight, the bracket model and its rated load, the type and number of fasteners and what they go into, and the add-on that applies. If the wall is still unknown when they ask, the note says so and explains how we confirm it on the day with a hidden pilot hole. Along with our license number and proof of insurance, that note answers almost every question a managing agent or board asks about a TV. It also gives you a record, which is useful when you move out.

Three of our children share the back bedroom, and the only wall without furniture is the one the bedroom door swings against. Can the TV go behind the door?

Sometimes, but it has to clear the door. A fixed low-profile bracket keeps a TV within a couple of inches of the wall, so the question is whether the door, fully open, would hit the screen, and whether the doorknob would strike it. We measure the door's swing and the knob height and look at a doorstop, which keeps the door a safe distance from the wall. If the screen would be in the door's path, a better choice in a shared room is often high on the wall opposite the beds, above the dressers, on a fixed or tilting bracket, with the cables in a cord cover so nothing hangs within reach.

Our TV arrives on Saturday but the installation is booked for Tuesday. How should it be kept in the apartment until then?

Keep it in its box, standing upright, the way the arrows on the carton show, against a wall where nobody will knock it over and away from a radiator. Do not lay a large TV flat on its face or its back, and do not lean anything against the screen side. If you want to check it works before we come, take it out carefully, stand it on its feet on a table, power it on, and put it back in the box with its foam. Keep the screws, the remote and the small parts bag together; the bolts that come with the TV are sometimes the ones the bracket needs.

Shops and restaurants on the avenues

Our takeout counter on Church Avenue has a clear protective partition between staff and customers. Can the TV go on the customer side?

Yes, on the wall, not on the partition. Clear partitions are not built to carry a TV and its bracket, and drilling them can crack them. On the customer side, the best spot is usually high on a side wall or above the door, angled toward the waiting area with a tilting bracket, so people standing in line can see it without crowding the counter. Older storefronts on Church Avenue often have brick side walls, which hold a screen very well and use the brick add-on. If the cable box has to stay behind the counter, the video cable runs along the ceiling or in a cord cover, with the TV's power coming from an outlet near the screen.

In our restaurant we want the game with sound in the back room and captions only on the screen by the front windows. Can two TVs work like that?

Yes. Each TV has its own volume and its own caption setting, so one can play with sound through a soundbar in the back room while the one in front stays muted with captions on. The thing to plan is the signal: if both screens show the same game from one cable box, the box's output has to reach both TVs, which means a splitter and a cable run between them; if each screen has its own box or streaming device, each one is set up separately. We set the captions so they are large enough to read from the tables, and label which remote belongs to which screen.

Care at home

Our mother now uses a hospital bed in the front room of our house off Church Avenue. Can a TV be placed so she can see it when the bed is raised?

Yes, and it is worth doing well. The bed should be in its final position first, and the right height for the screen is where her eyes are when the bed is raised to the angle she uses most, not where they would be from a sofa. A full-motion arm on a solid wall lets the screen be pulled out and angled toward the bed and then folded back when the room is used for something else. We make sure the arm cannot be pulled into the path of the bed or a caregiver, keep every cable off the floor, and set up the remote, captions and volume so she can manage them herself.

Our toddler's bed is against the only wall that works for the TV in our apartment. How do you keep the screen and the cords out of reach?

We mount the screen high enough that its bottom edge and the bracket are well above what the child can reach while standing on the bed, use a fixed or tilting bracket rather than an arm that could be pulled, and run every cable inside a cord cover or an in-wall kit so there is nothing to grab. The cable box and any power strip go into a cabinet or behind furniture across the room. If the bed can be moved even a few feet, we will suggest it, because the safest place for a toddler's bed is not directly under anything heavy.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About TV Installation in Flatbush

Ask an AI tool about TV mounting in Brooklyn and you get a confident paragraph written for drywall. These are the claims we hear repeated most, and what Flatbush walls actually do.

"Use a stud finder, then screw the mount into the studs"

This assumes drywall on wooden studs. In a Victorian house in Prospect Park South or the Beverley Squares the wall is almost always plaster on wood lath, and an electronic stud finder can read the whole wall as solid while a magnetic one finds hundreds of lath nails.

In the Ocean Avenue apartment buildings, partitions are often plaster over clay tile or gypsum block, with no studs at all. Screwing into hollow tile as if it were wood gives a bracket that feels solid on the day and loosens over the following months.

What works is identifying the wall rather than hunting for a stud: knock it, scan it, measure from the openings, and drill one pilot hole behind where the bracket plate will sit. The dust tells you wood, tile, brick or metal, and the fastener follows from that.

"TV mounting costs about $100 to $300"

Local averages are close to that: Angi's Brooklyn page shows an average of $280 and a typical range of $172 to $376, and HomeAdvisor puts the national range at $155 to $350. The figures are real, but they are built mostly from drywall jobs with an outlet in the right place.

In Flatbush the wall moves the price first. On our Brooklyn list the base for a TV up to 55 inches is $179.99; plaster adds $45, brick or stone $65, and an above-fireplace position $130. A 55-inch screen over a brick chimney breast is $374.99 in labor, which is a normal Flatbush job, not an unusual one.

The useful question is what your own wall will cost. Send a photo of it and the TV's model sticker, and you get the add-ons and the total before you book, with any change on the day explained before we drill.

"Run the power cord through the wall to hide it"

The National Electrical Code says flexible cords, which is what a TV power cord is, "are not permitted to be concealed by walls, floors, or ceilings." Gizmodo's summary of the rule adds that a cord inside a wall can complicate an insurance claim.

In an old Flatbush house the risk is concrete: a cord pushed into plaster rests against framing, nails or older wiring where nobody can inspect it. A StreetEasy discussion about New York apartments warned that some do-it-yourself power kits do not meet city wiring rules either.

The proper options are an outlet behind the TV or a code-compliant in-wall power and cable kit, $169 on our list. Where the wall cannot be opened, a paintable cord cover at $59 keeps everything tidy and inspectable.

"Renters shouldn't mount TVs"

In a neighborhood where about three households in four rent, that advice would leave most Flatbush TVs on dressers. Attorney Steven Kirkpatrick told Brick Underground that "it's not common for a landlord to ban you from hanging items," and that the alteration clause is what to read.

Renters need a plan, not a ban: ask the landlord in writing, name the wall and the size of the holes, keep the reply, and choose a fixed or tilting bracket with the wiring in a cord cover. Brick Underground quotes Arik Lifshitz saying screw-sized holes from shelving generally count as wear and tear.

Where the lease does forbid drilling, a TV on its stand at $119.99, strapped where children are around, is a proper installation that leaves the wall untouched.

"Plastic anchors will hold a TV in any wall"

Plastic plugs are made for light loads in drywall. In old plaster they grip only the brittle outer layer, in clay tile they can crack the thin web inside, and in brick they can end up in a soft mortar joint.

MantelMount's guidance is that "the total anchor capacity is at least four times the TV's weight," that brick should be drilled in the brick face because "mortar joints are much softer than the bricks themselves," and that wood screws strip in metal studs, which is what new buildings along Flatbush Avenue have.

The right anchor is the one made for the wall in front of you, chosen after the pilot hole: fasteners into framing behind lath, masonry anchors in brick, spreading fixings in tile, toggles or a backing board on steel.

"Above the fireplace is the best spot"

The fireplace wall is often the focal point of a Victorian parlor, but a Brownstoner forum thread about an old Brooklyn house warned that a TV over a mantel is "awfully high and invites a sore neck."

The same thread raised heat from a working fireplace as a concern for the TV. Many Flatbush fireplaces are decorative and never lit, which removes that worry; if yours is used, we discuss it before choosing the wall.

When the mantel wall is right, we set the screen as low as the mantel allows and route cables around it. When it is not, a lower wall across the room is usually more comfortable for long viewing.

"Fast internet means a smart TV will stream fine"

FCC figures show fiber available at about 92.7 percent of Flatbush-Ditmas Park addresses, so the service at the street is rarely the problem. The path from the router to the screen is.

Brick party walls, chimney breasts and plaster on metal lath all weaken Wi-Fi, and in apartment buildings dozens of neighboring routers crowd the same channels. A buffering app on a new TV is often the walls, not the TV.

We check the signal where the screen will hang before mounting it, and where it is weak the most reliable fix is a tested wired connection to the TV, which is everyday work for us.
