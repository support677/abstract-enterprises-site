The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Chinatown Properties Need Security Camera Repair

Brooklyn Chinatown runs along Eighth Avenue from roughly 40th Street to 65th Street, inside Sunset Park, and it is one of the busiest shopping streets in the borough. Restaurants, bakeries, markets and other shops sit shoulder to shoulder with community organizations and social service providers in buildings that mostly went up between the 1890s and the 1920s, when the elevated railways and then the subway brought row houses and industrial buildings to the area. The City's own archive photos show that most of these buildings are the same ones that stood in the 1940s and the 1980s. What changed is what goes on inside them, and that is exactly what makes camera repair here different from a quiet residential block.

A camera system on Eighth Avenue usually lives a hard life. It may watch a register that never stops, a kitchen door that opens all day, a cellar full of stock, and a sidewalk crowded from early morning until late at night. It was often installed in stages: a few cameras when the business opened, a few more after a scare, then a new recorder bought by a family member who knew enough to plug it in. Years later, when one picture goes black or the phone app stops connecting, nobody in the store has the password, the cables carry no labels, and the person who set it up has moved on. On a block like this, the repair is often not about a single broken camera at all. It is about a working business that has lost track of its own system.

The reason owners care is easy to find in the news. In August 2020, CBS New York reported that a man got into the Lucky 8 Restaurant on Eighth Avenue through a back door at about 8:40 in the morning, opened the cash register and another locked drawer, and took $600; police released surveillance footage of him at the register. A few blocks west, Patch reported that two men cut the locks on the front gates of King Mei Supermarket at 5101 Sixth Avenue at about 4 a.m. in June 2019, pried the door and took $3,000 in cash and $200 in lottery tickets. The camera caught them removing the locks, but the description police could give was an age range and hooded sweatshirts. In June 2026, News 12 reported a man forcing his way through the front door of a home at 53rd Street and Eighth Avenue and taking about $10,000. In each case the question afterward was the same: did the camera that should have seen it actually record, and is the picture good enough to use?

Power is the other half of the story. On June 25, 2025, News 12 reported that Con Edison was deliberately lowering voltage in parts of southwest Brooklyn, with Sunset Park named among the hardest-hit neighborhoods, to protect its equipment during a heat wave. Some buildings never lost power completely; their lights simply ran dim. That kind of event is harder on a camera system than a clean outage. A recorder or a power supply that sags below its working voltage can restart over and over, corrupt the drive it writes to, or come back with cameras that stay dark. Many Eighth Avenue businesses also cut the power to the whole store every night when they close, which puts the recorder through a hard shutdown and a cold start every single day. We cover both later on this page.

Then there are the systems themselves. Two brands come up again and again in Brooklyn storefront repairs, Hikvision and Dahua, and a family-built system may have its menus set to Chinese and its account registered to a relative's phone number or e-mail. In November 2022 the FCC barred new Hikvision and Dahua equipment from being authorized for sale in the United States. That rule did not make the cameras already on your wall illegal, but it does change which replacement parts we can supply, and it is one more reason a repair here starts with finding out exactly what is installed. We repair these systems every week, and we tell you honestly when a part can be matched and when the better answer is to replace one piece of the chain.

The narrow avenue storefront: one long room and a recorder wherever it fit

An Eighth Avenue store is usually narrow and very deep. The camera layout follows that shape: one camera at the door, one or two down the aisles, one over the register, and one at the back door or yard. The cables run along the ceiling, often stapled straight to an old tin or plaster surface, and end at a recorder that was put wherever there was room. We find recorders on top of refrigerators, under the counter beside the cash drawer, behind boxes of stock and down in the cellar next to the boiler. Each of those spots has its own way of killing a recorder, through heat, dust, damp or someone kicking the power cord.

The repair in this kind of store usually has two parts. The first is the fault itself: the dead camera, the failed drive, the crushed cable. The second is moving or protecting the recorder so the same failure does not come back in six months. That can be as small as lifting it off the floor onto a ventilated shelf, or as large as running new cable so it can sit in a locked cabinet in the office. We price both separately so you can choose.

The restaurant with a working kitchen: heat, steam and grease

Restaurants are where camera systems age fastest. Cameras in or near the kitchen collect a film of cooking oil that slowly turns the picture soft and yellow, and the infrared lights at night bounce off that film and make the image worse in the dark than it is by day. Steam from dish stations and rice cookers fogs domes. Heat from the line can warp cheap plastic housings. And the recorder, if it sits in a back office beside the kitchen, breathes the same greasy air through its fan.

Our work here is part repair, part housekeeping. We clean the camera windows and housings with a degreaser that does not scratch the dome, check that the dome has not clouded from heat, and look at whether a camera can be moved a few feet out of the smoke path without losing the view the owner needs. For the recorder, we clean the vents and fan, and if the room is too hot or too greasy, we price moving it. We also suggest a cleaning schedule that matches how hard the kitchen works, which is the cheapest repair there is.

The store with apartments above: two kinds of owner, one front door

Many buildings on the avenue have a business on the ground floor and two or three floors of homes above. Sometimes the store and the building owner are the same family. Sometimes the store is a tenant, and the building owner lives elsewhere. The camera system may belong to either one, and cameras at the shared front door and stair often feed the store's recorder simply because that was the easiest place to put them.

Before we repair a camera in a building like this, we confirm who owns the system and who approves the price, because the answer decides whose recorder we can open and whose footage we can export. When the store owns the cameras but the stair camera protects the families upstairs, the repair is a good moment to settle in writing who keeps the password and who can see which cameras. We set that up on the recorder so each party sees only what it should.

The side-street row house: stoop, yard and a shared basement

The numbered streets off Eighth Avenue are lined with brick row houses, most of them two- and three-family homes. A typical system has a camera on the stoop, one on the yard, one at the alley or side gate and sometimes one at the basement door, all wired back to a recorder in a closet or the basement. The owner may live on one floor and rent the others, and the recorder may be in a part of the house the owner no longer uses every day.

These systems are usually simple to repair, and the faults are predictable: a connector that let in water at the yard camera, a power adapter that died after a summer of heat, a drive that wore out after years of nonstop recording. What takes time is access. If the recorder is in a tenant's space, we need the owner to arrange entry, and we will not open a recorder in someone's apartment without that.

The early co-op and walk-up: one recorder, many households

Some of the oldest cooperative apartment buildings in New York stand nearby: Alku Toinen, built in 1917, and The Advance, built in 1921, on 43rd Street between Eighth and Ninth Avenues. Repairs here begin with an inventory. We count the cameras the recorder thinks it has, the cameras that are actually on the walls, and the ones that are dark, and we write it down. The warehouse and workshop: loading doors and long runs

Toward Third Avenue and the waterfront, the buildings get bigger: wholesale and food distribution space, workshops and storage, with loading docks, roll-up doors and high ceilings. Camera runs here are long, sometimes longer than a standard network cable can carry without help, and cameras sit high on walls and steel. Forklifts, pallets and trucks are the usual enemies of camera cable in these buildings.

The fixes are heavier: replacing long runs, adding a switch partway along a run that is too long, protecting cable in conduit where it crosses a loading area, and bringing a lift or a tall ladder for cameras at roof height. We schedule this work around deliveries so the dock keeps moving.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The First Twenty Minutes Behind an Eighth Avenue Counter

These notes set out how our technicians approach a camera system in a busy Brooklyn Chinatown business, where the store stays open, staff keep working around us and the owner wants to know quickly whether the problem is small or large.

Start where the owner looks. The technician begins at the monitor or the phone the owner uses every day, noting which tiles are dark, which are frozen and which look fine but are blurred. Then playback: picking an hour from yesterday on every camera shows at once whether the recorder has actually been saving, which is the question that matters most after a theft.

Find the recorder and read its surroundings. Is it on the floor, beside the fryer wall, under boxes, on a power strip shared with a fridge or a heater? Is its fan clogged with dust or flour? Is the date on the screen right, and is the language one the staff can read? Those few seconds of looking explain a lot of faults before any tool comes out.

Ask who set it up, and who has the password. On the avenue, the answer is often a relative, a previous tenant or a friend of the family. If nobody has the login, the technician starts the manufacturer's reset process early, because some brands take time to answer.

Measure before replacing. Voltage at the camera, power at the switch port, and a cable test from the recorder end tell us whether a dark camera is really dead. Very often it is a tired power adapter, a loose connector or a crushed cable, and the camera itself is fine.

Price before parts, then clean before leaving. You hear the cause and get a written price before anything is replaced. When the repair is done, the technician cleans the camera windows the staff cannot reach, clears the recorder vents, sets the clock, labels what was traced and shows the owner how to check playback.

Fault by Fault

Grease, Power, Pests, Passwords or Age: How Brooklyn Chinatown Camera Systems Break and What Each Repair Involves

Five causes sit behind most camera failures in older storefronts and homes like the ones on and around Eighth Avenue. Knowing which one you are dealing with tells you what the repair is likely to cost and how to stop it happening again.

Grease and dust: the slow fade

A camera near a kitchen never fails all at once. The picture gets a little softer each week as cooking oil settles on the dome, until one day the owner notices that faces at the door look like smudges. At night it is worse, because the camera's own infrared lights reflect off the film on the dome and fog the whole image. In bakeries and dry-goods stores, fine flour or dust does the same thing, and it also gets into the recorder's fan and vents, where it traps heat and shortens the life of the hard drive.

The repair is usually cleaning, not replacement: the right degreaser on the dome and housing, a check that the plastic has not clouded or cracked from heat, and clearing the recorder's vents. Our Brooklyn list prices a clean and check at $80 per visit when it is the only work. If a camera has to be moved out of the worst of the smoke, or a dome has been damaged by heat, the work becomes a single-issue repair in the $150 to $700 range.

Power: dimmed lights, nightly shutdowns and crowded power strips

Con Edison's voltage reductions in June 2025 are a good example of the kind of power trouble that hurts camera systems without anyone noticing at the time. When the voltage sags, a recorder or a camera power supply can restart again and again, and each sudden restart risks corrupting the drive. The same thing happens, on a smaller scale, in stores that switch off the main power every night at closing, and in back rooms where the recorder shares one power strip with a refrigerator, a heater or a rice cooker.

We measure the output of the camera power supply or the PoE switch, check the recorder's event log for repeated restarts, and test the drive. The fix may be a new power supply, a dedicated outlet for the recorder, or a battery backup that rides through short dips and lets the recorder shut down cleanly in a longer cut. Our Brooklyn list prices power supply repair at $150 to $400 and a hard drive swap at $200 to $450.

Pests, stock and renovations: the cable takes the damage

In old buildings with cellars full of stock, camera cable is exposed to everything that lives and moves down there. Rodents chew insulation, shelving gets pushed against a cable and crushes it, and a contractor renovating the dining room ceiling cuts a run without knowing what it was. The camera at the end of that cable goes dark, and the owner assumes the camera died.

A cable test from the recorder end finds the break quickly. Where the damage is near a connector, re-terminating the end costs $150 to $350 on our Brooklyn list. Where a whole section has been chewed or cut, we replace it and, in a cellar, run the new cable high and in protective conduit where the old one was exposed.

Passwords and accounts: the system nobody can open

Some Eighth Avenue repairs are not about hardware at all. The recorder works, the cameras work, but nobody can log in. The person who set it up chose a password and left; the app is registered to a phone number that changed; the account belongs to a relative who moved back overseas or a tenant who closed the business. Meanwhile the clock may be wrong, the motion settings unknown and the drive full of overwritten video.

Every major brand has an official way to regain control of its own recorder, usually by proving you have the device in hand. We use that process, never a shortcut that leaves the recorder open to the internet, then set new passwords, give them to the person you name, set the correct time zone and language, and turn on alerts so you hear about the next failure. When this is the whole job, it usually falls inside the $150 service call or the low end of a single-issue repair.

Age and the recorder: drives, firmware and equipment rules

Hard drives in recorders that write video around the clock wear out, and recorders that have been running for many years lose support for the phone apps people use. In November 2022 the FCC also stopped new Hikvision and Dahua equipment from being authorized for sale in the United States. The FCC did not order existing systems removed, but replacement cameras and recorders from those brands are now harder to source, so a failed part on an older system can mean choosing a compatible replacement rather than an exact match.

When a recorder is the problem, we tell you whether a new drive will do, whether the recorder should be replaced, and whether your cameras will work with a replacement. Our Brooklyn list prices a DVR that is not working at $300 to $700 including the drive and labor, an NVR that is not recording at $400 to $900, and a full DVR or NVR replacement with hard drive at $700 to $1,500.

What an Eighth Avenue owner needs from a repair

A store owner rarely calls because a camera is broken in principle. The call comes because something happened, or almost happened, and the owner wants to know whether the system would have caught it. That means the first job is always the same: confirm that every camera is not only showing a picture but recording, that the recorder keeps enough days of video to cover the time it takes to notice a problem, and that the clock is right so a clip can be matched to a receipt or a police report.

The second thing a business needs is a picture that identifies someone. The camera over the door should catch a face at the threshold, not the top of a head from the ceiling. We check each camera against the job it is supposed to do, and if a small change in angle or a different lens would make the picture useful, we say so and price it.

Gates, awnings and the view from the window

Cameras mounted inside the front window often have a problem the owner only discovers after a night-time incident. When the roll-down gate comes down, the camera may see nothing but the inside of the gate, or the gate's slats may catch the camera's infrared light and turn the image white. New York's 2009 storefront gate law requires gates that are at least 70 percent see-through. In June 2026 the City Council voted to drop the July 1, 2026 deadline, so under that bill a business only has to install a compliant gate when it replaces its gate for other reasons. Either way, a gate change is a good moment to re-aim and re-set the cameras behind it.

Awnings and signs cause the other common view problem. A new awning or a bigger sign over the door can sit right in front of a camera that used to see the sidewalk. We move the camera below or beside the new fixture, or change its lens, so the street is back in view.

Cellars, cold rooms and back rooms

Many Eighth Avenue businesses keep stock, and often their recorder, in the cellar. Cellars bring damp, pests and the risk of a leak, and cold rooms bring condensation on any camera that sits where warm and cold air meet. When a cellar camera or recorder keeps failing, the lasting fix is usually to move the recorder upstairs to a dry, ventilated spot, to run cellar cable high and protected, and to use cameras rated for cold and damp where they have to go.

Rules that touch a commercial camera repair

State licensing. The New York Department of State licenses companies that install, service or maintain security systems, and its rules treat a closed-circuit camera system used to detect intrusion or theft as covered work. An owner or an employee may work on the owner's own system, but a company you hire should be licensed. Ours is NYS license #12000287431.

Cameras and staff. New York's Labor Law bars employers from video recording in restrooms, locker rooms and changing rooms. If your cameras watch areas where employees work, such as a stock room or a kitchen, a clear sign that the area is recorded is good practice, and your accountant or lawyer can tell you what notice your business should give.

Police access is your choice. The city's retail-theft program lets businesses link their cameras to the NYPD in real time. CBS New York reported that the program is free and voluntary, that businesses have to opt in, and that the hours the NYPD can see the cameras can be customized. Permits. Under the 2025 New York City Electrical Code, low-voltage wiring under 50 volts for signaling and communication, such as camera cable, does not need an electrical permit when it is done by a qualified person. Work that adds new line-voltage outlets for equipment is electrical work and is handled by an electrician.

Landmarks. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's four Sunset Park historic districts, designated in 2019, cover blocks between Fourth and Seventh Avenues. Eighth Avenue itself is outside them. Security Camera Repair in Brooklyn Chinatown: Questions Answered

These are situations that come up in Eighth Avenue businesses and the homes around them. Each answer comes from how camera systems actually fail in buildings like these, and from what the repair involves.

After closing time on the avenue

We switch off the power to the whole store every night when we close, and turn it back on in the morning. Is that hurting the recorder?

Yes, and it also means the cameras are not recording overnight, when most break-ins happen. Cutting the main power stops the recorder mid-write, which over time corrupts the drive and can leave the recorder asking to be set up again one morning. It also means the recorder has nothing from the hours the store is empty. The fix is to keep the recorder, the camera power supply and the network switch on a circuit or outlet that stays live when the rest of the store is switched off, ideally with a small battery backup in between. We can show the electrician exactly which outlet needs to stay on, and we test the drive while we are there.

Our solid roll-down gate is due to be replaced with a see-through one. Will the cameras behind our window need to be changed afterward?

Usually they need resetting rather than replacing. A camera that has spent years looking at the inside of a solid gate at night will suddenly see the street through the new grille, but the slats can catch its infrared light and wash the picture out, and its motion settings will fire on every passer-by. After the new gate goes in, we adjust the angle, turn down or switch off the camera's own infrared if the streetlight is enough, set a motion area that ignores the sidewalk traffic, and check the picture after dark. If the camera cannot handle the glare, a model with better low-light performance is a single-camera replacement.

A new awning went up over our storefront, and the camera above the door now sees only fabric. Does it have to come down?

It has to move, but it rarely has to be replaced. The usual fix is to lower the camera below the awning frame, mount it on the side of the door opening, or put it inside the window looking out under the awning. Each needs a short extension of the existing cable, and a new mount that does not depend on the awning, since awnings get replaced. Tell us when the awning or sign company is coming next time, and we can move the camera on the same day.

Kitchens, bakeries and cold rooms

The camera over our restaurant's kitchen door goes soft and yellow within a few weeks of being cleaned. Is the camera failing?

Almost certainly not. That is a film of cooking oil on the dome, and it builds up fastest on cameras near the line or in the path of the exhaust. The picture usually looks worse at night, because the camera's infrared light bounces off the film. Cleaning it with the wrong product scratches the dome and makes it permanently cloudy, so we use a degreaser made for plastics. If the camera sits right in the smoke path, moving it a few feet or putting it behind a shield usually stretches the time between cleanings from weeks to months.

Our bakery's recorder sits in the back room where the flour is stored, and it has started shutting itself off. Is it the flour?

Quite possibly. Fine flour gets drawn into the recorder's fan and vents, packs around the hard drive and traps heat. A recorder that overheats may restart, shut down or stop recording, and the hard drive inside wears out faster. We open the recorder, clean the fan and vents, test the drive, and check the temperature inside. If the room is dusty every day, the lasting answer is to move the recorder into a closed, ventilated cabinet or into the office, away from where flour is handled.

The camera inside our walk-in cold room fogs up every time the door is opened. Do we need a different camera?

Probably, yes. When warm, damp air from the kitchen or the store rushes into a cold room, moisture condenses on any surface that is cold, including the camera's dome, and a standard indoor camera can also take on moisture inside. A camera rated for cold, damp spaces, mounted away from the door and with its cable entry sealed, stays much clearer. Sometimes the simpler fix is to watch the cold room from outside, through the door, rather than putting a camera inside it.

Who set it up, and in what language

Everything on our recorder and phone app is in Chinese because a relative set it up, but some of the staff now read only English or Spanish. Can you repair it and change the language?

Yes. Most recorders and their apps let you choose the menu language, and the choice is separate on the recorder and on each phone. We repair the fault, then set the recorder to the language the person running the store reads, and set each phone's app the way its owner wants it, so the family can keep Chinese and the staff can use English or Spanish. We also make sure the time zone and date format are right, because a recorder set up abroad can carry the wrong clock settings. We leave a short written note of what was changed.

The account for our camera app is registered to a relative who has moved back overseas, and nobody here can log in. How do we get control back?

Through the manufacturer's own recovery process, with the recorder in front of us. Most brands let the person who physically has the recorder reset its password, often by scanning a code on the screen and getting a reset key from the manufacturer. Once the recorder is back under your control, we remove the old account, register the app to the store's own phone number or e-mail, and set the new admin password for the person you choose. It is worth asking the relative to log in once and transfer the device before we start, if they can, because it saves a step.

Our recorder was bought from a seller overseas and has no warranty here. Can it still be repaired?

Often it can, as long as it is a standard recorder that works with common cameras and drives. Drives, power supplies, fans and cables are generic parts we can replace. What we cannot do is repair the recorder's main board or get firmware that the manufacturer does not publish for the United States. If the main board has failed, we price a replacement recorder that works with your existing cameras, and we tell you before buying anything whether your cameras will carry over.

When the building changes around the cameras

A new building went up next door, and our side camera now looks straight at a brick wall. Can it be saved?

The camera can be reused. What changes is where it goes. With narrow new buildings rising beside older row houses on and near Eighth Avenue, a side or rear camera that used to look down a gap may now face a wall a few feet away. We look at what the camera was meant to protect, usually a side gate, a yard door or a window, and move it to the closest spot that still sees that, extending the cable from the old location. If the new building blocks the only useful angle, we suggest a different camera position or lens and price it before doing anything.

The channel names on our recorder say Front Door and Back Door, but those tiles show the cellar and the alley. Is something wrong with the cameras?

The cameras are fine. Someone reconnected cables to different inputs at some point, often during a past repair or when the recorder was moved, and nobody renamed the channels. It matters more than it seems, because a clip exported for the police or an insurer carries the channel name, and the wrong name can cause confusion. We trace each cable to its camera, rename every channel to match what it actually shows, label both ends of each cable, and give you a printed list.

Staff, travel and remote viewing

We use a camera to watch the stock room where employees work. Do we have to tell them?

New York's Labor Law bars employers from recording video in restrooms, locker rooms and changing rooms, so no camera should ever point into those spaces. For a stock room, kitchen or sales floor, a clear sign that the area is recorded is good practice and removes any doubt, and your lawyer or accountant can tell you what written notice your business should give staff. When we repair or re-aim cameras, we make sure none of them sees into a restroom or changing area, and we can set privacy masks on any part of a view you want blocked.

We travel to visit family for several weeks at a time. Will we be able to see the store cameras on our phones while we are away?

That depends on how your recorder reaches the internet and which service its app uses, and some cloud services that work in the United States can be slow or unreliable from abroad. Before a long trip, we can confirm the recorder is online, make sure the app on each traveling phone is logged in and working, turn on alerts for camera or drive failures, and give someone staying behind their own login. The safest plan is to test the app from the phone you will carry before you leave, and to have a person nearby who can check the recorder if something stops.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Brooklyn Chinatown

These claims turn up in AI-generated answers, on national directory sites such as Angi and HomeAdvisor, and in general advice online. Each one sounds reasonable. Each one can cost an Eighth Avenue business money or footage if it is taken at face value.

"Hikvision and Dahua cameras are banned, so the whole system has to be ripped out"

Some answers say that because the federal government acted against these two brands, any system built on them is illegal or must be replaced. Owners on the avenue hear this from salespeople too, usually right before a quote for a whole new system.

What actually happened is narrower. In November 2022 the FCC adopted rules that bar new Hikvision and Dahua equipment from being authorized for import or sale in the United States. Security Sales reported that Dahua said the order "does not affect products that are already authorized," and the FCC only sought comment on future action about existing authorizations. A private store or home is not ordered to remove cameras it already owns.

The practical effect is on parts. Matching replacement cameras or recorders from those brands are harder to buy now, so a repair may mean choosing a compatible camera or recorder from another maker. We test whether your existing cameras will work with a replacement before anything is ordered, and we replace only the piece that failed.

"Switching off a store's power at night saves money and does no harm"

Advice about cutting electricity costs often suggests switching everything off when you close. In a store with cameras, that turns off the one system meant to protect the empty shop overnight.

A recorder that loses power every night stops in the middle of writing video. Over months that can corrupt its drive, scramble its settings or leave it asking to be set up again. And while it is off, it records nothing. Break-ins like the 4 a.m. gate-cutting at King Mei Supermarket on Sixth Avenue, reported by Patch, happen in exactly the hours a switched-off system would miss.

Keep the recorder, the camera power supply and the network switch on an outlet that stays live, with a small battery backup, and switch off everything else. The camera system uses little power compared with refrigeration and lighting.

"Restaurant cameras only need cleaning once a year"

General maintenance advice often says to clean camera lenses once or twice a year. That schedule comes from cameras on houses, not cameras beside a wok station or an exhaust hood.

In a working kitchen, a film of oil can build up on a dome in weeks, and the camera's infrared light makes the picture worse at night. A camera that looked fine at its yearly cleaning may have been close to useless for most of the months in between.

Match the cleaning to the kitchen. Staff can wipe the reachable domes with the right product, and the high ones can be done on a maintenance visit. Moving a camera out of the direct smoke path often cuts the cleaning to a few times a year.

"Wireless cameras avoid all the wiring trouble in an old building"

Many answers suggest that switching to Wi-Fi cameras solves cable problems in older buildings. On the face of it, no cable means nothing to chew, crush or cut.

Eighth Avenue's buildings are mostly brick and date from the 1890s to the 1920s. Masonry walls, cellars and long, deep stores are hard on Wi-Fi signals, and a camera still needs power, usually from an outlet or a battery that needs charging. In a busy commercial block, many networks compete for the same airwaves.

Wired cameras remain the most dependable choice for a store or a building. Wi-Fi cameras have their place on a row house stoop or as a stopgap, but a failed cable run is usually cheaper to repair than a whole wireless system is to buy and keep connected.

"If you can see a live picture, the camera is recording"

Many guides treat a working live view as proof the system is working. That is the most expensive misunderstanding in camera ownership.

Live view and recording are separate. A recorder can show every camera perfectly while its drive has failed, filled up and stopped, or been set to record only on motion that never triggers. Owners often find out only after a theft, when playback for the night in question is empty.

Once a month, open playback, pick a time from the day before, and check every camera. If any are blank, call us before you need the footage. We can also set the recorder to send an alert when a drive fails or a camera loses signal.

"Businesses have to give the NYPD access to their cameras"

Some online answers say that new city programs require stores to share their cameras with police. Owners worry that a repair will connect their system to the NYPD.

The city's retail-theft camera program is voluntary. CBS New York reported that it is free, that businesses must opt in, and that the hours the NYPD can see the cameras can be customized. Nothing about a repair enrolls your business.

If you do want to take part, a working, correctly timed recorder with a stable internet connection is what the program needs, and we can prepare it. If you would rather not, your system stays private, and you can still hand clips to the 72nd Precinct when you choose.

Brooklyn Chinatown Security Camera Repair FAQ

Can the coax cable already in our walls carry a higher-resolution camera?
+

In many cases, yes. HD-over-coax cameras and recorders can send a much sharper picture over the same coaxial cable that older analog cameras used, as long as the cable and connectors are in good condition. We test each run before recommending it. If a run is damaged or too long, we replace just that run rather than rewiring the whole store.

How often should the hard drive in a recorder that records around the clock be replaced?
+

There is no fixed date, because drive life depends on heat, dust, power quality and the type of drive. A recorder in a hot, greasy back office or a store that cuts power every night wears its drive faster. The useful habit is to have the drive's health checked on a maintenance visit and to replace it at the first warning, before it fails and takes footage with it.

We own two stores on Eighth Avenue. Can both camera systems be watched together in one app?
+

Usually, yes, if both recorders are from brands that work in the same app or are compatible with a common viewing app. We add each recorder to one account under the owner's control, name each store and camera clearly, and give managers access only to the store they run. If the two systems cannot share an app, we tell you what would need to change.

Can the relative who first set up our system stay on the phone during the visit?
+

Yes, and it often helps. The person who installed the system may remember passwords, where cables run, or which camera was added when. With the owner's permission we can talk them through what we see, and once the repair is done, we make sure the owner, not only the relative, holds the admin password.

Which payment methods do you accept for the service call and the repair?
+

You can pay by card through the secure payment link we send, or by Zelle. Card payments carry a 3 percent card fee, shown as its own line on the invoice. The service call is paid when you book, and the repair is billed on the written price you approve.

Is surge protection worth adding to the cable from an outdoor camera?
+

For cameras whose cable runs outside the building, on a facade, a roof or across a yard, it is often worth it. A surge on that cable can travel back and damage a recorder port or a switch. A small protector at the point where the cable enters the building costs far less than the recorder it protects. We look at which runs are exposed and suggest it only where it makes sense.

Can a camera watch our cash drawer without recording customers typing their card PIN?
+

Yes. Can a camera be mounted on our sign or awning frame?

It does no harm to leave it, but do not count on it. A camera with no lights at night or one hanging at an odd angle can tell an experienced thief it is not working.
