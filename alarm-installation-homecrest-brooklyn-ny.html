The Neighborhood

Why Homecrest Properties Need Alarm Installation

Ask three sources where Homecrest stops and you get three answers. Wikipedia draws it from Kings Highway south to Avenue X and from Coney Island Avenue east to Ocean Avenue. Brick Underground's 2022 buyer's guide uses the same east and west edges but ends it at Avenue U. CityNeighborhoods.NYC pushes it north to Avenue P and west to Ocean Parkway. Everyone agrees on the middle: the numbered East Streets from roughly East 11th to East 19th, the B and Q trains running along East 16th Street, ZIP code 11229, Community District 15 and the 61st Precinct on Coney Island Avenue. We use the generous version, because a house on Avenue W and a house on Quentin Road ask the same questions of an alarm, and we say so when a fact on this page comes from the edge rather than the middle.

What makes the neighbourhood unusual for alarm work is how many kinds of building share a few blocks. Wikipedia describes two-story private houses renovated into what it calls mini-mansions, condominiums built in the early 2000s, and, just south of Avenue V, one-story bungalows and rows of one-story brick homes with community backyards and driveways. Brick Underground's listings add the apartment side: a six-story, 66-unit co-op on East 13th Street built in 1960 with an elevator, a live-in super and a building security system; a 64-unit six-story co-op on East 14th Street from 1961 with parking; a three-story, 24-unit co-op from 1932; and eight-story condos on Ocean Avenue from 2006 and 2019, one with a 400-square-foot private terrace and two balconies. Each of those has a different weak side, and the weak side is rarely the front.

The burglary record points the same way. In October 2014 Bklyner reported a man police believed was behind a run of night-time burglaries in Homecrest, entering homes while people slept. The list police released included a house on East 13th Street near Avenue T, entered at 10:30 at night, where he was believed to have used a rear sliding door; a house on East 18th Street near Avenue S entered through a side window; and a house on East 12th Street near Avenue R entered through an unlocked front door, where he left with car keys and the car. A Brooklyn Paper 61st Precinct report from January 2015 describes a burglar prying open the front door of an apartment on Coney Island Avenue near Avenue S at 10:45 in the morning. None of those entries needed a special tool or a quiet street. They needed a door nobody was watching.

Businesses have the same problem from a different angle. In June 2018 Brooklyn Paper reported a burglar who got into an Avenue U business through its cellar door, took $6,000 from payroll drawers in the basement and left through the back door onto East Ninth Street, at the western edge of the area. In June 2022 ABC7 reported police looking for one man connected with seven overnight burglaries of closed businesses in Bath Beach, Homecrest, Gravesend and Bensonhurst, with more than $1,700 in lottery tickets, $800 in cigarettes and more than $6,000 in cash taken. And at Community Board 15's April 2025 meeting the 61st Precinct's commanding officer reported 51 burglaries so far that year against 40 at the same point in 2024, up 27.5 percent, while robberies and overall complaints were down. The precinct covers far more than Homecrest, so we use that figure only as context, not as a Homecrest statistic.

Start at the Slider

How We Alarm a Homecrest Sliding Glass Door

A sliding door is two or more big panes in a frame, one panel fixed and one that rolls on a track, held shut by a hook latch on the edge of the moving panel. That latch is the whole lock on many older doors, and it engages a small keeper on the jamb. Crime-prevention pages from police departments make the same two points about it again and again: older sliders can be lifted up and off their track, which defeats the latch, and a dowel in the track, a fold-down bar or an anti-lift pin through both panels stops that. The NYPD's own burglary-prevention flyer, printed in English, Korean and Chinese, tells New Yorkers to secure terrace doors with properly installed locks and calls burglar alarms a good deterrent. An alarm's job at a slider is to report the moment the moving panel leaves its closed position, whether it slides, lifts or comes out of the frame altogether.

So the first decision is where the contact goes. On most aluminium and vinyl sliders we fit the magnet to the top rail of the moving panel and the switch to the head of the frame, close to the latch edge. That spot travels the moment the panel moves even an inch, it is out of reach of feet, vacuum cleaners and dogs, and it stays put when somebody leans on the glass. A contact low on the track, where many kits tell you to stick it, gets kicked, gets wet when rain drives against the threshold and fills with grit. Where the frame is deep enough we recess a concealed contact into the head jamb so nothing shows at all. Where the frame is steel or heavy aluminium we use a contact rated for that frame, because a steel frame can weaken or bend a small magnet's field and make the switch report wrongly.

The second decision is the glass. A contact only knows whether the panel has moved. If someone breaks the fixed pane, or the moving pane while it is locked, the contact says nothing. For a big rear slider we add a glass-break detector on the ceiling or the wall facing the door, inside the distance its manufacturer lists, and we test it with a simulator at the handover. Where the room behind the door is large and open, as it is in many renovated Homecrest kitchens, a motion detector placed on a side wall watches the space a person has to cross after the glass. We avoid aiming a motion detector straight at a slider: sunlight, warm air from the door and swaying blinds are all things a passive infrared detector can mistake for a person.

The third decision is how the door is used. Families want to open the slider a few inches on a summer night, let the dog out, or slide the screen across while the glass is open. Each of those has an answer, and they are in the questions further down: a second magnet set at a ventilation stop, a pin that locks the panel at that stop, a pet panel treated as an opening of its own, and a separate zone for a second moving panel. We also look at the lock. Some newer sliders have a keyed lock or a foot bolt; a closed door is not the same thing as a locked door, and where the hardware allows it we can report both.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Homecrest's Architecture

These are the building types the sources above describe in Homecrest. The descriptions of where wiring goes and which openings matter are our own working practice for each type.

Renovated two-story houses

Detached and semi-detached houses enlarged at the back or upward. The new rear is usually glass: a slider or folding doors off a larger kitchen. The old front is often unchanged.

One-story brick rows

Rows of single-story brick homes south of Avenue V with a community backyard and driveways behind them. Every opening is at ground level and the rear faces shared ground.

Bungalows

One-story houses on their own lots south of Avenue V, frequently with an attic or crawl space and a back door straight into the kitchen.

Mid-century elevator co-ops

Six-story buildings of 60-plus units from around 1960 on East 13th and East 14th Streets, with lobbies, live-in supers and in some cases a building security system and parking.

Pre-war walk-up co-ops

Smaller three-story apartment buildings from the 1930s, such as the 24-unit co-op on East 14th Street that Brick Underground listed, with ground-floor units close to the street and the yard.

New condos on Ocean Avenue

Eight-story buildings from 2006 and 2019 with terraces, balconies and duplex penthouses. The terrace slider and the entry door are the two openings that matter.

Two-story houses: wire the old part, think hard about the new part

In an older two-story house the basement ceiling is often open, so the front door, the side door and the first-floor windows can be wired up through the floor from below, which is cheaper over the life of the system than batteries. The renovated rear is a different story. A kitchen extension is usually finished, insulated and drywalled before anyone thinks of the alarm, and the slider's head jamb may sit under a steel or wood header with no clear path. Where the extension is still open, a cable to the slider head and a box for a glass-break detector take minutes. Where it is closed, the slider and its glass-break detector are wireless and everything else stays wired. The upstairs bedrooms rarely need contacts unless a porch roof, an extension roof or a deck puts a window within reach.

One-story rows and bungalows: every opening counts, so choose carefully

On a single-story house there is no upstairs to leave off the system, and no stairs to slow anyone down. We put contacts on every exterior door and on the windows a person can step through from the yard or the driveway, then use one or two motion detectors in the rooms that every route passes through, usually the hall between the bedrooms and the living room and the kitchen at the back. That keeps the number of window contacts sensible on a house with a dozen or more low windows. In a bungalow with an attic or crawl space, the attic hatch and any gable vent big enough to climb through get looked at, and usually dismissed, during the survey.

Co-ops: inside the apartment, nothing in the hallway

In the six-story co-ops the board controls the lobby, the hallways and the building's own security system, and an apartment alarm should live entirely inside the unit. A wireless panel, a contact on the entry door, contacts on any window reachable from a courtyard, a fire escape or a low roof, and one motion detector are typical. Nothing is drilled through the entry door frame from the hallway side, the siren is inside, and the communicator is cellular so the alarm does not depend on the building's wiring. We bring an insurance certificate for the managing agent when the board asks for one.

Condos: the terrace door and the corridor door

In the Ocean Avenue condos, the openings are few but specific. The entry door from the corridor gets a contact; the terrace or balcony slider gets a contact and, where the terrace adjoins another unit's or sits above a setback roof, glass-break as well. Developer-installed smart locks and video intercoms stay as they are; the alarm sits alongside them.

The Back of the Row

Community Backyards, Driveways and the Embankment

South of Avenue V, Wikipedia notes rows of one-story brick homes with community backyards and driveways. For an alarm, a community backyard changes one basic assumption. On most Brooklyn blocks the back of a house faces a fenced yard that only the household uses, so anyone in it is out of place. Behind a Homecrest row, the open ground behind the houses belongs to everyone in the row, or is used by everyone in the row, and neighbours, children, delivery people and the person taking out the recycling all have a reason to be there. An outdoor sensor that alerts on movement back there will alert all day.

So on these houses we draw the protected line at the house wall, not at the yard. The rear door and the slider get contacts, the windows a person could climb through get contacts, and a glass-break detector covers the large panes. Inside, a motion detector watches the kitchen or back room where every route from the yard ends up. If a household wants to know about the yard itself, a camera that records and lets you look is more useful than a detector that sounds, and a light on the back wall that comes on when a rear door opens at night does more than any outdoor alarm sensor would. We do not mount anything on a shared fence, a shared light pole or the far side of the yard without the written agreement of whoever owns or manages that ground.

Driveways behind the rows bring cars right up to the back of the houses. That matters for two practical reasons. A car parked at the back door blocks the view of it from the neighbours, and a garage or carport attached to the rear gives an extra door into the house. Where there is an interior door from a garage or a covered carport into the kitchen, it gets a contact of its own and is treated as an exterior door, even though it feels like an inside one.

The other unusual backdrop is the Brighton Line. The B and Q run on an embankment along East 16th Street, and the houses on the blocks either side have back walls and yards that face the railway rather than another row of houses. An embankment is a long, quiet strip with few windows looking at it, so we treat a yard that backs onto it the same way as one that backs onto an alley: contacts on every opening on that side, a glass-break detector on large panes, and motion detectors indoors. Passing trains shake some buildings near elevated or embankment lines, so we fit shock sensors on that side only after watching the frame through a few trains, and we prefer acoustic glass-break detectors that listen for the sound of breaking glass rather than relying on vibration alone.

What we fit on a one-story house that faces a community backyard

  • Rear door and slider: contacts, plus glass-break for the slider; the slider details follow the method in the section above
  • Low rear windows: contacts on any window a person can step through from the yard or the driveway
  • Interior: one motion detector covering the back room or kitchen, and one covering the hall that links the bedrooms to the living space
  • Garage or carport door into the house: contact, treated as an exterior door with its own entry delay
  • Yard: no outdoor alarm sensors; a recording camera and a back-wall light linked to the rear door instead, where the household wants them

Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Homecrest: Questions Answered

These are the questions that come up once a Homecrest owner has decided to protect the back of the house properly, along with the ones store owners on the avenues ask.

Sliding glass doors

Police believed a 2014 Homecrest burglar came in through a rear sliding door on East 13th Street near Avenue T while the family slept. Where should the sensor sit on a slider so it reports before the door is open wide enough to step through?

At the top of the moving panel, on the latch edge. A magnet on the top rail and a switch on the head of the frame separate as soon as the panel travels about an inch, which is long before anyone can fit through. That position also catches the door if the latch is forced, because forcing the latch still moves the panel.

We avoid the bottom track for the contact. It is where feet, mops and dogs hit, where driven rain collects and where grit builds up, and a contact there slowly drifts out of line. If the frame is deep enough, a recessed contact in the head jamb does the same job and is invisible once the door is closed.

Our older slider can be lifted right off its track even when it is latched. Will a contact notice if someone lifts the panel out instead of sliding it?

It will if it is placed for that. Lifting a panel raises its top rail into the head of the frame and then tips it outward, so a contact on the top rail at the latch edge separates during the lift. We test it at installation by lifting the panel as far as the track allows with the system in test mode.

The better fix is to stop the lift. Police crime-prevention guides recommend screws in the upper track or an anti-lift pin through both panels, and both cost very little. We fit them when asked, and we tell you if the rollers are so worn that the panel lifts easily, because that is a door problem rather than an alarm problem.

We like to leave the patio slider open a few inches on summer nights. Can the alarm stay armed with the door partly open?

Yes, with a second magnet. We fit one magnet for the closed position and another at a ventilation stop, usually a gap of three or four inches, so the switch sees the door as secure in either spot. Open it any further and the alarm trips.

The magnet only reports position; it does not hold the door. Pair it with a pin, a track stop or a bar set at the same gap, so the door cannot simply be pushed wider from outside, and tell everyone in the house that the vent position works only up to that mark.

Vertical blinds hang in front of our slider and the air conditioning makes them sway. Will a motion detector in that room keep going off?

It can, if the detector looks straight at them. A passive infrared detector reacts to warm or cool patches moving across its view, and blinds that swing in a draught, sun on the glass behind them and hot air blowing from a vent can all do that.

We mount the detector on a side wall so it looks across the room, not at the door, mask the part of the lens that sees the blinds if needed, and keep it away from the air conditioner's outlet. The slider itself is covered by its contact and the glass by a glass-break detector, so the motion detector does not need to see the door at all.

Both panels of our patio door slide, not just one. Does each panel need its own sensor?

Yes. On a door where both panels move, either one can be opened, and a contact on one tells you nothing about the other. Each panel gets a contact at its top rail, and we label them separately in the app as the left and right panels.

Some owners prefer to fix one panel permanently with a bar or screws so it behaves like a fixed pane. That is fine too; the panel that still moves gets the contact, and the glass-break detector covers both panes either way.

Our slider has a pet door panel fitted into the track for the dog. Is that insert a way in, and how do you cover it?

Often it is. A pet panel is a separate frame wedged into the track beside the moving door, and many are held only by pressure and a small clip, so the whole panel can be pushed out. The flap itself may also be large enough for a small person or a child to reach through to the latch.

We treat the insert as its own opening with its own contact, so the alarm reports if the panel moves, and the moving door keeps its usual contact. A motion detector inside must be pet-tolerant and mounted for the dog's size, which we set at the survey after meeting the dog.

Can the alarm tell us the slider is shut but its lock is not engaged?

Sometimes. A door contact reports open or closed, not locked. On sliders with a keyed lock or a foot bolt we can add a small switch that reports the bolt position, so the app can show "closed, unlocked" before you arm. Whether that is possible depends on the lock's design and whether there is room in the frame.

Where it is not possible, the practical answer is habit plus hardware: a bar or pin that you drop in every night, which the alarm does not need to see, and a contact that catches the door the moment it moves if the bar was forgotten.

Shared ground behind the house

The community backyard behind our one-story row belongs to all the houses together. Can we put a sensor or a light out there, and who has to agree?

Anything mounted on your own back wall is your decision. Anything mounted on a shared fence, a pole, a neighbour's wall or the yard itself needs the agreement of whoever owns or manages that ground, which may be every owner in the row or an association, depending on the deeds. We ask to see that agreement in writing before we mount anything beyond your wall.

In practice most households do not need anything out there. A light on your own wall that comes on when the rear door opens at night, and a camera on your wall that records the yard, give you more than an outdoor detector would on ground your neighbours use all day.

Our yard backs onto the Brighton Line embankment along East 16th Street instead of another row of houses. Does that change anything?

It changes which side is unwatched. A yard facing the railway has few windows looking at it, so we treat every opening on that side as a priority, the same as a house on an alley. Contacts go on each door and reachable window there, and large panes get glass-break.

We also check for vibration. We watch the frames on that side through a few trains before choosing sensors, prefer acoustic glass-break detectors over shock sensors on that wall, and set the panel to report any sensor that trips repeatedly so a nuisance problem gets fixed rather than ignored.

Condos on Ocean Avenue

Our condo terrace is divided from the next unit's terrace by a low partition. Does that make our terrace slider an entry point?

Yes, treat it as one. If an adult can step over or around the partition, your terrace is reachable from the neighbouring unit and from anyone who gets onto that terrace, whatever floor you are on. The terrace slider gets a top-rail contact and the glass gets a glass-break detector.

Two further checks: whether a setback roof or a balcony below gives a second way onto the terrace, and whether the condo's rules let you change the slider hardware, for example to add an anti-lift pin. We ask the managing agent for the rules before we drill anything in the frame.

Stores on Avenue U, Kings Highway and Coney Island Avenue

Our store sells lottery tickets and cigarettes from behind the counter. Where should detection be densest after closing?

Behind the counter and on the way to it. The 2022 overnight burglar that police linked to closed businesses in Homecrest and nearby areas left with lottery tickets, cigarettes and cash, which is what sits behind a counter. We put a motion detector covering the area behind the counter, a contact on any cabinet or drawer that holds stock or cash, and glass-break covering the front glass, so an intruder is detected on the way in and again at the counter.

The panel and the communicator go somewhere an intruder will not find in the first minute, with a tamper switch on the cabinet, and the system reports over cellular so cutting an outside phone or internet line does not silence it.

Our shop and the one next door share one cellar, split only by a wood partition. How is our half protected?

As if the partition were not there. A wood or wire partition will not stop someone who gets into the neighbour's cellar, so our side of it is an exterior wall for alarm purposes. A motion detector covers our half of the cellar, positioned to see anyone coming through or over the partition, and the cellar stairs or the door at the top get a contact.

If your landlord or neighbour will agree, the better fix is a solid partition with a locked door. We also suggest keeping cash out of a shared cellar altogether; the 2018 Avenue U burglary began at a cellar door and ended at the basement payroll drawers.

People wait at the bus stop right outside our front window at all hours. Will a motion detector inside see them through the glass and set off the alarm?

A passive infrared detector does not see through ordinary glass in the way a camera does, but it can be fooled by sunlight, headlights and heat on the window itself, and a cheap detector aimed at the storefront is a common cause of false alarms. We aim interior detectors away from the front window and toward the counter and the back of the shop.

The front glass is covered instead by a glass-break detector that listens for breaking glass, and the door by a contact. That way the people outside, the buses and the street lights have nothing to trip.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Homecrest

We could not open Google's AI Overview, People Also Ask boxes or related searches from the environment this page was researched in, so nothing below is quoted from them. These are claims homeowners bring to us after asking chatbots or reading national cost guides from Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, checked against Homecrest's houses and the local record.

"Burglars come in through the front door."

Some do. The 2014 Homecrest list included an unlocked front door, and a 2015 report describes a front door pried open on Coney Island Avenue. But the same 2014 list included a rear sliding door and a side window, and the 2018 Avenue U burglary started at a cellar door.

The lesson is not that the front does not matter. It is that an alarm planned around the front door alone, which is how many starter kits are sold, leaves the doors that face the yard, the side and the cellar uncovered.

On a Homecrest house we count openings starting at the back. The front door always gets covered; it is simply not where the plan begins.

"One sensor on the sliding door is enough."

One contact tells you the panel moved. It cannot tell you the glass broke, it cannot tell you a second moving panel opened, and if it is stuck low on the track it may not survive a summer of feet and mops.

A large rear slider usually needs a contact on the top rail, a glass-break detector, and a motion detector covering the room behind it. A pet panel or a double slider adds another contact.

That is more than one device, but it is still a small part of a typical system and it covers the opening that most renovated Homecrest houses added at the back.

"Outdoor motion sensors give you the earliest warning."

On a private, fenced yard they can. Behind a Homecrest row with a community backyard, the yard is shared ground where neighbours and their children are entitled to be, and an outdoor sensor would alert all day.

Earliest useful warning on that kind of house comes from the house wall: contacts and glass-break on the rear openings, with a light and a camera on your own wall for the yard.

We would rather a household trusted every alert than learned to ignore them.

"A typical home alarm costs the national average."

National averages mix rural houses, suburban houses and apartments across the country, with different labour costs and different houses. They are not a useful estimate for a Brooklyn house with a basement you can wire through and a finished extension you cannot.

Our Brooklyn figures are published: a basic wireless system from $600, a hybrid system for a full house from $2,500 to $5,000, a small business system from $1,500 to $3,000. Homecrest pays the Brooklyn base rate.

The number of sliders and glass doors, and whether the extension is open or finished, move a Homecrest quote more than anything else.

"Apartments in buildings with a doorman or a super do not need alarms."

A building's lobby controls who comes in the front. It does not watch an apartment's terrace slider, a ground-floor window onto a courtyard or a unit door while the owner is away for weeks.

The Brick Underground listings for Homecrest's mid-century co-ops mention live-in supers and building security systems; those protect the building's common areas, not the inside of each unit.

A small wireless system inside the unit fills that gap without touching the building's equipment, and it stays with the owner if they move.

"Alarms need a landline to call the monitoring station."

That was true of older panels, and some of them are still on Homecrest walls dialling a line that has been disconnected. They show a trouble message and report nothing.

Current systems report over a cellular communicator, often with internet as backup. Nothing outside the house has to be cut for the alarm to fail, which matters for stores that close for the night.

On every job we send a test signal and wait for the monitoring station to confirm it, so you know the alarm actually reaches someone.

"You need a city permit before an alarm goes in."

We have looked for a New York City permit or registration requirement for private burglar alarms and have not found one, so we do not charge or quote a permit fee. The rule we do program to is the fifteen-minute siren limit in Administrative Code § 24-238.

What New York State does require is that the installer be licensed by the Department of State. Ask any company for its license number before it starts work; ours is #12000287431.

Fire alarm systems for restaurants and larger buildings are a different matter with their own code requirements, which is why we keep them on a separate page and a separate design.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Lift the Door Before You Trust It

This is a note on method, not a story about a particular customer. The last ten minutes at any slider are the most useful of the job. With the system in test mode and someone watching the keypad, we slide the moving panel one inch and confirm the zone opens. We close it, lock it and then try to lift it, hands flat on the glass, as far as the track allows. If the zone opens during the lift, the contact is placed right. If the panel lifts and the zone stays closed, the contact moves.

Then the vent stop, if there is one: open to the mark, confirm the zone stays secure, open another inch, confirm it trips. Then the glass: a glass-break simulator held at the far edge of the largest pane, aimed back at the detector, has to trigger it. Then the room: someone walks in from the slider along the route a person would take after breaking the glass, and the motion detector has to see them before they reach the hall.

The point of doing it in that order is that each test answers a different way in. A slider that passes all four tests is covered; one that passes only the first is the kind most kits leave behind. Every result goes on the handover sheet next to the device it belongs to.

Homecrest Alarm Installation FAQ

What does an alarm for a Homecrest one-story brick house with a rear patio slider cost on your Brooklyn list?
+

A small one-story house with one slider, a front and a back door and a few reachable windows usually fits the basic residential system from $600, with glass-break for the slider added inside that quote. A larger one with several sliders, a garage door into the house and full window coverage moves toward the complete home package from $2,500. NYC sales tax of 8.875% is added, and the price is fixed in writing after a walkthrough.

Can you fit the sensors on a new slider the same week the glass company installs it?
+

Yes, and that is the cheapest time to do it. If we know the frame model in advance, we can run a cable to the head of the opening before the door goes in and fit a recessed contact as soon as the frame is set, so nothing shows. Give the glass company our number so the two crews can agree on the order.

We lift the slider out every spring to clean the track and change the rollers. Will the contact still line up afterwards?
+

Usually, because the switch stays on the frame and the magnet stays on the panel. But new rollers can raise or lower the panel slightly, so after any roller change put the system in test mode and open the door an inch to check the zone reports. If it does not, the magnet needs nudging; that is a short service call or, under the parts warranty for AESS-supplied devices, a quick check.

We added security film to the slider glass. Does that change what the glass-break detector hears?
+

It can. Film holds the broken glass together, which changes the sound of a break and can make some detectors less sensitive to it. We check the detector's guidance on filmed or laminated glass, test with a simulator at the far edge of the pane, and move the detector closer or add a second one if the test is marginal.

Do magnetic contacts work on a steel or heavy aluminium slider frame?
+

Ordinary small contacts can misbehave on steel, because the frame draws the magnet's field away from the switch. We use contacts made for steel frames, which have stronger magnets and spacers, and we check the gap at which each one reports before we leave. Heavy aluminium frames are less of a problem but can still weaken a wireless contact's signal, which we read on the panel.

Do you also fit the anti-lift pin or the security bar on the slider, or only the alarm parts?
+

We can fit simple hardware such as an anti-lift pin, top-track screws or a fold-down bar while we are there, priced on the written quote. We do not replace rollers, latches or the door itself; if the door needs that, we say so and leave it to a glass and door company.

Will a glass-break detector in the kitchen go off when someone drops a plate?
+

A good acoustic detector listens for the pattern of breaking window glass, a low thud followed by a high-frequency crash, rather than any loud noise, so a dropped plate rarely sets it off. We still place it away from the sink and the dishwasher where possible, and we set its sensitivity to the size of the room rather than leaving it at maximum.

Our slider faces west and the frame gets very hot in summer. Will stick-on sensors fall off?
+

Cheap adhesive pads can soften and slip on a frame that bakes in the afternoon sun. On sunny sliders we fix contacts with small screws into the frame or recess them into the head jamb, and use adhesive only where screws are not allowed, with a pad rated for high temperatures.

Can the slider sensor be hidden so nothing shows when the door is closed?
+

Often, yes. If the head of the frame is deep enough, we drill a small hole for a recessed switch in the head jamb and a matching magnet in the top rail of the moving panel. Once the door is closed neither part is visible. Shallow or hollow vinyl frames sometimes rule it out, in which case a slim surface contact painted or chosen to match the frame is the fallback.

Do you cover the Homecrest blocks south of Avenue U down to Avenue X, or only near Kings Highway?
+

All of it. Sources disagree on where Homecrest ends, from Avenue U to Avenue X in the south and from Ocean Parkway to Coney Island Avenue in the west, so we treat the whole stretch from Kings Highway to Avenue X between Ocean Parkway and Ocean Avenue as our Homecrest area, all at the Brooklyn base rate.
