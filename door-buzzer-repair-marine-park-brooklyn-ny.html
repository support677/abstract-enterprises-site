The Neighborhood

Why Marine Park Properties Need Door Buzzer Repair

Marine Park is a neighborhood of front doors that each serve one or two households, and the buzzer trouble here looks very different from the lobby failures that fill the city's complaint files elsewhere in Brooklyn. When we filtered the city's land-use records to the blocks Wikipedia gives as the neighborhood's rough square, Gerritsen Avenue, Flatbush Avenue, Avenue U and Kings Highway, we counted 6,050 residential lots holding about 8,580 homes. Of those lots, 4,232 are one-family houses and 1,470 are two-families. Only 27 lots are apartment buildings of the kinds most door buzzer pages are written about, and together they hold 711 homes, a little over eight percent of the total. The average year built for every house class falls between 1925 and 1940.

That stock tells a technician what to expect before the van is parked. Brick Underground's 2022 neighborhood profile describes Marine Park's housing as mostly one- and two-family detached and semi-detached houses from the 1910s to the 1940s, with co-ops along the southern and western borders, a few small low-rise condos in the middle, and no rental apartment buildings at all: renters live in two-family houses or above shops in low-rise buildings. For a door, that means the "system" is usually a push button by the front door, a few feet of thin bell wire, a chime in a hall or kitchen and a small transformer in the basement that steps house power down to a low voltage. In a two-family there may also be a second button, a second chime upstairs, and an electric strike that the upstairs household releases from a button on its own floor.

Those small systems fail for ordinary reasons, and nearly all of them are repairable without opening walls: a corroded button, a loose or broken wire at the button, chime or transformer, a transformer that has burned out, or a chime damaged by a surge. HomeTips puts the most alarming symptom simply: if a bell "keeps ringing or buzzing continuously, the button is likely stuck or the wiring is shorted." In a house built in the 1930s, the wire behind the button may be the original, and the transformer may be the third or fourth one somebody has fitted.

The biggest change in Marine Park's front doors over the last decade has not been age but upgrades. Owners have replaced plain buttons with wired video doorbells, and those draw more power than the old bell circuit was designed for. Mark C. Ode, writing in EC&M in August 2025, described exactly this: an original transformer that stepped 120 volts down to 16 volts had burned out, and the fix was a 24-volt transformer and a 24-volt chime to match the new doorbell. He also warned that long or undersized runs cause voltage drop, and that many doorbell failures trace back to "a transformer never being installed or a chime never being connected." In a two-family, the same small transformer may be feeding a video doorbell, two chimes and a strike, which is more than it was ever sized for. That is the most common repair we expect to find here, and it is why we measure before we replace anything.

The apartment buildings on the edges are a different job, and they are where the city's records actually sit. On the Marine Park side of the boundary, HPD has written 21 orders mentioning an intercom, buzzer or door bell in 23 years, and 311 has logged eight bell, buzzer and intercom complaints since 2018. Most of those are on Flatlands Avenue, Nostrand Avenue and a few streets near Kings Highway. We cover both: the bell in a 1931 brick Tudor on Avenue T and the lobby panel in a six-story building on Kings Highway. Many owners pair a buzzer repair with a camera or intercom upgrade at the same door; if that is your plan, say so when you book, so the repair doesn't have to be undone a month later.

The City Record

21 Orders in 23 Years: What the City's Buzzer File Shows About Marine Park

Before we quote a rental building, we read what the city already knows about its entrance. Two public files matter. HPD's violation file records every order an inspector wrote against an owner, with the wording of the defect and the apartment it names. The 311 file records every complaint a resident filed about a bell, buzzer or intercom, with the date and how it was closed. For this page we searched both for ZIPs 11229 and 11234 in October 2026 and kept only the records whose map points fall inside the Kings Highway, Flatbush Avenue, Avenue U and Gerritsen Avenue square. That filter matters, because both ZIPs reach far beyond Marine Park: the two ZIPs together hold 422 HPD orders and 247 complaints of this kind, almost all of them in Sheepshead Bay, Madison, Gerritsen Beach, Flatlands and Mill Basin.

Inside the square, the HPD file holds 39 orders at 23 addresses since 2003. Eighteen of them are on the west side of Nostrand Avenue, at even-numbered buildings that the city counts in Community Board 15 and that most people would call Madison, so we leave them out here. That leaves 21 orders on the Marine Park side in 23 years. Six are at the store-and-apartment buildings at 2951 and 2953 Nostrand Avenue, on the east side. Four are spread across the Flatlands Avenue walk-ups at 3702, 3718, 3820 and 3824. Two are at 1544 East 34th Street, one at 1487 East 37th Street, a 41-unit building from 1925, and one at 3821 Kings Highway. The rest are single orders at houses with one to three homes: three on Ryder Street and one each on Hendrickson Street, East 32nd Street, East 37th Street, East 38th Street and Quentin Road. In no year did the area get more than four.

The 311 file tells the same story in fewer lines. Fifteen complaints fall inside the square since 2018, and seven of those come from the west-side Nostrand buildings again. The eight on the Marine Park side are at 1544 and 1564 East 34th Street, 1487 East 37th Street, 3820 and 3842 Flatlands Avenue, 4106 Avenue T and one Avenue M address whose map point looks doubtful. For comparison, ZIP 11234 as a whole has logged between 11 and 16 such complaints a year since 2020, which in our count ranked second lowest of Brooklyn's 37 ZIPs since 2022.

Why a quiet file doesn't mean quiet doors

It would be easy to read those numbers as proof that Marine Park's doors never fail. They don't prove that. The city's intercom rules are written with apartment buildings in mind, and HPD inspects when a tenant complains. An owner-occupied one-family house has no tenant to complain, and most two-family owners fix their own bells or call someone directly. A dead doorbell in a semi-detached house on East 35th Street will never appear in either file. What the record does show is where a building's tenants have had to ask the city for help, and in Marine Park those buildings sit almost entirely on the edges: the Flatlands Avenue row, the Nostrand Avenue store buildings and a few streets running up to Kings Highway.

For an owner of one of those edge buildings, read the order before booking: its wording often names the exact apartment and function, such as the "return buzzer" that releases the street door, and the repair has to fix what the order describes. For a homeowner, the useful history is what you remember: when the bell last worked, whether the doorbell has been changed, and whether the basement has been renovated or has taken water.

What the orders on the Marine Park side ask for

HPD writes buzzer orders under section 27-2005 of the Housing Maintenance Code, the owner's general duty to keep the building in good repair, and its standard wording reads along the lines of: properly repair or replace the broken or defective bell, buzzer or intercom system from the building entrance door to the apartment. Thirty of the 39 orders in the square are class B and nine are class A. None of that tells us which part failed, which is why the first visit on an order is a test of every function at the named apartment and its neighbors on the same wire, not a part swapped on the strength of the order's wording.

The Edge Buildings

Kings Highway Lobbies, the Flatlands Avenue Row and Nostrand Avenue Store Buildings

All 40 lots in Marine Park with four or more homes sit on an edge street or in the garden courts, and each edge has its own kind of building. A technician who knows which one he is walking into brings different parts. These are the groups, from the land-use records, with what each tends to need.

The elevator buildings near Kings Highway and Flatbush Avenue

At the northeastern corner, where Kings Highway meets Flatbush Avenue, three elevator buildings stand close together: 3715 Kings Highway with 66 apartments, built in 1938; 3626 Kings Highway with 72, built in 1939; and 3502 Kings Highway with 84 on six floors, built in 1950. Nearby, 1487 East 37th Street holds 41 apartments in a 1925 building. These are the only lobby-and-riser systems in the neighborhood: a panel in the vestibule, a supply in the cellar, pairs running up each line of apartments, and a strike on a heavy lobby door. All four predate the 1968 line in the state's Multiple Dwelling Law, which matters less than owners sometimes hope. The NY Attorney General's Tenants' Rights Guide says older buildings must provide self-locking doors and a two-way intercom if a majority of apartments request them, and Habitat Magazine's February 2024 summary of the law notes that the Housing Maintenance Code requires an intercom that exists to function, and that courts have treated intercoms as an essential service under the warranty of habitability. As Nancy Kourland of Lasser Law Group put it there, "As a result, the co-op has a legal obligation to repair or replace the intercom."

The cable in these risers usually holds up. Daniel Vasquez of Paragon Security and Locksmith told Brick Underground in 2019 that "It's all in the wire connections," and in buildings of this age the cellar terminals, the lobby panel and the back of each apartment station are where faults collect. We test calling, talking and release separately on each line before opening a junction.

The six-family walk-up row on Flatlands Avenue

Between about 3702 and 3724 Flatlands Avenue, near the Kings Highway end, stands a row of six-unit walk-ups built between 1928 and 1930, some with stores at street level. They are the nearest thing Marine Park has to a classic Brooklyn walk-up block, and they account for four of the 21 HPD orders on the Marine Park side, with 311 complaints at 3820 and 3842 further along the avenue. A building of this size has a short plate of six buttons, a strike on the street door, a transformer or small supply in the cellar and a pair to each apartment. When several near-identical buildings stand side by side, it is common for an owner to own more than one, for cellars to connect, and for repairs over the years to have been done by different people in different ways. We never assume one building's wiring matches its neighbor's, even when the facades are the same.

The faults that matter most on a busy avenue are mechanical. A street door that faces Flatlands Avenue traffic is pushed and pulled all day by residents, delivery drivers and store customers in mixed-use buildings, and a worn strike, a loose keeper or a tired closer will leave the door unlocked long before the electronics fail. HPD treats a defective self-closing door as a class C immediately hazardous violation that has to be corrected within 14 days of the notice, which is a different clock from the intercom order. If both are open at the same entrance, the door is usually the first job.

Nostrand Avenue, east side

The Nostrand Avenue frontage is split between two neighborhoods by the street itself. The even-numbered west side is counted with Madison, and its larger elevator buildings carry most of the HPD and 311 records in the area. On the east side, the Marine Park side, the buildings are smaller: store-and-apartment buildings from 1927 at 2953 to 2959, which carry six of the Marine Park side's HPD orders, and a 19-unit building at 2881 Nostrand Avenue completed in 2016. Those two ages need very different repairs. The 1927 buildings have a residents' door beside a storefront, a short button plate and wiring that may share a cellar, and sometimes a transformer, with the store below. A 2016 building is more likely to have a video panel, a programmed directory and a controller or network connection, where a failure can be a lost login or a changed router rather than a broken wire.

Where a store and the apartments above share a cellar, settle access first: if the residents' transformer is behind the store's lock, the visit is booked when someone with that key is there, and our record says which equipment serves the apartments.

The Garden Courts

Two Stories, Many Doors: Buzzer Repair in Marine Park's 1950 Garden Apartments

The one building type in Marine Park that appears nowhere else on our Brooklyn repair pages is the two-story garden apartment. The land-use records list three lots of them near Avenue R and Fillmore Avenue, all built in 1950: 2801 Fillmore Avenue with 52 apartments, 3001 Avenue R with eight, and 3004 Avenue R with 84, which the city places on the Community Board 15 side of the line. Property listings for 3001 Avenue R describe it as two buildings with two floors, and 2995 Avenue R, on the same block between Stuart and Burnett streets, as an eight-unit, two-story post-war building. The class name the city uses for all of them is simply "garden apartments."

What makes these buildings different for a buzzer technician is the number of front doors. A walk-up has one street door for every apartment in the building. A garden-apartment group usually has several small entrances, each serving a handful of apartments, set around lawns and paths rather than on the sidewalk. Each entrance may have its own button plate, its own strike and its own pair of wires, and they may all run back to one transformer or supply in one basement, or each building may have its own. When one entrance goes quiet, the first question is whether the fault is at that entrance, in the wire that connects it to the shared equipment, or in the shared equipment itself, which would usually take more than one entrance down at once.

The record is reassuring: none of the three garden-apartment lots appears in HPD's intercom file or in 311's buzzer complaints. That is consistent with buildings where each entrance serves only a few households, who notice quickly when their bell stops and are close enough to the owner or managing agent to say so. It also means there is no history to read before a visit, so the description you give us matters more. Tell us which entrance fails, which apartments behind it are affected, whether neighboring entrances still work, and whether anything has been dug, paved or replanted along the paths between the buildings, because wire between buildings in a group like this often runs underground or along a basement wall, and outdoor work is the usual reason it is cut.

The House Bell

Basement Transformers, Rear-Yard Doors and Two-Family Releases in Marine Park's Semi-Detached Rows

Most Marine Park buzzer calls come from the house that Brownstoner described in February 2022 when it wrote up 3615 Avenue T: a 1931 brick Tudor in a semi-detached row, with planting beds at the front and a basement reached by a staircase from the kitchen or by an entrance from the rear yard. Its neighbors include the 1,394 attached and semi-detached one-family houses and the 1,470 two-families on the land-use records. A house like that has a front door on the street, a back or basement door off the yard or a side path, and a bell circuit that was laid out for the front door alone. Everything a family has added since, a back-door button, a second chime, an upstairs release, a video doorbell, has been hung on that one circuit.

Where the transformer usually is

EC&M's August 2025 article gives the general rule: where the electrical panel is inside the house, the doorbell transformer is usually nearby, and otherwise "the transformer could be located in the basement, attic or somewhere else out of sight and not in a wet location." In a Marine Park house the basement is the usual place, often screwed to a joist or to the side of a junction box near the panel. In a basement that has been finished, the transformer may now be above a dropped ceiling, behind a panel or inside a closet, and the first part of the visit is finding it. If the basement has ever taken water, and residents on Reddit's r/Brooklyn have called the area "one of the biggest floodplains of Brooklyn," we look at the transformer's terminals and the wire splices nearest the floor for corrosion before measuring anything.

The two-family release

In a two-family, the front door often serves both households, and the upstairs household wants to open it without walking down. That is done with an electric strike in the front door frame and a release button on the upper floor, usually on the same transformer as the bells. When the release stops working, the cause is usually one of four things: a strike that is out of alignment with the latch, so it hums but doesn't let go; a transformer that can no longer supply both the bells and the strike; a broken wire somewhere between the second floor and the basement; or a worn release button. We test each in that order, because it is also the order of cost. A strike that only needs its keeper adjusted is a short job, a new transformer is a small one, and tracing a broken wire through occupied rooms in a two-family is the largest.

Back doors, side paths and garages

Many Marine Park houses have a garage at the back of a driveway or yard, and the family's daily door is often the side or rear door rather than the front. Bells at those doors were usually added later, by whoever was handy at the time, with wire stapled along a fence, a garage wall or a basement window frame. That outdoor wire is the most common place for a fault in the whole house, because it is exposed to sun, frost, gardening and dogs. We follow it from the button to the chime, replace the weathered sections rather than splicing them again, and route the new wire where it is protected.

How the building type changes the repair

Brick houses. In a brick Tudor the bell wire usually runs through the front wall at the door frame and drops straight into the basement, so most of its length is reachable from below. Where the button sits in masonry, we avoid drilling new holes in the brick; a corroded button is replaced in its existing opening, and a new wire is fished along the old one where it can be. If a previous owner surface-mounted a video doorbell on the brick with its wire run along the outside of the frame, we look at whether that wire can be brought inside before replacing it like for like.

Frame houses. In a frame house that has been re-sided, the button is often screwed to the siding rather than to the sheathing behind it, and it works loose as the siding moves with the temperature. The wire behind it may also have been cut and spliced when the siding went on. We mount the button on a block fixed to something solid, replace the spliced section, and seal the opening, because water running down siding into a bell box is a common cause of a bell that fails after rain.

Two- and three-family houses. In a multi-household house the key question is how many devices share one transformer. A transformer chosen decades ago for one bell may now be feeding two or three chimes, a strike and one or more video doorbells. Rules and practical limits at business and apartment doors

  • Apartment entrances. The NY Attorney General's Tenants' Rights Guide says multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968 must have self-closing, self-locking doors at all entrances, and those with eight or more apartments must also have a two-way voice intercom that can buzz the door open. Older buildings must provide self-locking doors and a two-way intercom if a majority of the apartments request them. Most of Marine Park's edge buildings were built before 1968.
  • Self-closing doors. HPD treats a defective self-closing door as a class C immediately hazardous violation and gives the owner 14 days from the notice to correct it. A buzzer repair at an apartment entrance is never finished while the door itself doesn't close and latch.
  • Line voltage. The Department of Buildings requires a licensed electrician, and a permit where the work calls for one, for work on the 120-volt side of a transformer or supply. Our work is on the low-voltage side; where a new circuit or a relocated transformer needs line-voltage work, the electrician's part is named separately in the quote.
  • Locking hardware and power loss. Allegion's guide to locking hardware explains that a fail-safe device unlocks when it loses power and a fail-secure device stays locked, and that with a fail-secure strike people can still leave by the lever. We confirm which kind a door has before replacing it, because swapping one for the other changes what the door does in an outage.
  • Magnetic locks. Where a store or office door uses a magnetic lock rather than a strike, the building code ties it to a way of releasing it for people leaving, such as a sensor and a push-to-exit button. We test those releases whenever we touch a maglock door.
  • Reach. The 2010 ADA Standards set 48 inches as the maximum high forward reach for operable parts, which is the height we work to when a button or release is moved at a business entrance.

Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Repair in Marine Park: Questions Answered

Houses and two-families

Both households in our two-family put wired video doorbells at the shared front door, and since then neither chime rings reliably and the upstairs release is weak. Can one old bell transformer run two video doorbells, two chimes and the strike?

Often it can't. A bell transformer has a rated output printed on its label, and a video doorbell draws power all the time, not only when someone presses it. Two of them, plus two chimes and a strike pulling current at the same moment, can easily exceed what a transformer chosen for one plain bell can supply. When that happens the voltage sags, the doorbells restart, the chimes go quiet and the strike barely moves. We add up what is on the circuit, measure the transformer under load, and replace it with one sized for the whole load, or split the circuit so each household's devices have their own supply. Either way the price is written down before we start.

We replaced the old button on our semi-detached house with a video doorbell, and now the mechanical chime in the hall only makes a faint click. Did the new doorbell damage the chime?

Usually not. A faint click means the chime is getting some current but not enough to swing its striker cleanly, which happens when the doorbell passes only part of the power through, when the transformer's voltage is too low for the new device, or when the chime itself is an older low-voltage model. EC&M's 2025 article on exactly this situation describes a 16-volt chime replaced with a 24-volt chime to match a new doorbell, and notes that some video doorbells use a small module at the chime to keep the camera powered. We measure at the chime, check what the doorbell's maker requires, and fix the mismatch, which may be the transformer, the chime, the module or a setting in the doorbell's app.

The bell at our garage door is wired along the back fence, and every winter it stops working and comes back in spring. Is something freezing?

Something is moving. Bell wire stapled along a wooden fence expands and contracts with the season, and the fence itself shifts, so a splice or a cracked insulation spot that just touches in summer opens in cold weather. Water that gets into a splice and freezes makes it worse. The fix is to replace the outdoor run with wire rated for outdoor use, with no splices along the fence, protected where it crosses the yard, and to seal the button at the garage. That is cheaper and longer-lasting than finding the one bad spot each winter.

We're converting our one-family into a legal two-family. The new upstairs apartment needs its own bell and a way to open the front door. Can it share the house's existing transformer?

It can if the transformer is big enough for what you're adding, and many aren't. A second chime and a strike are a modest load; a strike and a video doorbell for the new apartment are a larger one. We look at the transformer you have, the length of the run to the upper floor and where the new release button will go, and tell you whether to keep it or replace it. If a new transformer needs a new line-voltage connection, that part is an electrician's job under the Department of Buildings' rules, and we name it separately. It is easiest to run the new wire while the walls are open for the conversion.

Our semi-detached house and the house we share a wall with lost their doorbells on the same afternoon. Could one transformer be feeding both houses?

It's unusual, but it does happen when a single owner once held both halves of a pair, or when a previous owner borrowed power from next door rather than fit a second transformer. More often, two bells failing together point to something the houses share: a storm, a surge or a power interruption on the block, or work by a contractor along the party wall or the shared front walk. We start by measuring the transformer in your basement. If there isn't one, or its wire disappears through the party wall, we will tell you, and neither household's repair should be done without the other knowing.

Garden courts and apartment buildings

Our garden-apartment buildings were built in 1950 with doorbells only, no voice intercom. Does the city now require us to add intercoms at every entrance?

Not automatically. The state's rule for a two-way voice intercom applies to multiple dwellings of eight or more apartments built or converted after January 1, 1968. For older buildings, the Attorney General's Tenants' Rights Guide says self-locking doors and a two-way intercom must be provided if a majority of the apartments request them, and the landlord may recover the cost. What the owner of a 1950 building does have to do is keep whatever system the building has in working order. If the bells and releases you have work properly, that is the repair; if tenants ask for voice, we can price that as new work.

One transformer in a basement seems to feed every entrance in our garden court. If it fails, does every door go dead, and should each entrance have its own?

If one transformer feeds every entrance, then yes, a failure takes them all down together, and so can a short in any one entrance's wiring. That is a reason to check the transformer's capacity and the condition of each branch, not necessarily to split the system. Separate supplies make sense where entrances are far apart, where the runs between them are long and lose voltage, or where one entrance keeps failing and pulling the others with it. We test every entrance, measure the shared transformer at rest and with several releases held, and recommend splitting only where the numbers support it.

Our 1920s apartment building near Kings Highway has an HPD order for the intercom at one apartment and a separate 311 complaint from another. Can one visit deal with both?

The closer on our Flatlands Avenue walk-up's street door slams it so hard that the electric strike has cracked twice. Should we buy a heavier strike?

A heavier strike may help, but the closer is the problem. A closer set too fast, or with its latching speed adjusted so the door accelerates in its last few inches, drives the latch into the strike every time the door shuts, and on an avenue door that opens hundreds of times a day, the strike loses. We adjust or replace the closer so the door closes firmly but under control, check that the latch lines up with the strike's keeper, and replace the cracked strike with one suited to the door's frame and use. The door still has to close and lock by itself; the point is that it does so without destroying the hardware.

What AI Gets Wrong About Door Buzzer Repair in Marine Park

AI summaries, national cost sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, and vendor blogs are where many people start. Their general claims are often right somewhere, but several of them point a Marine Park owner the wrong way. These are the ones we hear most, with what we find at the door.

"Door buzzer repair means fixing an apartment intercom."

Most of what is written about buzzer repair in New York is about lobby intercoms in apartment buildings, because that is where tenants complain and where the city's rules apply. In Marine Park that is the exception. Seven in ten residential lots are one-family houses and nearly a quarter are two-families.

The repair in those houses is a bell circuit: a button, a chime, a transformer and, in a two-family, a strike and a release. The parts are smaller and cheaper, and the diagnosis is different, because a house circuit is often a patchwork of devices added by different owners.

If you search for help with a house bell and find advice about risers, amplifiers and HPD orders, it is not wrong, just aimed at a different building. Tell us what kind of house you have and we will treat it as what it is.

"If the doorbell stopped, replace the button."

Buttons do fail, especially where they face the weather, and a stuck or corroded button is a common cause. But Reolink's 2025 guide lists half a dozen causes, from loose wires to a dead transformer, and EC&M's Mark Ode found that many failures go back to a transformer that was never installed or a chime that was never connected.

Swapping the button first is a guess. If the transformer has burned out, a new button changes nothing, and if a video doorbell is drawing more than the transformer can give, a new button may make things worse.

We measure the transformer, then the voltage at the button and the chime, before replacing anything, which turns a guess into a known fault.

"Any video doorbell works on existing doorbell wiring."

Many are designed to, but "existing wiring" covers a lot of ground in a house from the 1930s. Ode describes the usual mismatch: an original 16-volt transformer and chime, a newer doorbell that wants 24 volts, and voltage drop on long or thin wire.

In a two-family, the same transformer may already be running two chimes and a strike. Add a video doorbell for each household and the circuit is asked to do several times what it was built for.

The doorbell's box may say it works with existing wiring, but whether it works on yours depends on the transformer's rating and the wire's length and condition. We check both before and after the doorbell goes up.

"Intercom repair in New York costs about $150 to $400."

Swiftlane's 2026 guide says minor apartment intercom repairs typically run $150 to $400, and HomeAdvisor gives a national average of about $2,500 for installing an intercom. Neither figure is a Marine Park price for anything in particular.

Our Brooklyn list starts with a $250 service call that includes diagnosis. A bell transformer replacement is $325 to $650, a strike with alignment $450 to $950, and a riser repair in one of the edge buildings $850 to $2,400.

The only number that means something is the one written down after someone has found the failed part. We give it before the repair starts, and if a fix turns out to need an electrician or a locksmith, their part is named separately.

"Every building must have a working intercom."

The rule for a two-way voice intercom applies to multiple dwellings of eight or more apartments built or converted after 1968. Most of Marine Park's edge buildings predate that line, and its houses are not multiple dwellings at all.

That doesn't mean an older building can let its system rot. Habitat Magazine's 2024 summary notes that the Housing Maintenance Code requires an intercom that exists to function and that courts have treated it as essential under the warranty of habitability, and older buildings must add one if a majority of apartments ask.

For a homeowner, the law is not the reason to repair the bell. Knowing who is at the door, and letting the upstairs household open it, is.

"A buzzing doorbell is just annoying."

HomeTips is right that continuous ringing usually means a stuck button or shorted wiring. What it doesn't add is that a short can keep a small transformer working at full load for hours, and a transformer that runs hot for days in a basement is not something to ignore.

A bell that buzzes by itself after rain is usually water in the button or an outdoor splice. One that buzzes in dry weather may have a pinched wire where siding or trim was nailed over it.

If your bell won't stop, call us; we would rather find the short than have the transformer replaced every few months.

"A wireless doorbell solves the wiring problem."

A battery doorbell does avoid the transformer, and for a garage or a side door with no wire it can be a sensible choice. But it doesn't open a door, and in a two-family the release is often the part that matters most.

Wireless bells also need their batteries changed and their signal to reach the chime through brick and basement walls, which isn't always reliable in a semi-detached Tudor.

Where a house already has bell wire, repairing it usually costs less over time than living with the workarounds. We will tell you honestly when the wireless option is the better one.
