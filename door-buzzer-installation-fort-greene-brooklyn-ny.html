The Neighborhood

Why Fort Greene Properties Need Door Buzzer Installation

Fort Greene covers a little over half a square mile between the Brooklyn Navy Yard and Flushing Avenue to the north, Downtown Brooklyn and the Flatbush Avenue Extension to the west, Atlantic Avenue to the south and Vanderbilt Avenue and Clinton Hill to the east. Most of its side streets were built up in the 1850s and 1860s as rows of brick and brownstone houses, and Portland, Oxford, Cumberland, Carlton and Adelphi carry the names of London terraces. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Fort Greene and Brooklyn Academy of Music historic districts in 1978. Around those rowhouse blocks sit Fort Greene Park, the Walt Whitman and Ingersoll public housing developments to the north, prewar apartment buildings, newer towers at the Downtown edge, and three commercial spines: Fulton Street, DeKalb Avenue and Myrtle Avenue.

For a buzzer installer, the defining fact about Fort Greene is that almost none of its houses still hold the number of households they were built for, and many have changed more than once. A Brownstoner listing for 51 Willoughby Avenue, a four-story brownstone built in 1905, describes it as "a four family home" that is "currently used as a double duplex": two households on four floors, each with a duplex. In 2014, 6sqft reported on a Carlton Avenue brownstone that MANIFOLD Architecture Studio turned from "an existing four-family residence" into a single-family home by combining the three upper apartments into an owner's triplex. Openigloo lists 50 Fort Greene Place, built in 1931, as three apartments on four floors. Each of those houses needs a different entry system, and the old bell plate on the front of each one was almost certainly designed for a household count that no longer exists.

That is why a buzzer here so often stops working without anyone noticing at first. When a house is renovated, the old bell wire is the first thing a demolition crew pulls out of a wall, because nobody marked it. One Brownstoner thread about a three-family brownstone starts exactly that way: the contractor removed a working doorbell, and the owner then had to find someone to install a new bell and intercom from scratch. In another thread, the owner of a divided brownstone with an old two-apartment buzzer wanted to see who was at the door, and was quoted more than $1,500, later remembered as about $3,000, for wired video. A reply described the cheap alternative many owners try first: a doorbell camera wired into the foyer and plug-in chimes, because the house's "old doorbell wiring was too deteriorated." That gives a picture, but it does not give the upstairs household a button that opens the door.

And in the larger buildings, the door itself matters most. The NYC Comptroller's resident-led survey of public housing entrances in 2022 found 40.1 percent of entrance door locks broken and 36.8 percent of entrance doors standing open across the 262 developments visited. Fort Greene's Whitman and Ingersoll Houses are run under the Housing Authority's own programs, but the lesson applies to every private walk-up and condo here too: an entry system is only as good as the door it releases. A station that calls every apartment perfectly is wasted on a door that drifts open behind each visitor.

Repair or Replace in Fort Greene

Need Repair in Fort Greene? Revive the Old Bell Plate, Rebuild Around It, or Replace It: How We Decide First

A Fort Greene call that begins "the buzzer is dead, we need a new one" ends up as one of three jobs once we have looked. The first is a system that is basically sound with one failed part: a transformer in the cellar, a strike that has worn out, one apartment station, or a splice that came loose when someone moved a radiator. The second is a system that still works but no longer fits the house: a brownstone divided into duplexes, a garden apartment that became part of the owner's home, a top floor that became a separate rental. The third is a house with nothing to save, because the bell plate was removed during a renovation, the wire was cut in a gut job, or the house has never had a way to answer the street door from upstairs.

Photos usually tell us which group a house belongs to. Send a picture of the doorway from the sidewalk, a close-up of whatever is beside the door, and any station inside you can reach, to (347) 934-8335 or on WhatsApp. If the system has to be tested in person, that is the $250 service call booked online, and the $250 goes toward whichever path you take. Where a repair and a replacement are both reasonable, you get both prices and a plain recommendation, and we will not sell a new system to a house that one part would fix.

Brick Underground's NYC intercom repair guide, quoting Daniel Vasquez of Paragon Security and Locksmith, explains why testing comes before replacing: the trouble is usually in the connections, at the base unit, an apartment station, the door strike or the power supply "in the basement or the lobby ceiling," and the core wiring of older systems is often fine even when the equipment is dated. In a Fort Greene brownstone, where opening plaster is the most expensive and least welcome part of any job, a sound old pair behind the wall is worth a lot. Building Specialists

Door Buzzer Installation for Fort Greene's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for nearly every buzzer we plan in Fort Greene. They differ in how many households share the street door, how many floors each household covers, how the cable can travel without damaging plaster, and who signs for the work. Each has a mistake we see when the job is rushed.

Brownstones kept as one home

Four-story Italianate and Greek Revival houses on the park blocks and the Oxford, Portland and Elliott streets, with one household from the garden floor to the top.

Two- and three-family brownstones

Houses divided into separate floor-through apartments, often with the owner on the garden and parlor floors and tenants above, all sharing one stoop door.

Double duplexes

Houses legally set up for three or four families but lived in as two duplexes, each household spread across two floors with its own inner door.

Prewar walk-ups near the park

Small apartment buildings from the early twentieth century, such as the 1931 three-apartment building at 50 Fort Greene Place, with a shared vestibule and a stair.

Condos and conversions

Condominium buildings, including the apartments created inside the Williamsburgh Savings Bank Tower at 1 Hanson Place between 2005 and 2007, and newer buildings at the Downtown edge.

Shop-and-apartment buildings

Three- and four-story buildings on Fulton Street, DeKalb Avenue and Myrtle Avenue with a store at street level and apartments above, reached by a narrow residential door.

Brownstones kept as one home: the button is easy, the answering points are not

A single-family brownstone needs only one button, so owners often assume the job is a doorbell. The hard part is upstairs. A family that cooks on the garden floor, sits on the parlor floor and sleeps on the top two floors cannot hear a single chime from every room, and walking down three flights to see who rang defeats the purpose. The design question is where to put answering stations so that whoever is home can see or hear the visitor and press release from the floor they are on.

Our usual plan is a station on the garden floor near the kitchen, one on the parlor floor and one on the top floor landing, or phone answering for everyone with one wall station as a backup. Cable reaches the upper floors best through a stacked closet, a disused dumbwaiter or the back of a built-in, so the plaster in the front rooms stays untouched. Where there is no such path, phone answering with one wired station can cover the house with far less cutting.

Two- and three-family brownstones: one stoop, separate households

The most common Fort Greene buzzer job is a brownstone with two or three households sharing the stoop door. Typically the owner occupies the garden and parlor floors and rents the upper floors as one or two floor-through apartments. Each household needs its own button, its own answering station, and the ability to talk to a visitor and release the street door without involving anyone else.

The vestibule is where these houses differ. Many have an outer door at the top of the stoop and an inner door with glass behind it, and in a divided house the tenants and the owner both pass through both. We decide with the owner which door the buzzer releases, which stays on a key, and whether the owner's part of the house has its own inner door that should never be released by a tenant's button. The panel goes inside the door opening or on the frame, sized for two or three buttons, and the cable usually runs down to the cellar and up a back wall or a closet stack.

The mistake we see most is a separate store-bought video doorbell for each household. Each family then has a camera but no release, so someone still walks down to open the door, and the stoop door ends up on the latch for deliveries. A proper two- or three-button system costs less than most owners expect, and it is the thing that actually lets the tenants upstairs answer their own door.

Double duplexes: two buttons on a house built for four

Double duplexes are especially common in Fort Greene's larger brownstones. The house may be a legal three- or four-family on paper, as at 51 Willoughby Avenue, but in practice it holds two households, each across two floors: a parlor and garden duplex below and an upper duplex above. The old bell plate on such houses often still shows four buttons, two of which ring nothing.

The right panel has the buttons the house uses today, two in this case, with each household answered on both of its floors. That means two answering stations per household, or one station and phone answering, and a release reachable from either floor. If the owner wants the option to rent the floors separately again, we leave labeled cable to each floor and choose a panel that can take more buttons by programming or a larger faceplate without new holes in the stone. What we do not do is install four live buttons for two households, because visitors press the wrong one and nobody answers.

Prewar walk-ups near the park: three to a dozen apartments and a vestibule

Between the brownstone rows, Fort Greene has small apartment buildings from the first decades of the twentieth century. 50 Fort Greene Place is one example in the public record: three apartments on four floors, built in 1931. Buildings like this have a proper vestibule, a stair hall and an apartment door on each landing, and usually an old audio system with a brass or aluminum plate and handsets in the apartments.

They are often the best candidates for reusing old wire. The bell pairs run up the stair hall in a single path, sometimes in a small conduit, and a modern two-wire system can often run on them once each pair is tested. Aiphone describes its GT Series as built for "new builds and retrofit projects," with the advantage of "using existing wire and infrastructure." Where a pair fails, we replace that one run in a painted raceway along the stair stringer, which tenants barely notice.

Condos and conversions: a board, a lobby and a system someone else chose

Fort Greene's condo buildings range from small conversions of a single brownstone to the apartments created inside the Williamsburgh Savings Bank Tower at 1 Hanson Place, the 41-story former bank designated an exterior landmark in 1977 and converted to condominiums between 2005 and 2007. Newer condo and rental buildings line the Flatbush Avenue Extension side of the neighborhood. In all of them, the entry system was chosen by a developer, and the people who live there now have to manage it.

The work in these buildings starts with paperwork. Who owns the system and its software account, who holds the administrator login, whether there is a subscription and who pays it, and whether the board or the managing agent can approve work in the lobby. Once that is clear, the job may be as small as recovering the account and rebuilding the directory, or as large as replacing a panel that no one can program. The board gets a written price that separates the shared parts, the lobby panel, the strike and the power, from any work inside individual apartments.

Shop-and-apartment buildings: the residential door beside the store

On Fulton Street, DeKalb Avenue and Myrtle Avenue, the typical building has a store, café or restaurant across most of the ground floor and a narrow residential door to one side leading straight to the stair. The apartments above need a buzzer; the business below often needs its own, for a back or side door, and the two must never be able to release each other's doors.

We keep them as two separate systems even when their cables share a cellar. The residential panel calls only apartments and its power supply sits on the landlord's circuit. The store's station calls the register or the manager's phone and releases only the store's door. Labeling in the cellar makes clear which is which, so the next technician does not connect the restaurant's door to apartment 3.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Count the Households, Read the Doorway, Then Find Where the Old Bell Wire Went

These notes describe how our technicians approach a buzzer installation in a Fort Greene rowhouse or small walk-up. They are our standard practice, not a story about any one address.

Count the households, not the buttons. Before anything else, the technician asks the owner who lives in the house and how. Which floors does each household use? Which doors do they come and go through? Is anyone planning to rent a floor separately, or to combine two? The old bell plate is checked against those answers. In a house that has been a four-family, a two-family and a double duplex in the space of a few decades, the plate is often the least reliable record in the building.

Read the doorway. Next, the technician looks at the opening the way the Landmarks Preservation Commission does: where is the plain masonry, the wood frame, the metalwork? Is there carved stone, cast iron or decorative molding that nothing may be fixed to? Is there already an intercom plate whose spot the new one can take? Then the doors themselves: does the stoop door close and latch by itself, is it a single leaf or a pair, is there glass, and does the inner vestibule door matter to anyone?

Find where the old bell wire went. Last, the technician goes to the cellar and looks for the transformer and the old pairs. Do they rise through a closet stack, a dumbwaiter shaft, the back of the stair, or behind the front-room plaster? Were they cut in a renovation upstairs? Each pair that survives is tested. The transformer's circuit is noted, along with whose meter it is on, because a buzzer fed from a tenant's outlet goes dead whenever that tenant's power is shut off.

Everything from that walk goes on the written price: the button count, the station locations on each floor, the closer, the strike, the pairs that will be reused and the ones that will be replaced, any Landmarks permit work and the route the new cable takes. Nothing is discovered on installation day.

Landmarked Doorways

One Panel per Door Opening and About Ten Inches of Conduit: What the Landmarks Guidebook Means for a Fort Greene Buzzer

Because the Fort Greene and BAM historic districts take in most of the neighborhood's rowhouse blocks, the Landmarks Preservation Commission's rules shape more buzzer installations here than almost anywhere else in Brooklyn. The rules are less mysterious than owners fear. The Commission's Permit Guidebook has a chapter on health, safety and utility equipment that covers intercoms, light fixtures, cameras and mailboxes in plain terms. What follows is how we read it for a buzzer job; it is not legal advice, and the Commission itself answers questions by email at info@lpc.nyc.gov.

Some work needs no permit at all. The guidebook says no permit is needed for "ordinary maintenance, repair, and retrofitting of health, safety, and utility equipment." It also says in-kind replacement of existing intercoms is exempt from the elevation and large-scale detail drawings a new installation may require. For a Fort Greene owner, that makes the existing plate valuable information: replacing a working or dead intercom panel with a similar one in the same place is a different and simpler matter than adding a new panel where none has been. We photograph and measure what is there before proposing anything.

New panels can be approved by staff. Where a permit is needed, "Staff can issue permits approving light fixtures and intercom panels" when the work meets the Commission's criteria, which avoids a public hearing. Those criteria are specific. Intercom panels and doorbells "must be installed within or adjacent to a door opening." An intercom may also go on "a fence or wall in front of the entranceway." Equipment "cannot be on or in front of a primary facade unless no other option exists." And "A maximum of two light fixtures and one intercom panel can be installed per door opening."

How it is fastened matters as much as where. The guidebook requires equipment to "be attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood, or metalwork" and says it "must not be attached through cast iron or on any decorative surfaces." On a Fort Greene stoop, that rules out the carved brownstone surround, any cast-iron railing or newel, and ornamental moldings. In practice, the cleanest location is usually the wood door frame inside the opening or the plain reveal beside it, and a slim station sized for the number of households is the panel most likely to fit there. The finish should be "compatible with the architecture of the building," which in practice means neutral and discreet, and painted to blend where possible.

Conduit is meant to disappear. Cable "must be concealed by going through plain masonry" or behind the installation. Where concealment is impossible, staff may approve "a maximum of approximately ten inches of visible conduit," painted to match and fixed into mortar joints. That rule pushes the cable route inside: through the door frame, down into the vestibule floor or the cellar, and up through the house. It is also one reason we test the old wire carefully, because an old pair that already passes through the frame is a route the Commission will never see.

One panel per opening shapes the design. Many Fort Greene rowhouses have two doorways: the stoop door to the parlor floor and the door under the stoop to the garden floor. The one-panel rule applies to each opening, so a house that wants both doors on the system plans one station at each and decides which households each one calls. A house that wants a separate panel for every apartment at a single doorway cannot have it; it gets one panel with a button for each.

Original Doors

Paired Leaves, Glass Panels and the Astragal: Fitting a Release to an Original Fort Greene Stoop Door

Many Fort Greene brownstones still have tall paired front doors at the top of the stoop, often with glass panels, and the question of whether such doors can take a buzzer release has been asked on Brownstoner for nearly two decades. In a 2007 thread, a brownstone owner with glass-windowed double front doors said they had been told no system could accommodate a buzzer on doors like theirs. A renovator who had done two sets of double doors replied that it could be done but warned: "You'll need to open up the walls, probably quite a bit," along with a channel behind the astragal, the strip where the two leaves meet, and a hole drilled through one door.

That answer is accurate for how many older installations were done, and it explains why owners put the job off. It is not the only way. The planning starts with which leaf people actually open. On most paired stoop doors, one leaf is active and carries the lock, and the other is held shut with bolts at the top and bottom. The release has to go where the active leaf latches, which on a pair is the inactive leaf or the astragal, not the frame. That is the reason the work is harder than on a single door: the release lives in a door that moves, or in a strip fixed to it, instead of in a solid frame.

There are three main ways to solve it, and the right one depends on the doors. On a pair where the inactive leaf stays bolted and the astragal is solid wood, a narrow strike can be set into the inactive leaf at the latch, with its wire brought through a power transfer hinge or a concealed loop at the hinge edge and down into the frame and the floor. On a door whose lock already has a motorized or electrified version made for it, replacing the lock body can avoid cutting the astragal at all, with power again reaching the door through the hinge. And where the original doors are too fragile or valuable to alter, the release can move to the inner vestibule door, which is often a simpler single leaf, with the outer pair kept on a key and left open during the day if the household wants.

Installation Day

No Door Left Unlocked for the Installer: Installation-Day Rules Shaped by a Fort Greene Lesson

In May 2014, DNAinfo reported from Ingersoll Houses at 88 St. Edwards Street, where the Housing Authority had told residents to give contractors "daily access" to their apartments for two weeks of electrical, plumbing, kitchen and closet work. Some residents left their apartment doors unlocked while they were out, and two apartments were burglarized on May 20. One resident, a retired teacher home at the time, described the moment she realized someone else was inside: "I heard my front door click." The Rules

An Essential Service, Not an Amenity: What New York Expects of Fort Greene Owners, Boards and Landlords

Fort Greene's buildings run from single-family houses that no entry rule touches to condos and walk-ups with a dozen or more apartments, so the rules land differently from door to door. Here is how they apply in plain terms. None of it is legal advice.

Larger apartment buildings must have talk and release. New York's Multiple Dwelling Law covers New York City, and its §50-a requires every class A multiple dwelling built or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments to have a self-locking main entrance door and an intercommunication system that lets tenants talk with a visitor and release the door from their apartments. Older buildings of that size must install both when tenants occupying a majority of the apartments ask or consent in writing; the work has to begin within 90 days and finish within six months. Most Fort Greene brownstones are well under eight apartments, but condo buildings, conversions and some prewar buildings are not.

A broken buzzer is a reportable condition. NYC 311 lists "Doorbells, buzzers, or intercoms that are broken or missing" as a maintenance complaint both inside apartments and in public areas, and asks residents to try the owner, managing agent or super first. "HPD investigates maintenance complaints and issues violations to owners who break the law." The same ButterflyMX guide sets out HPD's general correction periods: 90 days for class A violations, 30 days for class B and 24 hours for most class C conditions, and it notes a 2022 Bronx Housing Court decision that recorded a class B violation for an inoperable bell and return-buzzer system. If you have received a violation, tell us its class and date when you ask for a quote, because the deadline shapes the schedule.

Co-ops and condos carry the duty too. Habitat magazine's February 2024 article on co-op intercoms reported that courts have found "intercoms are an essential service" under the warranty of habitability. Nancy Kourland of the Lasser Law Group put it directly: "As a result, the co-op has a legal obligation to repair or replace the intercom." The article lists the remedies a shareholder has: a written demand to the board, a 311 complaint and an HP proceeding in housing court. For a Fort Greene board, that is a strong reason to plan a replacement before the system fails completely, rather than after the first complaint.

Everyone must be able to leave. Any door with a buzzer still has to open from the inside without a key, a code or special knowledge. With an electric strike that is automatic; Allegion's guide explains that with a strike "you can exit by turning the lever" whether it is fail-safe or fail-secure. Magnetic locks hold the whole door, so the building code's sensor-release provisions call for a sensor on the egress side, a manual release marked "PUSH TO EXIT" 40 to 48 inches above the floor within five feet of the door, release when power is lost or the fire alarm or sprinklers activate, and hardware listed to UL 294. On a Fort Greene stoop door we use strikes almost without exception.

  • Free exit at all times. Every business door with a buzzer, code or reader must still open from inside without a key or code; an electric strike keeps the inside lever working, so it is our default.

Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Installation in Fort Greene: Questions Answered

Questions Fort Greene owners, tenants and businesses bring to us, together with ones raised in Brownstoner forum threads, Brick Underground, Habitat magazine and the city's own guidance, answered for the houses and buildings here.

Houses that keep changing shape

Our brownstone is a legal four-family on paper, but we and one other household live in it as two duplexes. How many buttons belong on the new panel?

Two live buttons, one per household, with each household able to answer and release from both of its floors. A panel that shows four buttons for two households confuses every courier and guest, and the two dead buttons are the ones a stranger presses at night. Each duplex gets either a station on each of its floors or one wall station plus phone answering, so nobody has to run down a flight to reach the release.

If you might rent the floors separately later, we leave labeled cable at each floor and choose a station that can take more buttons by programming or a matching faceplate in the same opening. That keeps the option open without new holes in the doorway, which on a landmarked front is worth avoiding.

Our contractor gutted the parlor and garden floors and the old bell wire went with the plaster. What should happen before the new walls close?

Plan the buzzer with the electrical rough-in, not after the painters. We mark the station locations on each floor with the owner, pull low-voltage cable from the doorway to the cellar and up to each location while the studs are open, add a spare pair for a future station, and leave the ends labeled and coiled in boxes. The door station and the answering stations go in once the finish work is done.

The Brownstoner thread about a three-family brownstone whose contractor removed a working doorbell shows why this matters: once the walls are closed, the choices narrow to fishing cable through finished plaster or running raceway across it. A few hours of rough-in during the renovation costs far less than either.

Doorways, stoops and old wire

Our neighbor put a doorbell camera on the brownstone surround next door. If we want ours inside the door frame instead, is that just taste, or does it matter?

It matters on a landmarked block. The Commission's guidebook says equipment must not be attached "on any decorative surfaces" and should be "within or adjacent to a door opening," fixed at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork. A camera screwed into carved brownstone is the opposite of that, whatever the neighbor did. The wood frame inside the opening is usually the location least likely to cause a problem.

Our wired video quote for a two-apartment brownstone came in well over $1,500. Why does it cost so much more than a $40 doorbell camera with plug-in chimes?

Because it does more. The doorbell-camera workaround described on Brownstoner shows one household a picture and rings a chime; it does not give the second household its own button, and it does not release the door. A wired system puts a station for each household at the door, an answering station or phone account in each apartment, a strike in the frame, a closer that shuts the door and a battery-backed power supply, all wired so a guest can be let in from upstairs.

On our Brooklyn list, a video door station for a divided brownstone or small building falls in the $700 to $1,800 range, and an audio system with a release sits in the $400 to $900 range. Where a house lands depends on how many floors each household spans, whether old pairs can be reused and what the doors need, all named on the written price.

Tenants, boards and who pays

I rent the top floor of a three-family brownstone and the buzzer has been dead for months. What can I actually do, and can I hire you myself?

Write to the landlord first and keep a copy; 311's guidance asks residents to try the owner, managing agent or super before filing. If nothing happens, a 311 complaint about a broken doorbell, buzzer or intercom goes to HPD, which can inspect and issue a violation. Rent-stabilized tenants have other remedies, and a tenant attorney or tenants' group can advise on those.

We can install or repair only with the authorization of whoever controls the building, because the work touches the shared street door, the cellar and other apartments. If your landlord agrees in writing, we are glad to quote and work with you on access; you are welcome to pass along this page or our number.

Our condo board inherited the developer's entry system, and two board members disagree about replacing it. What should the board see before it votes?

Three things: what is actually wrong, what each option costs over several years, and who will manage it afterward. We test the existing panel, the strike, the power and a sample of apartment stations, check whether the software account and any subscription can be transferred to the board, and price both keeping it and replacing it. Swiftlane's cost guide puts software subscriptions at $5 to $15 per unit per month or $30 to $100 per door per month, so on a small condo the subscription can outweigh the hardware within a few years.

Businesses and mixed buildings

Our office takes the garden floor of a brownstone, and our clients use the door under the stoop. How do we keep them from ringing the family upstairs?

Give the garden door its own station that calls only the office, and label the two entrances clearly. Clients who are told to use the door under the stoop then find one button marked with the office name, and the stoop door's panel lists only the households above. Each station releases only its own door.

If the garden door is also the family's back route to the garden or the cellar, we decide with the owner whether the office's release should open it after hours. Usually the answer is a schedule: the office's station rings the office desk during business hours and the owner's phone at other times.

Our café on DeKalb Avenue has apartments above, and their residential door is a few feet from ours. Can our delivery driver be let in early without anyone upstairs being disturbed?

Yes, if the café's delivery door has its own station or code, separate from the residential panel. The driver rings the café's station, which calls the manager's phone, or uses a code that works only at that door and only during delivery hours. Nobody upstairs hears a thing, and the café's release can never open the apartments' door.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Door Buzzer Installation in Fort Greene

Search for buzzer installation and you get confident summaries assembled from national cost guides and vendor blogs. Some of it holds; some does not fit a Fort Greene brownstone at all. Below are seven common claims, where they come from and what the field shows. We have not quoted any AI-generated answer word for word; the claims come from the published guides those answers draw on.

"An intercom installation averages about $2,500"

HomeAdvisor's cost guide gives that average, with a range of $1,500 to $15,000, wireless systems at $150 to $1,500 and hard-wired systems at $1,000 to $15,000.

For a Fort Greene owner, the national average hides the two things that actually move the price here: how many floors each household spans and how the cable gets through the house. A two-family brownstone with an audio station and a release can sit at $400 to $900 on our Brooklyn list. A condo building with a directory panel and an answering point in every apartment sits in the building range, from about $2,500 to past $10,000.

Two houses with the same number of households on the same block can be far apart, because one still has sound pairs up a closet stack and the other lost every wire in a gut renovation.

"Wireless is the do-it-yourself option"

HomeAdvisor says so, and for a single home that only wants a chime it can be true.

It does not hold for a divided brownstone. The stoop door still needs a strike, power and a closer, which no wireless kit supplies. Radio through brick party walls and four floors of plaster is unreliable, and a tenant upstairs needs a release, not just a picture.

Brick Underground's repair guide advises against working on the wiring yourself. In our experience, the parts that fail on Fort Greene doors are the strike's alignment in an old wood frame, the closer and the power supply, and none of those are wireless.

"Allow one to two hours per station"

HomeAdvisor gives that per-station time, and Brick Underground's sources estimated about a day to replace a five-apartment building's system.

Those figures assume the wire is there. In a Fort Greene brownstone, a station on a floor with an open stud wall takes little time; a station on a finished floor with no surviving pair can take most of a morning, fishing cable down from an attic or up from the cellar without opening the front-room plaster.

The honest schedule comes from the cellar walk, not a per-station figure, and it goes on the written plan with the price.

"You can't put a modern buzzer on a landmarked house"

Owners hear this from neighbors and some installers.

The Commission's guidebook says the opposite: "Staff can issue permits approving light fixtures and intercom panels" that meet its criteria, and in-kind replacement of an existing intercom is exempt from the detailed drawing requirements. The criteria are about location, size, finish, fastening and conduit, not about whether the technology is modern.

A slim video station inside the door opening, fixed to the wood frame with its cable concealed, can meet them. A large panel bolted to the carved surround with surface conduit down the stoop will not.

"A cellular call box needs no building wiring"

ButterflyMX says its intercom "requires no building wiring," and cellular call boxes are widely sold as needing no data cable at the door.

The panel may need no data cable, but it still needs power, a strike in a sound frame and a closer that shuts the door. A recessed stoop doorway between two brick houses can also have weak cellular signal, which has to be tested at the mounting point before anyone relies on it.

On a landmarked front, any panel also has to fit the one-panel-per-opening and fastening rules, whatever its connection.

"Old buzzer wiring should always be replaced"

Summaries often treat century-old bell wire as a reason to start over.

Brick Underground's sources said the core wiring of older systems is typically fine and that problems sit in the connections, and Aiphone builds its GT Series for retrofit with "existing wire and infrastructure." The ButterflyMX guide adds the honest caveat that reuse "can't be promised in advance."

So we test. In a Fort Greene house, every pair we can reuse is a wall we do not open, and every pair that fails is named on the price before you approve.

"Video is required now"

Vendor material sometimes implies New York's rules call for video or app access.

ButterflyMX's own guide to the law says §50-a "does not, by itself, require video, mobile access, or remote management," and most Fort Greene brownstones are too small to fall under §50-a at all.

Video is still worth it in many houses here, because nobody above the parlor floor can see the stoop. It is a choice to make on its merits, not a legal requirement.

Fort Greene Door Buzzer Installation FAQ

Can the new station go exactly where our old bell plate was, so the brownstone gets no new holes?
+

Often, yes. We measure the old plate and its fixings before choosing the station, and where a model of similar size fits the same spot, its screws can go into the existing anchors and its cable through the existing opening. If the new station is smaller, a trim plate covers the old outline. Reusing the old location also keeps the work close to an in-kind replacement, which the Landmarks guidebook treats more simply than a new panel.

If cable has to be fished through our finished plaster, do your technicians patch and paint the access openings?
+

No. We do not do plaster or paint work. We fish cable with the fewest and smallest openings the route allows, usually inside closets or behind baseboards, and every opening is named on the written price beforehand. Your plasterer or painter finishes them afterward, and on a renovation the cable goes in while the walls are open so no openings are needed at all.

The easiest cable route runs through the cellar we share with the house next door. Can the buzzer use it?
+

Only with your neighbor's written agreement, and we usually suggest avoiding it. A cable that passes through another owner's cellar can be cut or disturbed in their next renovation, and servicing it means asking for access every time. We look for a route that stays entirely inside your own house first and price both if the shared route is much shorter.

During a blackout on our block, will the stoop door stay locked, and can anyone upstairs still answer it?
+

With the fail-secure strike we normally use, the door stays locked when power is lost and opens from inside with the lever as usual. A battery-backed power supply keeps the door station and wired answering stations working through a short outage. Phone answering also depends on the house's internet staying up, so wired stations are the dependable backup.
