The Neighborhood

Why Beverley Square Properties Need Door Buzzer Repair

Beverley Square is two small neighborhoods under one name. Wikipedia's account runs both from Beverley Road in the north to Cortelyou Road in the south. The open cut of the Brighton Line, the Q train today, divides them between Marlborough Road and East 16th Street. Beverley Square East runs from the tracks to East 19th Street; Beverley Square West runs from the tracks to Argyle Road, though some descriptions carry it one more block to Stratford Road. Both were the work of one developer, Thomas Benton Ackerson, whose company began the large houses on East 19th Street in 1898 and bought the farm that became the western half in 1901. Wikipedia says that land was Catherine Lott's farm; the Landmarks Preservation Commission's 2025 designation brief calls it the former Voorhies farm. We note both because sources disagree, not because it changes a single wire.

What it changes is the kind of building at the door. These are freestanding houses on lawns, not rowhouses. The Landmarks Commission describes Queen Anne houses in the northern part of the West, Colonial Revival houses after them and a mix of the two to the south; Brownstoner's coverage of the October 2025 hearing describes open porches on Argyle and Rugby Roads and colonnades and double porches on Westminster Road just outside. That means the front door sits back from the sidewalk behind a porch, often with a second door inside it, and the house has a side door, a cellar door and sometimes a separate entrance that a later owner cut in for a second household. Every one of those can carry its own bell.

The second thing that changes is how many households share the house. Our query of the city's PLUTO building file for the lots between Beverley and Cortelyou Roads, from Argyle Road to East 19th Street, finds about 283 tax lots. Some 120 are classed as one-family houses and 74 as two-family houses; 22 are walk-up apartment buildings and 11 are elevator buildings. On the interior streets alone, 72 of the 210 lots are two-family houses, and CBS New York reported in December 2025 that neighbors outside the new historic district fear faster development, one of them pointing to a Cortelyou Road building that had been a single-family house until a couple of years earlier. A house that was built with one bell at one door and now has an owner downstairs, a tenant upstairs and an attic or basement unit has, in practice, become a small intercom system that nobody designed.

The edges are a different world. Along Beverley Road and Cortelyou Road the same PLUTO extract finds six-story elevator buildings from the 1920s to the 1960s, the largest with 131 apartments, two smaller buildings finished in 2007 and 2014, and dozens of store-and-apartment buildings on the Cortelyou Road shopping strip. About 682 of the roughly 1,146 homes in our study area sit on those two frontages, and a few more six-story buildings stand on East 18th and East 19th Streets. Those buildings have lobby panels, risers and basement power supplies like any Brooklyn apartment house, and they are where the city's Housing Preservation and Development inspectors have written almost every intercom violation recorded on these blocks.

So this page has a thesis. In Beverley Square, door buzzer repair is two different trades that happen to share a ZIP code. In the houses, the work is finding which of several small, separate bells has failed, keeping each household's bell in its own apartment, and doing it on a wooden porch that may now be inside a landmark district. In the buildings, it is ordinary intercom repair under the Housing Maintenance Code, with paperwork that has to satisfy an inspector. The rest of the page takes each in turn.

Building Specialists

Door Buzzer Repair for Beverley Square's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for nearly every call on these blocks. The same symptom means different things in each, so the first question on the phone is always which of these you live in.

Queen Anne house, one family

Built around 1898 to 1905, with a deep porch, a front door set well back and often a second inner door. Usually a single push-button and a chime in the hall, fed by a small transformer in the cellar.

Colonial Revival house

Later and squarer, with columns at the porch and a centered door. The bell wire often runs straight down through the porch floor into the cellar, where damp and later repairs catch it.

House split into two units

The owner downstairs, a tenant upstairs reached by the main stair or a side door. Two buttons, two chimes and, all too often, one shared transformer and one crossed wire.

Three units, attic or basement apartment

A third household under the roof or below grade, frequently with its own entrance at the side. At three households the house is a multiple dwelling under state law.

Six-story pre-war elevator building

On Beverley Road and Cortelyou Road, from the late 1920s onward: lobby panel, vestibule door, basement power supply and a riser to every apartment.

Store-and-apartment and newer buildings

Shops with apartments above along Cortelyou Road, plus buildings finished since 2007, including two finished in 2007 and 2014, which may use newer telephone-entry or IP panels.

How each building type changes the repair

Wood houses. A bell circuit in a freestanding house is low voltage and low current, which is why it survives for decades and why its faults are almost always mechanical: a button that no longer makes contact, a splice that has corroded in a damp porch ceiling, a staple driven through the wire by someone fixing the trim. We trace those from the button back to the transformer with a meter rather than replacing parts in hope. Where a run has to be replaced, the new wire follows the old path, under the porch ceiling and down into the cellar, so nothing new appears on the face of the house.

Converted houses. The trouble with two- and three-unit houses is rarely a dead part. It is the wiring logic. A conversion done in stages often leaves the upstairs button connected to the owner's chime, or both chimes on one button, or a basement bell spliced into whatever wire was nearest. The repair is a short wiring diagram, drawn on site: one button, one chime, one apartment, labelled at the transformer. That diagram stays with the owner, so the next contractor does not have to rediscover it.

Elevator buildings. On the edge roads the work is standard building intercom repair: isolate the talk path from the release path, test the power supply, tone the riser pair for the dead apartment and check the door hardware before blaming the panel. These buildings also carry a second obligation that houses do not, which is the self-closing, self-locking entrance door under state law, and a door that does not latch is the most common reason a working buzzer seems broken.

Store-and-apartment buildings. Along Cortelyou Road, the apartments above shops usually have a separate street door beside the shopfront, a short stair and two to four bells. Those panels take weather, deliveries and a lot of leaning. We repair the buttons and speaker in the existing plate where we can, because a new plate rarely matches the old cut-out.

The Porch

On These Blocks, the Porch Is the Lobby

An apartment-building lobby is built to take traffic and weather: masonry, a steel door, a metal panel. A Victorian porch is not. It is painted wood, open to rain on three sides, and in Beverley Square it is often a hundred and twenty years old. The button and the wire that serve it live in that environment. Paint is the first enemy. Each repainting of the door casing adds a layer over the button, and after enough of them the button sticks in, shorts, or no longer moves at all. Water is the second. Bell wire was commonly run through the porch ceiling or down through the floor, and a splice in a porch ceiling that has leaked will turn green and fail long before the wire itself.

The porch also creates the double door. Many houses here have an outer door at the porch and an inner door behind it, with a small vestibule between. When an owner wants to buzz visitors in, the question is which door. Releasing the outer door alone keeps the inner door locked, which suits a house with tenants upstairs; releasing both suits a one-family house that wants packages left inside. Either works, but the electric strike has to go into a wood frame that has moved for a century, and a strike forced into a frame that is out of square will bind. Most of the work in a porch-door release is fitting, not wiring.

Finally, the porch hides things. Owners who want the porch to keep its look usually want the hardware small, the wire invisible and no new holes in turned columns or carved brackets. That is easier than it sounds. The old bell pair is usually still in the wall, and a modern button, a slim video doorbell or a one-button door station can often be fed from it. Where the old pair is too thin or too damaged, a new run can usually follow it. We do not run wire across the face of a porch, and inside the historic district the Landmarks rules described below point the same way.

The Rules

Two Households or Three: Where the Intercom Law Changes

Owners of converted houses often ask whether they are legally required to have a working buzzer. The answer depends on the number of households, and it is worth knowing before a repair because it decides how urgent the job is and what paperwork matters. Under New York State's Multiple Dwelling Law, section 4, a multiple dwelling is a building occupied as the home of three or more families living independently of each other, and a Class A multiple dwelling is one occupied for permanent residence. A one- or two-family house is not a multiple dwelling at all.

Section 50-a of the same law is the intercom rule. For Class A multiple dwellings erected or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments, it requires self-closing, self-locking entrance doors and an intercommunication system at the door to the main lobby that lets each apartment speak to a visitor and release the lock. For Class A buildings erected or converted before 1968, the same equipment is required only if tenants occupying a majority of the apartments request or consent to it in writing on the city's forms, after which the owner has 90 days to begin and six months to finish. An owner who fails to install and maintain the required equipment commits a misdemeanor, as does anyone who willfully damages, jams or removes it. Separately, HPD writes its intercom violations under section 27-2005 of the city's Housing Maintenance Code, the owner's general duty to keep a multiple dwelling in good repair, so an intercom that a building already has is expected to work.

What does that mean on Rugby Road or East 17th Street? A two-family house is outside the Multiple Dwelling Law, so the bells are a matter of the lease and good sense, not of section 50-a. A house that now holds three households has become a Class A multiple dwelling, and a house converted after 1968 with eight or more apartments is rare here but not impossible. The six-story buildings on the edges are plainly multiple dwellings, and their entrance doors and intercoms fall under the Housing Maintenance Code that HPD enforces. This is general information, not legal advice; for a specific building, ask HPD or a lawyer.

One more rule touches every building on the edges. HPD's June 2022 maintenance bulletin explains that Local Laws 62 and 63 of 2022 require self-closing doors to be kept in good repair and make a failure a Class C, immediately hazardous violation, with 14 days to correct and civil penalties of $250 to $500 plus $250 a day after the deadline. A building door that no longer closes and latches is both that violation and the most common reason a working buzzer appears broken: the release works, but the door was never locked. We check the closer and latch on every lobby call for that reason.

The Historic District

Fixing a Doorbell Inside the Beverley Square West Historic District

On November 25, 2025 the Landmarks Preservation Commission voted unanimously to designate the Beverley Square West Historic District, together with Ditmas Park West one block to the south. 6sqft and CBS New York describe the district as 118 freestanding houses built between 1894 and 1910 on Argyle, Rugby and Marlborough Roads between Beverley and Cortelyou Roads; the commission's own brief gives both 118 and 119, and Brownstoner reported 119 at the October hearing. Westminster and Stratford Roads were left out despite most hearing speakers asking for them, and Beverley Square East, across the tracks, is not designated. In our PLUTO extract, 118 lots carry the district's name.

For door hardware the commission's Permit Guidebook is clear and fairly practical. Ordinary maintenance, repair and retrofitting of health, safety and utility equipment does not need a permit, and neither does repainting in the same color. Installing intercoms and doorbells is a staff-level permit. The guidebook says light fixtures, intercom panels and doorbells must be installed within or adjacent to a door opening, allows an intercom on a fence or wall in front of the entrance, limits it to one intercom panel per door opening, and asks that design, size and finish suit the building. Applications need the manufacturer's cut sheets; in-kind replacements do not need elevation drawings.

In practice, most buzzer repairs in the district are maintenance: a new button in the same place, a new chime indoors, a splice re-made in the cellar, a transformer swapped. Those need no permit. The jobs that do are the ones that change what the street sees, such as swapping a plain button for a video doorbell or a door station, or adding a second panel for a new unit. Because the rule is one panel per door opening, a house with three households and one porch door is better served by a single panel with three buttons than by three separate doorbells, and a basement unit with its own side or areaway door can have its own. Equipment goes on wood, plain masonry or mortar joints, not on carved or decorative surfaces, and visible conduit is kept to a minimum and painted to match. When a change needs a permit, we provide the cut sheets and photos the application asks for; filing is the owner's.

Two side notes for owners in the district. The commission said it will write guidelines for accessory dwelling units in one- and two-family houses in both new districts, with interior units allowed in basements, cellars and attics, which is exactly the situation that creates a third bell. And Beverley Square East owners, though outside the district, live beside it; several told the commission in 2025 they wanted the protections extended, so a discreet panel is a sensible choice there too, even where no permit is required.

The Record

What HPD's Violation Records Show on These Blocks

We pulled every HPD housing violation recorded on the Beverley Square tax lots in our study area whose description mentions an intercom, a buzzer or a self-closing door, using the city's open violation data. HPD's own records spell the north edge Beverly Road, without the second e, which is worth knowing if you search them yourself. The result is a clear picture of where buzzer problems become legal problems here.

There are 67 intercom or buzzer violations on these lots, going back to 1991. Fifty-three were Class B, 13 Class A and one Class C. They sit on just 13 buildings, and all but one of those are apartment buildings: 27 violations on elevator buildings, 20 on store-and-apartment buildings and 17 on walk-ups. Fifty-nine of the 67 are on buildings fronting Beverley Road or Cortelyou Road. The only freestanding house in the record is one on East 16th Street, which drew three violations in 2002 and 2003 while it was being run as apartments. The typical wording is the inspector's standard order to properly repair or replace the broken or defective intercom system or return buzzer, often naming the exact apartment and wall.

The timing matters too. Twenty-nine of the 67 were written from 2021 through 2025, more than in any earlier five-year stretch. Self-closing doors tell a stronger version of the same story: 290 violations on these lots, 236 of them Class C, with 58 in 2025 and 47 more in 2026 through October, after the 2022 local laws made a failed self-closing door an immediately hazardous violation. Many of those records are now closed or dismissed, so the counts are a history of problems found, not a list of buildings that are broken today.

For a house owner, the lesson is reassuring: HPD intercom enforcement here is overwhelmingly about apartment buildings. For a managing agent on the edge roads, the lesson is the opposite. An intercom violation on one of these buildings usually names a single apartment, and the repair that clears it is usually a single station, splice or riser pair. Fix that, keep a dated record of what was replaced, and check the entrance door closer at the same visit, because the self-closing door is the violation that has been growing fastest.

New Units

Basement and Attic Apartments Need Their Own Bell

Two city changes are likely to put more separate bells on these houses. The first is zoning. The City of Yes for Housing Opportunity, which the City Council passed on December 5, 2024 according to the Governor's office, allows one accessory dwelling unit per one- or two-family building, up to 800 square feet, according to the Department of City Planning's guide, which names backyard cottages, garage conversions, attics and basements, while noting that basement units depended on changes to state law. The second is basement legalization: the Department of Buildings announced proposed rules in November 2025 under Local Law 126 of 2024 for existing basement and cellar apartments in one- and two-family homes in certain community districts. We could not confirm from that announcement whether Community District 14, which includes Beverley Square, is one of them, so treat that point as open.

Inside the Beverley Square West district, the commission has said its ADU guidelines will allow interior units in basements, cellars and attics, and units in existing accessory buildings such as garages, but not new backyard houses. Each of those units has a door, a visitor and a delivery, and in practice each needs a bell that rings in that unit and nowhere else. A basement unit reached by a side or areaway door can usually have its own button at that door. An attic unit reached through the main stair usually shares the porch door, which means a second button on the porch panel and a second release decision.

The cheapest moment to do this is during the conversion, while walls and ceilings are open and before the electrician closes up. One extra pair of bell wire, or a single network cable, run from the cellar to each new unit's door and chime costs very little then. Done afterwards, the same run means fishing wire through finished plaster or running it outside, which on a landmarked porch is exactly what nobody wants. We are glad to come before the walls close and mark where each wire should go, even if the electrician pulls it.

The porch and the front door

Can an electric strike go into a hundred-year-old wooden porch door frame without splitting it?

Usually, yes, if the frame is sound and the strike is chosen for it. Old frames here are often a softwood jamb with a thin stop, and the risk is cutting the pocket for the strike too deep or too close to the edge. We use a strike with a shallow body where one fits, cut the pocket by hand rather than with a large bit, and reinforce the jamb behind it if the wood is dry or has old screw holes. If the jamb has rotted, we tell you before cutting, because the right fix then is a carpenter's repair first.

The other half is alignment. A porch door that has dropped on its hinges will press the latch hard against one side of the strike, and a strike under that pressure will buzz without releasing. We adjust the hinge side or the strike plate until the latch sits loosely in the keeper before we connect a wire.

Where is the doorbell transformer in a freestanding Flatbush house, and why is it so hard to find?

Most often in the cellar, screwed to a joist or the side of the electrical panel, sometimes beside the boiler. It is a small box, often painted over or hidden behind later ductwork, with two thin wires leaving it. In some houses it is in a closet near the kitchen or behind the chime cover itself. Older houses can have two, one added when a second unit was created.

It is hard to find because nobody has looked at it since it was installed, and because bell wire is thin and easily mistaken for old telephone wire. We trace it from the chime with a tone generator rather than guessing, and once found, we label it with the units it serves.

Houses with more than one household

Our two units share one doorbell transformer in the cellar. Can it run a video doorbell for each unit?

Sometimes, but check before buying. Bell transformers in old houses are often small, sized for a couple of mechanical chimes, and a video doorbell draws power all the time rather than only when pressed. Two video doorbells plus two chimes on one small transformer can leave both doorbells rebooting or both chimes humming. The rating is printed on the transformer, and we measure its output under load at the visit.

If it is too small, the usual answer is a larger transformer in the same place, or a second one so each unit has its own supply. That second option also means a fault in one apartment's doorbell can no longer take the other unit's bell down with it.

How do we give a new attic or basement unit its own bell without opening the walls?

The options, from simplest to most robust, are a wireless doorbell with a plug-in chime in the new unit, a second wired button using a spare conductor in the existing cable, or a new wire fished through an existing chase such as a closet, a plumbing riser or the stair wall. In a balloon-framed house the stud bays often run uninterrupted from the cellar toward the attic, which can make fishing a wire easier than it sounds.

Wireless bells are fine for a basement unit near the door and less reliable for an attic three floors from a porch, through plaster and lath. We test the signal at the actual chime location before recommending one.

Our porch is being rebuilt this summer. What should the carpenter leave for the doorbell wiring?

Three things. A short length of the existing bell wire left coiled and labelled where the button will go, rather than cut flush; a clear path down to the cellar, such as a small sleeve through the new porch floor framing; and, if you may want a video doorbell later, a spare cable alongside the old one while everything is open. None of it costs much during the rebuild.

Ask the carpenter not to bury splices in the new porch ceiling. If a splice is unavoidable, it belongs in a small accessible box, not behind sealed boards, because that is exactly where porch bells fail. On a house inside the historic district, the porch rebuild itself may need its own Landmarks permit; the doorbell part follows the door-opening rule described above.

The historic district

Which side of the Q tracks puts our house inside the historic district?

The west side, and only part of it. The Beverley Square West Historic District covers Argyle, Rugby and Marlborough Roads between Beverley Road and Cortelyou Road. Houses on East 16th to East 19th Streets, across the tracks in Beverley Square East, are not in it, and neither are Westminster and Stratford Roads to the west, which the commission left out in 2025. The city's PLUTO file records the district name for each included lot, and LPC can confirm any address.

For a buzzer repair the line matters only when the work changes what the street sees. Outside the district, a new panel needs no Landmarks permit at all.

What will Landmarks staff look for if we replace the porch intercom with a video unit?

Location, number and appearance. The Permit Guidebook asks for intercom panels and doorbells within or beside the door opening, or on a fence or wall in front of the entrance; one panel per door opening; and a design, size and finish that suit the house, which in practice means a neutral finish and a modest size. The application needs the manufacturer's cut sheet, and attachment must be to wood, plain masonry or mortar joints rather than decorative surfaces.

We help by choosing a unit that meets those points before you apply and by supplying the cut sheet and photos of the proposed location. The permit is the owner's to file; if anything is unclear, LPC answers questions at 212-669-7817.

The apartment buildings on Beverley and Cortelyou Roads

Why do the big buildings on these two roads keep getting self-closing-door violations, and what does that have to do with the buzzer?

Since Local Laws 62 and 63 of 2022, a self-closing door that is not kept in repair is a Class C violation with 14 days to correct, and HPD re-inspects. On the Beverley Square lots, self-closing door violations rose to 58 in 2025 and 47 in 2026 through October. Entrance and stair doors in busy six-story buildings take a beating, and closers drift out of adjustment.

It matters for the buzzer because a door that does not close and latch makes the intercom pointless: the release works, but the door was already open. Conversely, a closer adjusted too hard drives the latch into the strike so the door will not release. We adjust or replace the closer as part of a lobby-door buzzer call, and we note it in the repair record.

Only the apartments on one side of our six-story building stopped hearing the lobby. Where does a technician start?

With whatever those apartments share and the others do not. In many buildings the riser is split into branches, one per line of apartments or per stair, joined at a terminal strip in the basement or on a landing. When a whole line goes quiet at once, the fault is usually at that junction, a branch splice or a terminal that has worked loose or corroded, not in each apartment.

We test at the junction first, then at the first apartment on the dead line. If the building has an amplifier with separate outputs, one failed output can do the same thing, and that is a component repair rather than new wire.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Door Buzzer Repair in Beverley Square

We could not open Google's AI Overview or similar answer boxes from our build environment, so we cannot quote one. What follows are the generic claims that appear again and again in national how-to articles and cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, set against what the record and the buildings here actually show.

Claim: a door buzzer is an apartment-building system

Generic advice treats a buzzer as a lobby panel wired to apartments by a riser. That is true on Beverley Road and Cortelyou Road. It is not true on Argyle, Rugby or Marlborough Roads or on East 16th to East 19th Streets, where most of the roughly 210 interior lots in our study area are one- and two-family houses.

In a freestanding house the system is a handful of independent bells, often added at different times, and the right repair is closer to an electrician's bell-circuit repair than to building intercom service. Treating it as an intercom leads to quotes for panels and risers that the house does not have.

So the first question we ask is not the brand of panel. It is how many households live in the house and which door each of them uses.

Claim: the landlord is always required to provide a working intercom

Cost guides and tenant articles often say this flatly. Under the Multiple Dwelling Law, the intercom rule applies to multiple dwellings, meaning three or more households, and for buildings from before 1968 it applies when a majority of tenants request it in writing.

A two-family house, which is 74 of the lots in our study area, is not a multiple dwelling. That does not mean a tenant's bell does not matter, only that the source of the obligation is the lease and the general duty to keep a rental in repair, not section 50-a.

For the apartment buildings on the edges the generic claim is closer to the truth: HPD writes intercom violations there under the Housing Maintenance Code's repair duty, and the record shows 67 of them.

Claim: a wireless doorbell solves everything

Wireless doorbells are sold as the easy fix, and for a basement unit next to its own door they often are. In a wood house with plaster and lath walls, though, a chime two or three floors above the porch can sit at the edge of the signal, and several wireless bells on one house can interfere with each other.

They also do not release a door. A household that wants to buzz a visitor in still needs a strike and a wired button or a door station.

We test signal at the real chime location and, where it is weak, use the existing wire or run a new one instead.

Claim: you can't change anything on a landmarked house without a long approval

Owners in the new district sometimes assume any doorbell work needs a hearing. The Landmarks Permit Guidebook says ordinary maintenance and repair of health, safety and utility equipment needs no permit, and a new doorbell or intercom is a staff-level permit, not a public hearing.

The rules are specific and short: within or beside the door opening, one panel per opening, a discreet design, attached to wood, plain masonry or mortar joints, with cut sheets in the application.

That leaves plenty of room for a modern video doorbell on a Queen Anne porch, provided it is chosen with those points in mind.

Claim: buzzer repair costs a flat national average

Cost guides quote national averages for doorbell or intercom repair. Those averages cannot know whether your fault is a painted-over button on a porch or a broken riser pair in a 131-unit building on Cortelyou Road.

Our Brooklyn list separates them: a $250 service call with diagnosis on site, an apartment station replacement at $250 to $525, a door release relay or button at $250 to $475, a transformer at $325 to $650, an electric strike with alignment at $450 to $950, lobby panel repair at $650 to $1,800 and riser repair at $850 to $2,400.

Most house calls here land at the bottom of that list. Most building calls land in the middle.

Claim: if the buzzer sounds but the door won't open, replace the strike

This is the most common piece of generic advice, and it is often wrong. In Beverley Square's wood frames the usual cause is alignment: the door has dropped and the latch is pressed against the strike. In the six-story buildings the usual cause is a closer adjusted too hard after a self-closing door complaint.

Replacing a strike under either condition just moves the problem to a new strike.

We fix the alignment or the closer first and replace the strike only if it fails on test after that.

Claim: old wiring has to be replaced

Generic articles often recommend rewiring an old system. In a house, the bell wire in the walls is usually sound for decades; the failures are at the ends, where buttons, splices and chimes are.

In the edge buildings, older multi-pair risers often have spare pairs, so a broken pair can be swapped without a new run.

We replace wire only where a test shows it is broken, and on a landmarked porch, a sound old wire is worth keeping.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Press Every Button Before You Open Anything

The habit that saves the most time in a converted house is simple. Before opening a single cover, press every button on the property, one at a time, with someone standing in each apartment, and write down what rings where. In a house that has been split into units over decades, that list usually shows the fault before any tool comes out: two buttons on one chime, a basement bell that rings upstairs, a side-door button wired to nothing.

The second habit is to go to the cellar early. The transformer, the splices and often the only access to the porch wiring are down there, and so is the evidence of past work: newer wire spliced onto old, a second transformer nobody mentioned, a run that disappears into a wall that was rebuilt. Ten minutes in the cellar tells us whether the job is a button or a rewiring of the logic.

On the six-story buildings the habit is different: check the door before the panel. Open the entrance door, let it go, and watch whether it closes and latches by itself. If it does not, the intercom may be fine. These are our working habits rather than a story from a particular address, and they are the same on every block from Argyle Road to East 19th Street.

FAQ

Beverley Square Door Buzzer Repair FAQ

Our house on Rugby Road is in the new historic district. Do we need a Landmarks permit just to fix the porch doorbell?
+

Not for a repair. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's Permit Guidebook says ordinary maintenance, repair and retrofitting of health, safety and utility equipment needs no permit, so replacing a button in the same place, re-making a splice or swapping a chime or transformer is fine. A permit, at staff level, comes in when you change what the street sees, such as replacing a plain button with a video doorbell or adding a panel.

We rent the second floor of a two-family on East 17th Street. The owner's bell works and ours does not. Whose wiring is that?
+

Usually it is one shared system: one transformer in the cellar feeding two buttons and two chimes. If the owner's bell works, the transformer is alive and the fault is on your circuit alone, most often your button, your chime or a splice between them. It is the owner's house and the owner's repair to arrange, so the quickest route is to send them a photo of your button and chime and a note of what happens when a visitor presses it.

Can the bell for a basement apartment be at a side door instead of on the front porch?
+

Yes, and when the basement unit has its own entrance at the side or under the porch, that is the better place. Visitors go to the door they will be let in by, and the porch stays free for the households upstairs. On a Beverley Square West house the side-door button follows the same Landmarks rule as any doorbell: within or beside that door's opening. We wire it to a chime in the basement unit only.

Our porch has a storm door in front of the main door. Where should the button go so visitors can reach it?
+

Outside the storm door, on the casing or the wall beside it, so nobody has to open one door to ring at another. If the storm door is kept locked, a button behind it is useless, and that is a common reason a porch bell seems dead when it works. On a house in the Beverley Square West district, a position on the casing or beside the opening fits the Landmarks rule for doorbells. The old button behind the storm door can be removed and its hole filled, or left as is.

Painters keep covering our porch button. How do we protect it the next time the porch is painted?
+

Ask the painter to remove the button before painting and put it back afterward, which takes a minute with a screwdriver on most buttons, or at least to mask it with tape. Painted-over buttons are among the most common porch faults we see: the paint glues the button in, and it either sticks pressed or stops making contact. If yours is already stuck, the button can usually be freed or replaced in the same hole.

Our six-story building on Cortelyou Road has an open HPD intercom violation. What paperwork do you give us after the repair?
+

A dated repair note that names the building, the apartment or location listed in the violation, the part that failed and what was replaced or repaired, signed by the technician. Owners use it as their own record when certifying the correction to HPD. We do not certify on your behalf, and HPD may still re-inspect, so we also test the apartment station and the entrance door while we are there.

We are converting the attic into an apartment. When should the new buzzer wire be run?
+

Before the walls and ceilings are closed. One extra bell pair or one network cable from the cellar to the new unit's chime location and to its door costs very little during the work and a great deal afterwards, when it means fishing wire through finished plaster or running it outside. We can mark the route and the chime location early, even if your electrician pulls the wire.

We want a new porch light and a video doorbell at the same time on a landmarked house. Can they be planned together?
+

Yes, and it is the sensible way to do it. The Landmarks Permit Guidebook groups light fixtures, intercom panels and doorbells together and asks for all of them within or beside the door opening, so one application with both cut sheets can cover the change. Keep them as two separate circuits: the light runs on ordinary house wiring and belongs to an electrician, while the doorbell stays on its own low-voltage transformer. A video doorbell with a built-in light can sometimes replace both, if the porch needs only a little light.
